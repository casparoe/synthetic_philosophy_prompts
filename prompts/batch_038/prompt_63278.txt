I'm a fourth-year political philosophy PhD student and I've somehow ended up as the "theory person" for a worker-ownership conversion fund run by a mid-sized Rust Belt city I grew up near — the program director read a paper I gave at a grad conference and decided that qualified me. The fund lends/grants money (average ask around $180k, roughly 35–40 applications a year, three-person committee that meets monthly) to buy out retiring or exiting owners of small firms and hand the business to the workers. The enabling ordinance has criteria that say the conversion has to "materially reduce exploitation in the workplace" and "increase worker control over the productive process," and in the four years the program has existed nobody has ever said what either phrase means. In practice the case officers — all ex-small-business lenders, none of them have read any philosophy and they aren't going to start — score applications on whether payroll went up and whether there's a board seat for workers, and the committee basically approves anything that looks solvent. My deliverable, due before the October cycle, is a procedure they can actually run.

So: write me that procedure. It has to be usable by a case officer with no theory background in about ninety minutes from an application packet plus a forty-five minute site visit, and it has to output one of four things — not fundable, fund, fund-with-conditions (with the conditions stated concretely enough to go into a term sheet), or escalate to the committee because the question in front of you is about values rather than facts. Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers terminate the run, and which ones you can only get by asking a worker something rather than reading the pro forma. For the grounding: I want it built on what Marx's own account of exploitation is actually claiming — the appropriation of surplus labour, the worker's separation from the means of production, labour power bought and sold as a commodity — not on the watered-down version where "exploitation" just means "the workers get a smaller share than they deserve," because if the procedure quietly does that substitution it becomes a fairness-of-the-split calculator and the ordinance language is doing no work at all. I also want you to bring in the analytic-Marxist reformulation, specifically the withdrawal-style test where a group is exploited if it would be better off leaving with its per capita share of the productive assets (and the others worse off), since that seems to me like the only piece of this literature that a case officer could genuinely evaluate from numbers in a packet — but be honest about what gets lost in that translation, because the withdrawal test measures property distribution and not the labour process. On alienation I don't want the four headings listed at me like a syllabus; I want you to tell me which of those distinctions survive being turned into a form a lender can fill out and which ones I should drop as unmeasurable, and I want the procedure to handle the fact that exploitation and alienation come apart in both directions — you can have a fully worker-owned firm whose members are more alienated than they were under a boss, and you can have real gains in control that change nothing about who appropriates the surplus. No reading list, no citations, nothing the officers would have to look up; explain the concepts inside the procedure itself in plain English.

Then run it on three real-ish applications from last cycle. (1) A 38-person precision machining shop, retiring owner selling to a 100% worker trust, one-member-one-vote, board elected from the floor, wages up 7% with profit share by seniority — but the business plan depends on five to eight agency temps every peak season, and the applicants told us straight that if the aerospace contract lands they'd keep non-member contract machinists on permanently, since members' shares would get too thin otherwise. This one is the director's poster child and it's already in the annual report, so I'm braced for the procedure to give me an answer the office will hate. (2) A home-care agency, about 60 workers, nearly all part-time and nearly all women, converting to a co-op; the county sets the reimbursement rate and the scheduling software breaks the day into fifteen-minute visits, so the members will own the agency and elect the board but will have no say over prices, caseloads, or the increments their work is chopped into. Everyone in the office thinks this one is unfundable because the wage gain is small and the "control" is nominal — I suspect the procedure should split it, and if it splits it I need the procedure to say which half governs the funding decision. (3) A twelve-person specialty coffee roaster offering all staff "equity points" and a participation council while the founder keeps 70% and veto power over hiring; the mayor's office likes it because it's a visible job creator. Also flag the failure modes you can see coming: the temp/contractor loophole, remote and gig-shaped work, applicants where more control means more unpaid labour and the members end up squeezing themselves harder than the market would have, peer discipline replacing managerial discipline so that relations among coworkers get worse rather than better, and workers who simply don't want a vote. Finally, I want a short section at the end stating what this procedure cannot establish — including whether a fund of this size operating inside a market economy can reduce exploitation in Marx's sense at all, or whether the most the honest procedure can do is score local reductions and tell the committee what its own success metric is and isn't entitled to claim. I'd rather that limitation be stated up front than buried, but I definately need the thing to still be usable, so don't let the pessimism eat the checklist.
