I teach a 75-minute weekly seminar called Minds, Machines, and Responsibility to about 20 second-year students, roughly half philosophy and half cognitive science, in a room with movable tables, a stationery cupboard, and no screens. Two weeks ago we covered the digger-wasp example — as I recall it, a wasp stings and paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of the burrow, goes in alone to prepare the nest, and then, if the cricket has been displaced during her absence, drags it back to the entrance and repeats the whole sequence, apparently unable to shorten the routine by simply carrying the cricket inside — and the class agreed in about four minutes that the wasp is a mechanism and they are not, with nobody able to say what the difference consists in. Several of them reached for the fact that they can just decide to break a rule, which struck me as question-begging; one replied that if she wrote down her decision procedure for when to break rules, that procedure would be a mechanism too, and the room had nothing to do with that. I want next week's session to be an activity rather than a discussion, so the group runs into the difficulty instead of reciting intuitions about it, and nothing should be named in advance: they should not hear sphexishness (a label I gather entered philosophical writing through arguments about free will and mechanism, though I have not chased down its provenance), nor the wasp, nor any talk of anti-mechanism, until the debrief.

Here is my sketch, which I would like you to develop into something runnable and to correct where it is naive. Teams of four each receive a sealed envelope containing a short numbered protocol for a simple manual task — sorting, matching, or assembling tokens on a grid — something completable in three minutes and cleanly scorable, with a protocol that works perfectly in round one. Before each later round I silently alter one feature of the environment, and one alteration must create a literal loop: the protocol's own conditional step should return the team to a step it has already completed, so that a team that follows the protocol to the letter repeats steps until the timer ends. Alongside this I want two competing scores: a success score, and a fidelity score awarded by an auditor who compares the team's action log against the protocol line by line, giving points for adherence that persist even when adherence guarantees failure, with the relative weights hidden at first and revealed partway through. My hope is that the most rigid team finishes rich in fidelity points and bankrupt in success points, and that the class then has to decide whether that team was irrational, obedient, or — the answer I expect them to resist — superbly adapted to the game I built for them. A third element: at the end of each round every participant privately writes the exact moment, if any, at which they departed from the protocol, and then tries to state that departure as a rule someone else could follow. I want those written rules collected, handed to a fresh team in a later round, and watched to see whether they work.

I have also been privately sorting rigidity into three kinds, and I want each produced by a different round: protocol-rigidity, where the environment has changed and the team knows it but keeps following the steps because the fidelity points are there; goal-rigidity, where the team abandons the steps but keeps chasing a target that is no longer attainable; and notice-rigidity, where the team never registers that anything has changed at all and completes a procedure whose point has evaporated, which I take to be the wasp's own predicament (please tell me if my natural history is wrong). Be honest about whether these three survive contact with actual students or whether two collapse into one in practice. The deeper fork I want the debrief to reach without my asserting it is this: if participants can write down a rule for when they broke the rule, then either the rule was doing the work all along, in which case their flexibility is a more elaborate mechanism rather than a different kind of thing, or the written rule is a post-hoc story about a decision that had no such procedure, in which case the flexibility they are proud of is something they cannot describe even to themselves. I do not want the session to end by declaring that people beat machines or that machines are people; I want them stuck on that fork.

Please deliver: (1) complete rules and a materials list, stationery-cupboard only, no money — say whether the points should convert into anything trivial; (2) the round-by-round protocol text and the exact environmental alterations, with the loop round spelled out precisely enough that I can build it tonight; (3) the scoring scheme, the auditor's job description, and the moment to reveal the weights; (4) the deviation-log instrument and the mechanics of the rule-handover round; (5) a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes including a debrief question sequence in which each question is tied to a moment the students will actually have lived through; (6) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure — including the near-certainty that one team guesses by round two that I am manipulating them and starts playing the facilitator rather than the protocol, and the risk that the loop round dissolves into giggling and loses its sting; (7) a contingency for the student who blurts out the wasp reference mid-game; and (8) a frank closing note on which features of the phenomenon this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words. I suspect there are at least two such features, having to do with the fact that my students know they are being observed, and with the fact that the wasp has no audience whose approval is being scored.
