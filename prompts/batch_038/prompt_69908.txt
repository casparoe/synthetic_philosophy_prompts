I'm a third-year PhD student in a program that's half philosophy of science and half quantitative social science, so our Friday methods group is a bit of a collision zone. Last week a visiting biostatistician (Nadia) and one of our own faculty (Theo, who works on causation) got into a long argument over one slide from a paper somebody was presenting, and I couldn't follow all of it live. I want to figure out who was right about which bits.

The slide: a city job-training program, outcome is employed at 12 months. Overall, 39% of enrollees vs. 57% of a comparison group of non-enrollees. But stratified by labor-market status recorded at intake, enrollees come out ahead in every stratum:
- employed within the last 6 months: 71% (n=300) vs. 68% (n=1200)
- unemployed more than 12 months: 39% (n=800) vs. 30% (n=420)
- never formally employed: 13% (n=350) vs. 8% (n=45)

The program was explicitly targeted at hard-to-place people, which is why the composition is so lopsided.

The exchange, roughly in order:

(1) Theo said this is the canonical Simpson reversal: the aggregate comparison is confounded by intake status, so you discard it and use the stratified numbers, which all point the same way — the program helps. He added that there's nothing deep about the paradox, it just shows associations don't amalgamate across heterogeneous groups, and the whole fix is to condition on the right variable.

(2) Nadia said "the right variable" is doing way too much work. The enrollees' intake status was filled in by caseworkers who knew the eligibility rules and had an interest in classifying borderline people as long-term unemployed; the comparison group's status came from state administrative records. So the stratifier isn't measured the same way in the two arms, and the cells aren't like-for-like comparisons even once you're inside them.

(3) Theo said that's a measurement quibble that doesn't touch the structure: conditioning on an imperfect proxy for a confounder still moves your estimate toward the truth, you just have residual confounding left over. The direction is preserved.

(4) Nadia said no — and this is the part I really lost. She said suppose there's something unmeasured, like motivation, that influences both how a caseworker classifies a person and whether they get a job. Then adjusting for the proxy can leave you more biased than if you hadn't adjusted at all. She said that's a known effect and not a curiosity.

(5) Theo said fine, but that leaves you with the aggregate number, which is guaranteed to be biased, since you can see with your eyes that the two groups are composed completely differently. A maybe-wrong estimate beats a certainly-wrong one. And he said his claim was only sign-level anyway: he doesn't need the stratified differences to be precise, only to have the same sign in all three cells, which survives a lot of measurement noise.

(6) Nadia said the sign-level framing is smuggled in. The never-employed cell has 45 people in the comparison arm, so that difference is just noise; the reversal is really carried by the middle cell, which is exactly the cell where the caseworker classification is most suspect. Saying "every stratum" is doing rhetorical work.

(7) Then she pulled up a doubly-stratified table adding whether the person did at least 20 hours of vocational coursework during the follow-up year — recorded for both arms, and partly caused by the program itself. Within intake status and coursework hours, enrollees did worse in most cells. Theo said that table is uninterpretable because you can't condition on something that happens after the program starts. She said agreed, and that was her point: nothing in the data tells you which table is the right one, you have to bring that in from outside.

(8) Theo conceded that, but said the argument had by then proven too much. If you can't trust the stratified table and you can't trust the aggregate table, the honest upshot is that the reversal shows nothing about effectiveness — only that the composition difference between the groups is enormous, which is a finding about targeting.

What I want: go through (1)–(8) and rate each move, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating. Then tell me which specific sub-claims each of them actually won. Is (3) false as stated or just incomplete? Is (4) a real phenomenon or is she overplaying it to win the point? Is "maybe-wrong beats certainly-wrong" in (5) a legitimate principle or a dodge, and does his retreat to a sign-level claim in the same move actually rescue him from (6)? Was Theo right in (7) that the mediator table is uninterpretable, and does that help him or hurt him? Is (8) the correct place to land, or is he giving up something he's entitled to keep? And where, if anywhere, are the two of them talking past each other rather than genuinely disagreeing.

Finally: what are both of them missing? Anything about what a reversal like this can and can't license that neither one said — including whether either table has a legitimate use they both overlooked — and what single additional piece of information about how the data was collected would most change your verdict.
