I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a cognitive scientist, and one of my oldest — about whether dreams are experiences, and I lost my temper somewhere around two in the morning. My own position is roughly Norman Malcolm's, as he developed it in *Dreaming*: that "I dreamed that p" is not a report of a conscious occurrence that took place while the sleeper slept; that the only thing there ever is to go on is the impression on waking, so that there is no fact of the matter, independent of that impression, about what was going through someone's head at four a.m.; that a dream is not an event and does not occupy time. I want to be careful how I put this, because it is so easily caricatured: I am not saying that people do not dream, and I am not saying that dream-telling is confused or that the reports are unreliable. I am saying that "dream" does not name an inner performance. My friend's view is that this was settled against me in 1976, by Dennett's paper of precisely that title in the *Philosophical Review* — which, as I understand it, objects that the received picture is an empirical hypothesis rather than a conceptual truth, attacks the assumption that a dream must be something like a recording composed during the night and replayed upon waking, and takes seriously the alternative that the content is composed at the moment of the report, an alternative he thinks a laboratory might in principle test. Then he brought out the decoding work: scanner data collected from sleeping subjects that predicts which category of imagery they will report on waking. My reply — that a prediction of the report is still only a prediction of the report — did not move him at all, and by the end he was making the charge I find hardest to answer, which is that a position no conceivable measurement could disturb is not a position but a refusal.

The reason I am asking you rather than him is that the argument is about to become professional. In six weeks my undergraduate mentor, Professor Helen Marsh, retires after thirty-eight years directing a sleep and dream laboratory, and I have been asked to give the toast at her farewell dinner. There will be about ninety people: her former students and technicians, colleagues from the sleep societies, three or four philosophers including my friend, her husband and her daughter (who still remembers, from age seven, being carried out of a nightmare), and a scattering of undergraduates who know her only as the person who lets them run the awakening shifts. She is, unlike me, a thoroughgoing Dennettian; she keeps a falling-apart copy of *Brainstorms* on her desk, she built a graduate seminar around the 1976 paper, and for twenty years she has introduced me to people as her Malcolmian fossil, which is her way of being affectionate. Two of her findings are famous in the field: that the same sleeper, awakened twice a few minutes apart, will give reports that differ substantially in content; and that awakenings from non-REM sleep are not blank but yield reports of a different kind — more thought-like, less imagistic. She has always described those as measurements of what happened to people in the night. I can only describe them as measurements of a disposition to report. That disagreement will be sitting at the head table.

So: write the toast, seven to nine hundred words, in a spoken register, four to six minutes. It has to be deliverable by someone who holds Malcolm's view without a single sentence that commits me to the received one — nothing about what her subjects saw in the night, nothing about being taken into their dreams, no "what the sleeping brain was doing at four a.m." used as the description of an experience — and, just as strictly, without a single sentence that implies to a room full of scientists that their life's work measured nothing. It must not argue with the audience or settle the dispute; there is room for exactly one joke about it, and it should be affectionate enough to work for someone who has never heard of Malcolm while landing differently for the four people at the top table who have. The praise needs to be specific and to remain true under both readings, which is the real difficulty. No quotations from either text — paraphrase only — and no citations, which would be grotesque at a dinner. No Descartes, and no "how do I know I am not dreaming now"; and please, no "dream job" pun, which has been made at every talk she has given since the nineteen-nineties. The final line must be one I can raise a glass to sincerely and that both a Malcolmian and a Dennettian in that room could drink to sincerely, so build toward it rather than bolting it on. After the speech, add two or three lines of notes telling me where, if anywhere, you think you bent the Malcolmian line to keep the peace. And if you conclude that my friend is right — that the toast cannot be written under these constraints, because any praise of her work I can honestly give commits me to her metaphysics — say so plainly in those notes, and then write the closest thing that can be written.
