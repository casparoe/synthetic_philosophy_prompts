I've read the simulation-argument literature and the longtermist exchanges around it for the better part of two decades, and I'm convinced both camps have been pushing on the wrong premise. Turn the following half-formed idea into a proposal for a three-month independent research grant from a small fund that supports work at the intersection of decision theory and long-run futures.

The idea. Nearly every discussion treats the simulation hypothesis as bearing on the *magnitude* of our expected future: are simulated futures long, are they switched off early, and what does the existence of ancestor simulations tell us about the base rate of civilizations that survive to run them? I want to argue that this is the least interesting channel, and that simulation mainly changes the *structure* of the decision problem rather than the size of the payoff. If our continuation depends on the simulators' interest in us, then our actions are correlated with whether there is a far future at all, and not solely through the causal chain running via our descendants. That makes the matter partly evidential: a civilization that looks like a finished experiment may be terminated, while one that keeps producing surprises may be continued — and the sign of this selection effect is not obvious in either direction. Fanaticism, the view that tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs should dominate present decisions, is unusually sensitive to it, because that view needs the payoff term to be roughly independent of the act that supposedly secures it.

What I need from you: the single question the project would answer, stated so that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the standard readings of the trilemma leave that question open; the method, week by week if three months allows, including which of the three channels — size of the simulated future, inheritance from the simulators' own history, selection on our behavior — I would model first and why in that order; what a positive result would look like and what a negative one would look like; and an honest verdict on whether the evidential/causal distinction is doing real work here or merely relabeling an ordinary anthropic calculation. No bibliography; I'll handle references myself. Open with a one-sentence statement of the question you would actually pursue, and keep the whole thing under 700 words — if you have to cut, cut the timeline rather than the framing.
