At a workshop on value drift in deployed systems, one of the speakers argued for a policy conclusion I found striking, and he supported it with an analogy I would like audited rather than with further argument. The conclusion was this: an agent that expects its own future self to hold different attitudes should, precisely to that extent, adopt them now; therefore there is no rational loss in permitting a system's values and credences to drift across training and deployment, and no standing reason to build in lock-in or anti-drift machinery, because whatever the later system expects to endorse is what the earlier system ought already to endorse. The principle he invoked was van Fraassen's Reflection, stated in its conditional form: my credence now in a hypothesis H, conditional on my credence in H at some later time being x, ought itself to be x. He then offered the following gloss, to show that the principle is not mysterious: your future self is like a consultant you have already retained. You know her track record better than you know anyone's, since it is your own; you have effectively paid her fee in advance; and by the time she reports she will have seen strictly more of the evidence than you have. So you should take her recommendations now, sight unseen, in just the way you would take a vetted expert's.

I want the analogy pulled apart, not the slogan re-defended. Please give me:

(a) An explicit mapping. Consultant, retainer fee, track record, the body of evidence she has seen, the client's own information, the advice itself, and the client's option to discount or dismiss it — what in the Reflection case plays each of these roles? Where the correspondence has no natural filler, say so rather than straining one into place.

(b) The features the source carries that the target lacks. In particular: the consultant is an independent source of information about a subject matter that stays fixed while the two of you investigate it, and she is assessed against standards she shares with you. Does the future self meet each of those conditions — independence, fixed subject matter, shared standards — and what follows if one of them fails?

(c) A separation of the disanalogies that actually bear on his policy conclusion from the merely decorative ones. Several things will be wrong with the picture without hurting the inference at all; I want to know which of them do hurt it.

(d) His verdict on three cases he did not raise: a system that expects its later self to have been shaped by sycophancy pressure or by adversarial fine-tuning; a person who expects to undergo a religious conversion; a person who expects to become a parent and to weigh things differently afterward. In each, does Reflection go silent, forbid the shift, or permit it — and is the consultant story describing the principle accurately or only describing the cases where it happens to feel plausible?

(e) An assessment of a reframing I suspect is doing quiet work. Reflection is usually motivated as a coherence constraint, via a diachronic betting argument about the prices one offers now and later, rather than as practical wisdom about whom to trust. Does recasting it as a claim about a trustworthy adviser license the move from "your credences must be mutually consistent across time" to "the later self's attitudes are the ones to adopt"?

(f) Whether the conclusion about drift and lock-in survives once (b) through (e) are on the table — and whether the analogy survives independently of that conclusion, since even if the policy claim fails, the gloss might still be the best available intuition pump for Reflection itself.

(g) A better analogy, if there is one, together with an account of what it gets right that the consultant does not.

Two further points I would like addressed inside your answer rather than treated as pedantic asides. First, the conditional form of the principle applies when the agent is certain what her later credence will be; say what the consultant story looks like when she is not certain, and whether any version of it extends that far. Second, in the analogy the client and the consultant are two people, whereas here the one paying the fee is the one delivering the advice; tell me whether that identification is load-bearing for his argument or merely a way of speaking.
