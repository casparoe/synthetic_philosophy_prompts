ok so I'm maybe 60% through a novel and I've got a problem that isn't really a craft problem, it's a thinking problem, and you're cheaper than my therapist.

The book follows one family across four generations — a great-grandmother who comes out of a war and a camp, her daughter who does very well for herself, the grandson who ends up prosecuting people. Structurally it's an argument about whether the arc of history bends toward justice, and I keep letting the grandson make these sweeping claims in court and at dinner tables. Because I don't want to write him as a strawman (or as a saint), I went and started reading Hegel on history, and I've got stuck in the introduction to his Lectures on the History of Philosophy — some old public-domain English translation I pulled off an archive site. This is the chunk I can't get through:

"What the history of Philosophy shows us is a succession of noble minds, a gallery of heroes of thought, who, by the power of Reason, have penetrated into the being of things, of nature and of spirit, into the Being of God, and have won for us by their labours the highest treasure, the treasure of reasoned knowledge."

[...]

"The events and actions of this history are therefore such that personality and individual character do not enter to any large degree into their content and matter. In this respect the history of Philosophy contrasts with political history, in which the individual, according to the peculiarity of his disposition, talents, affections, the strength or weakness of his character, and in general, according to that through which he is this individual, is the subject of actions and events. In Philosophy, the less deserts and merits are accorded to the particular individual, the better is the history; and the more it deals with thought as free, with the universal character of man as man, the more this thought, which is devoid of special characteristic, is itself shown to be the producing subject."

[...]

"But this tradition is not only a stewardess who simply guards faithfully that which she has received, and thus delivers it unchanged to posterity, just as the course of nature in the infinite change and activity of its forms ever remains constant to its original laws and makes no step in advance. Such tradition is no motionless statue, but is alive, and swells like a mighty river, which increases in size the further it advances from its source. The content of this tradition is that which the intellectual world has brought forth, and the universal Mind does not remain stationary."

[...]

"This is the function of our own and every age: to grasp the knowledge which is already existing, to make it our own, and in so doing to develop it still further and to raise it to a higher level. In thus appropriating it to ourselves we make it into something different from what it was before. On the presupposition of an already existing intellectual world which is transformed in our appropriation of it, depends the fact that Philosophy can only arise in connection with previous Philosophy, from which of necessity it has arisen. The course of history does not show us the Becoming of things foreign to us, but the Becoming of ourselves and of our own knowledge."

Help me actually understand this rather than summarising it back at me in nicer words. Four things:

1. He opens with a gallery of heroes of thought and then two paragraphs later says the less deserts and merits are accorded to the individual, the better the history is. Contradiction, or a distinction I'm missing — something like individuals being the vehicle but never the source? And "this thought ... is itself shown to be the producing subject": producing subject of what, exactly, and what would it look like for that claim to be false? I want to know if it's a claim that could fail.

2. What is the stewardess and the motionless statue and the swelling river doing for him? I get that tradition alters as it's passed on, but he also says appropriation makes the knowledge "something different from what it was before." Different how — better, or merely different? Is there any standard internal to the process that tells you the river got bigger rather than muddier, or is bigger just assumed to be better?

3. He seperates all this off from political history, where the individual's dispositions and character really are the subject of events. So where does moral improvement sit in that split? Does anything in these paragraphs license going from "knowledge accumulates and each generation re-grasps it at a higher level" to "things get more just"? Also — he puts the Being of God in there with the heroes, so is there a providence steering this, or is "universal Mind" just his name for the cumulative process with no one at the wheel? I need to know whether my prosecutor is allowed to borrow Hegel's teleology for justice or whether he's stealing it.

4. Do this one last and be blunt. The entire account is narrated from the endpoint. The river only looks like it was going somewhere once you're standing at its mouth, and "raise it to a higher level" is higher as measured by us, the ones doing the measuring and the inheriting. Is Hegel alive to that and answering it, or is he installing his own standpoint as the terminus and then calling the view from there objective? If there's an answer sitting in the passage, point at the sentence that's doing the work — and if there isn't, say so, because that decides whether my last chapter is honest or a fraud.
