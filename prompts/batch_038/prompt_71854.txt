I am writing a seminar paper in a philosophy of perception course, and my thesis is that Molyneux's question is substantive rather than a quarrel about how we have defined our words. The question, as I will put it without quoting anyone: a man born blind, now adult, has been taught by touch alone to tell a cube from a sphere of the same metal and nearly the same size; the two are set on a table and he is made to see; can he, by sight alone and before touching them, say which is which? Before I work through the answers, I want the specification of what I am entitled to demand of one. So: write the desiderata for an account of cross-modal spatial content that would be good enough to make the newly sighted subject's success or failure a fact about the world, not a verdict about stipulation.

Give me the requirements as a numbered list, each with the case or consideration behind it and each marked as non-negotiable or as the local commitment of a particular camp. Some of the raw material I can supply, since part of it is empirical and I have it from the seminar readings. First, preverbal infants who mouth a pacifier with nubs on it, without ever seeing it, subsequently look longer at the nubby pacifier than at a smooth one when both are shown, so some transfer is available before concepts or words. Second, patients treated in later childhood or adolescence for dense congenital cataract were, within hours of the bandages coming off, responsive to movement and to gross differences in brightness, had very poor acuity, and could not match a shape they had known only by hand to the shape they now saw; that matching ability developed rapidly over the following days and weeks. Third, the historical answers split: Molyneux and Locke said no, on the ground that the man has never learned how what affects his touch affects his sight; Leibniz inclined to yes, on the ground that extension is common to both modalities and can be carried from one to the other; Reid gave a conditional answer, separating plane figures, which look much as they feel, from solids, which do not, and allowing that a blind geometer might recover the correspondence by calculation.

Against that material, produce the list. At a minimum I expect it to say whether the question concerns the instant case or the case after a week of sight with no further touching, and why the other reading is not a dodge; to keep concept possession distinct from the capacity to deploy a concept in a new modality, and to say which the question is about; to separate correct forced-choice pointing from a report of which is which, including the case of above-chance pointing alongside a sincere denial of any idea which is which; to explain the plane/solid asymmetry rather than assume it away; to be non-circular about how the mapping is learned, if learned it is; and to issue in something checkable about shape complexity and familiarity. Then add whatever further requirements you judge genuinely binding, and say which requirements people in this debate demand that you think are prejudices.

There is one case I have not been able to formulate a requirement around, and I would like you to do it. Suppose the newly sighted subject is given, before he is shown anything at all, a complete verbal and geometric description of what a cube looks like from a fixed distance — the projected angles, the way a corner hides what stands behind it — and has had a lifetime of practice reasoning in exactly those terms. Whether the answer to Molyneux's question flips in this case seems to depend on claims about description, imagery, and access that the ordinary statement of the problem never touches. Tell me what your list requires here, and what an account owes either way: if the answer flips, an explanation of what the words supplied that the eyes did not; if it does not, an explanation of why the description is idle.

Finally, tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidates, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I have a standing suspicion that the non-circularity requirement collides with the cataract data — that no learning story gets off the ground unless something cross-modal is in place before sight, which is just what those data seem to deny — but I cannot bring the collision into focus, and if the list turns out to be an impossibility result rather than a checklist, I would rather discover that now. A short paragraph each on how the three historical answers fare is welcome at the end, but the list is the deliverable, and please do not send me to the literature.
