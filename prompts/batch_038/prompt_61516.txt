I'm a second-year archaeology undergrad, also doing a philosophy minor, and I'm in a phil-of-science seminar right now where we spent three weeks on Hempel's ravens. Somehow I've also been handed the opening remarks at a small redisplay our university museum is doing next Thursday — 6pm, free, they're expecting maybe 70 people. I get about four minutes.

The situation. The collection is something like forty Bronze Age coastal burials dug in the 1930s, and for ninety years the labels have told visitors it was a warrior aristocracy, on the strength of two or three rich graves with bronze weapons in them. Two postdocs in our department have just finished a reanalysis and they think pretty much the opposite: the rich burials look disturbed or mixed, and across the whole sample there's almost no differentiation — no ranking in grave size, no rich goods, no monumental anything, no craft specialization. So the museum is flipping its own labels. My job is to introduce that flip to a room.

Here's the problem I can't get around, and it's the raven thing wearing a trowel. The new reading leans almost entirely on absences — no rich goods, no big graves, no hierarchy — and every single plain grave in that sample "confirms" the flat-community claim in exactly the bloodless way that a non-black non-raven confirms all ravens are black. Whereas one undisturbed rich grave would have settled the old argument immediately. Absences are also cheap: the 1930s excavation was bad, the collection is only what survived curation, organic prestige goods rot, and maybe status was expressed in textiles or feasting or houses nobody ever dug. The inference is not symmetrical and I dont want to stand up there pretending it is.

The audience is a mess. Three faculty, one of whom defended the old interpretation in print and is not thrilled. Two donors who like confident stories about chiefs and treasure. A local heritage society whose members were taught the warrior-chiefdom version in school in the 1970s. About fifteen people from an outreach program, several of whom have never been inside a museum before. And a regional-paper journalist who has already been promised the headline "museum reverses itself."

Write the speech. It has to work as a real welcome — this is an opening, not a seminar, and it should feel warm — but nobody can leave believing the flat-community reading is established, and it can't trash the 1930s excavators (the donors funded them and the faculty trained under their students). The presence/absence point should drive the structure of what the speech is allowed to say and in what order, not show up as a cute digression in the middle. And I need one moment where the audience is made part of the inference rather than just handed a verdict — but it has to survive contact with the fifteen people who've never seen an excavation plan, so no jargon that only lands for the faculty.

Format: give it as exactly five numbered passages, in the order they'd actually be spoken, each three to five sentences, and nothing else — no preface, no delivery notes, no closing comment from you. The five passages should have visible jobs: the welcome, the old story, the flip, the honesty about absence, the invitation. Around 550 words total.
