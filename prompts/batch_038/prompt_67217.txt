I'm in my fourth year of a political theory PhD, and this spring I'm co-teaching a small seminar with a colleague from the law school's technology clinic on freedom and algorithmic governance. We have twelve students: four political theory graduate students, six law students in their second and third years, and two from the information school. All of them have encountered Berlin's two concepts of liberty, and nearly all have self-diagnosed as defenders of the negative conception, largely because it sounds more hard-headed than anything else on the menu. The law students in particular tend to treat republican non-domination either as a synonym for non-interference or as a soft way of saying that poor people need resources; two of them wrote last week's response paper arguing that a platform's discretionary power over a merchant's storefront is simply a matter of contract and therefore irrelevant to freedom. For our third session I want to break those self-diagnoses with a sorting instrument rather than another reading, so that students discover which conception they are actually committed to from how they answer cases. The session runs twenty-five minutes, my colleague insists the exercise be completable without the assigned readings in front of them, and I'll be printing it double-sided.

Please write a ten-item sorting quiz for this seminar. Every item should be a concrete scenario drawn from governance by algorithm, platform, or administrator, with two or three answer options, and each scenario should stay under roughly ninety words. I want the cases to include, at minimum: a credit-scoring model that has the standing capacity to refuse you a loan but has never exercised it against you, since you sit in its top decile; an employer's monitoring suite that logs everything you do but reviews the logs only when you trip a flag, under a policy reserving review to the firm's sole discretion; a municipal traffic system that continuously reroutes you around congestion and is calibrated to destinations you genuinely want; a storefront you operate on a platform that could remove it tomorrow with or without cause, under standards you did not help write and an appeals process on which you hold no seat; an immigration caseworker with very broad discretion who has, in every case to date, decided in the applicant's favor; a state-issued digital wallet that the issuing authority could freeze, where you hold a full democratic vote over who runs that authority; a household in which one partner controls all income and has never once refused a request; a person formally unrestricted in every legal respect who cannot afford to leave the town they live in; a predictive-policing model whose error thresholds you helped set through a participatory process, and which now constrains you; and a dormant statute criminalizing something you do weekly, unenforced in living memory and repealable only by a legislature you cannot influence. Across these items the quiz should probe the axes on which the conceptions genuinely come apart: whether an unexercised capacity to interfere costs you freedom; whether interference that tracks your own avowed interests is still domination; whether what matters is the source of a constraint in another's will or its effect on the size of your option set; whether a shortfall of resources is itself unfreedom or merely an obstacle to using freedom you already have; whether domination requires an identifiable agent, or whether you can be dominated by a system with no responsible will behind it; whether having a say in a decision removes the domination or merely renders it non-arbitrary; and whether democracy is constitutive of freedom or only instrumentally protective of it. No item may ask which label the respondent prefers, and none may use the vocabulary of the positions in its own text: domination, negative liberty, autonomy, capability, and so on belong in the scoring key, not in the questions.

The scoring key should sort by combinations of answers rather than by single items, and it should map onto at least these leaves: Berlin-style non-interference; neo-Roman non-domination, with its internal split over whether private as well as public power can dominate; the Kantian-right view that freedom is independence from being subject to another's choice rather than the absence of interference; positive liberty understood as self-realization or actual self-direction; the capability or effective-freedom view on which freedom is a function of what you can actually do and be; and a deflationary leaf holding that freedom is not the value at issue in these cases at all, and that legitimacy, authority, or justice is doing the real work. Designate two of your items as explicit tie-breakers for the pairs the instrument will struggle to separate, and flag those pairs honestly rather than papering over them: I expect the non-domination and Kantian-independence leaves to resist separation on the private-power cases, and I expect the traffic-system and participatory-model items to blur republicanism with the capability view. Then identify two or three unoccupied cells in the logical space your items generate, combinations of answers no recognized position endorses, and diagnose each briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Each leaf should close with one sentence on its most awkward commitment, phrased so that a student who lands there cannot wave it off. Please paraphrase rather than quote from the primary literature; I would much rather have a clean case than a mangled citation, and no bibliography is needed, since attribution by name is enough for a handout of this kind. Aim for roughly eighteen hundred words in total.
