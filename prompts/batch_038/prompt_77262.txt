I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for the past several years I have also served as outside adjudicator for an essay prize run by a university's interdisciplinary seminar on social choice and distributive justice. Finalists sit a set take-home under timed conditions, with no reference works permitted; I then read the scripts cold and return written feedback. I have not marked this year's script on the question reproduced below, and I do not want to steer you, so please approach it as an unknown quantity.

The set question was:

> Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. State its assumptions and its conclusion as precisely as you can, and then assess the claim that the theorem establishes classical utilitarianism. Two pages. No references.

The finalist's answer runs as follows.

> Harsanyi's aggregation theorem concerns a fixed population of individuals who face a common set of prospects — lotteries over social states — and whose preferences over those prospects admit an expected utility representation. Three assumptions do the work. First, each individual's preferences satisfy the axioms of expected utility theory, so each has a utility function over outcomes that is unique up to positive affine transformation. Second, the individuals share the same subjective probabilities over states of the world; if they disagree about the likelihood of a state, Harsanyi's argument does not go through. Third, social preferences satisfy the Pareto principle in the form of Pareto indifference: if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them as well. Social preferences are themselves assumed to admit an expected utility representation.
>
> Given these assumptions, the conclusion is that there exists a social utility function which is a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, with non-negative weights summing to one. That social function is itself defined only up to positive affine transformation, so the theorem is in a sense an ordinal result about a cardinal representation.
>
> The theorem does not yet deliver utilitarianism, because the weights are not pinned down. To obtain equal weights one adds a symmetry or anonymity condition: the social ordering must not depend on which individual occupies which position in the description of a state. This is where Harsanyi's veil-of-ignorance device enters, supplying an intuitive story about why an impartial observer would assign equal weights. It is worth noting that additivity is doing real work here. Because the social function must be linear in the individual utilities, positions that apply a concave transform to each person's utility before summing — prioritarianism, in effect — are excluded by the theorem rather than merely left unmentioned.
>
> Whether the theorem establishes classical utilitarianism depends on how much one is prepared to build into the premises. Two objections are standard. The first is that Harsanyi assumes utilities are interpersonally comparable, which is precisely what an ordinalist refuses to grant; the theorem therefore packs the controversial material into the assumptions and then unpacks it in the conclusion. The second is that, like Arrow's impossibility theorem, Harsanyi's argument takes non-dictatorship for granted, and it is only because dictatorship has been ruled out by assumption that the weighted sum can be presented as a genuinely social rather than a merely individualistic aggregation rule.
>
> On balance the theorem is better read as establishing a family of weighted-utilitarian rules than as establishing classical utilitarianism. The equality of the weights is a further substantive commitment rather than a consequence of the Pareto principle, and it is the part most in need of independent defense. The theorem is also silent about population: it says nothing about how to rank social states containing different numbers of people, so it cannot by itself settle any question about the ethics of bringing new people into existence.
