Context: I'm the tech lead for a simulation library that gets used in certification work (structural and aero-thermal). Most months we get a handful of proposals to add a closed-form relation — a drag correlation, a fatigue-life curve, a turbulence closure, a materials model. Every candidate has to be tagged in the metadata as either CONSTRAINT (solvers may enforce it as a hard equation, including outside the range it was fitted on, and downstream teams may extrapolate with it) or CORRELATION (interpolate only, hard error outside its validated domain). Right now that tag is decided by whoever has the most seniority in the room, and I want to replace that with a procedure a reviewer can actually run.

Why I think philosophy is relevant here: the distinction I'm trying to draw is pretty much the one philosophers argue about when they ask what makes a regularity a law of nature rather than a true generalization that's just an accident. Humeans say a law is what falls out of the best systematisation of everything — the one that best trades simplicity off against strength, with the later versions letting probabilistic axioms in too. Anti-Humeans say there's something over and above that: laws govern, they support counterfactuals, they'd have held if things had been otherwise. What both sides agree on is the part I care about: a statement being true of everything you've measured doesn't make it lawlike. That's exactly my problem.

So write me the procedure. Ordered questions, what counts as a passing answer to each, how much evidence is enough to move on, where it stops and defaults to CORRELATION, and where it hands the call to a human because the question isn't answerable from the data at all. Mark the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedural costume — I'd rather know than have my reviewers assume the form settled it. Constraints: about 45 minutes per candidate, reviewers have engineering degrees and zero philosophy, the output has to be the tag plus a one-line justification plus what specific evidence would change the tag, and it gets signed off by me and one independent reviewer.

It has to cope with the cases that break naive versions of this:
