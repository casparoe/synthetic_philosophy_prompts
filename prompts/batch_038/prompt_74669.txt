I argued with my friend Nadia for like three hours last night about who should get a vote, and I've come away pretty sure my own positions don't hang together, but I can't tell which ones I'm supposed to drop. So here they all are, roughly in the order they came out at the kitchen table. Sixteen-year-olds in school board and city elections: yes, easy. Sixteen-year-olds in national elections: also yes, and I don't feel any difference that matters between those two. Fourteen-year-olds: no. When Nadia asked what changes between 14 and 16 I had nothing except "we have to draw the line somewhere," which she said was exactly the problem. A specific 13-year-old I know who follows the budget debates more closely than most adults I know: still no, and I'm fine with that, because age is a blunt proxy and I'll happily use a blunt proxy here. A guy who's lived here 22 years, pays his taxes, kids went through the local schools, never became a citizen because he couldn't be bothered: should vote, local AND national. A tourist who happens to be here for two weeks during an election: obviously no. A citizen born abroad who's never lived here, never paid a cent of tax here, votes by mail from a country she's visited twice: no — and I know how bad that one looks sitting next to the previous one, since the only thing seperating them is a passport.

Then compulsory voting. Australia-style, turn up or pay a small fine: I'm for it. But if its going to drag you to a booth it has to let you hand in a blank ballot and count as having complied, and it shouldn't be able to fine you for deliberately spoiling your ballot as a protest. Prisoners: no vote while they're inside, restored on release — and I couldn't tell Nadia why the line is release rather than the end of the sentence, since people on parole are still being punished. My neighbour with advancing dementia, who's voted in every election for fifty years and still lights up when his daughter drives him to the polling station: absolutely keep him on the roll, and no, I don't want anyone administering a competence test to decide. Which is where I got properly stuck, because two minutes earlier I was happy to exclude a bright, well-informed 13-year-old on the grounds that testing people individually is impractical and invasive. Blunt instruments are fine going one direction and intolerable going the other, and I have no story about why. And the case I keep flip-flopping on, the one she finally got me with: proxy votes for parents — Demeny voting, she called it, where a parent casts an extra half-vote for each underage kid. My gut says no, it's ghoulish, you're handing parties a bonus for breeding. But she said if my principle is that everyone whose interests are on the line should have a say, then children are the single most affected group there is on things like climate and public debt, they have zero say, and refusing to let anyone speak for them isn't respect for children, it's just silence with a nicer label. I had no answer to that and I still don't. Relatedly: Mill's plural voting, extra votes for the better educated, from Considerations on Representative Government — I think that's flatly unjust. But I do think political judgment comes in degrees and that I'm allowed to act on that when it comes to a 13-year-old, so I'm not sure I'm consistent there either.

So: is there a principle of enfranchisement that gets all or most of this, and what does it actually key on — interests affected, being subject to the state's coercion, capacity to make a competent choice, membership, or some combination with a priority ordering between them? State it as precisely as you can, one or two candidates if they genuinely differ, and say which of my cases each clause is there to handle, because I want to see whether the clauses are ad hoc patches or doing real work. Then run it on three cases I didn't bring up: a long-term resident non-citizen voting in a referendum on independence, i.e. on whether to create the state they'd then belong to; someone with two passports who can vote in both countries in the same year; and lowering the age to 16 only if you also pass a civics quiz we'd never dream of giving a 45-year-old. And finally tell me straight which of my verdicts have to go. If the cheapest coherent principle costs me the dementia case, or costs me the bright-13-year-old case, or forces me to accept proxy votes for kids, I'd rather know that than get handed a baroque theory with eleven clauses that saves everything and explains nothing.
