I am an adjunct teaching one fifty-minute discussion section of a required introduction to political theory, and this week's assigned text is an excerpt from Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theater — his 1758 reply to d'Alembert's suggestion, in the Encyclopédie article on Geneva, that the city would be improved by a playhouse. The section is a problem. Eleven of the fourteen students are cross-registered from the conservatory's acting and design programs to satisfy a humanities requirement, the university announced last month that the mainstage season is being cut from four productions to two, and three of these students were in the cast of the show that got dropped. They have skimmed the excerpt, they have not read the Discourse on the Arts and Sciences or the Social Contract, and they arrive expecting either a Puritan who wants to ban their art or, worse, a philosophical alibi for the administration's budget memo. I have a whiteboard, no slides, and no ability to assume anything about eighteenth-century Geneva beyond what I can put on that board in two lines.

I do not want you to explain Rousseau's view to me; I want a plan for explaining it to them. Lay out the sequence and, more importantly, the dependency structure behind it: which claim has to be in place before another one is even intelligible. For instance, must the thesis that theater pleases us by lending a polished form to sentiments we already hold — rather than by correcting them — come before the institutional critique of spectatorship, or is the reverse order stronger with this audience? Where does his handling of the claim that tragedy purges the passions belong: in the middle as a hinge, up front as a provocation, or cut entirely? And at what point should the open-air festival he counterposes to the theater, the one in which the crowd becomes its own spectacle, arrive — as the payoff at the end, or as an early hook that gives them something to want before I take the theater away? Then tell me which single case should carry the whole fifty minutes, and what that case must have and must lack. I am inclined to run everything through Molière's The Misanthrope, since Rousseau singles it out for making the upright man ridiculous and the accommodating one agreeable; but I suspect that with this group a play none of them love will let them treat the argument as a museum piece, while a production of their own will let them treat it as a personal insult. Say what the ideal carrying example needs to supply, and whether it should be a work, a specific night in a specific house, or one of their own performances.

I also want the misconception map, because the failure modes here seem unusually predictable. My list: that Rousseau is a censor objecting to the content of plays rather than to the structure of an institution; that he denies the purgation of the passions out of piety rather than out of an account of how pleasure works; that the festival is just a warmer, cheaper kind of theater, so the whole distinction is aesthetic preference dressed up as principle; that he objects to actors because acting is lying; and that any play which demonstrably changed someone's mind refutes him outright. Tell me where in the sequence each of these should be headed off, what exactly to say at that moment, and — this matters — which one I should deliberately leave standing as a live objection for them to press instead of defusing it. Give me one closing check for understanding that would distinguish grasping the argument from merely disliking it, ideally a small case they would have to classify under Rousseau's own criteria (a devised piece performed in the round by their own classmates may be the right shape, but tell me whether that gives the answer away), and finish with an explicit list of what I should cut at this level and announce as cut — the Genevan economics, the remarks on the actor's trade and on actresses, his own career writing for the stage, the link back to his earlier polemic against the arts and sciences — so that the omissions are decisions rather than accidents. Write the plan in American English, and do not include a bibliography, a list of editions, or any suggested further reading: the deliverable is a teaching plan for an explanation, not the explanation itself.
