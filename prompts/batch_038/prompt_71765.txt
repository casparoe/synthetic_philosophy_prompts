I'm writing a paper for a seminar on infinity and the philosophy of time, and I can't resolve a difficulty with Thomson's lamp. As I read it, the lamp is toggled at intervals that keep halving — a half-minute, then a quarter, then an eighth — so that all of the switchings are finished by some fixed later moment, yet the on-off sequence never settles, which is why Thomson says there is no coherent state for the lamp to be in at that moment. But I don't see why he needs the stronger claim that the task is logically impossible, and I'd like to know whether the following holds up. The switching times converge perfectly well, so the lamp has executed a completely determinate infinite schedule, every step of which occupies a finite stretch of time; and there seems to be a principled way to fill in the missing state, since over the whole run the lamp is switched on exactly as many times as it is switched off, which makes the two candidate outcomes symmetric and suggests the lamp should end in the state it began in. Isn't the honest verdict, then, that the final state is merely underdetermined by the description — a gap in what we could know about such a machine, rather than a limit on what machines can do?
