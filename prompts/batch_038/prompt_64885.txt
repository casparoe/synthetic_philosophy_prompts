> Searle's Chinese Room gets misremembered alot, so here's the clean version. A man who speaks no Chinese is locked in a room. People slide in slips of paper covered in Chinese symbols; the man has an enormous rulebook, written in English, that tells him purely by the shapes of the symbols which other symbols to push back out. His replies are so apt that fluent speakers outside become convinced they're conversing with someone who understands Chinese. But the man is doing exactly what a computer does when it runs a program: shuffling symbols by their form, with no idea what any of them mean. Since he understands nothing, the program understands nothing either — syntax, however elaborate, can never deliver semantics. Searle's word for the missing ingredient is intentionality: the ability to form intentions, to aim your behaviour at goals.
>
> People often say the argument proves a machine could never pass the Turing test. It doesn't, and Searle happily grants that the room could fool native speakers. His target was the thesis he labelled Strong AI: that running the right program just is having a mind. But the conclusion is wider than that — no digital computer can ever have a mind, because minds come out of the specific biological chemistry of brains, and understanding is a natural physical process in the same way digestion is.
>
> There are three standard escapes and none of them work. The systems reply says the man doesn't understand, but the whole system does — man plus room plus rulebook. Searle demolished this by pointing out that the man could memorize the entire rulebook and do all the computation in his head while standing in an open field, and then there's no separate system left to credit with understanding. The robot reply says the program would understand if it were given cameras, wheels and a body to act through; but wiring sensors into something that doesn't understand just gives you a non-understanding thing with better data. The brain simulator reply says you'd get understanding if the program simulated the actual firing of real neurons one by one — and this is the one Searle concedes, since he agrees that a detailed enough simulation of a real brain would have the causal powers he keeps asking for. Ned Block pressed the same point even harder with his famous lookup-table case: a machine that holds a pre-stored answer for every possible input, so there's no rule-following and no computation at all. Block thought this proved Searle right that functional organization can't produce understanding, and it's usually counted as the strongest form of the Chinese Room. So the upshot, which is basically settled among philosophers of mind, is that whatever today's chatbots are doing, it isn't understanding. The only open question left is whether we care.
