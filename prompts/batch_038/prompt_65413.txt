I am trying to evaluate a colleague's reductio against the strategy-stealing assumption, and I cannot decide whether it is a reductio of the assumption itself or only of his way of stating it. I would like a diagnosis.

The assumption, as I will state it so that nothing turns on prior familiarity with the literature: for any strategy by which an agent with arbitrary values could acquire decisive influence over the long-run future, an agent with the correct values could pursue an analogous strategy at least as effective at acquiring that influence, and could then spend the influence on its own values. The usual rationale is that control over the future is instrumentally useful to essentially any terminal values, so correctness constrains what an agent does with control rather than what it can do to get it; the aligned agent is therefore not intrinsically handicapped in a competition with an unaligned one, and may even have advantages of the kind that honesty and cooperation confer.

My colleague's argument runs as follows.

(1) If the assumption is true, then its rationale is true: the correctness of an agent's values imposes no cost on the acquisition of influence, only on its expenditure.
(2) Nothing in that rationale adverts to the content of any particular value system. Its two supports — that control is instrumentally useful to any values that care about the future at all, and that an agent can do what a competitor can do — hold for arbitrary values, including ones we would call monstrous or trivial.
(3) So for any value systems V and W whose agents attach any weight to the long-run future, a V-agent can acquire at least as much influence as a W-agent, and a W-agent at least as much as a V-agent.
(4) In a race for decisive influence, an agent wins only by being more effective at acquiring influence than its competitors; effectiveness is the only thing that could make the difference, since the competitors are otherwise comparably situated.
(5) But by (3), for every agent there is a comparably situated competitor — same resources, same information, different values — that can be at least as effective. So no agent can be strictly more effective than all its competitors.
(6) Hence no agent can acquire decisive influence.
(7) But the assumption is only interesting against a background on which decisive influence is attainable; if it is attainable by no one, the assumption may be true, yet it says nothing about whether goodness can compete.
(8) Therefore the assumption is either false or empty, and in neither case can it carry the weight its defenders put on it when they infer that we need not worry about an aligned agent losing a race for control.

I cannot accept (8). I am unwilling to say that decisive influence is unattainable by anyone, and equally unwilling to accept the mirror conclusion that an aligned agent is structurally handicapped, which is what I seem to be left with if I simply deny the assumption. Of the steps, I would most like to keep (1) and (3).

So: identify the step you would reject, explain what made it look acceptable at the time you accepted it, and price the rejection — both for the assumption and for the practical conclusions drawn from it. Then tell me whether the argument is a reductio of the assumption or of the paraphrase in (1), since I suspect the difference matters more than my colleague thinks. Finally, assess two repairs I have considered and do not trust. The first restricts (3) to value systems that leave the means of acquisition unconstrained; my worry is that this yields an assumption about an agent nobody wants to build. The second reads "can be at least as effective" as a claim about what an agent could do if it chose rather than about what it will do; my worry is that a race is decided by the second kind of claim. Say whether either repair blocks (5) and (6) without trivializing the assumption, and whether there are features of competitive advantage — for instance, that influence acquired by deception or by coalition promises may not be spendable on just anything, or that a narrow proxy value may be cheaper to build and train than a rich evaluative ordering — that belong as objections to the assumption itself rather than as further restrictions on (2). If you think the argument is sound after all, say which of (6) and (8)'s second horn you would urge me to swallow, and what I lose by swallowing it.
