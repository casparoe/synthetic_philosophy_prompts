Our philosophy of biology seminar takes up the function debate this week — the etiological or selected-effects account associated with Wright, Millikan, and Neander, against the causal-role or dispositional account that Cummins develops through functional analysis — and I find that I can follow the arguments in class but cannot hold onto them afterward, because none of the terminology maps onto how I actually reason. My degree is in mechanical engineering, and I have spent the last two years in reliability and product-development coursework plus a co-op on an aerospace component team. I am fluent in, and would like you to write in, the following vocabulary specifically: failure modes, effects, and causes as rows of an FMEA, together with detection and risk priority numbers; functional decomposition and the requirements traceability matrix that ties each requirement to a verifying test; configuration management, including form-fit-function as the criterion for interchangeability, the design authority, part revisions, and change requests; reliability practice, meaning field returns and warranty data, service bulletins, and the way accumulating field evidence drives the next revision; first-article inspection of a design that has no service history yet; carryover and repurposed commercial parts; reverse engineering of an undocumented legacy component; and CAD design intent as distinct from as-built geometry.

Restate the debate in that vocabulary, claim by claim, as faithfully as you can. I want the following handled explicitly: (1) the core analysis each side gives of what it is for a part or trait to have the function it has; (2) malfunction, which is the principal thing the selected-effects account advertises itself as explaining; (3) by-products and side effects — the noise a pump makes, the sounds of a beating heart — and why each account says these are not functions; (4) the promiscuity or liberalism objection to causal-role accounts, the worry that since everything contributes causally to something, and any capacity can be decomposed into sub-capacities without limit, everything ends up with a function; (5) the historical requirement, tested both against a feature that has not yet accumulated any selection history and against its mirror image, a feature with a selection history that no longer contributes anything; (6) the fact that on the causal-role account a function is always a function relative to some containing system whose capacity is being explained, so that function attributions are relative to a chosen decomposition, whereas the selected-effects account treats a trait's function as non-relative — and whether my habit of fixing a system boundary and a top-level requirement before writing any FMEA captures that relativity or quietly assumes it away; and (7) derived or reassigned function, where a structure is co-opted for a new role, as with a carryover part installed into a different assembly.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the point of the exercise rather than as an appendix. For each of the seven items, say whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all in my vocabulary; where the answer is distortion, name precisely which feature of the original claim the distortion drops. A table for the seven classifications is welcome, but I want prose reasoning behind it.

Two things I want you to be scrupulous about. First, do not smuggle. Field-return data driving the next revision is structurally seductive as an image of natural selection and I expect you to use it — but every time the engineering picture supplies a competent agent reading the data, writing the service bulletin, and holding configuration authority, flag it in the text as a place where the translation has imported intentional purposes that the biological account explicitly does not have. Second, use my terminology in its actual technical sense: if you write risk priority number, first-article, or form-fit-function, mean what those mean in change control and on the floor, not loosely.

Finally, adjudicate the residue rather than merely inventorying it. Tell me whether what fails to translate indicates that the selected-effects account is not genuinely intelligible without a designing agent standing behind it; or that my vocabulary simply lacks the resources to express the normativity that functions are supposed to carry, so that the failure is a limitation of engineering practice rather than of the philosophy; or that a substantial portion of the seminar's dispute is verbal — two legitimate and different engineering notions of function, one historical and one dispositional, both real, with the quarrel being over which deserves the word — and, if that is your verdict, identify which parts of the disagreement are not verbal and would survive a decision to use two distinct terms.

As a check on the whole translation, work through one case in detail: reverse engineering an undocumented legacy component, where there is no design authority to consult and function must be inferred from geometry, wear patterns, and service history. Say which of the two accounts my practice in that situation actually presupposes, and whether the verdict would change if the component came with a complete revision history.

I have read the assigned papers, so please do not summarize the readings back to me, and give me no bibliography, no further reading, and no citations; where a philosopher's name is genuinely needed, a surname will do, and I do not want any quotations. Nine hundred to fourteen hundred words. My own working intuition — that the whole dispute reduces to design intent versus measured performance, and that once you have both an FMEA and a change history you possess everything the philosophers are reaching for — should either be vindicated or shown exactly where it breaks.
