I teach a small graduate seminar on the philosophy of well-being, and next week's session is supposed to be about the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: what the pleasure of eating a very ripe apricot, the pleasure of suddenly seeing why a proof goes through, the pleasure of hot water on cold hands, and the pleasure of relief when a migraine finally breaks are supposed to have in common, if anything. The problem is that my students arrive having read the same handful of summaries, and within five minutes the discussion splits into two camps that talk past each other — one says all these experiences share a single pleasant quality that rides along inside them, the other says they share nothing at all beyond our wanting them, and neither camp can articulate what would settle the matter. I want a handout that makes them commit, question by question, to structural claims they can see the consequences of. So please write me a decision tree, roughly ten to twelve branch nodes, that sorts a respondent into a specific account of what pleasure is, and please build it from the logical space first: lay out the discriminating questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then attach the names of the actual positions to the leaves.

The leaves I need are these, whether or not you use exactly these labels: the view that every pleasure shares one and the same phenomenal ingredient, a common hedonic tone that can vary in intensity and that is present whether or not the experience has any object at all; the family-resemblance or disjunctive view, on which pleasures form no unified kind and are lumped together only by our dispositions; the attitudinal or propositional view, on which pleasure is not a sensation but a favorable stance toward a state of affairs one believes oneself to be in; the desire-based view, on which pleasure is the experience of getting what one wants; the view on which a pleasant experience carries a built-in directive to keep going, so that what makes it pleasant is that it commands continuation rather than that it feels a certain way; the view on which a pleasant experience represents its own object as good or as worth continuing; and at least one deflationary or eliminativist leaf, on which our ordinary concept does not carve out a single phenomenon and should be replaced rather than analyzed. Each leaf should carry the most standard name for the position if it has one, and should be followed by one sentence naming its most awkward commitment — the thing the view is least able to say.

Two constraints on the questions, and one on the cases. First, every branch node must be answerable by someone who has never read a page on the subject, so no node may contain a theory name or smuggle its own answer: a node reading "Do you think pleasure is a propositional attitude?" is disqualified, whereas a node asking the respondent to choose between two concrete descriptions of the same situation is exactly what I want. Second, the tree must use my case list rather than invent its own from scratch — the apricot, the proof, the hot water, the end of the migraine, the first cigarette enjoyed by someone who no longer wants to smoke, the compliment one enjoys believing sincere when in fact it was not, Schadenfreude taken in the misfortune of a rival one judges not to deserve it, a flow state in which one is absorbed and would stop immediately if interrupted, and a free-floating euphoria with no object whatsoever — and at least one node must turn on whether pleasantness can vary in intensity independently of the experiences it accompanies, and another on whether an experience can be pleasant while its subject is indifferent to whether it continues. Wherever two answers are logically available but no position in the literature occupies the resulting cell, say so explicitly and diagnose whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has thought to defend it; and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, together with the further case that would be needed to pull them apart. Please write the whole thing in American English, formatted as an indented text tree with numbered nodes rather than as prose, and keep it to about two pages.
