I did 34 years in an ultrasonics lab — transducer design, a lot of staring at spectra — and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy head first, which my wife finds hilarious. I've just worked through the aesthetics stretch of Schopenhauer's big book (I got the old three-volume translation with "Idea" in the title at a church sale) and the music part has me completely hooked: the claim that music isn't a picture of the Ideas the way the other arts are, but a picture of the will itself, so it stands off to one side of the whole hierarchy. Anyway, I've been asked to give a 50-minute talk on it to a community music-appreciation group that meets in a library room downtown. About 25 people, lots of amateur string and choral players, two or three retired engineers like me, nobody has read a word of Kant and nobody is going to read any afterwards. I have an electric keyboard and a laptop with a tone generator.

So — don't explain Schopenhauer to me, I've read him. Plan the talk.

What I want out of you:

1. The order the pieces have to go in, and what genuinely depends on what. My worry is that I can't say anything about music standing outside the hierarchy until I've built the hierarchy, and building it means will, representation, and the grades of objectification — which is 25 minutes of metaphysics before anybody hears a note. Is that actually unavoidable, or is there a way to start from the phenomenon (why a tune gets in under your skin in a way a painting of the same subject never does) and back-fill only as much metaphysics as the phenomenon demands? Pick a side and defend it, because I genuinely don't know which way to jump.

2. The carrying example. Here's my own idea and I want you to tell me honestly whether to keep it or kill it. I build a harmonic stack live on the keyboard: sine fundamental, then add the overtones one at a time so they hear the same pitch gradually grow a "body." Then I say this is the picture he has in mind — the deep bass as the crudest, most inert grade of the will's objectification, the middle voices as the finer ones — and get someone to play a tune over the top, and that's the melody-as-striving bit: wanting, a brief arrival, wanting again. My concern is that this makes it look like I'm doing acoustics, that Schopenhauer is making a claim about the physics of sound, which is not his claim at all, and that half the room will leave thinking I've proved him right with a tone generator. Should the demo carry the whole thing, be a footnote, or is there a better single example?

3. A distinction I want the talk to hold onto, though I can't tell if it's mine or his: whether music pictures the will, versus whether music is somehow direct evidence that there is such a thing as the will. People slide from one to the other constantly. Tell me where in the sequence to plant that so it's already in place when the questions come.

4. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually show up, and the exact moment to head each off. My list so far: "so he's just saying music expresses feelings"; the engineer one, "the will is basically energy, he was a century ahead of them"; "he's the pessimist guy so this must all be about sad music"; somebody asking whether a drum solo or an atonal piece counts on his view — which I suspect is a genuinely damaging question and I don't want to fudge it; and maybe "he lifted the thing-in-itelf straight from Kant." Which of these will I really get from this room, which am I over-worrying about, and what am I missing entirely?

5. One question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether the idea landed or whether they just enjoyed the keyboard.

6. What I should say up front that I'm not covering — the ethics and the denial of the will, the whole thing-in-itself quarrel — and say it early enough that nobody feels shortchanged.

And be blunt about anything in my plan that a philosopher sitting in the back row would wince at. There's likely to be at least one.
