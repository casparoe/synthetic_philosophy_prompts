I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy of mathematics, writing a dissertation on the epistemic standing of non-deductive argument in number theory. I have names for the particular models I have been reading about — the informal arguments are usually attached to individual mathematicians — but I have no name for the pattern of justification they share, which is what actually interests me, and the methodological vocabulary I have been trained on does not seem to capture it. Let me describe the pattern as concretely as I can, in three instances, and then say what I am after.

First instance. To estimate how many pairs of primes differ by two below a large bound, the argument treats "n is prime" and "n + 2 is prime" as though they were two unrelated coin flips, each landing heads with probability about 1/log n, multiplies the two probabilities, sums over n, and obtains a predicted order of magnitude. The premise is known by everyone to be false: the two events cannot both obtain if either number is even, and their divisibility by small primes is systematically coupled. The argument is nonetheless not abandoned. Instead the falsehood is treated as a first approximation, and one then computes, for each small prime, how far the actual joint behaviour departs from the unrelated-flip picture, and multiplies the original estimate by a convergent product of these departure ratios — a specific constant a little above one. The corrected figure agrees with the data remarkably well, and the conjecture is regarded as very strongly supported.

Second instance. To predict how widely spaced consecutive primes can be, one imagines a random subset of the integers in which each n is included with probability 1/log n, and asks how the longest run of excluded numbers below x behaves in such a set. This gives a definite prediction, on the order of the square of log x. A theorem is then proved showing that in certain short intervals — intervals of a constructible kind, positioned so as to be atypically depleted of small prime factors — the primes are distributed quite differently from what the picture requires. The response is not to discard the picture. It is retained, its prediction is said to hold for almost all intervals rather than for all, and in some formulations the prediction is amended by an additional constant factor, attributed to couplings the original picture was too crude to register.

Third, briefer instance. The same shape appears where the argument was eventually made rigorous: the number of distinct prime factors of a large integer is predicted by treating "divisible by p" for different primes p as though they were unrelated events with the obvious individual rates, which yields a bell-shaped distribution that turns out to be provably correct.

What I want is the carving, not a bibliography, and a bare label would not satisfy me. Specifically:

1. Is this practice a recognized object with an established name, either in the methodology of number theory or in the philosophy of mathematics? If it has a name, characterize it precisely; if it does not, coin one and give a definition with real content.

2. How does it differ from the nearest concepts I can think of? I have considered ordinary idealization, but idealizations are usually understood as deliberate falsehoods whose consequences come out approximately right, whereas here the falsehood is corrected by a further computation whose output is taken to be exactly right. I have considered the patching of auxiliary hypotheses, but in the second instance the amendment is independently motivated, computable from the same picture at finer grain, and its success is treated as confirming rather than as rescuing. I have considered inference to the best explanation, but nothing here purports to identify a mechanism; the probability model is a stand-in for a structure known not to be random in the relevant respects. I have also come across the idea of a deterministic structure that provably behaves as if random, which seems to be almost the reverse of what is happening in the first instance. For each of these, tell me where the fit fails and which, if any, is doing the real work.

3. Is there any principled criterion separating a licensed use of this pattern from an illegitimate one, or does the practice amount to "the correction can be computed and the data agree"? In particular, when the correction step is performed, is the resulting argument still an argument from the original model, or has it silently become an argument from a much more complicated model that nobody would describe as random?

4. Give me at least two boundary cases: one that looks like this pattern but is not, and one that is genuinely borderline, with an explanation of what turns on the verdict.

Please write your response in American English.
