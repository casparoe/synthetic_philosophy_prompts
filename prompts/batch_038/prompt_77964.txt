I work on safety-filtered learning-based control: a learned policy proposes an input, and a barrier-function filter overrides it only when the state approaches the boundary of a certified safe set. In nearly every logged episode the filter never fires, so the realized trajectory is exactly what the unconstrained policy would have produced. Reading the free will literature, I keep running into the same architecture — Frankfurt-style cases, where an intervener is poised to force the choice but never actually intervenes because the agent makes it on his own, and the conclusion drawn is that the agent is responsible without alternative possibilities, since only the actual sequence matters.

Restate that debate in the vocabulary I actually use — policies, state trajectories, invariant and reachable sets, barrier functions, supervisory switching logic, robustness margins, closed-loop behavior under disturbance — as faithfully as you can, mapping the positions one by one: the incompatibilist who insists the principle of alternative possibilities is necessary for responsibility, Frankfurt's counterexamples to him, and the actual-sequence response with Fischer and Ravizza's split between regulative control and mere guidance control. Two controllers can produce identical closed-loop data across every logged episode and still be different systems, because the object of study is the map from initial conditions and disturbances to trajectories — the invariance and robustness properties — and a filter that never activates still shrinks the reachable set. If that is right, then the claim that the actual sequence is unchanged and so the intervener is irrelevant is something my framework cannot so much as express, and the actual-sequence theorist must be asserting something substantive and contestable rather than something obvious.
