Long-time reader of the rationalist blogs — LW/SSC-adjacent forums since roughly forever — and for about ten years I've had a hobby horse about state apologies for historical wrongs. It stopped being a hobby horse this spring. My state legislature stood up a nine-person working group to draft an official apology covering two things: (a) the involuntary sterilization program that ran here from the 1920s into the 1970s, and (b) the unilateral rewriting of a treaty with one of the nations in the state a generation later, which was then used to take land. I'm on the group because I'm the local person who reads this stuff for fun. Six months, twice a month, and the deliverable is draft resolution language plus a recommendation about what if anything should accompany it (some states have done compensation programs for sterilization survivors; that's on the table here too).

My problem is that the conceptual issues people in my orbit want to litigate are way more numerous than our attention budget, which is maybe 100 person-hours of non-lawyer time total. Some context on where the friction actually is: councel's draft already contains a clause saying the resolution creates no legal liability and can't be cited as evidence in any claim, and one of the community reps says that means we're not apologizing at all, just performing. Another rep wants every individual act named. The historian on the group thinks apologizing to dead people's descendants is a category error and we should issue a "statement of acknowledgment" instead. And there's a fight brewing about bundling: the two affected communities are currently on bad terms politically, each objects to being lumped in with the other, and each suspects the other's harm will get more airtime.

Here are the candidate issues. Tell me which deserve our hours, in what order, and which I can tell people to drop:

1. Whether the liability disclaimer is compatible with the apology being sincere at all, or whether it converts it into a different speech act.
2. Who can recieve an apology when every wronged individual is dead — descendants, the nation as a continuing entity, nobody.
3. Specificity: does an apology that doesn't name the acts fail, or just land weaker?
4. Whether an apology offered as a substitute for material redress is defective in a way an apology offered alongside it isn't.
5. The bundling problem above — a partial apology that excludes one group vs. a general one that dilutes both.
6. Whether a later legislature can retract or reinterpret an apology, i.e. how durable this act actually is.
7. Apology inflation: does adding ours to the existing pile cheapen the institution?
8. Whether the state today is the same agent as the state that did these things, given population turnover and a new constitution since.

I want the criteria used and defended, not just applied: (i) does getting it wrong make the apology *defective* rather than merely worse; (ii) does it change any actual words in the resolution or in the accompanying recommendation — I want to be able to say "this is why line 4 reads the way it does"; (iii) how much of it is really ours to decide vs. fixed by things outside the group's control; (iv) how tractable it is for nine people with no philosophy training over two hours; and (v) what would move an item up or down — what we learn in the next few months, what a hearing, a tribal council response, or a budget number would do to the ranking.

Also please stress-test my own pet distinction, because I half suspect it's just me enjoying my vocabulary. I keep arguing there's a difference between apologies that change the *description of the past* — once the state officially calls the land-taking a taking rather than a settlement, the earlier events get relabeled and stay relabeled regardless of who's in office — and apologies that change *obligations going forward*, which are revocable, amendable, and hostage to the next legislature. My claim is that the first kind is the only durable one, and that the liability disclaimer is therefore fine, because it only blocks the second kind. Is that load-bearing, or is it a distinction without a difference?
