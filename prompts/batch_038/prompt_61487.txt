So I'm teaching myself game theory out of a library book (no class, just me at the kitchen table) and I hit a wall my older brother, who took a college econ course, basically shrugged at. Maybe you can do better.

The book's big idea early on is: cross out any strategy that's strictly dominated by another one, then cross out more, keep going until nothing's left to cross out. But it also says a strategy counts as dominated even when no single strategy beats it, as long as some coin-flip mixture of strategies beats it. Its example is basically this: rows A, B, C against columns L and R. A pays you 3 if the other guy plays L and 0 if he plays R. B pays 0 against L and 3 against R. C pays 1 either way. Neither A nor B dominates C, but a 50/50 mix of A and B pays 1.5 no matter what he does, so C gets crossed out.

Heres my problem. When I actually play, I never get 1.5. I get 3 or I get 0. The 1.5 only exists as an average across a bunch of games that never happen. So the book isn't crossing out C because there's something I could really *do* that beats C, it's crossing it out because a gamble beats C on average — and calling that "beats" only works if you already assume I rank gambles by expected value. But I thought the whole selling point of dominance reasoning was that it's the safe, low-assumption part of game theory: you don't have to know what people want or how their heads work, just that they won't play something that's worse no matter what. Now it looks like to run the procedure you have to assume everyone is an expected-utility machine with a quarter in their pocket. And then you iterate, so round two and round three lean on deletions that only the coin flip justified, and one shaky step compounds into all of them.
