I spent four years on a regional panel that ranked competing proposals for a fixed pot of health funding, and one of the recurrent disputes there was not about numbers but about whether certain numbers were allowed to enter at all. Two options came up repeatedly. The first funded an acute post-surgical pain service: a few thousand patients a year, each in severe but brief distress. The second funded a self-management program for people with long-standing low-grade pain: two hundred thousand patients, each much less badly off, none of them ever getting better. A colleague argued the second option contributed nothing whatever to the moral case for spending the money. Not less than the first, and not a smaller benefit that happened to lose the comparison, but nothing at all: on her account the mild pain was not a cost that got outweighed, it was simply not a cost, and multiplying it by any finite number of people or years left it exactly where it started. When I asked whether she meant the mild pain had a small weight that was always trumped, she said no, that it had no weight, and that a weight which can never be enough of anything is not a weight.

She holds a matching view about the other direction, which she illustrated in a reading group I go to. She thinks a life containing one episode of the very worst kind of agony is worse than never having been lived, however long and joyful the rest of it is, and that this is not because the joy fails to add up to enough: on her telling there is no rate of exchange between joy and agony to begin with, so the question of sufficiency never arises. Oddly, she has no trouble whatsoever ranking agonies against each other, or one mild ache against another, or a longer ache against a shorter one; the scale she denies across the two kinds of experience she cheerfully uses within each kind. And she insists the dividing line is precise rather than fuzzy: there is a fact of the matter about the exact intensity at which pain begins to count, even though nobody could say where it lies. Pressed with a series of cases each barely distinguishable from the last, running from a tickle up to screaming, she conceded that no single step looks like the crossing point and that she could not identify it in practice, but she refused to conclude that there is no crossing point. I recognize the shape of that last exchange from the sorites, but I do not know what her response to it is called, or whether importing it here costs her something she has not noticed. There is one further wrinkle: she still thinks it is wrong to cause mild pain for no reason, but she explains that by appeal to character and to rules rather than to any badness in the mild pain itself, which struck several of us as an awkward patch.

So: does this composite have a name, and if the composite does not, do its parts? I am especially interested in whether there is an established distinction between a harm that carries no weight and a harm whose weight is always trumped, which side of that distinction her view falls on, and what turns on the difference. I would also like to know whether the one-sided refusal to trade good experiences against bad ones is a recognized position in its own right, and whether holding it commits her to some particular account of what pleasure and pain are, since if they are one kind of state differing only in degree her position looks hard to sustain. Does the objection that no principled line can be drawn through a continuum of intensities have a name of its own? And if nothing in the literature matches the whole thing, please say so plainly and coin a term, with a definition and boundary conditions: tell me what would not count as an instance, so that I can check whether I am looking at one position or at two that are usually run together, such as someone who thinks small harms matter less and someone who thinks many small harms should never be traded against one large one. I am not after references or a reading list, which I would have no way of chasing down; what I want is the carving, and where its edges are.
