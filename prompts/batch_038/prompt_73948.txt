ok so I'm in a moral psych seminar and I'm presenting my paper next week. The claim: you can't sort dispositions into preferences vs biases using any of the usual tests — is it reason-responsive, does the agent endorse it on reflection, is it evidence-sensitive, does it track their values — because heaps of perfectly ordinary preferences fail those tests too. My liking one kind of music over another is arbitrary, doesn't survive much reflective scrutiny, and is totally insensitive to evidence, but nobody calls it a bias. So instead I argue the line is second-personal: a disposition is a bias when the agent couldn't avow it, i.e. when someone pointing it out to them leaves them unable to speak for it in their own voice; a preference is one they can stand behind when addressed. Mechanism doesn't matter, answerability does.

Room is about 15. Half are psych PhD students who work with implicit measures and think of bias as a measurable construct — they'll want to know how the hell you'd operationalize "couldnt avow," and they're deeply suspicious of self-report. Two are Frankfurt-ish autonomy people who'll say avowal just collapses back into hierarchical endorsement, which is exactly the move I'm rejecting. And the prof is a virtue ethicist who thinks dispositions come in character-shaped packages and that slicing them into preference/bias cuts at the wrong joint entirely.

Pretend you're a professional philosopher who's sat through a lot of hostile question periods. First sketch, for yourself, the main failure modes of my view, then give me the prep sheet: the 6-8 objections this specific room will actually raise (not the ones a metaethics audience would give me), ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased in the objector's own vocabulary — the psych people should sound like psych people — with about a one-minute reply and a note on where my reply is thinnest. Mark which ones I should concede in the first thirty seconds and which I have to hold onto. And end with the single question I'm praying nobody asks.
