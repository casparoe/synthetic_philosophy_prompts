Ok so I run a small Discord reading group on modality — mostly ex-LW types, me included, though my day job is data infra so I'm very much an amateur here — and we've been chewing on the counterpart-theory-vs-transworld-identity fight for a few weeks. I volunteered to play devil's advocate for Lewis and built the argument below, originally to show that counterpart theory doesn't just fail to save de re modality but leaves you with nothing at all. The problem is it now feels like it proves way more than I wanted, and I can't tell whether that means it's good or means something slipped. Please act as a professional metaphysician who works on modal semantics and its ontology for a living: think this through carefully and tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs.

Here it is. Call R the relation that's supposed to make 'a could have been F' true.

1. Any analysis worth the name makes 'a could have been F' true only if something that is F bears R to a. If nothing bears R to a, the claim has to be taken as primitive, and we were looking for an analysis.
2. R is either identity-across-worlds or a counterpart relation. Those are the two things people actually defend.
3. Identity-across-worlds requires one and the same concrete thing to be wholly located in more than one world, but worlds are maximal: if a is in w, it's in no other. So R, if there is one, is a counterpart relation.
4. Counterparthood is grounded in overall similarity, and similarity comes in degrees with no sharp cutoff — between any two things you can find a third that's intermediate.
5. If a relation's ground comes in degrees with no sharp cutoff, then either everything bears it to everything or nothing bears it to anything, since carving out an intermediate subclass would mean drawing a precise line the ground doesn't support.
6. The nothing-horn is absurd: it makes 'a could have been F' false for every a and every F, so I couldn't even have skipped breakfast this morning.
7. So everything is a counterpart of everything.
8. Recombination guarantees that for any consistent F, some world contains something that is F.
9. So for any a and any consistent F, 'a could have been F' is true, and for any a and F, 'a could have failed to be F' is true.
10. So nothing has any essential properties: every de re possibility comes out true and every de re necessity comes out false or vacuous, which is indistinguishable from having no modal information at all.
11. So CT doesn't analyze de re modality, it dissolves it. And (3) closed off the only rival. Hence there's no analysis of de re modality on the table, and de re modal talk is either meaningless or systematically uninformative.

My friend Marcus says the trouble is (8) — that recombination doesn't obviously get you a thing that is F *and* similar enough to be the relevant counterpart — but I don't see how that helps, because if (7) is right there's no similarity requirement left to satisfy. Two things I'd really like to keep if I can: the worldbound picture in (3), and the datum that I could have skipped breakfast but could not have been a poached egg. I also want to know whether (11) genuinely takes out the competing views or whether I've smuggled in something distinctively Lewisian that a transworld-identity theorist or an essence-primitivist gets to deny for free — because if the argument quietly assumes the thing it's supposed to be neutral about, that's a bigger problem than any single premise. And I tried patching (5) by stipulating a threshold, so that counterparthood requires similarity above some cutoff; but then the cutoff has to be precise, which is exactly what (5) said the ground can't support, and different conversations seem to want different cutoffs, so I end up with a continuum of them and no fact about which one is in play. Is that patch hopeless, or is it the right shape and I'm just describing it badly?
