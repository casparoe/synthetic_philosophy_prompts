Weird thing happened after I did that series on our county's jail overcrowding bond: a commissioner who liked the piece invited me to sit on the jail's community advisory panel, apparently on the theory that a journalist who wrote about the place must have opinions about running it. So now, once a month, a group of us — a public defender, a retired probation officer, two victims' advocates, a county budget analyst, a pastor, and me, the only one who's ever read anything about corrections — get handed proposals for programs and small facility changes and have to vote on whether to recommend them to the commission. It's usually six to eight a quarter, we get maybe twenty minutes a proposal, nobody on the panel has any training in this, and the sheriff's office sends two people who can effectively veto anything they say compromises control. The jail is a 1970s pod design, the median stay is somewhere under two months because most of the population is pretrial or serving short sentences, and we have about $50k a year of discretionary money, so almost nothing we approve is a rebuild — it's a curriculum here, a visitation room there, a policy about who's allowed to hand out what.

What I want from you is a screening procedure the panel can actually run in that twenty minutes, grounded in whatever the Norwegian approach really commits you to rather than in the Halden press photos. I mean the actual operating principles — the normality idea, that the punishment is the loss of liberty and nothing more; dynamic security, where safety comes from officers being in constant unarmed contact with prisoners rather than from hardware; the import model, where school, health care and library services are delivered by the same outside agencies that deliver them to everyone else, not by the prison itself. Set that against what our jail defaults to, which is classification and control, program access as a privilege earned by good behavior, and services run by custodial staff because that's who's on payroll. And be straight with me about which parts of it don't travel to a short-stay county jail and why, because I suspect at least half the panel's instinct — "let's build a reentry village with a mock bus stop and apartment kitchen inside the secure perimeter" — is the brochure problem, a phrase a friend of mine who does correctional design uses for anything that photographs as transformative and does nothing once you're inside it. Related to that, here's a distinction I keep reaching for and can't defend: some changes are *inside-the-walls* changes (what happens within the facility) and some are *across-the-walls* changes (the standing relationship between the facility and the community outside it). My hunch is that only the second kind is doing the real work in Norway, and that a panel that approves lots of the first kind can be completely faithful to the Halden aesthetic and still change nothing. Tell me if that hunch is wrong, and if it's right, build the distinction into the procedure.

Concretely, I want questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a one-word stamp the panel can record for each proposal, and the places where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a person because it's a question of values — how much deprivation someone deserves, what a victims' advocate is owed — and not a question of facts. Also flag the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes; I'd rather know which line of the flowchart I'm actually just voting my gut on. No reading list and no citations, please — I can't chase anything down and I'm not going to pretend I will; if a claim needs a study behind it, name the study in half a sentence or drop it. Structure it like this, roughly: "What the Norwegian model actually commits you to" (~250 words), "What doesn't travel to a county jail" (~250), "The screening procedure" (~600, numbered, with the stamps), "Where the procedure runs out and a person has to decide" (~200), "Three proposals, run through it" (~500), and "Bottom line" (~100). For the three, use: an eight-week cognitive-behavioral thinking-skills curriculum that's evidence-based, cheap, and taught by the jail's own officers inside the perimeter, with a completion certificate that buys priority for work-release slots — I expect half the panel loves this one, so if your procedure rejects it, say so plainly and give me the sentence I can use in the meeting; a proposal to take out the plexiglass visitation booths and let a county library staffer run a room where kids can sit on a parent's lap; and the dynamic-security one — officers eating in the dayroom with detainees, no keys or restraints carried on the floor — which I think passes your test cleanly and gets killed anyway by the union and the sheriff, so build in a slot for "passes, won't survive" and tell me what the panel should do with that stamp.
