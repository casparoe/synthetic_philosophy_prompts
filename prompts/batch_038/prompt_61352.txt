I chair a seven-member appeals board that applies a rule with two independent conditions: a finding is sustained only if both a procedural condition and a substantive condition are met. Members vote on each condition separately, and we have long used the premise-majority to determine the outcome. Having thought about this for a decade, I have come to accept a fairly strong view, in three parts. First, that a group applying a general rule cannot simultaneously be fully responsive to what its members actually judge about the reasons and be guaranteed coherent in its collective verdicts — and that this is a structural constraint of the same kind as Arrow's theorem, not a defect of our particular design. Second, that for institutions whose business is rule-application, coherence is the thing worth buying: it is better to sustain a finding that some members think unsupported than to issue verdicts that cannot be reconstructed as the application of any rule at all. Third, that the proposed escapes — quota and supermajority rules, fixed orderings of the propositions, restrictions on which judgment sets are admissible — merely relocate the cost rather than remove it.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed proponent would themselves concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss, and on what grounds. Address at least the following.

Which features of an agenda make it vulnerable and which make it immune? If the constraint is not universal but depends on how the propositions are logically connected and on how much diversity of judgment is admitted, then say what our own agenda would have to look like for the escape routes to be genuinely available to us, and whether an agenda of our shape can ever be made safe.

Can quota rules in fact purchase consistency, and if so, what is the price — anonymity, decisiveness, or the risk of a body that simply fails to reach any verdict? Is a procedure that reliably produces no decision better or worse, for a rule-applying institution, than one that reliably produces a coherent decision nobody fully endorses?

What would have to be true about the correlation among members' judgments for the dilemma never to bite in practice? In eleven years we have recorded fewer than fifty contested cases and not once has the premise-majority diverged from the outcome-majority. Does that count against the view, confirm it, or is it simply silent? What further pattern in such records would be decisive either way?

The choice between deciding by premises and deciding by outcome is itself a collective decision, so the dilemma appears to recur one level up. Does that iteration strengthen the view by showing there is no neutral standpoint, or does it reduce the view to absurdity by making every institutional choice arbitrary?

I also harbor a suspicion I cannot discharge: that any rule guaranteeing coherence does so by making outcomes depend on a small subset of members, so that we have traded responsiveness for oligarchy rather than for coherence. Assess this. If it is right, does my second claim survive?

Finally, state what my view predicts that its rival — that this is ordinary institutional design with trade-offs and no deep impossibility — predicts otherwise, and say honestly whether my position risks anything at all or has been insulated by the concession that something must give. If it is insulated, is that a defect here, given that the claim is supposed to be structural rather than empirical, or is a view of this kind entitled to that protection?
