Retired four years back after 35 years in an auditory neurophysiology lab, and philosophy of mind is what I took up instead of golf. Been chewing on Block's access/phenomenal distinction and on global workspace theory, which as I read it makes being conscious of something pretty much the same thing as that something being broadcast for report and reasoning. If that's right, phenomenology with no access shouldn't be possible at all, yet people keep offering overflow cases.

I want a ranking, not an essay. Sort these into tiers by how much weight each really carries in the argument over whether access and phenomenology genuinely come apart, and give each a score out of 10 for how hard it would be for a workspace theorist to explain away:

1. Sperling's partial report — people swear they saw all the letters, and then they're gone.
2. Inattentional blindness, the invisible gorilla.
3. Change blindness: we miss big changes, yet insist the scene looked rich and detailed throughout.
4. Blindsight — accurate pointing with nothing it's like, i.e. the reverse direction.
5. My own, which I don't think gets the attention it deserves. You sit reading with the fridge running, notice nothing; the compressor cuts out and you suddenly realize the hum had been there ten minutes. The twist: immediately after it stops, have the subject match the pitch and loudness of the hum on a tone generator. If they can do it accurately, something about that sound was there and available in a way that shouldnt have been possible. If they can't, the whole case collapses into a retrospective illusion.

Then two things on top. Which single item should a global workspace theorist lose no sleep over, and which one can they not dismiss without giving up something they actually care about? And give me a rough number — what fraction of the five would you bet survives a really careful review, e.g. 0.2 if you think only one holds up.
