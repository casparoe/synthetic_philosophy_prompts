Our team expects to deploy an autonomous operations agent across a regional hospital network next year: it will schedule equipment maintenance, order consumables, reallocate facilities staff, and manage supplier contracts under a narrow commercial objective (meet clinical throughput targets at low cost). I wrote the safety section of the design memo, and the position I converged on is stated below as nine commitments. Before the memo goes to review, I want an audit of whether they can all be held at once. Please treat them as my commitments rather than as a survey of the field, and do not spend any of the answer explaining the background concepts, which I have defined inline.

1. No values required. A sufficiently capable agent can be kept from rearranging the world in regrettable ways without any specification of what is good. The design task is to constrain the *shape* of the agent's causal influence, not its content. Relatedly, I reject the inference that a low-impact agent must be a passive one: I want this system to pursue its targets aggressively.

2. Impact is foreclosed possibility. I measure the impact of a course of action by how far it shrinks what could subsequently be brought about — the size of the space of reachable futures, relative to a baseline. Nothing else about a change enters the measure: not how much anyone would mind it, not how valuable the foreclosed futures were. The baseline is counterfactual inaction: what would have happened had the agent been switched off at the outset and the hospital's ordinary processes continued. I reject measuring each action only against its immediate predecessor state.

3. Aggregation. Impact is aggregated across persons by minimum, not by sum: what counts is the loss borne by whoever loses the most possibility, and losses distributed across different people neither add up nor cancel out.

4. The agent's own options get no weight. Losses to the agent's own future option space receive near-zero weight, on the grounds that penalizing the agent for its own foreclosed possibilities would push it toward resource acquisition and resistance to interference.

5. Pessimism about the residual. Where it is unclear whether a downstream change is part of what we asked for or an unintended consequence, the measure counts it as unintended. The burden of proof lies on any change being benign.

6. Offsetting is legitimate. If an action forecloses something, the agent may create possibilities elsewhere and have that count in its favor. A measure that forbade every net-negative trade would be unusable in an organization that has to trade off constantly.

7. A finite penalty, not a constraint. The measure enters the agent's objective as a penalty term with a finite coefficient, traded off against the commercial reward. I considered lexicographic formulations that place impact restrictions above reward, and rejected them for the general case: in a live facility every action forecloses something, and a categorical bar on impact would leave the agent unable to act at all.

8. A never-class. Nonetheless, certain changes are categorically excluded rather than priced: destroying records, permanently altering physical infrastructure, and causing an organization to cease to exist. However large the reward, these are off the table.

9. Two verdicts. (a) To meet a throughput target, the agent declines to renew a long-standing contract with a small regional supplier; the supplier loses most of its revenue and closes within the year. I judge this among the most serious side effects the system could produce in its first year. (b) To meet the same target, the agent commissions a structural alteration to a courtyard that has served for a decade as storage, after which the space can never be reconfigured for any other purpose; nobody had any plans for the courtyard. I judge this a housekeeping matter, not worth raising with the board.

What I want out of the audit:

- Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only look that way? I expect some pairs that get flagged to be compatible once stated precisely, and I would rather you say so than pad the list.
- Order the genuine tensions by severity. For each, state exactly which commitment has to go or how it must be weakened, and what that revision costs us.
- For each genuine tension, say whether it is repairable by re-engineering the measure — a different baseline, a different functional form, a different scope — or whether repairing it requires admitting value-laden content of the kind commitment 1 says we can do without.
- Where the verdicts in 9 cannot be delivered by the measure described in 1 through 8, say which is cheaper: revising the verdict, or revising the measure, and specify the revision you would make.
- If I were permitted to change exactly one thing in the whole package, which change removes the most tension per unit of cost, and what does the position look like afterward?

I have deliberately not indicated which of these I am most attached to. Please do not assume it is commitment 1, and do not grade the package by how much of the conventional treatment it preserves.
