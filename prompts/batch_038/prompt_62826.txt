I'm in the final weeks of a graduate seminar on political ethics, and next week each of us has to defend a prediction in front of the group. Our tutor has spent the term issuing verdicts on a battery of constructed cases, and I have been keeping a record of them, because I think her rulings are jointly consistent with several quite different theories of dirty hands and she has never said which one she holds. Here is the record.

1. An interior minister secretly authorizes an unlawful search of a journalist's home, uncovers evidence that stops a planned bombing, then discloses what she did and accepts a prosecution she could easily have avoided. Verdict: dirty hands — and the disclosure is part of what makes the act defensible, not a way of cleaning it after the fact.
2. The same minister, the same act, but she conceals it and reports no misgivings. Verdict: worse than case 1, and not properly described as dirty hands at all; she calls it corruption, because the cost is denied rather than carried.
3. A diplomat lies to a captor about safe passage in order to secure a hostage's release, and afterwards says the lie was required by his office and cost him nothing. Verdict: a dirty-handed act, and in addition a further failing, since the absence of any felt cost is itself a moral defect.
4. A backbencher breaks a campaign pledge in order to keep a coalition alive long enough to pass climate legislation; no right is violated, nobody is harmed, and most of the voters who were promised now prefer the outcome. Verdict: not dirty hands. Ordinary political judgment under conflicting reasons; calling it dirty sentimentalizes promise-breaking.
5. The chair of a triage committee allocates scarce antivirals by expected benefit and cannot save one group; she grieves, and nothing she did was impermissible. Verdict: a tragic choice, not dirty hands. Conflating the two lets officials claim a kind of absolution for what was merely hard.
6. A judge imposes a mandatory minimum she believes cruel, and says from the bench that the sentence is unjust while passing it. Verdict: dirty hands, but the dirt is not primarily hers — it is the law's — and her acknowledgment discharges only part of it; the remainder obliges her to work against the statute.
7. A leader declines to authorize an unlawful surveillance measure that would very probably have prevented an attack, and says his hands are clean because he did nothing wrong. Verdict: a mistake, and one she has a name for — the dirt of the clean-hander. Refusing to bear a cost that one's office requires one to bear is another way of getting dirt on one's hands.

The new case she has handed out for next week: a deputy health minister in an authoritarian regime attends a press conference and endorses a policy she knows to be both false and cruel — she praises the detention of the physicians who first reported an outbreak — because doing so is the condition on which she retains control of the national immunization program, which plausibly saves many thousands of children over the following years. She feels no guilt, sleeps well, tells colleagues the endorsement was worth it, and has no intention of resigning or confessing. She does, however, keep a private written record of exactly what she said.

What I need is a prediction I can actually defend. So: what will she say? Give me your best guess at her verdict, with some sense of how confident you are and why. Then lay out at least three candidate rules that fit all seven earlier verdicts — stated precisely enough that they disagree somewhere — and show how each rules on the new case; I expect at least two of them to come apart here, and I want to know exactly where and on what feature of the case. Tell me which of the seven is the closest analog to the new one, and where that analogy strains. Since I suspect the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, I would also like an honest statement of what principle she would be committing herself to on each possible verdict: in particular, whether felt guilt is constitutive of the dirt or merely evidence of it, whether dirt attaches to agents or to institutions, and whether justification and excuse come apart in cases like this one. Finally, propose the single follow-up case that would discriminate best between the candidate rules, and say which verdict on it would settle which. Around nine hundred words, and please spend them on the reasoning rather than on surveying the literature — we have all read the Walzer piece and the agent-regret material, and restating them will not help me in seminar.
