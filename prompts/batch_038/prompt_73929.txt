I am giving a job talk this spring whose spine is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and my advisor has insisted that the slide immediately before questions be something light. The audience will be a mixed room of economists and philosophers who have just sat through forty minutes on the setup: each individual's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, the social ordering over the same prospects does too, Pareto indifference holds (when every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent as well), and the conclusion follows that any social utility function representing the social ordering must be a positive affine combination of the individual utility functions. What I need is a joke built out of that material rather than around it.

The one non-negotiable requirement is that the punchline turn on the actual content of the theorem, so that a specialist laughs at the joke and not merely at the fact that I made one. I would be equally happy with a joke hinging on any of the following, or on something comparably specific that you think is funnier: (a) the gap between what the theorem delivers — an affine combination with positive but otherwise arbitrary weights — and the utilitarian slogan of equal weights, which has to be bought separately with an anonymity assumption or with the impartial-observer argument; (b) the fact that Pareto indifference is anything but innocent once risk enters, since an ex post prioritarian who passes well-being through a concave transform will agree with everyone about certain outcomes yet refuse to be indifferent between two lotteries that leave every individual indifferent, on the grounds that a concave function of an expectation is not the expectation of a concave function; (c) the complaint, associated with Weymark, that the theorem is really a claim about which expected-utility functions may represent a social ordering rather than about the social ordering itself, so its bearing on utilitarianism as a moral doctrine is thinner than advertised; (d) the assumption that individuals and society are ranking the same prospects under the same probability assignments, and the trouble that causes when people disagree about likelihoods and are unanimous for mutually incompatible reasons; (e) the quiet work done by cardinal, interpersonally comparable utilities that the theorem consumes without explaining where they come from.

Please avoid the following: jokes that would work unchanged if I substituted Arrow's theorem, the veil of ignorance, the trolley problem, or the Prisoner's Dilemma; mathematician-and-philosopher-walk-into-a-bar formats; puns on utility that would serve equally well for a power company; and any punchline whose entire content is a flat assertion that interpersonal comparison is impossible, which my audience has heard several hundred times. Nothing in the joke should depend on knowing a citation, a page number, or a verbatim line from a paper, since I will be delivering it aloud without notes. For the deliverable, give me two or three self-contained jokes of no more than four lines each, and follow each with a single sentence stating which hinge it uses and whether it requires the audience to have tracked the technical part of the talk or whether it will also land with the people who came for the free wine. I am looking for the sort of joke that gets an actual laugh in a room of social choice theorists, not a courteous one.
