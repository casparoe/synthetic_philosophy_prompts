ok so my sister got me one of those mail-in DNA kits for my birthday and I've been arguing with her about it for a week — she thinks the little bar charts in the report tell her how much of her personality is "caused by her genes," and I keep failing to explain why that's not what the numbers mean without sounding like a bore. I want to make the point by sending her a parody instead of another lecture. Could you write me a fake direct-to-consumer genetic report from an invented company (call it something like Helixly, or whatever you like better), formatted the way these things actually are: a greeting, three or four trait sections with cute made-up traits, a "Heritability Meter" described in words, star ratings, confidence intervals, and then asterisked footnotes that get progressively longer and more desperate as the report goes on, ending in a dense legal block. Around 750–900 words.

The important constraint is that the company must never actually say anything false — no invented statistics errors for me to point at. The comedy should come entirely from things that are literally defensible but framed so they mislead: a confidence interval that turns out to span nearly the whole scale, a trait whose score changes when the company switches which reference population it used, a "premium tier" that promises you your very own personal heritability number as if a variance ratio could belong to one person, a footnote admitting the estimate went *up* after everyone in the study started going to equally good schools, a disclosure about which assumptions the twin-based numbers lean on (the equal-environments one, assortative mating, the gap between the twin estimates and the ones you get from measured DNA), and one buried line conceding that a high within-group number says nothing about why two groups differ on average. Also work in the PKU-shaped point somewhere — that a trait can be about as genetic as it gets and still be switched off by changing the environment — and have it appear in the worst possible place, like fine print on a page about an upsell.

Two more things: include a short customer-support chat transcript where the customer asks a perfectly ordinary question ("so how much of my messiness is my genes?") and the agent answers accurately and completely without ever answering it, and include one quoted line from the company's Chief Science Officer — a made-up person — in the press-release voice, saying something reassuring that a statistician would immediately recognize as a non-sequitur. Keep it deadpan the whole way, no winking, and make sure someone with zero stats background can still tell what's being made fun of. If a behavior geneticist read it, I want them to laugh and then say "yeah, that's exactly how these reports read."
