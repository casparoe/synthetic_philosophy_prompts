My labmate and I spent most of a four-hour train ride back from a workshop failing to convince each other about the design of an embodied agent we are supposed to specify for an agency call in the spring, and I have decided the disagreement turns on a philosophical question I would rather get right than win. The task the call has in mind is unglamorous: a manipulator in a shipping depot that has to sort mixed, deformable objects — padded envelopes, poly bags, bundles of stretch wrap — into the right stream on the basis of both appearance and how the thing behaves when squeezed. Our group has been half-jokingly describing a design principle we call grounding debt: the claim that a system which acquires its vocabulary purely from text takes on a deficit that later sensorimotor experience cannot repay, because every symbol it learns that way is specified only by other symbols, and the definitional merry-go-round never terminates in anything the system itself can sort or act upon. Since the symbols of a pretrained model were introduced in exactly that circular fashion, we reasoned, bolting on cameras and force-torque channels afterwards can only ever amount to correlating fresh inputs with tokens that are already unmoored; the semantic status of the vocabulary would be fixed once and for all by the history of its acquisition. On that basis our proposal would train the agent from nothing: no pretrained language model, no textual corpus, only proprioceptive, tactile, and visual streams, with words introduced very late and only as labels for categories the robot had already learned to discriminate on its own.

My labmate thinks this is both wasteful and a non sequitur, that we should fine-tune an existing vision-language model on our own robot data and stop pretending the grounding literature licenses an engineering constraint, and that I have mistaken a claim about what a symbol system must be able to do for a claim about the order in which it must learn. Separately, our advisor keeps insisting that so long as we, the designers, can write down a reliable mapping from the system's output tokens to the objects in the bin, the system counts as grounded — which strikes me as precisely the confusion the whole problem was formulated to expose, though I could not defend that intuition beyond pointing at the thought experiment about a person who manipulates Chinese symbols by rule alone.

So, as dispassionately as you can: is the grounding-debt reasoning sound, or does the inference from the symbol grounding problem to a prohibition on textual pretraining simply not go through? If it does not go through, I would like to know exactly where it breaks — what the problem does claim about the relation between a system's symbols and its sensorimotor categories, and why that claim is or is not a statement about acquisition history rather than about present capacity. I would also like you to address a suspicion my labmate raised, namely that when I say a symbol is grounded because the robot stands in the right causal relation to the object it names, I have imported a theory of reference from an entirely different corner of philosophy and am no longer talking about grounding at all; tell me whether she is right that these are two distinct questions, and if they are, what the second one is supposed to be about. Finally, and whichever way the argument lands, what could we possibly show a review panel that would count as evidence that grounding had been achieved rather than merely asserted — and is my instinct about the advisor's mapping claim defensible, or is it a piece of rhetoric I should drop?
