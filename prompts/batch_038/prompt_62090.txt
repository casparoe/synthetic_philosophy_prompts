I'm TA-ing a grad seminar this term on the practical side of the Vienna Circle — the part where these people actually tried to build things rather than just argue about protocol sentences — and next week's session is on Otto Neurath's picture-language work, the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics that later got called Isotype. Half the class is philosophy, half is information design, and the last time I attempted this session it collapsed into a shouting match: the design people said the picture method is the ancestor of every bad infographic in the world, the philosophy people said they were missing the whole point about notation and who gets to check a claim. I want to hand out a written dialogue at the start of class that gives both sides at full strength, so people have to engage with the actual argument instead of their tribal reflex.

So: write a dialogue between a defender of the Isotype method and a defender of the plain coordinate chart — line graphs, scatterplots, aligned scales, small multiples, the works. Both are sharp and both are right about something. Put them in front of one concrete object and let them fight over it: say a wall chart of tuberculosis deaths per year in some city from 1900 to 1935, rows of identical little symbols, each symbol standing for a fixed number of deaths, with the title, the legend and the source printed on the chart itself. I'd like the two positions seeded roughly like this, though you should push them past it. The Isotype side holds that the first rule of the method is that a bigger quantity gets more of the same-sized symbol and never a bigger symbol, because scaling makes you compare height or area and the eye is bad at that, whereas repeated symbols can genuinely be counted; that the figures are standardized and repeatable and drawn without perspective; that the real intellectual work is the transformation step, where somebody decides what to show, what to leave out and in what order, and that this work is done in the open and printed on the face of the chart; that the goal is a comparison a person can make standing up in ninety seconds and still remember next week, which is why the museum's motto was that remembering simplified pictures beats forgetting accurate figures; and, deeper down, that a notation is only democratic if non-specialists can check it, combine it and argue back with it, rather than a specialist formalism that quietly outsources all verification to experts. The coordinate-chart side holds that position along a common aligned scale is the channel people judge most accurately, so a plot carries more real information per square inch than a grid of icons; that quantizing everything to a fixed unit means the method structurally cannot show a rate, a change in slope, the shape of a distribution, an outlier or any uncertainty at all — you cannot read a second derivative off a row of little men; that there's the remainder problem, and if you answer it by drawing half a symbol you've smuggled scaled area back in through the back door and lost the rule you were proudest of; that forcing a continuous variable into countable units is a fiction about the world dressed up as honesty about it; that nobody actually counts thirty-seven icons, they subitize four and then estimate the block's area, which is exactly the sin the first rule was meant to prevent; and that the same simplification machinery will sell any message whatsoever, so the transformation step is precisely where the politics hides while the chart looks homespun and neutral.

A few things I care about. Neither side gets to be a strawman and neither should win outright, but both should land real hits and get pushed back on them — I want the reader to feel the cost of each view, not just the charm of it. Somewhere in the middle the argument should visibly change register: they start off trading claims about perception and accuracy and end up realizing that at least part of what they disagree about is who the chart is for and what would even count as the chart being right — but have them notice that shift inside the exchange, please don't have anyone step out and summarize it. Don't let either character quote or cite a real text, paper or slogan as if from a source; if they need a number or an authority, invent one, since I'd rather have a clean invention than a fake citation. Keep it somewhere around 900 to 1300 words, natural spoken register, and let a bit of the room be present — they're standing in front of the thing, one of them keeps pointing at the rows — but not so much stage business that it drowns the argument. And it has to work for people in my class who have never heard of Isotype in their lives, so the positions need to come out of what they say to each other rather than from exposition dumps. Oh, and no closing narrator voice telling us what we just learned, that's the one thing that would make me want to throw the handout away.
