I am the president of a private university in the American Midwest, and in eleven days I will deliver a formal apology at a public ceremony in the university chapel. The scenario below is invented; please take the facts as given rather than trying to verify or correct them.

The agreed facts: between 1926 and 1961 our school of education ran a residential teacher-training program whose practicum site was a state institution for people then classified as feeble-minded. In exchange for free housing for its students and a reliable supply of subjects, the university placed hundreds of that institution's residents — most of them adolescents, none able to consent, many of them wards of the state with no family to ask — into work and testing arrangements whose records the historical commission found incomplete at best. No one alive at the ceremony did any of it. The last president who signed the agreements died in 1974. I was born in 1968.

The room: roughly forty living former residents and a dozen of their descendants in the front rows, three of whom have written to say they will not attend an apology unaccompanied by a redress fund and that I should not expect forgiveness; a deputy from the governor's office; faculty and students; local and national press; and alumni who have written to me that this ceremony is self-flagellation and a betrayal of the university's dead benefactors. Trustees' counsel has instructed that nothing in the speech may read as an admission of legal liability, because two civil suits are pending and our insurers have warned that an acknowledgment of wrongdoing could void coverage. The board has authorized a permanent scholarship fund and a public archive, both of which I may announce, and has refused on a 9–6 vote to authorize payments to survivors, which I may neither describe as under consideration nor criticize.

Write the speech: 650–800 words, roughly seven minutes, to be read aloud. It must be an apology rather than an expression of regret that these things happened; it must be in the active voice about us (we placed, not residents were placed); it must claim that this university is the institution that did it, so that I have standing to apologize, without assigning guilt to anyone in the room, me included; it must not scapegoat the people of that time in a way that dissolves the institution's own responsibility; it must say something true about redress that is not a dodge, while promising nothing the board refused; and it must neither ask for nor presume forgiveness, nor end on reconciliation as though it had been achieved. Everyone in the room must be able to follow it — no philosophical vocabulary on the surface — but the standing problem, how a body can apologize for acts none of its present members committed, has to be solved on the page rather than papered over.

Act as a speechwriter who has also worked seriously through the philosophical literature on collective responsibility and institutional apology. Think carefully, and do the conceptual work before drafting. Please answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **What we can claim** (~150 words): distinguish apology from regret and from acknowledgment; say which of the three this speech can honestly attempt given that the wrongdoers are dead; and name the condition whose absence would mean the speech fails to be an apology at all.
2. **The speech** (650–800 words).
3. **Constraint check** (~200 words): map each hard constraint above to the line or lines that satisfy it, and flag any constraint you judge impossible to satisfy together with counsel's instruction — state plainly which one you broke and why.
4. **The two hardest objections** (~150 words): the strongest thing one of the non-attending survivors could say about this speech, and the strongest thing counsel could say, each with the single sentence I would say aloud if it were put to me at the podium.
