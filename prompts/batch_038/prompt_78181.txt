ok, I need to get this down while I'm still annoyed. I just spent three hours arguing with my friend Dara about something I actually have to vote on, and I want a memo I can circulate. I sit on the Remembrance and Public Space committee for Harrowell — seven of us, council-appointed, mostly retirees and me as the token under-50. Our town is built on one story: in October 160 years ago, during our civil war, a company of local men held the stone bridge over the Alder for two days against a much larger force, and the story everyone knows is that they bought the time the retreating army and the civilians needed to get clear. All of them died. There's a statue of the youngest one holding the colours, "they held the bridge" is literally on the council letterhead, and every October the schoolkids come down to the water and read out the 61 names. Two years back an archivist went through the regimental papers and turned up material that doesn't survive contact with the ceremony: the army had already crossed the previous evening, so on the second day there was no retreat left to cover; the company was mostly conscripts rounded up that August, including something like twenty men from the mill district who'd petitioned to be exempted; the fighting was about nine hours, not two days. Worst part is a letter from the sergeant commanding them saying their orders were to keep the crossing shut against "the rabble going the wrong way" — fleeing civilians and stragglers from their own side. It's published, its not contested, and the county historical society has more or less confirmed it. Worth noting too that the statue and the "they volunteered" framing both date from the 1890s veterans' association, thirty years after the fact, so the version we're defending isn't even the original one. The big anniversary is next October and we have to say what Bridge Day is. On the table: (a) run it as usual with a small interpretive panel by the statue, (b) keep the day and the names but rebuild the ceremony around the corrected record — no colours, no motto, something about conscription and the mill district instead, (c) retire Bridge Day and mark the date another way. Complications: a donors' group descended from the veterans has offered 90k to restore the statue, money we do not otherwise have, conditional on the inscription staying untouched; two of our members think the archive is beside the point because the day belongs to the families and not to the historians; one thinks (a) is a lie we'd be telling to children's faces.

Here's where Dara and I actually got stuck, and it's the thing I want the memo to wrestle with instead of smoothing over. Dara's line is that the fix is easy — tell it accurately, swap myth for history, done. I don't buy that, because I don't think there's any version of Bridge Day that is merely accurate. A commemoration doesn't describe, it honors: it picks people out and says these ones and not those, and it recruits everyone standing there into saying it too. So when you strip out "they volunteered and they saved us" you haven't produced a truer version of the same honor, you've produced a completely different act — possibly the act of honoring men for dying under orders they never chose, which is its own strange thing and not obviously something a town should be doing at a riverside with eight-year-olds. My worry is that (b) is just a myth with the serial numbers filed off, and it will feel like one to the families within a generation. Dara's worry is that I'm using that as cover to leave (a) standing. Neither of us wants the old story told on purpose, and Dara keeps saying I'm confusing what a ceremony claims with what it does, which might be right but hasn't been argued.

So: draft the memo I'll send to the other six before Thursday. I need a clear recommendation — (a), (b), (c), or something else you can defend — with a concrete timetable across the twelve months to next October, including an explicit call on the donors' money and whether we take it on their terms, and an answer on the statue itself. Then a section that treats the pro-(a) position at full strength rather than swatting it: the families, the idea that the day's meaning isn't a factual claim at all, six generations of people who formed themselves around this story, and the fact that a panel nobody reads is functionally the same as silence. Then criteria the committee could apply to the next case, because there will be one — we've got four other memorials on the inventory and I want something seperate from my own gut. Then the two things that will decide the vote: do the 61 names stay, and do the schoolkids still read them aloud, and if so in what framing and with what said to them beforehand. And Dara and I both want you to engage the claim/done distinction I sketched above — you can sharpen it, rename it, or tell me it's confused, but say which and why, don't just write past it. Roughly 900 to 1200 words, plain prose, short headings, and written so that a committee member who ends up disagreeing can still tell they were taken seriously.
