I spent thirty-eight years in reactor physics — core design, transient analysis, a bit of licensing work — and I've been retired about four years now. Lately I've been reading all this AI stuff about "takeoff speeds" and I keep getting the feeling that the two camps are arguing past each other because nobody has bothered to say clearly what actually sets the speed of the event.

In my old world the answer is brutal and simple. A thermal power reactor is controllable almost entirely because of delayed neutrons: they're only about 0.65% of the neutrons from U-235 fission, but the precursors emit on a scale of seconds rather than ten-to-the-minus-five, so the mean generation time gets stretched from microseconds-ish to something like a tenth of a second. Three orders of magnitude, and that is the whole ballgame. As long as you stay below prompt critical, the power can't run away faster than the slow species arrives, and the control rods — negative feedback applied by humans who have seconds to think — sit comfortably inside the loop. Go prompt critical and the period is milliseconds and nothing human is in the loop anymore. Same fuel, same geometry, same physics. The speed is set by whether any necessary link in the chain is one of the slow ones.

So here's my question, and I want you to actually engage with the analogy rather than wave at it:

1. What are the genuinely distinct quantities people are slopping together under "takeoff speed"? I count at least four: the rate capability itself grows, the gain of the feedback loop, the supply time of the slowest necessary input, and the warning time available to whoever is trying to steer. How do those relate to each other, and which one is each camp really betting on?

2. Does the delayed-neutron structure carry over? My guess is that fabs, grid power, training data, capital, physical deployment and human review are the slow species — if every round of self-improvement needs them, the loop can't outrun them no matter how smart the system gets. Is that sound, or is it a bad analogy, and if bad, where exactly does it snap?

3. Does "capability overhang" have a counterpart? To me it looks like the energy already sitting in the fuel: the inventory exists before you insert reactivity, so the size of the excursion isn't limited by how fast you can manufacture fuel. Does that hold up or am I flattering myself?

4. The place I suspect my picture really fails: in a reactor, reactivity is a parameter you set and power grows at a fixed period. But self-improvement is a loop in which the gain itself rises — each generation gets better at building the next. That isn't exponential, it's something worse. Explain that difference properly, and specifically what it does to the argument "progress has always been smooth, so it will stay smooth."

I don't care about date predictions. I want the structure of the argument, and an honest verdict on whether neutron economics is a real help here or just an old physicist reaching for the nearest familiar system. Don't send me references, I'm not going to look anything up — just reason it through with me.
