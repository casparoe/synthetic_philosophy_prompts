So I've been volunteering for about three years on the programming committee of a small human rights film-and-talk series that runs out of a community center. Nothing fancy — four events a year, one of them always a commemorative thing in the spring. This year two groups came to us within a week of each other. One is an association of families descended from survivors of a famine a government deliberately caused back in the 30s; nobody's ever agreed on the death toll, the estimates swing by millions, and the people who actually lived through it are basically all gone now, so it's mostly grandchildren doing the remembering. The other is a group of survivors of a killing campaign in the 90s, I think around 800,000 dead in a few months, and they have living survivors, some of whom would come testify in person, plus a couple of the perpetrators are apparently still working their way through courts somewhere. I'm leaving both unnamed on purpose, I don't think it changes what I'm asking, except that the second one gets a lot more public attention around here anyway.

We have the budget and the room for exactly one of them. The committee spilt 3-3, and as chair I hold the tiebreak, and honestly the argument has stopped being about the event. Half of them say the two things can't be compared at all and that even trying is gross — that "which one was worse" is the wrong question entirely, each was its own absolute horror, and one of them said something like a single person's death is already infinite so you can't do arithmetic with it, they're both infinitely bad. The other half say that's sentimental nonsense, that of course you can compare, more people killed is worse, and refusing to compare is just a way of not having to think. Somebody brought up that old line about one death being a tragedy and a million being a statistic, which I think they meant as support for the second side, but then everyone argued about what the line even means. Meanwhile last spring we tried rotating between communities so nobody had to be chosen over, and both of them were offended, so "take turns" isn't the easy out either.

I keep feeling like each side is right about something and wrong about something else, and also like the fight they're having isn't quite the fight we need to be having. Can you help me see what's actually going on here? Is ranking two atrocities that differ this much in scale and in shape even a coherent thing to do, or is the no-comparison crowd onto something real that the numbers people are just steamrolling — and if comparing is possible, what would have to be true for a bigger death toll to actually make something worse rather than just bigger? Seperately, does my tiebreak vote even turn on the answer to that, or is there a different question buried underneath it that six tired volunteers could actually agree on? I'm not asking you to tell me who to pick. I mostly want to walk into Thursday's meeting knowing what the disagreement is really about.
