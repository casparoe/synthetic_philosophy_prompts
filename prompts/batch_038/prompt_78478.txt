I teach RE at a non-faith secondary school in the north of England and something has been nagging me. By law the pupils have to have a daily act of collective worship, most of which should be "of a broadly Christian character" — which in pratice gets read as 51% of school days in a term, with the rest up to us. Parents can withdraw there kids, sixth-formers can decide for themselves, and the local SACRE can lift the Christian requirement for a whole school. Meanwhile the RE I teach is meant to be even-handed about all the principal religions, and I can't be required to lead or attend the worship myself even though the pupils have to be there.

What theory of the relation between a political community and religion does that package presuppose? Two bits especially. Why is establishment a proportion — 51% of days — rather than all-or-nothing; what has to be true about religious claims for a majority share of the calendar to be the right shape of settlement? And what does it mean that the adult's conscience gets protected (I needn't lead it) while the child's attendance is the default?

Tell me which rival views this rules out, whether the even-handed RE half can really sit with the worship half, and where the whole thing is philosophically underdetermined — i.e. which very different theories would license the same arrangement. And if you swapped the presupposed view for a public-reason one, what would concretely have to change?

British English please.
