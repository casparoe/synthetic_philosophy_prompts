I spent forty-one years as a physical chemist in a national laboratory and have been retired for three, during which time I have worked through a good deal of logic on my own. I have now come to the view usually called dialetheism, and to the family of non-explosive logics built to accommodate it. My instinct, formed over a career, is to distrust a hypothesis until I know what it would take for it to fail. So before I devote another year of retirement to this literature, I should like a specification rather than a survey.

Write out the desiderata that a dialetheist thesis must satisfy in order to be taken seriously by someone in my position: not as an ingenious treatment of the Liar, but as a claim about the world, of a kind a natural scientist could in principle have reasons for or against. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a partisan preference of one school — I suspect that a fair amount of what is demanded of these theories by their critics is really one camp's taste dressed as a condition of adequacy. Where two requirements pull against each other, say so; and if some subset of them cannot be jointly satisfied, state it plainly, since that would mean the disagreement is misframed rather than merely unresolved. A numbered list with the motivation and the negotiability attached to each entry is what I am after; a table is acceptable provided it does not cost you the reasoning.

Please build the list against cases of the sort I actually met, so that it does not float free of practice. In linear elastic fracture mechanics the stress field diverges as the inverse square root of distance from a crack tip, so the theory predicts conditions that violate the assumptions from which it was derived. A van der Waals isotherm below the critical temperature assigns mechanically unstable states to the fluid, which the equal-area construction then discards. A finite sample has an analytic partition function and therefore, strictly speaking, no phase transition at all, yet melting points are measured daily. Molecular dynamics propagates nuclei as classical point masses over potential energy surfaces computed quantum-mechanically. An optical engineer designs propagation with Maxwell's equations and predicts detector statistics with photons. In every one of these a competent practitioner holds and uses commitments that a strict reading renders inconsistent, and in none of them does anything go wrong: no explosion, no predictive failure, no loss of grip on the parts of the theory that matter.

That contrast raises what I take to be the crux, and I should like the specification to meet it head-on. There appear to be two projects in the neighborhood. One is paraconsistency as a discipline for reasoning from bodies of information that are jointly inconsistent — which is roughly what my colleagues were doing, whether or not they would have put it that way. The other is dialetheism as the metaphysical claim that some contradictions are true of the world rather than of our descriptions of it. Say what the requirements look like for each, where the two come apart, and above all what the dialetheist must supply over and above the paraconsistent apparatus in order to earn the stronger claim. If the honest conclusion is that scientific practice of the kind above gives that stronger claim no support whatsoever, then the list should demonstrate why, and should say what sort of case, if any, would supply it.

Relatedly, address confirmation. I was trained to expect that a claim about the world has consequences that could have come out otherwise, and I want to know whether the desiderata should include a requirement of that shape — that a true contradiction must be capable of being evidenced, or must alter what one is licensed to infer or to do — and, if such a requirement is included, exactly what it costs the dialetheist and whether it can be met without collapsing the view into the weaker project.

Finally, name the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating candidates, and then rank the surviving families of response very briefly: dialetheism proper; gappy or fixed-point treatments of truth; revision-theoretic approaches; contextualist diagnoses that relocate the inconsistency to the circumstances in which sentences are assessed; and the classical position that the paradoxes reveal nothing except that our expressive habits require policing. A few sentences each will suffice. I am not asking for references or a reading list, which I can assemble for myself; I want the reasoning exposed where I can examine it.
