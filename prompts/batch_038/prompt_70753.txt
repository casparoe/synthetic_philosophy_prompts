I retired nine years ago after thirty-four years running a trace-analysis laboratory, and I have spent the retirement reading epistemology, which keeps supplying vocabulary for things I watched happen for decades without being able to describe. I am writing about one thing it has not supplied, and I should like to know whether it exists under a name I have failed to find. If it does not, I should be glad of a coined term — but with a definition and stated boundary conditions, since a bare label would tell me nothing about whether I have actually identified a single phenomenon or two phenomena sitting next to each other.

The pattern, in three instances.

First. In the late 1970s a small number of careful studies concluded that a certain trace element was elevated in a particular population. The papers were accepted, cited, taught, and treated as settled. Nothing was subsequently found wrong with them: no fabrication, no confounder anyone could name, the instruments calibrated as well as instruments were then calibrated. What changed was the use. The finding became the basis of a regulatory limit, and the limit became the basis of a public-health programme. From that point onward I heard colleagues say of those same papers that they "never showed" the effect, and that the effect "was not established at the time." The phrasing was never *we would not accept that today*. It was *it was not known*.

Second. The same man, in two decades. A senior collaborator of mine said in 1979 that we knew a class of compounds behaved a certain way at low concentration; in 2006 he said we had never known it. He insisted — and I believe him — that he had not changed his opinion about the measurements. No new measurement had appeared. What had changed, in his words, was what now hung on the answer.

Third. Two people, one report. A researcher deciding whether to spend a sabbatical pursuing a result calls it solid; an engineer deciding whether to halt a production line on the strength of it calls it, at best, suggestive. Neither appears to be using a private sense of "solid." They are not talking past one another, as far as I can observe; they disagree about whether the evidence suffices, and each seems to me to be reasoning properly.

I have read that some philosophers hold that whether a given body of evidence suffices depends on what it costs to be wrong, while others hold that what varies is the standard carried by the word "knows" in the mouth of whoever is assessing the case. I follow both arguments and I take no side here. What I notice is that both are about one case assessed at one moment. Neither, so far as I have found, addresses what I keep running into, which is that a raising of the threshold appears to travel backwards through time.

So, three questions:

1. Is there an established concept for the backward-looking part specifically — the reclassification of a past verdict when present costs rise, with no new fact about the original evidence and no allegation of error against the people who rendered it? What are the nearest concepts in the literature, and precisely how does what I describe differ from each?

2. Which commitment does the practice carry? When my collaborator says we never knew it, is he saying the standard moved and the earlier verdict was correct by the standard then in force, or that knowledge is one fixed thing and the earlier generation simply lacked it? Can both readings be sustained, and what would decide between them? I would especially like to know whether the position one takes on my first and second instances constrains the position one can take on the third.

3. If there is no name, please give one, define it, and mark its edges. I want to know what falls outside it: a result overturned by new data plainly does not count, and neither does a discipline merely changing its taste in methods. Where exactly does this stop being the phenomenon and become ordinary error-correction, or ordinary progress, or the mundane fact that each generation thinks the last one was sloppy? A good answer, for me, is one that lets me look at a real case from my own career and say which side of that line it is on.
