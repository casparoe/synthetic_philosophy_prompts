A friend and I argued late into the night about virtual realism — the Chalmers-style view that virtual objects are real digital objects rather than fictional ones — and we ended in a stalemate neither of us is satisfied with. Her charge was that virtual realism and virtual fictionalism never actually disagree about any case we could name: is a virtual sword a sword, does a virtual killing harm anyone, does a server shutdown destroy something — both views deliver the same verdicts once you fix what the code does and what the player experiences, which makes the position either trivially true or a merely verbal dispute. My reply was that the two views must come apart somewhere off the beaten path, in cases the paradigm examples screen out: objects whose computational realizer is distributed or only intermittently instantiated, glitches that break the functional profile without any user noticing, saved worlds that persist after the service ends, virtual harms with no experiencing subject at all. But I could not produce a single one of those convincingly, and that failure is what I want to work on.

Write this up as a two-page proposal for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship. I need: a precise formulation of the question — whether the virtual realist/fictionalist dispute is substantive, and if so, which class of cases the substantiveness turns on; an account of why the existing positions leave that question open rather than answering it; the method, including why the attempt to construct genuine divergence cases is the right route rather than the obvious alternatives, such as collecting intuitions about ordinary gameplay or importing a general metaphysics of artefacts and social kinds; a rough month-by-month plan; a statement of what a successful result looks like and what would count as the project failing, including what I should conclude if it turns out that no divergence case can be built at all; and a candid assessment of what is realistically achievable by one person in twelve months.
