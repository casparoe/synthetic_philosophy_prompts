You are a philosopher who works on autonomy, paternalism, and choice architecture. Please think the case through carefully before answering, and take both sides at their strongest rather than at their most quotable.

Our university's digital-wellbeing committee approved a program last term, and two of its members, Naomi and Dev, have been arguing about it ever since. The program works as follows. During course registration, a box is pre-checked authorizing the university's study app to block social and video sites on devices using the campus network; the box can be unchecked at no cost and with no follow-up. The blocking schedule is not uniform — the app uses each student's own network-activity history from the previous term to concentrate that student's blocks in the hours when that student historically scrolled most. To switch the program off mid-term, a student must book an appointment with an academic advisor; appointments are offered only on weekdays between 9 and 4, and the current wait is roughly two weeks.

Their exchange:

(1) Naomi: This is paternalistic and objectionable. The university is substituting its judgment about how a student should spend an evening for the student's own judgment at the moment the choice arises, and announcing the mechanism in advance changes nothing — being transparent about a technique does not make it any less a substitution of judgment.

(2) Dev: There is no substitution, because the student authorized it. The box is checked by default, but it can be unchecked freely, so what the app does later executes the student's own earlier, calmer judgment rather than the committee's. Far from paternalism, this is a student using the university as an instrument against her own predictable weakness.

(3) Naomi: That earlier judgment is a product of the program. The authorization was extracted by a default, during a rushed registration window, on a form written by the people who want compliance. Consent that a program manufactures cannot also be the ground of the program's legitimacy; otherwise any manipulation can be laundered by attaching a pre-checked box to it.

(4) Dev: Your standard proves too much. On that account every preference anyone holds was manufactured by some prior arrangement of circumstances, so nothing could ever count as a person's own judgment, and no policy could ever be non-paternalistic. You have argued yourself out of a distinction you need. And the exit is genuine: it costs an appointment, not a fee and not a prohibition. Under the principle this committee itself adopted — that an influence technique is permissible if it would survive being openly announced and scrutinized — the program passes.

Two requests. First, adjudicate turn by turn: for each of the four, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and against what exactly; then say who is more right overall about this program, and identify what both of them are leaving out, since I suspect at least one live issue is being stepped around entirely. Second, be explicit about the epistemic status of your claims: mark where people who work on this are genuinely settled, and where your reading is one contested position among several, and do not let a contested view pass for consensus.
