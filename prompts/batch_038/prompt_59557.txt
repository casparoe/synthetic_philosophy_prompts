After forty years spent calibrating instruments — I am a retired analytical chemist — I have taken up philosophy of mind in earnest, and I have been reading integrated information theory, on which consciousness just is a quantity Φ computed from a system's cause-and-effect structure. A former colleague, likewise retired and likewise reading in the field, insists that Φ is caloric in a lab coat: a fluid postulated to name a phenomenon, invoked to explain heat flow but never itself isolated, and abandoned once better measurements arrived. His evidence is that IIT's predictions look adjustable after the fact — the cerebellum contains most of the brain's neurons yet is written off as nearly Φ-free because its modules run in parallel, while a far smaller patch of cortex counts for much more — and he concludes the theory should be discarded as caloric was, in the spirit of the open letter in which more than a hundred consciousness researchers branded it pseudoscience. I suspect he is overreaching, but I cannot yet say where.

Please audit his analogy for me. Set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of heat, of the thermometer, of the self-repelling fluid, of Rumford's cannon-boring and Joule's paddle wheel. Then mark where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the breaks that matter for his use from the merely decorative ones, and say what the caloric story quietly imports about measurability, conservation, and reduction that Φ may or may not share. Finally, tell me whether his discard-it verdict survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if some other historical analogy — temperature before kinetic theory, phlogiston, vitalism — fits better, say which and what conclusion it would license instead.
