So, bit of context before the ask. My brother is a prison officer at a Category C jail in the Midlands, and over Christmas he watched some documentary about Halden in Norway — the place everyone keeps calling the most humane prison in the world — and he came back to the kitchen table absolutely convinced that we've been doing it wrong for decades and that if we just copied the Norwegians (single rooms, recording studios, guards who eat with the prisoners, no life sentences, that indeterminate preventive-detention thing for the worst cases instead) our reoffending numbers would collapse too. I spent most of Boxing Day trying and failing to explain to him why I don't think the architecture is the load-bearing part, and why "the stats are better over there" doesn't by itself tell you whether anything morally better is happening. He's a good officer and he's not stupid, I just couldn't get the argument straight in my own head while he was talking, and it's been rattling around ever since.

Which is useful, sort of, because I'm a final-year PhD student and the lecturer who normally sets our second-year Philosophy of Punishment paper is on research leave, so it's landed on me. ~45 students, two-hour exam, answer two of four questions, each question worth 25 marks. The problem: for as long as anyone can remember, the rehabilitation question on this paper has produced the identical essay. Students list the standard four justifications of punishment, say rehabilitation is the kinder one, note that Norway has nicer prisons and lower reoffending, note in passing that "nothing works" was the consensus in the seventies and that it's been revised, and conclude that we should be more like Norway. It's fluent, it's accurate, it gets a 2:1 every single time, and it demonstrates nothing at all. I want to write a replacement question that a student cannot answer by reciting that comparison.

Here's the seed I've got, and please build the question out of it rather than around it. Imagine a pilot running in three English prisons — call it the Rehabilitation Credit. A prisoner serving four years or more can have up to a third of their tariff remitted if they complete a twelve-month programme combining cognitive-behavioural work with something more inward-facing, say a course of therapy aimed at their impulse regulation and their attitudes to their victims. Formally, participation is entirely optional and nobody loses anything by declining. But the scheme has two features that I think are where all the interesting pressure sits. First, the parole board is instructed to treat a prisoner's declinature as relevant evidence about unresolved risk, so declining doesn't leave you where you started — it quietly makes your release date harder to reach. Second, the remission isn't granted for good behaviour or for earned qualifications; it's granted for a documented change in the person's own dispositions, assessed by a psychologist, so the thing being bought and sold is the prisoner's character rather than their conduct. And here's the bit that makes the Norway comparison actually do work instead of being decoration: Halden gets its low reoffending without any of this. There's no sentence discount on offer for becoming a better person — the Norwegian model, such as it is, keeps the sentence fixed and tries to make the conditions inside resemble life outside. So a student who wants to say "just make rehabilitation voluntary and it's fine" has to explain why the English pilot's voluntariness is doing the justificatory work that the Norwegian approach doesn't need it to do, and a student who wants to say "the pilot is coercive, so it's impermissible" has to explain what's coercive about it that isn't equally coercive about the whole arrangement of imprisonment plus parole, and what baseline they're measuring against when they use that word.

What I'd like from you, in British English please:

1. The exam question itself, worded so that it's fully self-contained — a student who has never heard of Halden and knows nothing about Norwegian sentencing should be able to answer it from the information given in the question, so include whatever short framing paragraphs or stipulations they need. Keep it answerable in about 45 minutes.
2. A model answer of roughly 900 words, at the level a strong finalist could realistically produce under time pressure, that doesn't just line up the usual objections and counter-objections but actually takes a position and defends it. I want to see the answer wrestle with the two features I flagged above rather than with rehabilitation in the abstract.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 built around five or six specific moves a good answer must make, with the marks each is worth, and — this is the part I care about most — a line or two on each saying how students typically botch that move, so markers are calibrated. I'd also like a short list of the mistakes that should actively cost marks, e.g. treating "it's voluntary" as settling the question, sliding from "reoffending rates are lower" to "these people are morally better", invoking coercion without ever stating the baseline it's relative to, or assuming that because the Norwegian system produces good outcomes it must be aiming at rehabilitation rather than at something else.
4. A brief marker's note — three or four sentences — on what to do with the answer that's well-written and well-organised but is really just the old Norway-vs-America travelogue in new clothes. Should that be a fail, or a 2:1 capped, and how do we tell it apart from an answer that engages with the pilot but gets the philosophy muddled?

One more thing: don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, and please don't have the model answer gesture at named thinkers it can't properly cite — I'd rather it argued from the concepts than from authority, and I can bolt the readings onto the paper myself. And if you're not sure of a detail about how English parole or remission actually works, keep it at the level of the fictional scheme rather than inventing procedural specifics, because I don't want to have to unpick errors in front of forty-five second-years. Ta very much.
