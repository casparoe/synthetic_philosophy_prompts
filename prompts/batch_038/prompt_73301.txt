Context: I co-run a very small decision-theory podcast (think a couple hundred downloads, mostly people I've been arguing with on LessWrong and old OB comment threads for fifteen years). Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends cooperating in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma via superrationality — but *not* in Hofstadter's original spirit, and she'll get prickly if you lump her in with evidential decision theory or with anything mystical about "logical causation." Her pitch, roughly as I understand it: when both players are running the same decision procedure, there aren't two choice variables in the game, there's one. The error in orthodox game theory isn't an error about evidence or about counterfactual influence, it's an error of arithmetic — it counts the same decision problem twice. So "what should I do?" collapses into "what should the shared procedure output?", and the answer is C. She's explicit that this is perfectly compatible with a bog-standard causal picture of the world: no copies of her in the multiverse, no simulations, no predictor, and definitely not "my cooperating is evidence that you cooperate."

She's very good at one deflection: whenever you press her, she says your objection "presupposes there are two agents," i.e. she re-asserts the counting thesis and moves on to the next question. She also freely grants that superrationality evaporates once the players are relevantly dissimilar, and that how similar they need to be is an empirical question with no principled threshold — which strikes me as where the whole view could come apart, but she treats it as a footnote.

Write me seven or eight interview questions that actually get purchase on the counting thesis instead of inviting her to restate it. I want coverage of at least: whether "one decision problem, two tokens" is a substantive claim or a redescription — what would the world have to look like for there to genuinely be two, and how would we ever find out; the dissimilarity threshold, since if there really is one variable, why doesn't near-identity get you most of the reason to cooperate, and does 90% similar mean 90% of the pull?; how her view differs from Bacharach-style team reasoning and the "we"-mode, and whether she's just saying that with extra steps; whether the thesis generalizes — what does it tell her in Chicken, in asymmetric games, in voting, and in the one-boxer/two-boxer fight she's been studiously avoiding; and a closer on what she would accept as evidence against her own view. For each, give the reply you expect her to give and a follow-up keyed specifically to that reply — I want at least two of the follow-ups to block the "you're assuming two agents" move rather than just walk into it.

Format: for each item, a one-line "target:" stating what the question is supposed to force her to commit to, then the question, then "expected reply:" and "follow-up:". Hard cap of 700 words total. No warm-up questions, no "what got you interested in decision theory," no padding — assume she's read everything and gets bored fast, and that our listeners have too.
