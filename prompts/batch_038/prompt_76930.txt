I do equity analysis for a mid-size city transit authority, so I spend my life comparing neighborhoods on metrics. Two patterns keep showing up and I don't have words for either, but it's the second one I care about.

First one: we used to score areas on service level — stops within a quarter mile, bus-hours per household. Two neighborhoods would come out exactly equal, and yet in one people ride constantly and are measurably better off, and in the other almost nobody does, because of stuff we don't fund and cant see from the data: no safe walk to the stop, shift work that doesn't line up with the schedule, a lot of residents who can't manage the last three blocks. Equal inputs, wildly unequal anything-that-matters.

So we shifted toward measuring what people are actually able to do and use. That's when the second pattern showed up. Some households don't ride because they'd genuinely rather drive — a real preference, freely arrived at — and if we count their non-use as a shortfall we're penalizing them for wanting something else. But sitting right next to them are households that stopped wanting it after years of being treated like the service wasn't for people like them. In our numbers those two look identical. I know about expensive tastes, and about adaptive preferences, but neither of those is this: expensive tastes is about people who want more, and adaptive preferences names only the second of my two cases, not the fact that you can't tell the two apart from the inside.

Is there an established term for that second thing — the indistinguishability itself? If not, coin one: define it, give me the boundary cases (what counts, what doesn't), and say how it differs from its nearest neighbors. Don't stop at a label, I want the carving. Also tell me how sure you are that any term you offer is really used that way versus you reconstructing it. And if you think the distinction I'm chasing is confused — that the two cases aren't seperable even in principle, or that I've invented a problem — say that plainly rather than playing along.
