I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences were still being posted in real time, and the thread that never let go of me wasn't decision theory or AI risk — it was the recurring, never-resolved argument about whether pleasure is one thing. Classical utilitarianism treats it as a single quantity that admits of a calculus; almost anyone who has looked closely at phenomenology says the pleasure of cold water on a hot day, of finally landing a proof, of a filthy joke at a funeral, of a hand on your shoulder, and of the first long exhale after a hard run have nothing in common that isn't also shared by plenty of non-pleasures. I'm now a program officer at a small private foundation whose trustees have approved a modest allocation — roughly $180,000 over two years, enough for one postdoc and a nine-person residential workshop, plus an option on a second, methods-trained postdoc slot if the case is strong — for work on precisely this: the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and what would have to be true for hedonism about well-being to survive it. The deliverable we care about is not a settled theory. It is a shared vocabulary and a defensible answer to a narrow question: when two experiences are both pleasant, what, if anything, is the same in them, and is that "anything" the sort of thing that can be put on a single scale and traded off?

Here is the candidate list our advisors have generated, with the labels I'll use. **Q1 (shared-feel vs. shared-job):** is pleasure unified by a common phenomenal tone, or only by a common functional role — being such as to dispose its subject to want its continuation — and is the second option a theory of pleasure or a change of subject? **Q2 (felt location):** pains are conspicuously localizable and pleasures conspicuously are not; is that an introspective artifact, or does it mean the puzzles in the pain literature don't transfer to pleasure? **Q3 (the attitudinal escape):** if pleasure is best analyzed as a propositional attitude — being pleased that p — with no distinctive feel at all, does heterogeneity dissolve, and at the cost of rendering hedonism about well-being vacuous or unfalsifiable? **Q4 (painful pleasures):** chili burn, the hurt after sustained effort, grief and nostalgia, horror, masochism; do these show that valence is at least two-dimensional, and if so, which dimension should a hedonist be tracking? **Q5 (the metric):** even granting some shared feature, can pleasures be cardinally ranked and interpersonally compared tightly enough to support aggregation, or does hedonism shade into a plurality of incommensurable goods? **Q6 (the background problem):** most of a life's hedonic character is diffuse baseline affect rather than discrete episodes; do theories that begin from salient episodes systematically misdescribe the thing that actually matters? **Q7 (the neuroscience anchor):** how much evidential weight should philosophers give the wanting/liking dissociation and the valence–arousal models out of affective science, and at what point does deference to animal models become over-reading?

What I want is a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Rank the seven by how much of the rest depends on their answer, and tell me flatly which one or two I should fund and which I should quietly cut from the workshop program. For each placement, give me your criteria and the trade-offs among them: upstream-ness; tractability within a two-year window by a philosopher paired with a methods-trained psychologist rather than by a lab with imaging access; neglectedness relative to the airtime the item already gets; and whether settling it would change what anyone downstream does. Say which of the seven you take to be a pseudo-problem resting on a false presupposition, and name the presupposition. Note where the ranking is sensitive to the composition of the room, and specify what concrete result — or failed attempt — over the next eighteen months would move each item up or down. Our trustees like machine-readable summaries, so please close with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" giving the complete ordering from first to last, using only the Q-labels separated by ">" and nothing else on that line.
