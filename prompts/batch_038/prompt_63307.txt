I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and this term I co-run an informal reading group on the foundations of statistical learning with two friends from the statistics department. We have spent six weeks going in circles over whether the no-free-lunch results are a rigorous restatement of Hume's problem or an exercise in averaging that has been mistaken for an epistemology. One camp in the group — a visitor I would rather not name — argues that because off-training-set error, averaged uniformly over all target functions compatible with the observed data, comes out identical for every learner and for a blind guesser, no inductive method can be non-circularly preferred to any other, and that this is a theorem rather than a mood. The other camp argues that the result is silent about anything anyone actually does: it is a statement about marginal expected error under one very peculiar measure, it says nothing about the joint behaviour of several learners' errors, and it does not obviously touch a meta-inductive strategy that weights predictors by their observed success. For our end-of-term session each of us has to contribute one page of something in a non-argumentative register, and I would like mine to be satirical, aimed at the first camp and, more precisely, at the discursive habit of moving from a claim about uniform averages over all conceivable target functions to a claim about whether a particular firm should keep shipping a particular churn model.

What I would like you to write is a resignation letter of roughly 900 to 1,100 words from the departing head of machine learning at a mid-sized freight logistics company, addressed to its chief executive, explaining that having at last understood the no-free-lunch theorems the writer can no longer in good conscience deploy a predictive model, and setting out what should replace it. It should read as genuine corporate correspondence — salutation, ordinary paragraphs, sign-off, perhaps a postscript — delivered with the unhurried patience of someone who has thought about this for years and fully expects to be called unreasonable. The comedy has to come from the content of the theorems rather than from the character being an idiot or from generic nerd humour. At least one passage should state the result accurately and recognisably — the averaging over permutations of the target function, the fact that what is being compared is marginal expected generalisation error and not anything about correlations among learners' errors, the corollary that for any two algorithms there are, loosely speaking, as many priors favouring one as favouring the other — so that a specialist can see the writer knows the material; every error should be located in what the character then does with it. Please build out concrete consequences of the conversion: a demand that hold-out validation be abandoned because cross-validated accuracy merely launders a prior as a measurement; a proposal to substitute a uniformly random label generator, which is provably no worse on the relevant average; a section on the firm's maximum-entropy-flavoured modelling assumptions as a metaphysical commitment disguised as engineering; and a passage in which the character mangles the distinction between a prior that is uniform over bit patterns and one that is uniform over bit frequencies, confidently arguing for the wrong one on grounds that sound almost respectable. I would also like at least one place where the character is right and the company is confused — for instance that cross-validation does presuppose something like exchangeability in the data-generating process, so the model selection committee's claim to be assumption-free is empty — because I want a specialist reader to find the satire fair rather than a straw man. Let the letter end on something quietly self-undermining that the writer does not notice, such as making plans for their own future, or expressing confidence that the chief executive will follow the argument, and then reclassifying that confidence as a freely chosen prior on which they have decided to stake their remaining salary. Do not wink at the reader and do not explain any of the jokes. Two further constraints: because I cannot check sources and you cannot either, please put nothing in quotation marks as a quotation from Hume, from the originators of these theorems, from the meta-induction literature, or from anyone else, and paraphrase instead; and please use invented people and an invented company throughout, with no citations, footnotes, or bibliography of any kind.

Format-wise I want this as unadorned as possible: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, just continuous prose of the kind one would send in an email or a letter. After the letter, leave a blank line and continue in the same plain prose with a note of about 250 to 350 words, introduced by a phrase in the running text rather than a heading. In that note, tell me which of the letter's claims about the theorems are true as stated and which belong to the character's confusion rather than to yours; where the piece is least fair, meaning which defender of the no-free-lunch reading of induction would have the strongest objection to it and what that objection would be; and what would change your mind about your own verdict that the character's argument fails — I want the specific consideration that would make you think the uniform average really is the only non-question-begging measure, or that weighting predictors by past success does not in fact escape the result. Finish by saying where you think your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong; I would much rather you flag a place where you have reconstructed one of these theorems loosely than smooth it over.
