For some thirty years I ran an experimental laboratory, and only since retiring have I taken up philosophy with any seriousness; I find the dispute over the epistemic standing of the Church–Turing thesis the most instructive thing I have yet encountered, and also the most slippery. I should like a dialogue, of perhaps six to eight exchanges, between two equally accomplished philosophers of computation concerning what could possibly count as evidence against the thesis. One maintains that it is, at bottom, an empirical claim about the physical world — the claim that no physically realizable device can compute what a Turing machine cannot — and so is in principle refutable by experiment. The other maintains that it is a conceptual or definitional thesis about what we mean by an effectively calculable procedure, and hence not the kind of thing a laboratory result could overturn.

Please set the conversation before a concrete case rather than in the abstract. A research group has exhibited an analog apparatus, exploiting a physical field whose evolution they contend encodes a non-computable real number; the readout, however, is reliable only to some finite number of places, and the apparatus functions only for inputs within a bounded range. Let each interlocutor press the other on exactly what this does and does not settle — above all, whether finite-precision, statistically characterized evidence can bear at all upon a thesis stated about exact values on the natural numbers.

Neither figure should be a straw man, and neither should be permitted a merely rhetorical victory; each must engage the other's strongest formulation. Conclude without a declared winner, but with an explicit account of what each is prepared to concede.
