Two weeks ago I sat in on a journal club in a vision lab that drifted, by the end, into an argument about the color phi phenomenon. I am not in the lab, I have no stake in how it came out, and I will not tell you which of the two I found more persuasive; I took fairly careful notes and have reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can. I would like a genuine adjudication of it.

Framing, so the rest is readable. In the classic color phi setup — first run by Kolers and von Grünau in the mid-1970s, apparently in response to a question from Nelson Goodman — a red dot is flashed at one location and, a short interval later, a green dot at another. At the right spacings and intervals, subjects report a single dot that travels across the gap and changes color somewhere around the middle of its path: at a place and time where no stimulus was ever presented. Dennett's use of this is that content about the midpoint must have been produced after the second flash arrived. He distinguished two ways that could happen, naming them after two styles of censorship. On an **Orwellian** account, the subject really does have an online experience as the sequence unfolds, and the record is edited afterward, before the report; something false was experienced and then rewritten. On a **Stalinesque** account, the content is settled before it becomes conscious at all, so experience of the transition is delayed until the second flash has landed, and nothing false is ever experienced. His further claim is that the two accounts are empirically indistinguishable: color phi tells against a "Cartesian theater" picture of consciousness, but not in favor of either timing story over the other.

The disputants are Miriam, a philosophy graduate student, and Theo, a postdoc in psychophysics. Ten moves, in order.

**Move 1 (Miriam).** Color phi is direct evidence for post-dictive editing. The subject reports green at the midpoint; there was no green at the midpoint. So the system produced content about a moment before the content was available, and the only way to do that is to write the record after the fact. Any view on which experience is a running, moment-by-moment presentation of what is currently impinging on the senses is false, and color phi is the cleanest demonstration of it.

**Move 2 (Theo).** Move 1 equivocates between the time of the report and the time of the experience. Of course the report is assembled after the second flash — reports are memories, and all memories are constructed; that is uninteresting and tells us nothing about consciousness. The interesting question — was there an experience of a green midpoint during the gap that was later edited, or did experience of the transition only begin once the second flash landed — is precisely the question the two accounts answer differently while predicting identical reports. If Miriam's reasoning proves anything, it proves that both accounts are true of reports.

**Move 3 (Miriam).** They are not indistinguishable, because they differ in counterfactuals about the gap itself, not in final reports. Stalinesque says that during the gap there is no experience of a color transition at all: experience of it has not yet begun. Orwellian says there is an online experience — red at the first location, and as things unfold nothing yet in between — which is subsequently rewritten. Those are different states of affairs *during* the interval, and states of affairs during an interval can be probed during it. Concretely: play a short tone at a random moment inside the gap, and afterward ask the subject what color the dot was at the instant the tone sounded.

**Move 4 (Theo).** The probe is still a report, so it inherits the whole problem. Whatever the subject says about the tone moment is produced after the trial and is as revisable as anything else. If she says "green," the Orwellian says the answer was written by the same post-dictive process that wrote the mid-path content; the Stalinesque says no transition content was ever experienced and the answer was constructed on request from what the subject knows about the sequence. The probe adds one more item to the record to be revised. It does not escape the record.

**Move 5 (Miriam).** Then use measures that are not reports. Two designs. (a) *Congruency*: require a speeded manual or saccadic response to the tone, where the correct response depends on the dot's color. If mid-path content is genuinely present during the gap, responses should be systematically faster or more accurate when the required response matches the illusory mid-path color than when it matches only the physical first stimulus. Latencies cannot be edited retrospectively. (b) *Temporal order*: have subjects judge whether the color change came before or after the tone, and compare the psychometric function against a control sequence in which both dots are the same color, so there is no transition to time. If experience of the transition is displaced later by some delay, the whole function should shift by that delay relative to control.

**Move 6 (Theo).** (a) changes the subject. Response latencies index information available to the motor system, and nobody denies that motion-related information is available during the gap — extrapolation, predictive smoothing, call it what you like. Availability of information is not experience of it. If Miriam infers from a latency effect that the transition was experienced during the gap, she has defined experience as access, and then the Orwellian/Stalinesque question was never a question about experience in the first place. (b) is worse: a temporal-order judgment is a report, and both accounts predict exactly the same shift, since both agree the reported time of the change is displaced. Design (b) tests the phenomenon, not the distinction.

**Move 7 (Miriam).** If no online measure can ever count, the Stalinesque account is unfalsifiable, and that is not neutrality — it is immunity. And there is a design that separates them without any timing judgment at all: catch trials. Withhold the second flash on a random minority of trials. On the Stalinesque account the delay never terminates on those trials, so no transition is ever experienced; the subject should be at chance about whether a color change occurred and about where. On the Orwellian account the transition was experienced as the sequence unfolded, and the open question becomes what rewrites it when the licensing stimulus never arrives. The accounts come apart over truncated trials, and a report about a sequence that has already ended is not a report about a delay.

**Move 8 (Theo).** Two replies. First, parsimony: an account on which content is written once, as the system's best estimate of what is out there, is more economical than one on which it is written twice — first online, then again by an editor. Second, the catch trials do not separate them either. Nothing requires the revising content to come from the second flash *of that trial*; it can come from the block. Subjects learn the statistics of the sequence, so on a truncated trial the Orwellian predicts a report of a mid-path change constructed from the prior, and the Stalinesque predicts the same report with the same content from the same estimate. As for "unfalsifiable": that applies the wrong standard. Claims about when experience happens are not obliged to be settled by behavioral data, any more than claims about what it is like are.

**Move 9 (Miriam).** The parsimony claim begs the question, because the Stalinesque account needs a gate. Something must decide that content is now final and may become conscious; until it fires, experience of the transition is withheld. But such a gate must know that no further revision is coming, and that is information about the immediate future. So the economy Theo advertises is paid for with exactly the prescience the distinction was invented to avoid. The Orwellian editor needs nothing of the kind — ordinary memory updating does the work — and the fact that it can be wrong is a prediction, not an embarrassment.

**Move 10 (Theo).** The gate does not need to know the future. A fixed processing latency *is* a gate: experience begins when a bounded interval has elapsed and the estimate has converged. No clairvoyance is involved, and the visual system already runs on delays of that order. And the objection turns around. An Orwellian editor that rewrites the record into specific mid-path content must also know what the final content will be before writing it — or else it writes something that gets written over again, in which case Miriam's "written twice" is really "written indefinitely often," which is not more economical than my account but indefinitely less so.
