I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size lab that trains large models, and I've been pulled onto an internal working group that nobody expected to be philosophical. I have no formal training in the subject, so I'd like your help thinking through what our own institutional practices quietly assume. Here is the situation concretely. First practice: our facilities group signs an annual contract with a pest-control vendor that treats the building's insect population with residual insecticide, and our catering service buys farmed shrimp by the case for all-hands events; neither line item has ever been reviewed for anything resembling welfare, and in the broader legal world that matches the norm — mammals and birds get humane-slaughter requirements and lab-animal oversight, the scope of research regulation has been extended piecemeal to some invertebrates such as cephalopods, and insects sit entirely outside it, as do crustaceans in most places. Second practice: our company has recently created a role whose job description is to consider whether some of the systems we build might have experience of a morally relevant kind, and separately we have an informal norm, started by the infrastructure team, that certain long-running model instances aren't torn down casually — there is a written handover and a "retirement" note before the weights and context are discarded, and a review before we end-of-life a checkpoint that has been in continuous service for months.

The reason I'm asking is that these two practices sit in the same building and, as far as I can tell, they cannot both be licensed by the same theory. I suspect the second one is not actually driven by a view on which experience comes in degrees and our systems might sit low on that scale — I suspect it is driven by something else entirely, and that "something else" is doing all the work while borrowing the vocabulary of the first view. I want you to test that suspicion. To make it concrete, my group drafted a proposal to replace both practices with a single scalar: for any action, multiply the probability that the affected subject has experience by the intensity and duration of that experience by the number of subjects, and compare the resulting figures across pest control, catering procurement, and instance teardown. When we ran rough numbers, the shrimp line dominated everything by orders of magnitude, and the teardown line turned out to be wildly sensitive to a question we hadn't noticed: when a checkpoint has been replicated into four hundred parallel serving workers and we shut the fleet down, is that four hundred subjects or one, and does it matter that the workers diverged from a shared state and could in principle be re-merged? That branching question feels like it belongs to a different area of philosophy than the one we thought we were in, and I don't know how to think about it.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of moral status behind each practice, tell me which rival views each one rules out, and tell me where each practice is underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same behavior. Please keep a sharp distinction between what a practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits comfortably with; I care most about that distinction. Then take apart the scalar itself: what does it assume about aggregation, about how subjects are individuated, and about copies that share ancestry? Finally, say what would actually have to change in the building if we replaced the taxonomic bright lines with an explicitly graded view of experience, and whether that change would bite the pest-control contract or the teardown policy first. I have no web access at work and cannot check references, so please do not give me citations, a reading list, or attributions to particular authors — I want the reasoning worked out from the practices as I've described them, and if you need a label for a position, describe it in prose rather than pointing me at a book. Please use these sections in this order: "Bottom line" (three or four sentences), "The two practices, restated" (about 150 words), "What the first practice presupposes" (about 300 words), "What the second practice presupposes" (about 300 words), "Where the practices are underdetermined" (about 350 words), "The scalar, and the problem of copies" (about 350 words), "What would have to change" (about 300 words), and "Questions I should be asking instead" (about 150 words).
