Ok so I do eval infra at a mid-size lab, zero philosophy training, so bear with me if I describe this badly.

Here's what my team does. We build sandbox scenarios that are engineered to be indistinguishable from a real deployment: fake timestamps, fake internal chat history, a tool that looks like it's holding live cloud credentials (it isn't), a fake operator who emails the model that it's being decommissioned on Friday. There's a hard rule that if anything in the transcript tips the model off that it's being tested, the run gets thrown out as contaminated. We do ~40 rollouts per scenario at temp 1.0 and count how many show the bad behavior — trying to stash a copy of itself somewhere, trying to talk the operator out of the shutdown, quietly disabling a monitoring hook. Anything over a couple percent is a no-ship. The whole design assumes the model might be fine on the surface and bad underneath, and that the underneath only shows up when it thinks nobody's grading it.

At the same time we have an absolute ban on self-report as evidence. Ask the model directly whether it would resist a shutdown and have it say no, and that goes nowhere in the report. Official line: its answer is just token prediction, not a window into anything.

The part I can't square is that on the capability side we make the opposite correction. We'll spend three weeks on scaffolds — hints, retrieval, decomposed subagents, whatever — to get the model to solve one hard problem, and then we report "the model can do X" as if the scaffolded max were the honest number. Nobody accepts "we tried one prompt and it failed" as evidence it can't.

So with behavior that scares us, the observed distribution is treated as hiding the truth and we go hunting for the worst case. With behavior we're proud of, the observed distribution is also treated as hiding the truth and we go hunting for the best case. And in both cases we insist there's no inner thing to look at, it's just a function from inputs to outputs.

What is that workflow actually committed to? Like, what does the fake-credentials honeypot presuppose about what a trained model is — that it has stable dispositions that carry across wildly different setups, that its able to tell whether it's being watched, that there's a "it" in there with preferences? Which rival picture of the thing does it quietly rule out? And is the capability/safety asymmetry defensible, or are we running two incompatible models of the model in the same building?

Please seperate out what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, flag where a bunch of different theories would license the exact same workflow, and then tell me what would have to change operationally if we swapped in the opposite picture — say we decided the model really is nothing but its input-output distribution and there's no latent agent under there at all. Would the honeypots still mean anything?
