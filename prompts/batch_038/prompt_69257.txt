My friend runs a little poetry-translation site where readers send in competing versions of the same source poem each month and she posts a ruling on each one — keep / send back / reject — with a sentence or two of reasoning. She's clearly read too much Hofstadter, the book where he translates that tiny Marot poem dozens of times over, but her rulings are consistent enough that I've started trying to model them, and there's a case coming up that I genuinely can't call.

Her record so far, more or less in her own terms:

- A villanelle rendered as free verse that keeps every image, every clause, even the line order: reject. She said it was a fine poem but not a translation of that poem.
- The same villanelle redone as a villanelle, correct rhyme scheme and correct refrains, but with two images swapped out so the rhymes land in English: keep, and she called it the best thing that month.
- A haiku given as 5-7-5 about blossom and a falling petal: reject — and her complaint wasn't the syllables, it was that the English had no cut, that it just described one continuous thing where the original pivots.
- A concrete poem shaped like a swan, translated into something shaped like a swan with the words almost entirely changed: send back. She wanted the bird kept but the words pulled back toward the source.
- A poem whose whole joke is an untranslatable pun, where the translator moved the pun onto a different word three lines later and kept a straight face: keep. Her note was roughly that the pun has to live somewhere.
- A prose poem that somebody set into rhyming quatrains: reject, harshly — she said the translator had added a form the original never asked for.
- A wordless-e novel submitted as the English of a French lipogram, constraint held, whole passages invented to hold it: keep. She called it the only honest option.
- A version done from a German rendering of a Russian original, the Russian never consulted: reject, no matter how well it read.

Now the one I care about. Somebody's sent in a translation of an old devotional lyric from a language where the sound-echo the poet leans on comes practically for free — the endings repeat whether you want them to or not — and the alliteration is everywhere in the source. Nobody at the time seems to have noticed it or remarked on it; it was just how that language fell. The submitted English keeps the alliteration scrupulously and it's dazzling, but to get it the translator has compressed and wrenched the sense in maybe a dozen places, and it reads to an English audience as virtuoso display that the original never made. A rival submission drops the echo entirely and keeps the sense line by line; it's sober, almost flat.

Which does she keep and which does she reject, and what rule is she actually running? I've got at least three that seem to fit everything above: (i) carry the form over whatever it costs semantically, because the poem just is its constraints; (ii) reproduce in a comparable reader what the original produced in its own readers; (iii) protect the sense and treat form as a tie-breaker. Tell me which of those her alliteration ruling falls out of, whether any of them is actually committed to a verdict here rather than merely compatible with both, and where they part company — because (ii) and (iii) look like they land on the flat version for completely different reasons and I want the follow-up case that would seperate them. If she keeps the dazzling one, what has she given up on the haiku and the swan, and if she keeps the flat one, what happens to the lipogram and the relocated pun? I'd rather have the pressure points named than a tidy answer.

Two more things. I keep wondering if she'd say the echo in the source was a choice or just the language happening, and whether that distinction does any work in her other rulings. And she's said before that a translation shouldn't be better than its original — or maybe that it shouldn't be better than its original was for its own readers. I can't remember which and it matters here, since the alliterative version is plainly the more exciting poem in English.
