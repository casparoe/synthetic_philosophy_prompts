Here's the half-formed idea. The strategy-stealing argument — the one Nash had for Hex and that's since been used on Chomp, generalized tic-tac-toe, Sylver coinage "enders," and a bunch of maker-maker games — goes: suppose the second player had a winning strategy. The first player makes any old move, which by hypothesis can't hurt, then plays second-player against that strategy, ignoring his own extra piece (and when the stolen strategy happens to want the square the extra piece is sitting on, the ignored piece migrates). Contradiction, so no winning strategy exists for the second player; if draws are impossible, the first player wins.

The standard gloss is that this is a non-constructive proof, filed on the same shelf as the √2^√2 example. My suspicion is that the gloss is wrong, and that fixing it is a real question rather than a quibble. Everything here is finite — the tree is finite, the reductio runs over a finite object — so excluded middle costs nothing intuitionistically, and the usual diagnosis (you leaned on a non-constructive existence principle) just doesn't apply. What's lost looks like something else. Read as a procedure, the argument hands you a way to beat any given second-player strategy, but only relative to knowing that strategy; and the opponent's strategy is exactly the thing the reductio discharges, so you can never be handed one. What the proof delivers is oracle-relative, and what everyone hears in the theorem is unconditional, and the gap between those is where the non-constructivity actually lives. Not existence versus construction — uniform-relative-to-an-oracle versus unconditional. There's also the separate step where you go from "the second player can't win" to "the first player can," which seems to need determinacy for finite games; I want the proposal to be explicit about whether that step is doing extra work or is free.

There's a complexity result I half-remember — recent, I think it shows that finding a winning move in some games where strategy-stealing proves one exists is PSPACE-hard — which makes me think the gap isn't just a bookkeeping irritation but something you can put a lower bound on.
