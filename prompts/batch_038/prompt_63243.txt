I sit on the editorial board of my university's philosophy journal — seven of us, one chair, and I am the secretary — and I have got into a dispute that our bylaws do not seem able to settle. I should say at the outset that I am taking a seminar this term on the philosophy of the social sciences, but we have not reached the part of the syllabus that would be any use to me, and this is a live problem rather than an exercise: there is an author waiting on an answer.

The bylaws state that a submission is accepted if and only if a majority of the voting editors find both that it meets the journal's standard of argument and that it falls within the remit of the issue. Votes are supposed to be taken on each of those two questions separately and recorded. In practice, however, our chair has always closed the meeting with a show of hands on the whole question — accept or reject — and it is that count, not the two earlier ones, that has historically gone into the acceptance letter. Nobody objected to this arrangement until last month.

Last month we reviewed a single submission. Here is the tally I logged, editor by editor. On whether it meets our standard of argument: five yes, two no. On whether it falls within the remit of the issue: four yes, three no. On the overall question, put at the end of the meeting: three accept, four reject. I want to stress that no one voted incoherently — every editor who said yes to both questions said accept, and every editor who said no to either one said reject. The chair's email to the author, sent the following morning and signed on the journal's behalf, said that the board had declined the piece.

Three complications. First, one editor was away and did not vote, but wrote in our group chat beforehand that she was content with whatever the board decided; depending on which of the three tallies you treat her as deferring to, she either helps reverse the result or entrenches it, and we cannot agree about what she said. Second, one of the four who voted reject insists that there was no such thing as a board decision at all: she offered her own opinion, as did the rest of us, and she denies both that she is answerable for a verdict she disagrees with and that the journal is the sort of thing that can have one. Third, the chair now believes the written rule was the operative one and therefore that the journal's position is acceptance; she wants to write to the author again and reverse her own email. Half the board says she cannot do that on her own authority, the other half says she can, and nobody can say what would settle the matter.

For next term two fixes have been proposed: either drop the two qualifying votes entirely and have editors vote only on accept-or-reject, keeping their reasons to themselves; or keep only the two qualifying votes and let the rule in the bylaws compute the outcome mechanically, with no overall question put at all.

What I would like is advice on the following questions.

1. What, if anything, did the board decide last month, and what should the journal now tell the author?
2. Is the editor who denies that there was any board decision — and denies that she is bound by one — making a coherent claim, or is she describing a position she is not in fact entitled to occupy?
3. Was the chair's email an act of the journal, and if so, is she in a position to retract it?
4. Which of the two proposed fixes should we adopt, and does either one actually settle the question underneath them, or does each merely relocate it?
5. What should I write in the minutes?

Please begin with a table, one row per numbered question, with exactly these columns: question (restated in a few words); verdict (your answer in one or two sentences); principle (the consideration that decides it, in your own words); strongest objection (the best case against your verdict); confidence (high, moderate, or low, plus a phrase saying why). Then give me your reasoning in prose, question by question. Do not send me a reading list or a list of names to look up, and do not cite anything — I want your own argument, and if you need a term of art, define it in a clause. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: for each verdict, say what fact or argument would overturn it, and say plainly which step in your own reasoning you think is most likely to be wrong.
