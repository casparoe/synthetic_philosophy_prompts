I'm TA-ing an upper-division environmental policy class and I've got about forty of these to mark tonight, so I want a second pair of eyes on one of them before I commit to a number.

The question I set was:

> "Germany's nuclear phase-out and France's nuclear-heavy power system are usually treated as the two natural experiments for the role of nuclear in decarbonizing electricity. In 900 words or fewer, compare the two trajectories since 2010: what actually replaced retired nuclear capacity in Germany, what happened to emissions and to supply security in each country, and what a policymaker in a country with similar industrial structure should conclude. Use specific numbers where you can, and be explicit about which of your inferences are causal and which are merely correlational. (25 points)"

Rubric bands: 22-25 excellent, 18-21 good, 14-17 satisfactory, 10-13 weak, under 10 failing.

Here's the answer, reproduced in full:

---

Germany and France make a clean natural experiment because they started from similar places — rich, industrial, export-heavy economies with roughly comparable per-capita energy use — and then made opposite bets in the same decade.

Germany. Nuclear supplied about 22% of German electricity in 2010. After Fukushima in March 2011 the government shut eight reactors immediately and set a hard end date, and the last three plants were closed in April 2023. Nuclear is baseload: it runs flat out and can't be ramped up and down to follow load, so the lost output had to be replaced by something that also runs around the clock. In practice that meant coal, and above all lignite. Coal-fired generation rose sharply after 2011 and Germany's total emissions went up in almost every year between 2011 and 2023 — the cleanest single piece of evidence that phasing out nuclear costs you carbon. Renewables grew over the period but nowhere near fast enough to absorb the loss, which is why the substitution ran to coal rather than to wind and solar.

France. France still gets roughly two thirds of its electricity from nuclear, and its reactors run at capacity factors around 90%, the highest of any large fleet, which is why France is a consistent net exporter to its neighbors including Germany and why its grid carbon intensity is around 50-60 gCO2/kWh against Germany's 350-400. The point that matters for the comparison is that France has never had a supply shock of its own: the fleet has been steadily available for four decades. The risk France manages is financial — enormous cost overruns on new build — not operational. So the two countries were not exposed to the same kinds of risk. Germany traded a low-carbon baseload fleet for a coal bump; France traded cost overruns for energy security.

Per capita, France's emissions are less than half Germany's, and essentially all of that gap is the power sector: that is what a nuclear-heavy grid buys you.

What a policymaker should conclude. Two things. First, closure is survivable once variable renewables pass a certain scale: Germany's emissions fell in both 2023 and 2024, after the last three reactors went offline, and the closures actually helped by freeing up grid capacity and pushing wholesale prices down far enough to keep flexible gas competitive with coal. Second, new nuclear build in a country without France's state-utility structure is not a decarbonization strategy on any relevant timescale, because the units don't arrive until after the decade you needed them in. So: run existing reactors as long as safety reviews permit, don't order new ones, and put the capital into wind, solar, grids and storage.

On the causal/correlational point the question asks about: the coal claim in paragraph two I'd call correlational, since coal rose at the same time as the closures but other things were changing too, whereas the 2023-2024 claim in paragraph five I take to be causal.
