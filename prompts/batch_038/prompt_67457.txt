Ok so I have accidentally become the person at my org who decides whether stuff gets released to the public, and I need help building an actual procedure, because right now I'm just vibing and it's not going well.

Context: I'm the research lead at a ~40-person nonprofit that builds AI tools for other nonprofits (document processing, hotline triage, grant-writing helpers). My teams publish a lot — blog posts, weights, datasets, code — and until last month nobody reviewed any of it. Then one of them posted a fine-tuned model as a "look how bad this could get" demo, it got scraped and repackaged within about ten days, and I spent a week doing cleanup and apologizing to a funder. Now the ED has made me release reviewer for everything.

Here's what I want the procedure to actually be about. Please approach this as a professional philosopher / expert on the epistemology of emerging-technology risk, not as a compliance officer writing a policy template. The thing I keep running into is the unilateralist's curse — roughly, as I understand it: when any one person can take an action that affects everybody, and each person acts only when their own judgment says go, the group ends up acting way more often and way sooner than it should, because you only ever see the go decisions, and the decider is usually the person who's most excited and most invested. So the fix can't just be "be careful." It's more like: go get the judgment of people who aren't you — especially informed people who think it's a bad idea — and give their view real weight even when you've done the analysis yourself and disagree.

What I need: a decision procedure I can run on a release request. The questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it hard-stops at no; and where it hands the call to a human because it's about values rather than facts. Real constraints: I get two to four requests a month, I have three business days, I'm not a specialist in the requesting team's subfield, we have no outside experts on retainer, anything that comes out "no" has to be co-signed by the ED, and it has to be explainable to a 26-year-old researcher without making them feel surveilled. Also build in a bias check on *me*, not just the requester — including the awkward fact that I'm a co-author on one of the items currently in the queue. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Think carefully, and before you write the procedure itself, brainstorm out loud: what from the idea has to survive translation into a checklist, and what can be dropped without gutting it.

Then run it on three cases: (1) that phishing-model demo again — the team wants the weights out to prove the risk is real, and argues two other groups will publish something equivalent within a month regardless; (2) a de-anonymization result on a public-health dataset we received under a data use agreement, where the intresting part is the method and the method is also the harm; (3) a genuinely boring internal tool where I personally want to hold the post and can't fully articulate why. For at least one of these, the procedure should tell me something I don't want to hear.

Finally: what the procedure can't do. Where it will misfire systematically, and what running it for two straight years does to the culture of a small lab.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
