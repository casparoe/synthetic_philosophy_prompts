I work as a methods coordinator for a health technology assessment body: I staff expert panels and oversee systematic reviews. I am not a philosopher, though I read some epistemology on the side, and I have spent several years running into a particular failure pattern that I cannot find a name for. I would like to know whether one exists, and if not, whether the pattern can be given a precise enough definition to be worth naming.

Here is the pattern. Two analysts of comparable training start from the same body of evidence and reach different conclusions about, say, whether an intervention works. Each regards the other as an equally competent judge of the material, and each responds to the disagreement by moving partway toward the other's assessment — which looks like the right thing to do, since neither can see any reason to prefer their own take that does not simply presuppose their own take being correct. Their views converge. Some months later, one of them is drafting the case summary and cites the fact that both analysts now agree as a further reason for confidence: two people examined the evidence and landed in the same place. But the agreement is not a second line of support. Each analyst's current view is partly a diluted copy of the other's earlier view, which was itself partly a copy of the first one's. The convergence now being cited as corroboration was produced by the compromise, and what is being counted twice is each analyst's own original judgment, coming home after a detour through somebody else's head.

The same shape appears at panel scale, which is what makes it a professional problem for me rather than a curiosity. Members drift toward the middle out of mutual deference; the resulting unanimous report goes to a ministry as the agreement of eleven independent experts. I have watched that number eleven carry real weight in documents whose eleven signatures were generated by averaging.

Two obvious labels come to mind and I think both miss. People would call this groupthink, but groupthink suggests social pressure, loyalty, and a bad climate; in my cases the participants are scrupulous, courteous, and arguing in good faith — the trouble is arithmetic, not temperament. People would also call it an echo chamber, but that term seems to be about a group shutting out voices from outside, whereas here the difficulty is entirely internal to a group that has been open and mutually responsive. I have read enough about peer disagreement to know that the reasonable-seeming response to a disagreeing peer — moving toward them — is contested, and that its opponents hold that you should keep your position. So I also want to know whether this pattern is an objection to the conciliating response itself, or only to what people do afterward, when they cite the resulting agreement as support. On that I want your view, not a summary of the two sides: if you think a view that tells reasonable people to split the difference is thereby committed to licensing this kind of self-support, say so and say why; if you think the bug is downstream of the updating and the conciliator can quarantine it, say that instead and explain what quarantining it would require in practice.

Then the edges. Please tell me which of the following fall inside the phenomenon and which fall outside, and why:

1. Only one analyst moves; the other holds firm. The mover later cites the steadfast colleague's unchanged view. Is that the same thing, or does the pattern require movement on both sides?
2. The two started from genuinely non-overlapping evidence — different datasets, different methods — and compromised only slightly. At what point does partial overlap make their agreement worth something, and can that be made precise?
3. A third party cites the agreement without knowing it was negotiated. Is the defect in the citing or in the convergence?
4. Neither party thinks the other is a peer; one simply defers to seniority. Does the pattern survive the loss of the peerhood assumption?

I would rather have a definite verdict than a survey. If one of the existing concepts in epistemology, in the study of testimony and witnesses, in social choice, or in the tradecraft literature on intelligence analysis already covers this, name it and argue that it covers it — do not hedge by listing five candidates as equally apt. If you conclude that none does, coin a term, define it, and defend the boundaries you have drawn, including the four cases above. Since I will not be able to chase anything down, please explain any concept you invoke in enough detail that I could use it in a methods document, rather than gesturing at where it comes from. Please write in American English.
