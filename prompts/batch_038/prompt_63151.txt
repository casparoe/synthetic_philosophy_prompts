I am assembling my own intuitions about mechanical rigidity — call it *sphexishness*, after the digger wasp — and about when rigidity, or its absence, has anything to do with freedom. What follows is my data: thirteen cases with the verdicts I actually have, not the verdicts I think I ought to have. I know the wasp story as philosophers usually tell it, and I know the ethological rejoinders (that some wasps do eventually break the loop, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be perfectly adaptive). Spare me both. What I want is a principle that earns its clauses against these cases.

1. A digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it outside, and goes in to inspect. While it is inside, an experimenter shifts the cricket a few inches. The wasp finds it, hauls it back to the mouth, and is again moved to inspect the nest before taking it in — so the loop can be run again, and again. Verdict: sphexish, and unfree.
2. The same wasp, but suppose that after some number of repetitions it gives up on the routine and simply drags the cricket in. Verdict: less sphexish, yet not thereby free — the escape looks like another fixed rule, a counter that ran down. I take sphexishness to admit of degrees; I cannot say what the degrees measure.
3. The wasp drags the cricket by its antennae. Cut the antennae off and it will not think to haul it by a leg. Verdict: sphexish, and the cleanest case on my list; I feel no ambivalence here at all.
4. A navigation system that reroutes instantly around closures, roadworks and traffic, but which keeps directing the driver into a lake because its map says the road continues. Verdict: not sphexish overall — it answers to a vast range of counterfactuals — and yet rigid in precisely the respect that bears on the journey. Rigidity seems to be relative to a domain, and I do not know what fixes the relevant domain: the system's own purposes, or the purposes I attribute to it from outside.
5. A chess engine, formidable in every other respect, that is caught by the same sacrifice motif every time, at any time control, with any amount of search. Verdict: not sphexish. Limited, not rigid.
6. My uncle, intelligent and articulate, who can explain at length why advance-fee frauds work and who nevertheless wires money to a fresh variant of the same story every year. Verdict: sphexish — a wasp in a cardigan. Structurally this looks like case 5, and I need the difference.
7. An assistant program that handles open-ended novel tasks but returns the same canned refusal to one class of request, unmoved by any argument however good. Verdict: sphexish in that respect, and more disturbing than case 5, because the system plainly has the general competence to weigh arguments and that competence seems switched off here rather than absent.
8. A partisan, brilliant about everything except one commitment, who recruits new arguments as fast as the old ones are refuted. Verdict: sphexish, and worse than 7 — here the flexibility is recruited in the service of the rigidity.
9. An addict who plans with real ingenuity to get the drug, lies to people he loves, and sincerely repudiates the craving. Verdict: not sphexish — nothing mechanical about the ingenuity — but plainly not free in the sense I care about. So unfreedom without rigidity.
10. A scrupulous man who, after years of reflection, adopts an absolute rule against lying and keeps it even where the costs of keeping it are visible to him in advance and he says he wishes he could break it. Verdict: free — freer than I am. Yet structurally this is case 6 again: foreseen bad outcome, fixed response, incorrigible. This is the pair I most want to keep and cannot reconcile.
11. A clerk who demands a form whose stated purpose is already satisfied by other means, in front of an applicant who is dying, though he has waived the form before and could waive it now. Verdict: sphexish, and blameworthy in a way the wasp is not. The wasp could not have done otherwise in any sense I care about; the clerk could.
12. A monk who rings the bell at the same hour for the rest of his life after his monastery has been destroyed and there is nobody within a day's walk to hear it. Verdict: not sphexish, though I cannot say why. There is no worldly purpose left for the ringing to be thwarted in, or rather the purpose is the ringing.
13. A room thermostat. Verdict: neither sphexish nor unfree — the vocabulary does not apply. But both it and the wasp are fixed-response devices, and I cannot say what the wasp has that the thermostat lacks.

My own tentative principle is that a system is sphexish in some respect when a behavior of its is *locally immune from a competence the system otherwise possesses*. It gets 6, 7 and 8 right. It wrongly clears the wasp in 1 and 3, since the wasp has no general competence for the immunity to be an exception to. And it wrongly condemns the addict in 9, whose craving is about as locally immune from his capacity for reconsideration as anything could be. So it fails at both ends, and I suspect it needs either a repair or a replacement.

What I want from you:

- State a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precisely — as conditions I could apply, not as a slogan. Label each clause with the case number that motivates it.
- Run your account over all thirteen cases and report where it lands, flagging every disagreement with me.
- Say whether sphexishness admits of degrees, and if so what the scale measures: the number of relevant counterfactuals handled, the distance between trigger and purpose, whether the rigidity is defended by the system's other capacities, or something else. Case 2 is the test.
- Say whether sphexishness and unfreedom are one property, two properties, or whether one is a species of the other. Cases 9, 10 and 11 are the evidence I have, and I expect they will force a decision.
- Give me verdicts on three cases I have not listed. Choose ones where your account is most likely to break, and for each say what the main rival account would say instead.
- State plainly which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate, and which of them you think I should give up — in particular whether I must surrender case 10 or case 9.

No survey of the literature, no reading list, no quotations from any text. Use whatever conceptual machinery you like, including familiar machinery, but apply it to these cases rather than describing it.
