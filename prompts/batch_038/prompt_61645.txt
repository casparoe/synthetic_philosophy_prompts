I am co-teaching a graduate seminar on measurement and value, and this week's screening is Bennett Miller's *Moneyball* (2011). The obvious classroom move is to use it as an illustration of Goodhart's law, and I want to resist that move, or at least earn it: I would like a critical reading that treats the film as a work and the law as a substantive claim, and that reports honestly where the two do not fit.

The framework we are working with distinguishes four ways a proxy fails. Regressional: selecting on a noisy proxy necessarily selects for the noise, so the winners look better than they truly are. Extremal: the proxy–target correlation was estimated in a regime that strong optimization pushes you out of. Causal: intervening on the proxy severs its link to the target, because the proxy was an intermediary or a common effect rather than a cause. Adversarial: agents reorient their behavior toward the measure once it governs their rewards. Goodhart's own formulation, in the context of British monetary control, was the adversarial one: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." The familiar gloss — that a measure stops being a good measure once it becomes a target — came later and blurs the four apart.

Please answer the following in one continuous piece of criticism. Which failure mode or modes does the film actually dramatize? I suspect that the scouts' rankings of a prospect by his looks, his swing, and (as I remember the opening meeting) the attractiveness of his girlfriend are being offered as an adversarial story when the film's own logic is regressional, and that the film never notices the difference; test that hunch and tell me if I have it backwards. Then work through the places where the film seems to undercut its thesis: Art Howe's refusal to bat the high-on-base players where Brand wants them; Beane's trade of Jeremy Giambi, who fits the model on base, on grounds the film states in frankly non-statistical terms; and the fact that the dramatic climax is a twenty-game winning streak that Beane himself disavows as the target he cares about, followed by a first-round playoff exit and a closing epilogue in which he declines the richest contract in baseball history and the Red Sox win the 2004 World Series with his ideas. Is the streak the film Goodharting itself, or doing something deliberate with a proxy it knows to be one? Finally, say where the film resists the reading altogether, and what it contributes to the debate that argument alone does not — for instance, whether its portrait of the scouts whose broad, low-resolution judgment is discarded wholesale rather than integrated adds something the four-category taxonomy cannot express.

Do not quote the film's dialogue; work from the scenes as you recall them and flag any you are unsure of rather than inventing detail. Keep the whole thing under 800 words, in prose without headings or bullet points, opening with the two or three sentences that state your overall verdict.
