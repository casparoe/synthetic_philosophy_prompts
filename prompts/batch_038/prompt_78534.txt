For three years I have covered the consent panel of a state medical board for a national magazine; its rulings are published each month with a paragraph of reasoning, and I am writing a piece arguing that the record, taken as a whole, does not pin down the principle the panel thinks it is applying. Its decisions so far:

It sanctioned a surgeon who never mentioned that his own complication rate for the procedure was roughly double the regional average; the patient signed in the pre-op area, premedicated, fifteen minutes before the first incision, and later testified she would have sought a second opinion had she known. It sanctioned a physician who disclosed every relevant fact in writing but took the signature during a night handover, against a forty-minute deadline set by the surgical schedule, from a patient who said she had felt she had no choice. It sanctioned an oncologist who had disclosed everything and allowed a week for reflection, but who conceded under oath that he opened the conversation with an account of a patient who refused treatment and died, selected, he said, because he knew it would frighten her; she now states she would have declined without it. It cleared a surgeon who omitted the same kind of comparative outcome data, where the patient had two unhurried consultations and testified she would have proceeded regardless. It cleared a physician who told a hesitant patient plainly that he considered waiting foolish, disclosed everything, gave her two weeks, and accepted her refusal before she returned on her own initiative and has never expressed regret. And it cleared a consent taken from a patient on a low-dose anxiolytic who was assessed as lucid, fully informed, and under no time pressure; her complaint was only that she had been asked while medicated.

The case now before it: an ophthalmologist recommends surgery, discloses every material risk including the one that subsequently occurs, and gives the patient three weeks with no deadline. Because she has been hesitating, he schedules the consent conversation to follow immediately on the clinic's standard patient-education film about how the disease progresses untreated — factually accurate, and graphically distressing — and he acknowledges arranging that sequence deliberately. She signs, suffers the disclosed complication, and now says she would have waited had she not seen the film, while also maintaining that the film did not change what she believed about her prognosis.

Predict the ruling. Reply with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, and no introduction or conclusion: the first naming every principle that is consistent with all six prior decisions, compressed into that single item even if there are several; the second stating what each of those principles implies for the new case; the third giving your prediction and why you weight the competing considerations as you do; the fourth identifying the closest precedent and saying where the analogy breaks down; and the fifth describing the kind of future case that would force the panel to reveal which principle it has been running.
