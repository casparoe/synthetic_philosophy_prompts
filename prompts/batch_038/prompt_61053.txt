ok so — I've been reading LW and SSC and having the same twenty arguements in comment sections for like fifteen years, and my city's rationalist-ish meetup has asked me to run a session on Rawls. Specifically the original position / veil of ignorance and the difference principle. 90 minutes, about 20 people, someone's living room, a wobbly flipchart, no way to do handouts. I said yes before I thought about it and now I'm dreading it, because half this room has already googled the veil of ignorance and is quite sure they know what's wrong with it.

I want the teaching plan, not the explanation. Please don't explain Rawls to me, I know the material fine — I want to know how to sequence it for *this* room.

What I need:

**The dependency chain.** What genuinely has to be in place before the next thing can land, and where the order is forced vs. where it's just my preference. Concretely: can they understand the difference principle at all before they've got that the parties are mutually disinterested rather than altruistic? Can they get why the veil is a filter on bargaining information rather than an empathy simulation without something else going first? Spell out what depends on what, and flag the spots where you'd order it differently than I'm assuming.

**The one carrying example**, and what it has to have and what it must not have. Everyone in that room will reach for cutting a cake where the cutter picks last — Rawls himself uses something like that for pure procedural justice — and I'm fairly confident that's a trap here, because it makes justice look like splitting a fixed pie between two people, which is exactly what the difference principle is not about. Tell me if I'm wrong. If I'm right, what do I use instead? Hard constraint: no money and no politics in the example, because the second dollars come up the room turns into a tax policy fight and I lose the 90 minutes.

**The misconception map**, with the point in the sequence where each one gets diffused — not at the end, at the point where it would otherwise form. The ones I'm expecting:
- "the veil is just max expected utility with a uniform prior over positions." One guy has literally written this up on his blog and will say it in the first five minutes. The annoying thing is he's half right — it's the old Harsanyi line — and I need to be able to say what the actual disagreement is, not just "no."
- "maximin is an insane decision rule, nobody would ever use it in real life." My instinct is that the right response is that Rawls only thinks it's the rule under some pretty specific conditions, and that the real weight is carried by strains of commitment — the parties have to be able to actually live with and carry out what they chose, forever, with no exit. But I'm genuinely unsure whether that framing is right or whether it's me being generous to a book I mostly disagree with. Say which.
- "the difference principle means inequality is fine as long as the poor are better off than they'd be under equality" — and I expect them to slide between *better off than under equality* and *as well off as possible* without noticing they've moved.
- "so the veil is about imagining yourself poor / compassion."
- "so this settles whether the CEO should get a raise" — basic structure vs. individual transactions.

Tell me which of those I've got wrong or overstated, and add the ones I've missed. I suspect there's something about primary goods being an index rather than a stand-in for welfare that they'll fumble, and something about "they don't know their conception of the good" getting heard as "they have no values at all."

**One question I can ask near the end** that distinguishes "they got it" from "they're nodding politely at a famous dead guy." Ideally one where the correct answer requires having given up one of the misconceptions above — so a person who never had the misconception answers differently from a person who's had it knocked out.

**And what I should openly refuse to get into** and announce that I'm refusing. I'm thinking the Sen-style / disability attack on primary goods as an index, and the fact that the argument against average utilitarianism never really gets finished — but tell me what else has to be cut, and how to cut it without looking like I'm ducking the hard part.

No readings, no bibliography, no "participants could consult chapter such-and-such." Nobody's reading anything and I'm not going to make you invent citations. Just the plan.
