You are a philosopher of perception who has worked carefully through both the conceptual literature on the architecture of the mind and the experimental literature on so-called top-down effects. Please treat what follows as a request for an audit rather than a survey: I am circulating a short statement of my own position to a graduate seminar next week, and I want to know beforehand whether it hangs together.

My commitments are these.

(1) Cognitive penetration is a synchronic, content-sensitive relation. A case counts only if a belief, desire, or comparable state changes perceptual phenomenology in a way that is semantically coherent with that state's own content, and only if the change is not explicable instead by the deployment of attention or by a shift in the subject's response criterion.

(2) The desire-congruent spatial findings — a wanted goal appearing nearer, an incline appearing steeper under a load — are the best-documented genuine cases we currently have. I take them to survive the broad skeptical review of the literature precisely because their content-matching is so tight.

(3) Perceptual expertise — the radiologist who sees the lesion rather than a shadow, the player who sees the position's structure — is penetration too, but it does not offend (1), because it is diachronic: learning recalibrates the system over time rather than letting cognition in while the system is running.

(4) I am a modest foundationalist. Experience confers justification non-inferentially, and that is what allows it to arrest a regress.

(5) I also hold that an experience brought about by a desire carries less justificatory weight than an otherwise identical experience that was not, even where both are accurate.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine ones by severity; for each, state what has to give and what the cheapest available revision costs. Say plainly if one of the pairings is a non-problem I should stop worrying about, and do not report every pair as troublesome simply to be thorough. Before the audit proper, sketch in a few sentences which candidate pairings you considered and discarded. Close by telling me how confident you are in each verdict you have issued, and by singling out the point on which you are least sure and why.
