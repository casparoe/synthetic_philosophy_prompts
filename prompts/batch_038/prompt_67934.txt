Two years ago my daughter, then eleven, had surgery for dense congenital cataracts in both eyes. Before it she had never had patterned vision: light, dark, a hand waved close to her face, and nothing else. She learned the world with her hands, her ears, and a cane. She now measures as having usable acuity — she can track a moving ball, notice an open doorway, tell a face from a wall at two metres. But she cannot find her own cup in a cupboard without reaching in and feeling for it; she cannot say whether an object held at arm's length is a cube or a sphere unless she is given a great deal of time to look; and after two years of lessons she still cannot read print. Trying to work out whether that was the expected outcome sent me, of all places, into the philosophy of perception, and specifically into Molyneux's problem — the question Locke printed from his correspondent concerning the man born blind, taught by touch to tell a cube from a sphere, who is then made to see: whether by his sight, before he touched them, he could now distinguish and tell which is the globe, which the cube.

What unsettles me is that this question has been debated for more than three centuries, that we now have the cases it was a thought experiment about, and that the argument has not moved an inch. The old answers are still the live ones. Berkeley's negative: there is no necessary connection between the tangible and the visible, and any connection between them is established only by experience. Leibniz's qualified affirmative: extension is common to both, so the newly sighted man can reason his way from what he felt to what he now sees. Reid's conditional answer: plane figures should transfer, solids should not, but a blind geometer could calculate which visible figure corresponds to the solid he knows by touch. Then the modern camps, which I have found harder to tell apart: those who read the cataract-restoration studies — children who before surgery could do no better than discriminate light from dark, and who afterwards performed at or near chance on cross-modal matching before improving over weeks and months — as settling the question in the negative; those who say the same studies show only that these children missed the sensitive period for visual development, and therefore leave Molyneux's question untouched; those who point to experienced users of sensory-substitution devices, who report the world as lying out in space and who can identify objects they have only ever received as sound or as vibration, and conclude that modality was never the operative variable; and those who insist the question was never empirical at all, because "could he distinguish" is really asking whether sight and touch share content, and no forced-choice matching task bears on that.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than accommodate, is that the dispute has outlived its question because three questions have been run together under one heading: an architectural question about how visual object recognition is acquired, a conceptual question about whether any properties are available to more than one sense, and a phenomenological question about what seeing is like. The experiments bear on the first; the arguments are conducted in the vocabulary of the second; the third is what people usually mean when they say the answer is obvious. I suspect this is too tidy, and that the conceptual question cannot be peeled off, since what counts as the same shape seen and felt may determine what the experiments measure in the first place.

So diagnose the stall rather than adjudicate the problem. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one turning on "distinguish," "see," or "recognize," several questions answered under a single name, a hidden empirical question that the philosophers treat as conceptual, or a conceptual question that the scientists treat as empirical? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and be specific about the points where they ought to agree and do not: whether the man in the thought experiment is allowed time to look and to move; whether a brief forced-choice match is the same task as unhurried identification; whether failure by the newly sighted is evidence against transfer or evidence of a system that never developed; whether a cube seen from an unfamiliar angle is even the shape he learned by touch.

Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a stipulation, an experiment that dissociates transfer from deprivation, a result about what any adequate account of perceptual content must imply, a change of subject — or argue that none should be expected, and say why.

Finally, and this is the part I most want answered without being patronized: does the philosophical stall mean that three centuries of this literature can tell a parent nothing about a thirteen-year-old who has had sight for two years and still navigates by hand? If that is what your diagnosis implies, I would rather hear it plainly.
