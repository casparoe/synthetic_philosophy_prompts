In my epistemology seminar two people got into it over a case the TA handed out, and I couldn't tell who was winning. Nadia has a cheap digital thermometer in her lab fridge. She's never had any independant reason to trust it. Every morning she looks at the display, comes to believe the fridge is at 4 degrees, notes that the display says 4 degrees, and puts the two together: the thermometer got it right today. After three months of that she says she now knows the thermometer is reliable.

(1) Marcus says this is bootstrapping — she used the thermometer to vouch for the thermometer, so the assumption that it's trustworthy is doing work at every step and the conclusion never actually gets earned. (2) Yuki says look at the individual steps: each one is just "display says 4, fridge is at 4, so the display was right today," nothing circular in any single inference, and a hundred good inferences don't add up to one bad one. (3) Marcus: fine, now suppose the thermometer is stuck and always displays 4, and the fridge really does sit at 4 the whole time. Nadia runs the exact same reasoning to the exact same conclusion, so the reasoning can't be what gives her knowledge. (4) Yuki: in that case her beliefs are true but she doesn't know — agreed — but that's a defect in the case, not in the reasoning; when the instrument is genuinely tracking the temperature, the very same reasoning does deliver knowledge. (5) Marcus: but she can't tell which situation she's in, and if a pattern of reasoning is equally available whether or not it's tracking anything, then deploying it can't be a source of justification. (6) Yuki: by that standard no inference survives — modus ponens is available to paranoid people too, and availability was never the test; success is.

Neither of them budged. Can you go through moves 1-6 one at a time and say who's right at each step and why, and then tell me what both of them are missing?
