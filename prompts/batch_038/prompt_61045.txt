I spent thirty-eight years in a biophysics laboratory, mostly concerned with how macromolecular assemblies do work, and I have only since retiring taken up philosophy with any seriousness. I am writing to ask for an audit of an analogy that is doing a great deal of work in a conversation I cannot seem to settle on my own.

A former colleague of mine, now advising a company that hopes to simulate neural tissue at scale, argues as follows. Von Neumann, he says, compared the brain to a computing machine in his unfinished lectures on the subject, published posthumously by Yale in the late 1950s; the comparison was made by the man who defined the modern stored-program architecture; therefore the claim that the brain is a computer is not a philosopher's slogan but a settled technical finding. From this he draws a further conclusion: whole-brain emulation is an engineering problem of resolution and compute, not a scientific problem awaiting new principles, and anyone who insists that emulation would require a theory of mind we do not yet possess has simply misread von Neumann, who already located all the relevant differences and showed them to be quantitative.

Reading the lectures myself, I came away with the opposite impression, and I would like to know whether my impression is defensible or merely the reflex of a sceptic. My recollection, which you should correct freely if I have got it wrong, is that von Neumann treated the differences as open questions rather than as overhead: that the individual elements of the nervous system operate with far less arithmetic precision than a machine, that its memory does not appear to be organized by addresses in the way machine memory is, and that he entertained the possibility that the logic and mathematics native to the nervous system might be structurally unlike the formalisms we write down. He also, as I recall, made much of the fact that the system achieves dependable behaviour out of unreliable components by statistical redundancy.

That last point is where my own difficulty lies, and I would like it addressed squarely. My colleague treats redundancy as confirming the analogy, since the theory of reliable organisms built from unreliable components and the theory of error-correcting codes come from the same hand. But it seems to me that redundancy buys reliability precisely by discarding fine-grained state — the ensemble agrees on a coarse output while the particulars of individual elements are allowed to be sloppy — whereas emulation is supposed to require those particulars. If that is right, the very mechanism that makes the analogy work at the level of behaviour may be what breaks it at the level of replication.

Please give me the following, in this order. First, an explicit correspondence table: for the machine of that period, which features are being mapped onto which features of nervous tissue — switching element, characteristic time, depth of arithmetic, memory and its addressing, precision, redundancy, program — and for each, whether von Neumann proposed the mapping, left it open, or argued against it. Second, a partition of the disanalogies into those that are load-bearing for the emulation conclusion and those that are merely cosmetic, together with the criterion you are using to sort them; I suspect speed and size fall on the cosmetic side and I want to know why or why not. Third, an account of what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks: a specification separable from its implementation, an author of the program, the presumption that the states of the machine are exhaustively captured by the description under which we built it. Fourth, a distinction among three readings of the analogy — that the brain literally is a computer, that the computer is a fruitful model of the brain, and that the computer is a licence for building brain-like artefacts — and an identification of which reading my colleague's conclusion actually requires, and which the text supports. Fifth, a verdict on whether that conclusion survives the disanalogies, and if it does not, the strongest weaker conclusion the analogy will carry. Finally, if you think a better analogy exists for this purpose, propose it and say what it must preserve.

I want reasoning, not a bibliography, and no reading list. Where you are uncertain about a point of detail in the lectures, say so plainly rather than supplying something plausible.
