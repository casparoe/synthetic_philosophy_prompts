**Question 3 (20 marks).** State van Fraassen's Reflection Principle as precisely as you can. Explain the intuition behind it. Say how Reflection relates to Conditionalization. Then describe a case that seems to violate Reflection and give van Fraassen's own way of handling it.

**Student's answer:**

Reflection says that if you are certain that at some later time you'll have credence r in A, then you ought to have credence r in A right now. The intuition is that your future self is a kind of expert: you should defer to the opinion you expect to hold, the same way you defer to someone you take to know more than you do. So the principle is silent when you're unsure what you'll end up believing — it only bites when you have a definite view about your own future credences, which is the situation its designed for.

Really it's just Conditionalization in disguise. Conditionalization says your new credence in A should equal your old credence in A given the evidence you get; Reflection is the same instruction except that the "evidence" is the fact about what you're going to believe. So Reflection follows from Conditionalization, not the other way round, and it doesn't put any extra constraint on belief changes that come from pure thinking rather than from evidence.

For the problem case, van Fraassen uses Ulysses. Ulysses now believes he should stay lashed to the mast, but he knows that once he hears the sirens he'll believe the opposite and beg the crew to untie him, so his present credence and his anticipated future credence come apart. Van Fraassen's answer is that Reflection only applies when you regard your future self as better informed than you are. Ulysses knows the song won't tell him anything true about the value of getting home, it will just corrupt him, so he doesn't count his future self an expert and Reflection gets no grip on him.
