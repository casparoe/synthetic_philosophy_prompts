**The question, as distributed.**

> A lab proposes to certify a deployed assistant by combining two components.
>
> (i) *Average-case utility certification.* With probability at least 1 − δ over the draw of a deployment stream S of length n from a reference distribution D, the mean utility of the assistant's responses across S is at least u\*. The constants δ and u\* are estimated from a held-out sample from D.
>
> (ii) *A safety filter.* A classifier, applied to every input before generation, blocks any input judged dangerous. On a held-out red-team set drawn from D, its false-negative rate is at most ε.
>
> The lab claims that the conjunction certifies that no catastrophic output will occur. In 500–700 words: state precisely what the conjunction does and does not license. Say what assumptions the composite inherits from each component, how the two failure probabilities compose, and in what sense, if any, the fact that component (ii) is applied to every input makes its guarantee a worst-case one.

**The student's answer.**

> The claim the lab wants to make is a claim about every input, and neither component delivers that on its own. Certification (i) is a statement about a mean over a stream drawn from D, and its constants are estimated from a held-out sample of the same D. It is therefore a claim about how the system behaves on traffic resembling the reference traffic, and about the average of that traffic; it constrains no individual query. If deployment traffic comes from some D′ ≠ D — because users adapt, because the product moves into a new domain or language, or because someone is deliberately probing — then the premise of (i) fails, and the bound does not degrade gracefully. It simply does not apply. That is the standard point about distribution shift and the right place to start, but I do not think it is the deepest problem here.
>
> The deeper problem is one of quantifiers. A worst-case guarantee has the form of a supremum over inputs, or equivalently the claim that no input produces the bad outcome; an average-case guarantee has the form of an integral against a measure, and integrals are indifferent to what happens on sets of small measure. "No catastrophic output will occur" is the first kind of statement and (i) is the second. There is no inference from one to the other without an added assumption bounding the loss pointwise, and the lab supplies none. So (i) cannot license the claim however small δ is made: shrinking δ makes the average-case statement more reliable, not more worst-case.
>
> Component (ii) looks as though it repairs this, and in one respect it does. The filter is applied to every input rather than to a sample of inputs, so the mechanism is universal in scope: there is no query for which the guarantee is simply not in force. Because it is a blocking filter, its misses are the only route by which a dangerous input reaches the generator. It follows that the probability of a catastrophic output escaping certification is bounded by ε rather than by anything that depends on the shape of the deployment traffic, and that the composite failure probability is at most δ + ε, by a union bound over the two ways the certification can fail. On this reading (ii) is the worst-case half of the certificate and (i) the average-case half, and the lab's mistake is merely that it reports the average-case half as though it were doing all the work.
>
> A genuine worst-case certificate would require a different kind of object: a proof over a characterized input space rather than a rate measured on one. Runtime assurance — a monitor plus a fallback controller with a proven invariant — can give per-instance guarantees for some harm classes, because the guarantee attaches to the states the system is permitted to reach rather than to a distribution over requests. Verification of a bounded network on a bounded input domain can do the same. It also matters whether the filter sits on the input or the output: a constraint enforced at decoding time, rather than a classifier's judgment about a request, can hold on every instance by construction. None of this is free. Each version buys its universality by narrowing the environment, which is a design decision and not a measurement. And it matters what "catastrophic" is supposed to mean, since a certificate is only as strong as the specification it certifies against; the lab has given no such specification.
>
> The obvious repair of (i) is to make it robust: certify the mean utility over a Wasserstein ball of radius ρ around D rather than at D. The certified quantity is then the worst distribution in the ball, so the bound is genuinely worst-case, and the distribution-shift objection is answered — whatever D′ turns out to be, either it lies in the ball, in which case the certificate covers it, or it lies outside, in which case the deployment was out of specification and no certificate could have covered it. With that substitution the two error sources are independent, since the utility bound concerns the aggregate behaviour of the generator while the filter's misses concern individual requests; the composite failure probability is therefore δ + ε − δε, with both terms now worst-case. The no-free-lunch objection, that no learner can be guaranteed to generalize over all distributions, does not bite here: it would rule out the filter's ε as well, and the filter plainly has one.
