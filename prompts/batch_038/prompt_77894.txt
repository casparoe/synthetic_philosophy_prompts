I'm helping set the agenda for a small three-year program on cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions, and I want a prioritized list of what it should actually work on — defended, not just enumerated. The constraints: roughly $600k total, one postdoc who's a philosopher, one cultural psychologist, no ML people, and basically no budget for large-scale field surveys or big cross-national sampling projects. The end we care about isn't more papers. It's changing how a working analytic philosopher treats a vignette-and-judgment argument when someone objects that the judgment doesn't travel — i.e., we want something that lands in the middle of ordinary debates in ethics and metaphysics, not something that only people who already work on experimental philosophy will read.

Candidates I've got on my list so far, and please tell me if I have the wrong ones:

- The stimulus problem: is a translated vignette even the same case, and how would you show that its the same or not without begging the question against whichever side you want to win?
- Something I keep coming back to and want pressure-tested: a "comprehension variance" hypothesis. A lot of the reported between-group differences might not be differences in the underlying normative or modal intuition at all, but differences in how the vignette gets parsed — who counts as the acting agent, whether the case reads as a hypothetical versus a report of something that happened, whether the local concept picks out the same slice of what English "intentionally" or "knows" picks out. If that's right, most of the existing literature is measuring the wrong thing. But I don't know how you'd separate comprehension variance from real intuition difference without circularity, and maybe that's not possible at our scale.
- The reporting problem: variation in what people think they're being asked to endorse, and in how willing they are to say something weird on a form, versus variation in the judgment itself.
- The symmetry problem: this data gets used to debunk intuitions, but the same pattern of results would presumably be used to vindicate them if it came out the other way. Is there a principled statement of what would count as evidence in either direction, or is the whole debunking move unfalsifiable?
- Scope: should we chase between-culture differences at the country level, or within-culture ones — religiosity, class, first language, whether someone has any philosophy training? Which of those is more likely to actually bite on the philosophical questions?

For each one you keep: say whether it's upstream of the others or downstream of them, how tractable it is for two people in three years with no big data budget, and what a concrete deliverable looks like that a metaphysician or an ethicist would notice rather than ignore. Then give me the ranking, and name the two you'd cut outright and why — cutting is the point of the exercise, I have too many.

Argue about the trade-offs between your criteria rather than pretending they line up. I especially want to see you handle the case where something is very neglected and also probably not doable by us, because I suspect at least two items on my list are in exactly that spot.

Last thing: tell me where you think my framing is most likely wrong. Which of these candidate questions is a pseudo-problem, what assumption am I smuggling in about what "an intuition" even is, and what argument or result would make you reorder the whole list? I'd rather you flag the weak point in your own ranking than present it as settled.
