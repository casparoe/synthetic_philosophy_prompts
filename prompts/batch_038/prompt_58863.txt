I serve on a hospital's surgical-review board, which decides whether a surgeon's cluster of adverse outcomes reflects negligent practice. At last month's governance meeting two other members — I'll call them N and R — argued over a proposal to enlarge the board from seven members to twenty-one. Here is their exchange, as close to verbatim as my notes allow.

(1) N: Condorcet's jury theorem settles it. If each member is more likely than not to reach the correct verdict and the members vote independently, majority accuracy increases with board size and approaches certainty. On the theorem's own terms, twenty-one beats seven.

(2) R: Neither premise holds for us. We all read the same case file compiled by one office, trained under the same guidelines, and deliberate together before voting, so our errors are correlated; adding members multiplies the same judgment rather than adding information. And 'the correct verdict' is questionable — perhaps a third of our docket turns on whether conduct counts as substandard once the written criteria run out, and there is no fact there for a majority to track.

(3) N: You're overstating the first point. The theorem tolerates interdependence provided the average dependence among votes declines as the board grows, and each new member does contribute at least some private information. On the second, accuracy can be defined against our documented standards; you don't need metaphysical truth to have a right answer.

(4) R: Then you've surrendered, because our correlation has a fixed common cause. A file does not become less misleading as you add readers. If a shared document biases everyone in the same direction, twenty-one members can be unanimously wrong with the same probability as one, and averaging over dependence that never decays is not averaging.

(5) N: That is an argument for splitting the file so different members review different material, which lowers correlation — not for keeping the board at seven. The theorem is a design brief.

(6) R: It is then a different theorem. Once members see different evidence they are no longer answering the same question on the same information, and the premise that each is likelier right than wrong must be re-established for every fragment.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, say who is more right overall, and identify anything consequential that neither of them noticed.
