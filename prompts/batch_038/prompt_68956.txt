In the second chapter of my dissertation I defend a position I will call the shallow-induction thesis: the pessimistic meta-induction is sound, but it bites only on realism about theoretical posits — the entities and mechanisms a theory claims are there — and not on realism about mathematical structure. My reasoning is that across successive theory changes the novel posits are discarded while the mathematical relations responsible for a theory's successes are retained, reinterpreted, or recovered as limiting cases, so structural realism is insulated from the induction rather than refuted by it. The induction is real, but shallow.

I want a rigorous map of where this thesis is exposed. Please give me:

1. Candidate historical episodes in which the mathematical or structural content itself — not merely its ontological gloss — was discarded or substantively rewritten when a successful theory was superseded. I have two in mind and I am not sure either survives scrutiny: the overthrow of parity conservation in the weak interactions, and the replacement of Galilean spacetime structure by Minkowskian structure. For each candidate, and for any better ones you can supply, say explicitly whether it is a genuine counterexample or whether the structural realist can redescribe it as reinterpretation-plus-limit, and what that redescription costs.

2. Conceptual defeaters that do not depend on history at all: whether the thesis can be trivialized by defining "structure" broadly enough after the fact to absorb any loss; whether structure can be individuated independently of the theory carrying it; whether the induction can be re-run one level up, against structural commitments themselves, via unconceived alternative formalisms; and whether correspondence relations deliberately engineered by a successor theory provide any independent evidence, given that the successor chose what to preserve.

3. What the thesis predicts about future theory change that an unrestricted pessimist does not, and which observation would falsify it rather than merely embarrass it.

Then rank the defeaters by severity. For each, say whether a committed structural realist would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and why. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind or a legitimate feature of a view about the reach of an historical argument.

Format: lead with the table, using exactly these columns — defeater | kind (historical episode, conceptual, or predictive) | what would have to be true for it to land | severity (fatal, serious, awkward, deflectable) | best reply available to the defender | would the defender concede it? | residual force after that reply. Follow it with two or three paragraphs of overall assessment, including your single strongest reason to think the thesis is false.

Two further requests. Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the arguments, not the scholarship. And be explicit about your own epistemic position: tell me which consideration would most plausibly move you to a different severity assignment, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail — in particular, whether you are testing the standard structural-realist rebuttals or simply rehearsing them. If you are unsure of the details of any historical episode, flag it as uncertain rather than smoothing it over; I would much rather have a marked gap than a confident invention.
