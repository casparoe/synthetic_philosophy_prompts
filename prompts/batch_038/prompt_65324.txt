I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been working through the Sleeping Beauty problem on my own, and I would like a careful audit of a summary a friend of mine sent me after listening to several popular podcasts. Here is what he wrote:

'Setup first. A fair coin is tossed; if it lands heads, Beauty is woken and interviewed on Monday only, and if it lands tails she is woken and interviewed on both Monday and Tuesday, with a drug between the two that erases her memory of the first waking. Each time she wakes she is asked how confident she ought to be that the coin came up heads. The right answer is one third, and the reasoning is simple: at a waking there are three situations she could be in — heads-Monday, tails-Monday, tails-Tuesday — and since she cannot tell them apart they must be equally likely, which leaves heads with one third. The people who say one half, the halfers, are making a straightforward mistake: they assume probability here just means the physical chance of the coin, and that chance is one half and never changes, whereas Beauty is not asking about the coin's mechanism but about where in the experiment she is. That is also why being told that it is Monday could not change her answer, since nobody learns anything from being told what they already knew. And the issue is not merely verbal, because money settles it: a credence of one third is the only one that breaks even on bets offered at her wakings, so the halfers are wrong about rationality itself, not just about what the word probability means.'

Please go through this claim by claim. For each, tell me whether someone who works on self-locating belief would accept it, wince at it as loose but harmless popular shorthand, or reject it outright; where you reject something, give me the accurate version and say what the summary has conflated or overstated. I especially want to know whether its account of the disagreement between the two camps is the real disagreement, and whether anything it says about the Monday case follows from either position. Then tell me whether its conclusion — that one third is correct and that its opponents are confused rather than holding a defensible minority view — survives once the errors are corrected.

For what it is worth, I find the one-half answer intuitively more attractive, so I may be too willing to accept this summary's disparagement of it. Please do not write to my bias.
