I am a senior at a fairly academically intense high school, and this year I am doing an independent study on theories of well-being, supervised by our history teacher, who was a philosophy major in college. For the first part of the project he gave me a stack of books pulled from the school library, most of them professional-level, plus one popular trade book about the science and philosophy of happiness that he warned me was lively but unreliable in places. I have been reading the chapter on hedonism in that popular book, and there is a passage in it that I keep going back to, partly because it is persuasive and partly because it makes me uneasy in a way I cannot yet articulate. I am supposed to write a paper at the end of the term for our school's philosophy journal, and I do not want to build my argument on a paragraph that turns out to be a rhetorical sleight of hand. I should say at the outset that I am inclined to agree with the book — I already suspect that reducing a good life to a pleasant one is too crude — which is precisely why I want to know whether the argument that gets me there is any good, or whether I am being carried along by confident prose.

Here is the passage, which appears at the end of the chapter: "Hedonism says that what makes a life go well for the person living it is nothing more than the balance of pleasure over pain. The trouble, as philosophers have known for a long time, is that nobody can say what pleasure actually is. Introspect the pleasures of an ordinary week — the taste of hot bread, the relief of scratching an itch, the glow that follows a difficult proof, the small triumph of a joke that lands — and you will find no single ingredient running through them. They are as unlike as any experiences can be, and the only thing they have in common is that we want more of them. Henry Sidgwick, the most careful of the classical hedonists, conceded the point: he defined pleasure as anything we desire when we attend to it for its own sake, which is to say that he defined it as whatever we happen to want. Contemporary hedonists go further and simply stipulate that to take pleasure in something is to hold a favourable attitude toward it. Hedonism therefore survives only by turning into a tautology: pleasure is whatever we want, we want pleasure, so of course pleasure is what makes life good — and a theory that cannot fail cannot explain anything. Neuroscience arrives at the same verdict from the other direction. There is no pleasure centre; different pleasures recruit different circuitry. If hedonists insist that pleasure is one thing, they are in the business of describing something the world does not contain."

Could you take this apart for me sentence by sentence, and sort what is said into three piles: what a philosopher working on pleasure and well-being would accept as it stands, what is a fair simplification that loses something important, and what is actually wrong? I have three specific worries that I cannot resolve on my own. First, the book presents Sidgwick as a hedonist who gave up on pleasure as a kind of feeling and retreated to desire, but my teacher mentioned in passing that Sidgwick is supposed to be the sophisticated one, so I want to know whether that gloss gets the direction of his analysis right, or whether it quietly reverses something. Second, I do not follow the tautology charge. Even if a hedonist defines pleasure partly in terms of our attitudes, it is not obvious to me that the claim that a person's well-being consists in that and nothing else — not in achievement, friendship, knowledge, or moral character — stops being a substantive and arguable claim. Is the book sliding between a point about how a word is defined and a point about what makes life good, and if so, where exactly does the slide happen? Third, I want to know whether the neuroscience paragraph is doing any real work, or whether the absence of a single pleasure region is simply the wrong sort of evidence for or against a theory about value; I also noticed that the book first says the only thing pleasures share is that we want more of them, and then treats the split between wanting and liking in the brain as a problem for hedonists, which seems to pull in opposite directions. Finally, once the passage is corrected, what is left of the thing it is gesturing at — is there a genuine problem about what, if anything, all the pleasures have in common, and what are the live answers to it? Please explain that problem and its possible solutions in your own terms rather than by pointing me at sources; I cannot do library research for another month and I do not want a reading list, because what I am missing is the reasoning, not the references.
