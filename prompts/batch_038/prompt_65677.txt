I am in the middle of a novel whose principal characters are a Cartesian natural philosopher working at Leiden in the 1640s, his assistant, who prepares the dogs for the dissection table, and a young woman who has taught a magpie to reproduce half a dozen words. Several of the book's central scenes are arguments about whether beasts think. My difficulty is that these arguments keep collapsing into the same two slogans — animals are clocks, animals cannot speak — which any reader will recognise as stock furniture of the period, and which my characters can hurl at one another without either of them being touched. Before I write the set-piece debate, I want a clear conceptual account of where the genuine pressure point in Descartes' position lies, so that I can have my characters attack it rather than each other's caricatures.

Please explain the following, in ordinary prose and with as much precision as you can manage.

First, in Part Five of the Discourse, Descartes appears to give two distinct arguments for denying thought to animals. One turns on language: he is explicit that he does not mean the absence of organs of speech, since parrots and magpies can be made to articulate words as well as we can, and some animals can be trained to signal their wants to their trainers; what is missing is the capacity to arrange signs so as to reply appropriately to whatever may be said in their presence, however remote from the situations for which they were conditioned. The other turns on the opposite feature: animal performance is said to be too perfect and too invariant, each individual of a species acting in the same way on every occasion, in the manner of a clock that tells the time more accurately than we judge it. Explain what each of these arguments is doing, what each is meant to rule out, and why they are not redundant with one another. Say plainly which of the two carries the weight, and whether they can both be true of the same creature without strain — that is, whether a being can be simultaneously too rigid to be intelligent and yet possessed of the sort of situationally apt responding that the language argument treats as the mark of thought.

Second, and this is the crux I want understood: Descartes also holds that the human body is a machine, and that a great deal of ordinary human behaviour — habit, appetite, the passions, the involuntary movements he catalogues in his last work on the passions of the soul — proceeds mechanically, without the will's intervention. Given that, what non-question-begging line remains between an animal automaton and a human automaton? His publicly available test seems to be behavioural, while his ontology assigns thought to an unextended substance that cannot be characterised in the mechanical terms his physics admits. Explain what this mismatch costs him: what could in principle count as evidence, on his own account, that any given body houses a thinking thing, and whether his view leaves the presence of thought in other people knowable at all, or only in oneself.

Third, explain the difference between denying that animals think and denying that they feel. Does the beast-machine doctrine require Descartes to say that the wincing, the crying out and the struggling under the knife are wholly without any felt quality, or can he allow a mechanical analogue of pain while withholding sensation proper? Show why the distinction matters for the internal consistency of the position, and then show the further move his contemporaries pressed against him: if matter arranged in a certain way can produce the whole of animal behaviour without thought, what forbids a materialist to say the same of ours.

I do not want a bibliography, a list of the letters in which he qualifies himself, or any citations. Paraphrase freely, and where your recollection of a particular text is uncertain, say so rather than supplying a confident attribution. What I need is the structure of the problem stated so sharply that I can tell which of my characters is winning.
