I am one of two external members of the board of trustees of a mid-sized municipal museum in a northern European city — an ethnographic collection of roughly 40,000 objects, founded in 1901, with an annual budget smaller than a single curatorial department of the national institutions we are usually compared against. In 1953 the collection was substantially enlarged by the bequest of a private collector, and the deed of gift carries a condition that the objects given shall not be alienated from the collection; our counsel advises that the condition is enforceable under national law and that transferring title to a bequeathed object without a court order would expose the trustees personally. Among the bequeathed objects is a brass plaque of the kind made for the royal court of the Kingdom of Benin. The museum's accession file records that the donor bought it from a dealer in Hamburg in 1924 and that nothing before that purchase is documented; the file contains an undated, unsigned typed note saying the piece "probably" derives from material dispersed after the British expedition of 1897, but no bill of sale, no photograph, and no correspondence. Three weeks ago we received two separate claims, and the board meets next month.

The first claim is from the national commission for museums and monuments in Nigeria, which asks for a transfer of title and offers in return a reciprocal loan of contemporary Edo work. The second arrived eleven days later, and the director did not inform the board of it until the following meeting: it comes from an association registered in this country whose members describe themselves as descendants of the hereditary guild of casters who made such plaques for the Oba's court. They do not want the plaque placed in a national museum in the capital. They ask that title pass to the association so that it can be held in Benin City by the guild's own people, on the argument that handing it to the state repeats the centralisation the taking began. The commission has since written to us saying it regards the second claim as lacking standing. Meanwhile a regional newspaper has filed a records request for the accession file, the plaque is scheduled to appear in a touring exhibition of the museum's highlights in the autumn, and several trustees have taken to saying that the sensible course is to wait for a sector-wide agreement that everyone privately expects will never come.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the eleven trustees ahead of that meeting. It should end in a definite recommendation — transfer of title, an indefinitely renewable loan, retention under some form of shared stewardship, refusal, or deferral — with the steps and the timeframe attached to it, and it should state plainly what the board ought to do about the enforceable 1953 condition, since that is the constraint the trustees keep circling back to without resolving. I want the reasoning to engage three questions rather than gesture at them. First, whether the wrong here was completed in 1897 or is one this museum commits afresh each year it continues to hold the plaque, and what follows from that for a board whose donor acquired the object in good faith and for value and whose possession has been undisturbed for a century. Second, whether anything survives of the claim that we hold the object in trust for a public that includes, but is not limited to, the descendants of those who took it, or whether that formulation is simply a way of restating our interest in keeping it. Third, how to choose between two claimants whose accounts of who was wronged are mutually inconsistent, given that we have no means of verifying the genealogical claim and that preferring one side would appear to make this board the arbiter of an internal dispute it has no competence to adjudicate. Close with a short section setting out the strongest case against your recommendation — the one a thoughtful trustee who disagrees would actually press, not a straw man — and say either why it does not move your conclusion or what specific fact or argument would move it. I do not want a survey of the scholarly literature or an inventory of what other museums have decided; I want a document eleven non-specialists could vote on.
