I'm convening a twelve-person graduate reading group on what happened to the notion of analyticity after "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," and our first session exposed a problem I want to solve before the second one. When I asked people where they stood, almost everyone volunteered a banner — "broadly Quinean," "sympathetic to Carnap," "I take the later deflationist line" — and the banners turned out to predict nothing about what they said when pressed. Two self-described Quineans disagreed about whether a speaker who has fully mastered a term can be straightforwardly wrong about a sentence she assents to; a self-described Carnapian agreed with a Quinean that even a logical law might have to be given up in the face of recalcitrant experience, which I had thought was the whole point of the quarrel; and someone who calls the entire debate merely verbal then spent twenty minutes insisting that the verbal-dispute diagnosis is itself a substantive thesis. So I want a sorting instrument, not a doxography: a decision tree that runs a participant through a handful of concrete questions and lands them on a named position, sorting them by what they actually say rather than by which label they are attached to.

Please build the tree out of the logical space first and only then locate the historical positions in it. The root must not be "do you accept the analytic–synthetic distinction?" — that question gets an evasive answer from everyone. I'd rather it begin from which explanatory job the participant wants the notion to do: grounding synonymy and necessity, delivering knowledge that doesn't lean on evidence, accounting for the status of logic and mathematics, making sense of meaning change and theory comparison, or diagnosing disputes that are merely verbal. After that, the branches I most want represented are: whether truth in virtue of meaning and justification in virtue of understanding come apart for you, and in particular whether one can accept the first while denying the second (I suspect that cell is far more crowded than my group assumes, and I want a leaf for it); whether any sentence at all is immune to revision, including the distributive law and the law of excluded middle; whether meanings are fixed by what a competent speaker can know by reflection alone, or partly by facts outside her head and her community's causal history; whether talk of implicit definitions or constitutive rules names a real mechanism or is a placeholder that does no work when cashed out; and whether analyticity, if kept, is all-or-nothing or a graded, interest-sensitive matter. At least three nodes should be concrete predicaments a participant can respond to without any acquaintance with the literature — for instance: a speaker who learned "vixen" only through "female fox" and now insists that some vixens are not female foxes (is she mistaken, incompetent, or has she quietly changed the subject?); two physicists who apply "mass" identically to every measurement either could perform yet disagree about whether mass is invariant across frames; and a researcher who quietly replaces a classical logical rule because her empirical theory is simpler without it. I want between eight and twelve leaves, each with a name if the literature supplies one — radical elimination of the distinction, a Carnapian tolerance-and-explication revival, the early reply associated with Grice and Strawson, an epistemic-analyticity revival, an implicit-definition account, quantifier variance and easy-ontology deflationism, mathematical fictionalism, a two-dimensionalist reconstruction, and whatever else you think genuinely occupies space here — or the label "unnamed" plus a one sentence diagnosis if nobody defends it. Flag at least one pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate and say why, and list the answer-combinations it permits but leaves empty, marking each as incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

A few constraints and one request. No bibliography, no reading list, no page references: name a philosopher only where a position already goes by their name or their school, and never invent an attribution to fill a gap in the tree. The handout has to fit on two sides of a page, so keep each node to one or two sentences and each leaf to a label plus a single clause on its most awkward commitment. And commit rather than hedge: give me one tree, not two candidate designs, and where the ordering of questions is arbitrary, say so and explain why you ordered it that way anyway. Then close with your own position on two questions I'd like the group to argue about. Which single node on your tree does the most genuine diagnostic work in the actual debate, and why that one rather than the others? And is the debate substantive at all — is at least one leaf wrong about something real, or are all of them ultimately describing the same linguistic facts under different vocabularies? Give me a definite answer with reasons. Finally, add a brief note on where the instrument is not neutral: if some node quietly presupposes that meanings are the sort of thing that could make a sentence true, or that competent speakers are authoritative about their own concepts, tell me plainly which nodes beg the question against which leaves.
