**Evidential cooperation in large worlds: an all-or-nothing problem**

1. Start with the setup everyone knows. I'm in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with some agent I can neither see nor signal nor otherwise causally touch — maybe outside my light cone, maybe in another universe. Nothing I do reaches them. But if the world is big enough, or if there are enough worlds, then somewhere in all that there are other agents whose situation and whose reasoning are relevantly like mine, and if that's so then my choice is evidence about theirs. That's the whole trick: the correlation isn't causal, it's the correlation between similar reasoners in similar spots. Evidential decision theorists say cooperate; causal decision theorists say the other agent's move is causally independent of mine, so what I do makes no difference to them and I should defect.

2. Now the magnitude claim, which is where I think things get interesting. People who take this seriously often argue that the larger the world, the more is at stake in cooperating, because there are more agents like me to correlate with. Write N for the number of agents relevantly similar to me who are in my situation, and δ for how much my cooperating raises the probability that any given one of them cooperates. Then the expected gain from cooperating rather than defecting is roughly N·δ·g, where g is what a pair gains by both cooperating instead of both defecting. Since N scales with how big the world is, and δ is seperate from N, the reason to cooperate is supposed to grow without bound — big enough, on some tellings, to reorganize what a consequentialist should spend their life on.

3. My worry: N and δ aren't independent at all, and the trade-off between them is doing all the work while nobody watches. What makes my choice evidence about theirs is similarity of reasoning. A very large world doesn't just contain tight matches — near-instantiations of my own deliberation — it also contains a vast penumbra of agents who are only loosely similar: same broad shape of problem, different cognitive architecture, different priors. For tight matches δ is large; for the penumbra δ is small. And I can't tell from the inside which regime I'm in, because both look exactly the same to me — I only have my own reasoning to go on. So the calculation needs a prior over the similarity structure of the world, and the argument never supplies one.

4. That gives the dilemma. If the prior concentrates on tight similarity, N is small and the gains from evidential cooperation are modest: a real reason to cooperate in these strange one-off cases, but nothing that should change what you do with your career. If the prior spreads out over the penumbra, N is enormous but each δ is tiny and the product doesn't recover, so the gains are modest again. Either way the "a large world makes this the most important thing" conclusion fails. But suppose instead you insist the penumbra counts and the product does recover. Then N·δ·g genuinely swamps everything local, and the theory tells an agent to weight the welfare of untouchable strangers scattered across the multiverse far above that of their actual neighbors — which looks less like a discovery in decision theory than a reductio of the prior that produced it.

5. So evidential cooperation in large worlds is either a modest, somewhat exotic reason to cooperate when you have independent grounds for thinking you're being mirrored, or a claim with such extravagant implications that its own defenders should want it to be false. There's no stable middle, and the middle is where the interesting version of this view has to live.
