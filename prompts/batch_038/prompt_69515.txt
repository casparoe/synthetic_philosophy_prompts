I don't have any formal training in philosophy. I read on my own, keep notes, and occasionally think I've worked something out, only to discover weeks later that I've merely arranged my confusions tidily. I'd be grateful if you'd tell me which this is. Please treat me as an intelligent layperson: define whatever terms you need, but don't dilute the substance.

The topic is a dispute between two ways of thinking about how good a state of affairs is. The first, which I'll call the priority view, holds that what matters is how well people's lives go, with a benefit counting for more the worse off the person who receives it is; so the same gain counts for more at the bottom than at the top. The second, which I'll call the equality view, holds that how people fare relative to one another matters in its own right, so that a more equal distribution can be better in some respect even when nobody's life goes better in it.

The familiar objection to the second view runs as follows. Compare a world in which some people are very well off and others badly off with a world in which everyone is at the same, lower level. Nobody is better off in the second world, and some are worse off, yet the equality view must say it is better in one respect, namely more equal. The objector calls that absurd — a change that is good in some respect but good for nobody is not good at all — and concludes that the equality view is refuted. The priority view appears to escape, because on it a change is better only if it is better for somebody.

My own argument is that this objection destroys considerably more than equality, and that if it succeeds it succeeds against commitments the objector plainly wants to keep.

(1) If the objection is right, the principle doing the work is this: no state of affairs can be better than another in any respect unless somebody is better off in it.

(2) Consider any value that is not simply a matter of how well somebody's life goes: a promise kept, a species saved from extinction, a serious work of art made, a truth discovered, a grave wrong punished, an ecosystem left intact.

(3) For each of these I can describe a pair of states of affairs in which the value is realised and everybody is, by a hair, worse off. The promise is kept although keeping it costs both parties slightly; the painting is made although the painter and her patrons are a little poorer for it; the truth is discovered although the discovery makes everyone marginally less contented and nobody gains anything.

(4) So by (1), none of these states of affairs is better in any respect than its alternative.

(5) Which is to say: promises kept, species saved, art made, truths found and wrongs punished contribute nothing whatever of value to the world over and above their effects on people's well-being, and can never form part of the reason why one state of affairs is better than another.

(6) But (5) is unacceptable. It reduces the objector to a pure well-being monist, and it implies that a world in which everyone is equally contented and nothing is promised, known, made or protected is in no way inferior to a world where all these things abound, provided well-being is held constant.

(7) Therefore (1) is false: a state of affairs can be better in a respect without being better for anybody.

(8) Yet once (7) is granted, the objection loses whatever made it distinctive. Equality simply becomes one more value of this permitted kind, alongside promises and paintings and surviving species — not refuted, but merely outvoted; outweighed perhaps, but not absurd.

(9) So the priority view cannot claim to occupy the only non-absurd position. Its disagreement with the equality view must be a bare disagreement about which patterns matter, with no argument of this general shape available to settle it.

Something has to give and I can't find it. I half suspect that (3) is doing more work than I noticed, and I half suspect that the move from (4) to (5) slides from "not better in a respect" to something much stronger, but I can't pin either down. If instead the argument is valid, I don't know which premise I'd least regret surrendering: I want to keep (6), and I also want to reject (9), because I do feel the priority view has some advantage that this argument seems to take away from it.

So I'd like a committed answer rather than a survey. Tell me which step you reject, or which you say I must reject if the argument is valid, and argue for that choice. Explain why the step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down, what it equivocates between or assumes that it shouldn't, and what the objector pays for giving it up — in particular, whether the priority view retains any real advantage over the equality view once the objection is withdrawn, or is left with nothing but a clash of intuitions. If you think (5) overstates what the objection commits its user to, state the weaker and correct version of the objection and say whether it still bites. And if you think there is a principled difference between equality and the values in (2) that survives (7) — something that makes equality unlike promises and paintings — commit to it and defend it, rather than listing candidates and leaving me to choose. Please write in British English.
