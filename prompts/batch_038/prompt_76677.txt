I do a job that mostly consists of other people's arguments landing on my desk, and I've hit the limit of what improvisation can do. I'm the analytic standards lead at a small firm — nine analysts, roughly twenty written assessments a week for clients who move money and policy on the strength of them, and our fast-turnaround notes have a three-hour clock. My authority is procedural only: I don't get to say an analyst's conclusion is wrong, just that the route to it doesn't meet our bar, and I have to be able to defend that call to a managing partner who thinks this whole function is throat-clearing and to an HR person who gets very twitchy about the word "impaired" being attached to an employee. The situations that come to me all have the same shape. An analyst's last three calls went badly wrong in the same direction and she's now extremely confident about a fourth. Two analysts of identical seniority land on opposite conclusions, each knows the other disagrees, and one insists the difference is that she actually read the primary documents rather than the summaries. A guy realizes mid-note that he's been awake twenty-two hours and is furious at the client, and tells me he knows his confidence numbers are junk but that he still can't see the conclusion as false — he wants to publish it anyway and I don't have an answer for him. We already have a flags form with four checkboxes (fatigue, conflict, adverse feedback, peer disagreement) and it's worthless: people tick nothing or tick everything. I also tried a bright-line rule that anything produced after sixteen hours awake gets a second reader, and it backfired — analysts just stopped reporting when they'd been up. And now clients have caught on that alleging analyst bias is a way to make a finding they dislike go away, so whatever I write has to be impossible to weaponize from outside: if a bare allegation can suspend a conclusion, the function is finished.

What I want is a reusable procedure I can run on a flagged case in about twenty minutes, built out of the actual theory of higher-order evidence, defeat, peer disagreement, and epistemic akrasia, but translated so nobody on my team has to know any of those words. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the terminal states — ship it, re-read it, redo it, reassign it, or escalate to a named human because the question is about values and not about facts and no procedure should pretend otherwise. I need it to take a side on the peer-disagreement question rather than survey both camps: when two people of equal standing disagree and both know it, does the procedure say split the difference, hold firm, or go hunting for the asymmetry in who did the work — and I want to know what you're betting on when you pick. It has to handle the regress, because the person telling me an analyst is compromised often has their own axe to grind, and it has to handle the fact that an analyst can't un-know the contents of the case, so "just have someone unbiased look at it fresh" is usually a fiction. Most importantly, tell me whether the akratic analyst is in a permissible resting place or a broken one: can someone who judges her own belief unsupported still sign her name to it, and if yes, what has to change about what she's allowed to *do* with it even if nothing changes about what she's allowed to *believe*? I want that trade stated explicitly, because I suspect it's the one place where the theory has something to say that a compliance shop would never come up with on its own. Then tell me which distinctions from the literature have to survive the simplification for the procedure to still be doing what the theory says, and which ones I'm allowed to throw away to keep it usable — I'd rather be told I'm dropping something load-bearing than find out later.

Finally, run it on three cases: the confident analyst with three same-direction misses behind her, the two-peer disagreement where one claims to have read the primary sources, and the sleep-deprived guy who wants to publish anyway. Make one of them come out against me — I expect at least one is going to say I have to burn a client deadline and reassign work I personally think is fine, or else that I have to let something ship that I'm sure is inflated, and I want to see the procedure say it flatly instead of softening it. Build in the anti-gaming machinery (who's allowed to invoke impairment, whether it has to be declared in advance, how the rule applies symmetrically to findings the firm likes and findings it doesn't), and end with an honest section on what the procedure cannot settle and which of its steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. Please write the whole thing in American English.
