I direct the operations unit of a regional missing-persons search organisation: we coordinate volunteer ground teams, canine units, and drone pilots on behalf of families and, when asked, alongside law enforcement. Between twenty-five and forty times a month we receive an unsolicited tip from someone who claims non-inferential knowledge of the missing person's whereabouts — a dream, a waking impression, a dowsing session, a self-described clairvoyant's "vision," a retired officer who reports a strong feeling about a particular stretch of road. Most are vague; a handful name a specific location. Our decision windows are short (the first seventy-two hours matter most), volunteer-hours are finite, and every allocation is visible to the family and often to local media. At present the on-duty coordinator handles these tips by instinct, which means we occasionally spend forty volunteer-hours on a field someone dowsed and occasionally refuse a bereaved family's request with no articulable reason. I need that instinct replaced by a written procedure.

Please construct it as an operationalisation of Henry Sidgwick's approach to psychical research — the evidential standards he and his colleagues at the Society for Psychical Research actually worked with, not a generic posture of scepticism and not the popular caricature of a Victorian séance-debunker. So that the exercise does not depend on your recalling the historical details, here are the four components I want the procedure built from; treat these as given and make them usable:

1. The balance of improbabilities. A report of an anomalous coincidence warrants belief only if the falsehood of the report — through deliberate deception, honest error, self-deception, or chance coincidence — is itself more improbable than the anomaly reported. The weight of testimony cannot be estimated at all until one has estimated how many opportunities there were for a match to have occurred.

2. The census method. Single cases settle nothing; the evidential unit is a population of reports. The Society's census of hallucinations, conducted roughly between 1889 and 1892 with some seventeen thousand respondents, was designed to establish what a world containing no anomalous connexion would look like, and then to compare the observed rate of death-coincidences against that baseline. The committee's announced conclusion, in the wording reported in a standard reference work on hallucinations, was that "between deaths and apparitions of the dying person a connexion exists which is not due to chance alone." I want the procedure to be faithful to the method and explicitly not to inherit that verdict: the method is what transfers, and the procedure should be capable of returning a negative answer indefinitely without ever being falsified by an accumulation of negatives.

3. Advance fixing of the claim. What counts as a hit must be specified before the outcome is knowable. Sidgwick's central worry about séance evidence was that a report can be reshaped after the event by expectation, by fallible memory, and by the reporter's wish to please the investigator — hence the preference for arrangements in which the target is fixed by chance and recorded in advance.

4. The normal-channel default. The investigator must weigh, as separate hypotheses rather than as a single catch-all, that the reporter is lying, that the reporter is honest but mistaken, and that the reporter had ordinary access to the information they now present as non-inferential. The third is the default hypothesis to be actively excluded first, not the last resort when everything else fails.

Constraints on the deliverable. The procedure must be runnable in about ten minutes per tip by an on-duty coordinator with no training in statistics or philosophy, including by phone at two in the morning. It must terminate in exactly one of three dispositions — deploy now; log and re-test when specified further information arrives; decline with a stated reason — and it must say who signs off on each, given that deployment decisions commit other people's labour and that the coordinator cannot unilaterally re-task a ground team. Set it out as a numbered sequence with a one-line decision rule per step, and for each step state what counts as a sufficient answer, what the answer is when the required information cannot be obtained, and where the sequence stops with a "no."

It must also include a standing data-collection requirement in the spirit of component 2: a tip register that lets us compute our own hit rate and our own chance baseline over years. Specify the fields the register needs, what must be recorded at intake rather than at resolution, how often the baseline should be recomputed, and what the register can honestly establish given that we will never have seventeen thousand respondents and our tips are self-selected rather than sampled.

Then be explicit about the seams. Identify the steps that look like procedure but are in substance judgment — I expect at least the question of whether the reporter had normal access to the detail, the counting of opportunities for a match, and the threshold at which a detail counts as specific — and for each, say what would make two competent coordinators reach different answers and give a tie-breaker they can actually apply. Separately, say where the procedure must knowingly disregard evidence-relevant considerations for non-evidential reasons (family morale, volunteer confidence, reputational and legal exposure), and how those reasons should be quarantined so that they inform the deployment decision without contaminating the evidential score that goes into the register.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases, showing each step and its output:

(a) A self-described clairvoyant with no known connection to the family calls thirty-six hours after a nineteen-year-old disappears from a lakeside trailhead and says that the missing person is in water, beneath a structure, to the north rather than the south. Our teams have already worked the south shore. Nothing was recorded in advance beyond the time-stamped call log.

(b) The missing person's aunt reports a recurring dream of a specific disused well on land her family once owned — land both she and the missing person know intimately. The family has asked publicly that we search the well, and a reporter has already put the question to us.

(c) An anonymous email arrives nine days after the disappearance, and after the person has been located alive in another county, containing three details that match the eventual police account almost exactly.

For case (c) the procedure must be able to return "no action, no evidential weight" notwithstanding the high match rate, and your walkthrough should explain why a correspondence established after the outcome is known carries nothing under component 3. I expect at least one of the three dispositions to be one I will not like; say which, and say so plainly rather than softening it.

Close with the limits: what the procedure will systematically get wrong; what it cannot do at all — I take it that it can never tell us whether the anomaly is real, only whether acting on it is warranted, and I want that boundary stated; how a well-informed advocate of these tips would attack the design (I anticipate objections that our baseline is estimated from a self-selected archive, that the normal-channel default presupposes what it needs to show, and that requiring advance fixing renders a genuinely useful tip undeployable at precisely the moment it would be most useful); and what I should tell the board when someone asks why an organisation that does not believe in psychics keeps a permanent register of their claims.

Do not include a reference list or citations; I will supply those myself.
