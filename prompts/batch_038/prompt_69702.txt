"Nozick's tracking theory reduces knowledge to a counterfactual test: you know that p only if, were p false, you wouldn't believe it. That's the whole idea, and the beauty of it is that it hands the skeptic everything he has ever asked for. Take the brain in a vat: had you been a disembodied brain, you would still believe you had hands, so your belief doesn't track, so you don't know you're not a vat. Nozick accepts this cheerfully. What he denies is the step from there to universal skepticism, because he rejects the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment. So you can know you have hands without knowing you're not a brain in a vat, and the skeptic's argument simply doesn't get going. The upshot is a kind of liberation: philosophy's demand for certainty turns out to be empty, since knowledge never required certainty in the first place. Notice, though, what the theory is really committing to. Because Nozick defines knowledge by what you would believe in the nearest situations where you are wrong, tracking is just infallibilism in plain clothes — it refuses the name of knowledge to anything that could have been false. The costs are well known and Nozick lives with them. Necessary truths drop out, since there is no nearest situation in which arithmetic comes out different; and there are cases where we are confident the subject does know, like the grandmother who is shown her grandson alive and well and who would have been told he was fine had he died. Nozick's fix is to insist that the counterfactuals be evaluated with the belief's method of formation held fixed, which is a technicality that does very little work."
