I spent 38 years at a national lab doing laser interferometry, mostly trying to pin down distances to a few nanometers, and since I retired I've been slogging through Kripke's Naming and Necessity with a Tuesday-night group in the library basement. Slow going, but the rigid designation stuff clicked for me in a way I didn't expect, because I sort of lived through the punchline. When I started out, the meter was defined by a certain number of wavelengths of krypton-86 light, and c was a thing you measured, with error bars, and the accepted value would shift in the last couple digits every few years. Then in 1983 the CGPM flipped it around: they fixed c at exactly 299,792,458 m/s and made the meter whatever length falls out of that, and the 2019 revision did the same trick with Planck's constant and the kilogram. So one commitee of delegates voted, and overnight a bunch of our best measurements stopped being measurements.

Here's what's bugging me. Before 1983, "light travels 299,792,458 meters per second in vacuum" looks like a clean case of the necessary a posteriori Kripke is so fond of: if it's true it's true in every possible world, but you could only find it out by building the apparatus and doing the experiment, which is exactly what my colleagues did for decades. After 1983 it's true by fiat and we know it without measuring anything, yet it's still necessary. So it seems like those delegates converted a necessary a posteriori truth into a necessary a priori one just by writing down a definition, which would mean the a posteriori part was never doing any real work. And if you can legislate your way into a priori knowledge of a metaphysical necessity, then the necessity was thier all along, a fact about our conventions rather than about the world. Which raises the obvious next step: why couldn't we have stipulated in 1900 that "water" just means whatever has the molecular structure of the stuff in the lab sink, plus H2O, and skipped the chemistry?

Dale, who was a tax attorney before he joined the group, says I'm overthinking it and that the meter case is just about bookkeeping. But I don't buy that, because bookkeeping doesn't usually change what's knowable without evidence. So tell me where my reasoning goes wrong, if it does. Is the necessary a posteriori just a temporary filing cabinet for necessities we haven't gotten around to defining yet? And if the meter case isn't really an instance of it, what exactly is different between fixing a unit and fixing a natural kind, given that both look to me like picking out a referent and then reading off consequences?
