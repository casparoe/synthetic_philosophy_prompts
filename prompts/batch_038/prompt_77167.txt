I've been trying to pin down when I think "we don't know enough yet, so don't do it" is actually a good argument rather than a mood, going purely by gut on cases. Here's the pile.

Banning neonicotinoid seed coatings on thin but real evidence of pollinator collapse: restraint was right, even though farmers lost yield and the evidence was genuinely contested. Banning a common preservative because of a viral claim that it causes headaches: restraint was wrong, leave it on the market. Those two look structurally identical to me — diffuse harm, weak evidence, loud campaign, cheap-ish compliance — and the only difference I can name is that I trust the ecologists and not the wellness accounts, which is a fact about my priors and not about the risk. Its basically tribal and I don't like admitting it.

Acting early on the CFC/ozone signal was right. But if we'd frozen recombinant DNA work in the mid-70s pending certainty about escaped engineered bacteria, we'd have lost recombinant insulin and the hepatitis B vaccine, and I think that freeze would have been wrong — even though the shape is nearly the same: brand new technique, global scale, potentially irreversible harm, evidence thin at the time.

Delaying a malaria vaccine over a rare adverse-event signal cost lives and was wrong. Delaying a sleeping pill over a rare cardiac signal was right.

Stratospheric aerosol injection: I'd happily fund the modeling and even a small balloon release, but I think committing to an actual deployment program is a trap — mostly because whoever ends up holding the thermostat can't be disarmed later, and because a sudden stop would be worse than the warming it was replacing.

New nuclear: in a country with a regulator that actually works, building now is close to obligatory. In a country without one, I'd say no, even though the emissions arithmetic is exactly the same. So capacity seems to be doing real work in my verdicts and I don't know where to put it.

Gene drive to suppress malaria mosquitoes: contained island trial, yes; open continental release, no. I can't say why the line sits where it sits.

Moratorium on making flu strains more transmissible in the lab: yes. Which surprises me, because I've just spent this whole list saying that waiting for more evidence is usually a bad argument.

My tentative principle, which I know is broken: restraint is justified only when the harm would be slow or impossible to undo, when someone can be named as having caused it, and when the people exposed didn't opt in. Three knobs. It handles the gene drive split (nobody downwind opted in) and maybe the preservative case, and it falls apart completely on climate change, where nobody is nameable and everybody is exposed — and where the same three knobs would forbid both emitting and geoengineering, which is obviously not a usable verdict.

What I want is the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best fits this pile. State each clause precisely and tell me which case above forced you to add it — I want to see the clause-to-case mapping, not a general essay on uncertainty. Then tell me what it says about two or three cases I haven't listed, ideally ones where it lands somewhere uncomfortable. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save and has to overturn, and why overturning that verdict beats buying an extra clause to keep it. If one of my verdicts is just bias wearing a principle's clothes, say which one.

And there's a worry I can't shake: maybe any principle of this shape is only a relabeling. "Restrain when expected harm exceeds threshold t" is a decision rule everyone already has, so all the interesting content lives in the priors and the discounting — which is exactly where my neonics-versus-preservative split actually lives. So say whether you think a principle like the one you build has non-trivial content over and above ordinary expected-harm reasoning, or whether it's mostly decoration with a nice vocabulary.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are in the main claims you're making, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of. I'd rather have a flagged weak link than a uniformly confident writeup.
