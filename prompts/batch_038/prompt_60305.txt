I sit on the board of a small trust that's spent the last fifteen-odd years funding conflict-prevention work — locally run stuff, mostly mediation and early-warning networks in a couple of places in the Sahel and the western Balkans. Our chair has come back from a strategy away-day convinced we should pivot towards long-run catastrophic risk, and the draft ten-year strategy paper he's circulating opens by asserting that the world has been getting measurably less violent for centuries, and steeply since 1945, so that conventional peacebuilding is a field of declining marginal returns. Two trustees object. One says the claim is a category error, because everything depends on what you're counting — battle deaths per capita, homicides, structural violence, displacement — and because per-capita rates can fall while absolute numbers, and the capacity to do harm, keep rising. The other is a statistician and argues the distribution of war deaths is so fat-tailed that seven quiet decades can't licence a forecast, so we'd be writing a trend line into a strategy document as if it were a law of nature. The chair says they're both doing motivated reasoning dressed up as rigour.

Could you draft the memo I'll table before we vote on Thursday? It needs an actionable bottom line: does the sentence go in, go in amended, or come out — and what follows either way for the two programmes, which would realistically be wound down inside three years. Take the chair's case seriously rather than just scoring points for the objectors, and tell me what the board would need to be shown to revisit the decision in five years' time.

Also, and this matters to me: be explicit about which parts of the empirical picture are actually settled among people who work with these data and which parts are live arguments. Don't let a contested position masquerade as consensus, in either direction. No bibliography or reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references.
