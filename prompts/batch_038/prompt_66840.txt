**Role and task framing.** You are a metaphysician who has taught graduate seminars on material constitution for twenty years and who has also served many years as an external examiner. I am setting the final paper for a final-year undergraduate metaphysics module and I want you to produce one complete assessment item around a case I have constructed. Treat this as a formal commission: I need the question as it would be printed, a model answer at distinction standard, and a marking scheme detailed enough that two markers would agree to within two marks. Please work carefully and, before you draft anything, set out your reasoning about where the case is genuinely difficult.

**The case.** In a limestone cave, dripping water deposits a freestanding column which, through nothing but the accident of where the water fell and where the stone dissolved away, ends up the exact size and general outline of a standing human figure — the right massing of stone, arms suggested at the sides, no eye sockets, no fingers, no tool marks anywhere on it. A caving club photographs it and christens it "The Sentinel". Forty years later a regional museum extracts the column intact, mounts it on a plinth in its entrance hall, labels it "The Sentinel, limestone, c. 4000 BP", and charges admission to see it. The stone is soft and the hall air is dry, so it begins to flake. Over the following thirty years a conservator, working a few grams at a time, consolidates and replaces the lost material with a synthetic compound that is chemically distinguishable from limestone but visually and dimensionally indistinguishable, taking care that the outer profile is never allowed to change. Every gram of original stone removed is bagged, labelled with its position, and stored in the basement. In the thirtieth year a retired conservator takes the bags, follows the labels, and reassembles the original stone into an exact duplicate of The Sentinel, which the museum installs in its courtyard under the label "The Sentinel (as found)". When the museum asks its insurer to pay for the deterioration of the entrance-hall object, the insurer declines, on the ground that the policy covers works of art and nothing in the entrance hall was ever made by anybody.

**What makes this case worth setting.** A candidate who recites the standard menu of responses to the statue-and-the-clay puzzle — eliminate artifacts altogether; identify the statue with the lump and explain away the apparent modal difference; appeal to temporal parts; relativise identity to sortals or to a dominant kind — should not score well merely by reciting it. Two features of my case are non-standard and the question must bite on them. First, no one ever intended an artifact: the shape was never imposed on the matter, so any account that makes artifact status or dominant kind depend on an agent's intentions is left with nothing to latch onto, while permissive ontologies must say what sortal the object falls under when its form has no author. Second, the classic problem about coincident objects and the classic problem about gradual replacement have been made to collide in a single object, and the reassembly in the courtyard means the candidate cannot treat the two problems in isolation. Third, the insurer's refusal raises the question whether a stipulation about the extension of an ordinary word can have any metaphysical consequences at all.

**What I need from you, in this order.**

1. **A short analytic preamble** — no more than 250 words — identifying the real cruxes of the case, which of them the question should *force* rather than merely permit, and which you have deliberately left out and why.
2. **The question as printed**, under 150 words, self-contained, telling candidates exactly what to address. It must not name any philosopher, any "ism", or any of the standard positions, so that it cannot be answered by identifying a school of thought. It should make plain that candidates are expected to take a position on the insurer's ground, not just describe the options.
3. **A model answer of 800–1,000 words**, written to a distinction standard: one a very good candidate could produce by hand in the 45 minutes the paper allots to each question. It should take a definite line rather than survey, and it should engage the intention-free origin and the replacement-plus-reassembly structure head on.
4. **A marking scheme out of 25 marks.** Give five to seven must-hit moves, the marks attached to each, and for each a one-line note on the way candidates most often botch it. Then give a short list of specific errors that should *cap* the mark, each with the ceiling it imposes — for instance, treating "is it a statue?" as the same question as "is there such an object?", invoking the Ship of Theseus without noticing that it is a different puzzle from the one in front of them, or allowing the insurer's stipulation to settle the metaphysics by fiat.
5. **A calibration note.** State what separates a 22/25 answer from a 17/25 answer, described in terms of moves the candidate makes rather than facts they include. Then state explicitly which of the 25 marks are simply unreachable by a candidate who has memorised the labels of the standard positions but has never thought about a case with no maker in it, and say why those marks are unreachable.

**Constraints.** Think the case through before writing, and let the preamble show that thinking. Refer to views by their theses. You may name a philosopher only where the attribution is uncontroversial and you are confident of it; do not include any quotations, citations, page numbers, or bibliography of any kind, and do not pad the marking scheme with references to literature a candidate would not have in the room. Where the case admits of more than one defensible verdict, the model answer should say so and explain what would settle it — but it must still come down somewhere.
