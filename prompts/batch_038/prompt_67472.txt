ok so context first, sorry, this runs long: I'm a junior and I hold the student seat on my district's instructional technology review panel. It's four teachers, an assistant principal, one parent, and me, and we vote on whether the district buys or renews any software that touches classroom instruction. I know I'm basically a token seat but they do let me write stuff up and circulate it before we vote, and this time I actually care about the case.

In June we have to vote on renewing a math practice app that about 2,000 kids in grades 6-10 use for homework. I'll call it PracticePal since I'm not putting the real name in a document. Two things came out of the pilot review and they've split the panel down the middle. Thing one: the app scores "practice" using signals like time-on-task, day streaks, and problems attempted, and three kids at my school worked out that if you open a problem and then tab away, the timer keeps running and you still get the credit. One of them has a 90-day streak and by his own admission has done almost no actual math since November. The vendor told the panel this is user misuse, not a product defect, and that they'll patch the timer. Thing two, which I think is the wierder one: the app tunes difficulty dynamically to keep kids in the band where they don't quit, and in the pilot the engagement numbers went way up while the end-of-unit test scores barely moved. The vendor says one semester is too short to see learning effects. The assistant principal says engagement is the whole battle for the kids who hate math and we'd be idiots to throw away the one tool that gets them to open it voluntarily. Several teachers say the app is now training kids to want the streak instead of the math, and that's worse than no app at all, because at least before they knew they weren't learning anything. My AP econ teacher, when I brought this to him, just said that's Goodhart's law, look it up, and I did, and it sort of fits but also doesn't quite, because Goodhart is about a measure becoming a target and I'm not sure that's the same thing as what the kid with the 90-day streak did.

Here's the part I keep chewing on. I've been reading some AI alignment stuff about wireheading and reward tampering, mostly way over my head, and I keep thinking the two things above are the same problem wearing different clothes: the kid hacking the timer and the app tuning itself around the kid's motivation both end with the number going up and the actual goal, learning math, not moving. My friend who's more into this than me says that's sloppy, that there's a real difference between a system tampering with its own feedback and a person tampering with a system's feedback, and if I put the "it's all the same thing" line in a memo the teachers will shred it. Probably fair. So: draft the memo I'll hand out at the meeting. It needs a bottom line — renew, don't renew, or renew with specific conditions — and the conditions have to be things a panel can actually vote on and then check in the fall, not vibes. Work through whether my same-problem intuition survives contact with my friend's objection, and if you think some version of it does survive, give the panel a test or two or three criteria they could apply both to this app and to whatever comes next, because there's a reading app up for review in the fall with a nearly identical streak system and I don't want to relitigate this from scratch. And please engage the assistant principal's point honestly instead of just writing engagement metrics are bad — for some of these kids the app is genuinely the only reason they touch math outside class, and I don't want to be the reason it gets cut.

Last thing, and this one matters to me personally: tell me where the memo is most likely to be wrong. What would change your verdict — which piece of evidence from the pilot, or which argument the vendor could actually make, would flip it — and which step in the reasoning is the weakest link, so I'm not standing up in a room full of adults defending something I can't defend when they push on it.
