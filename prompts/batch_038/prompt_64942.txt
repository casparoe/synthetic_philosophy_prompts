I'm on the bargaining committee for our nurses' union and we take a strike authorization vote in three weeks. My brother in law is the smartest libertarian I know and he hit me with this last weekend and I havent been able to answer it since. He laid it out like:

(1) A strike is permissible because your labor is yours to withhold, and another person's need for it doesn't give them a claim on it.
(2) What's doing the work in (1) is completely general: you own the thing being withheld, and you're under no standing obligation to supply it.
(3) Apply (2) to the surgeon who closes up mid-operation and says she'll finish for triple the quoted fee, and to the guy who owns the only working well in a village in a drought and shuts the pump until the village doubles what it was paying.
(4) In both, the withholder owns the skill or the water, and the other side's desperation gives them no claim.
(5) So if (1) is right, those are permissible too.
(6) They obviously aren't, so (1) is false — there's no right to withhold labor at all. What we call the right to strike is just a truce employers tolerate because suppressing it costs more than conceding.

He says he's willing to let me bite the bullet and keep (1), but then I owe him an account of why the nurse walking off a med-surg floor differs from the surgeon. I don't want to bite that bullet, and I especially don't want to concede (6), because the whole pitch to our members is that they don't have to ask permission — a right, not a courtesy.

Useful facts: our current contract runs through June and has a no-strike clause for its term, and we've told members we won't walk before it expires. Also about a fifth of us are ICU, ER, and labor-and-delivery.

Which step breaks? Tell me what to give up and price it — specifically whether whatever I keep survives contact with the ICU and the ER, since that's where his surgeon example bites hardest on me. And I need something I can say out loud at a membership meeting, not a paper.
