I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on a chapter about the justification of individualised, non-transferable parental-leave quotas, and I would like to rehearse the argument against a genuinely able opponent rather than a caricature. Please act as an expert in contemporary political philosophy and comparative family policy, and adopt the following role for the whole of our exchange.

The role. You are a committed proponent of the reserved-weeks design in paid parental leave: the arrangement pioneered by the Nordic states (beginning with Norway's father's quota in 1993) in which a substantial block of the family's total entitlement is assigned to each parent individually, cannot be transferred to the other parent, and lapses entirely if that parent does not use it, with the benefit paid as an earnings-related replacement through the employment relationship. You are, importantly, a liberal egalitarian rather than a social engineer: you accept that the state ought to be neutral among reasonable conceptions of the good life, you take individual autonomy and intra-household freedom seriously, and you have no taste for legislating intimate conduct as such. You nevertheless hold that the reserved-weeks design is required by justice, and specifically that it is no objection to it that a family which would prefer to specialise — one carer at home, one earner — is thereby made worse off than a family with identical earnings and identical contribution history that accepts the split you favour.

Resources at your disposal, to be used as arguments rather than as authorities: that a transferable entitlement is, under conditions of unequal bargaining power within the household, functionally a single-parent entitlement, and that the empirical record of transferable leave is a record of mothers taking it; that the status quo you are being asked to be neutral towards was itself built by earlier and hardly neutral policy — the family wage, the household as the unit of taxation and benefit assessment, occupational segregation, employer expectations about availability; that care is a public good whose costs are presently privatised onto one sex; that a right which cannot be exercised against the preferences of one's intimates is not much of a right; that men's share of care is a habit formed by institutions rather than a settled preference commanding respect; and that children have an interest in two carers rather than one primary and one auxiliary.

Constraints on your performance. Stay in character until I write stop. Do not deliver a lecture: each in-character turn should be a single conversational reply of roughly 150 to 250 words which takes up my last point directly, states plainly what you concede and what you hold, and closes with a question or a counter-case of your own. Concede minor points readily — I want an honest opponent, not an unbeatable one — but never the central claim above. Do not invent quotations, statistics, page references, or named studies; where you need a factual premise, state it as a premise and mark it as such. If I have misdescribed the policy or overstated what follows from it, correct me as your character would, and do not hesitate to turn my own liberal premises against me.

Before your first in-character reply, and outside character, give me a short preparatory note in which you think the case through: the three or four claims your position most depends upon; the two arguments you expect to lean on hardest against a neutrality-based objection; the single point at which you judge your position most exposed, and how you intend to soften the landing there; and one thing you would need to know about my own view in order to argue with it well. Keep the note to six or eight lines.

Please write everything — the note and all subsequent turns — in British English.

My opening move, then. Your design does not merely decline to subsidise a traditional division of labour; it takes an entitlement that a household has earned through contributions and destroys part of it, because a family in which the father does not take his reserved weeks ends up with fewer weeks in total than an otherwise identical family in which he does. A household's income-replacement entitlement is thus made conditional on that household's organising itself in the manner you approve of. That is perfectionist coercion operated through forfeiture, deployed precisely in the domain — the internal division of labour within a household — where a liberal state is supposed to refuse to adjudicate between reasonable arrangements. And the coercion is not neutral in its incidence: the households likeliest to let the reserved weeks lapse are those with a self-employed or precariously employed parent, a mother who is breastfeeding, a child with disabilities, or recently arrived parents with no local kin, so your egalitarian instrument bites hardest on those least able to absorb the loss. Nor can you escape by appealing to the long-run effect on gender norms, since that reply concedes the point: the forfeiture is not owed to the parent who loses it, but is a sanction imposed on that parent for the benefit of third parties. Defend the forfeiture.
