I'm drafting the central argument of a paper for my seminar on the Enlightenment and its critics, and I would like a check on its structure before I commit to it. My thesis is that the domination Horkheimer and Adorno later locate in Enlightenment reason is already visible inside Kant's 1784 essay on enlightenment — in the essay's own vocabulary, not in any external evidence about Kant's politics. The reason: Kant licenses free reasoning only in its private use, where an individual speaks in his own name to a dispersed readership, whereas the public use — the one exercised in a civic post, an office, or from the pulpit — is expressly subordinated to obedience. On my reading the result is that the freedom Kant grants is precisely the freedom that cannot touch institutions: the subject may think whatever he likes in the register that has no purchase on power, and must comply in the register that does. The essay then reads less as a manifesto for autonomy than as a philosophical apology for enlightened absolutism, and the Frankfurt School's verdict is pre-paid by Kant's own definitions.

Two questions follow. Is it defensible to present the essay as a precursor of the Frankfurt School's diagnosis, or does the same material point more naturally toward Foucault's later claim that the Enlightenment bequeathed an attitude of permanent self-critique rather than a legacy of domination? And how much of my thesis survives if a reader treats the obedience clause as a strategic concession to Frederick the Great rather than as a principle of Kant's?

Please open with the single correction my argument most needs, in two or three sentences, and only then take up the questions. Keep the whole response under 400 words; I am not looking for recommended sources.
