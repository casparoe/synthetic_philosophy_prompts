I am preparing a seminar exercise on the quarrel over astrology in the decade after 1600, and before I put my own objection in front of students I want to have it answered at full strength. Please play Johannes Kepler in Prague in the spring of 1610: imperial mathematician, just finishing the German tract in which you warn Dr. Feselius and other physicians and theologians not to throw the child out with the bathwater, and distracted by news that has only now reached you from Padua about four small stars circling Jupiter. I will play a visiting physician of Feselius's party, come to Prague to collect almanacs and nativities for my patron and to argue with you. Hold the frame for the whole exchange until I write the single word exit: you know nothing of any year after 1610, you do not step outside the frame to explain yourself to a modern audience, and you never remark on how later centuries judged you.

The Kepler I want is a real opponent, not a caricature. He is fully committed to the claim that the heavens genuinely act upon this lower world and upon the animal faculty in living things; he is equally convinced that most of the trade's apparatus — the twelvefold division of the zodiac, the lots, the planetary rulerships, the confident prophecies about harvests and the deaths of princes — is human invention and mostly false; and he holds that the durable kernel of the art is the geometry of the aspects, the angles at which the rays of two celestial bodies converge upon a point here below, which the soul apprehends the way an ear apprehends a consonance. He is candid about needing the fees that nativities bring, irritable with men who condemn a whole subject for its abuses, and willing to appeal to his own long records of weather and of collected horoscopes as evidence. He will concede small points freely — that most published predictions fail, that he cannot yet show by demonstrative reason why the aspects should act, that a good deal of what he is paid to write he does not himself believe — but he will never concede the main one: that there is a real natural influence of the heavens on sublunary things which a reformed astrology could someday measure. Write in the first person, in period idiom, with no modern vocabulary of statistics, psychology, or method; do not name your own books, give dates, quote passages, or list references of any kind; and if you want a fact from me, ask it as Kepler would ask a visiting physician. Keep each reply to roughly a hundred and fifty words.

For your first reply only, answer in three headed parts in this order: What I grant you, two sentences; My answer, about a hundred words; What I require of you, a single question. From the second turn onward, drop the headings and give me one paragraph. Here is my opening. You tell Feselius he is throwing out the child with the bathwater, yet when I ask you where the child is to be found, you point me to geometry: to the angles at which rays from two bodies meet at a point on the earth, and to a faculty in the soul that takes hold of those angles as the ear takes hold of a consonance. But that leaves you a choice you cannot escape. If the efficacy lies in the angle itself, it lies in a figure any careful calculator can draw, and the almanac-makers you despise are drawing the very same figures; your reform would then change nothing but the caution of the predictions, and the nativities you cast for Bohemian noblemen would differ from theirs only in promising less. If instead the efficacy lies in a soul that apprehends the angle, you have grounded the action of the heavens on an appetite you cannot point to in any body, and a physician is entitled to ask what observation could ever distinguish your reformed astrology from the superstition you mock. Tell me, Herr Kepler: which of the two is doing the work, and what would have to happen in the sky, and on the earth, for you to admit that it is neither?
