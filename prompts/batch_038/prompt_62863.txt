I sit on the description working group at a state archives and we've been deadlocked for about a year on one narrow policy question: can a catalog note point out what *isn't* in a collection? Think of a 40-year institution whose records were accessioned more or less whole, and contain almost nothing from the people it administered.

Camp A says no. Their line: description records what the creator made and why, that's what gives the record its evidential value — and absence claims are speculation anyway, the letters might never have been written, might have been burned by the recipients, might have rotted, we don't know. Flagging a "silence" is politics smuggled into metadata.

Camp B says the neutrality is the illusion. Appraisal already decided whose paper survives, description inherits that decision, and "there's no correspondence from the governed" is a checkable fact about the boxes in front of us, not an editorial.

The part that bugs me: they agree on every fact. Both grant recordkeeping is selective, both grant the specific gaps, nobody disputes the history of the institution. And still nobody moves an inch.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: this isn't sloppiness about the word "neutral," and it isn't a hidden factual question either — the camps give different answers to what a description is *for*, one treating it as evidence of the creating activity, one as an epistemic resource for whoever reads it later, so they're argueing past each other under a shared label. Is that right? If not, what's the disagreement really about, and what would move it — a distinction, a pilot collection, a reframing of the question, or nothing, in which case tell me why nothing.
