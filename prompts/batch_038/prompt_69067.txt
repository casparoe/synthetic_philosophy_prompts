Ok so, context first. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background to speak of beyond one elective a decade ago, but I went down a rabbit hole on collective intentionality after a sprint where a coworker and I were pair-programming a gnarly refactor and I couldn't stop thinking about whether what we were doing was literally one joint action or just two individual actions that happened to line up. I've been reading around it and the thing that bugs me is how stuck the whole debate seems — like 35 years of very smart people going in circles. My rough map, which may be wrong: Bratman builds shared intention out of ordinary individual planning states that interlock, with mutual responsiveness, a commitment to support the other's role, and meshing sub-plans, so nothing collective is added at the bottom level. Tuomela splits things into a we-mode and an I-mode and insists the we-mode can't be reconstructed from the I-mode. Searle thinks the we-intention is primitive but still sits in the individual head — irreducible to I-intentions plus mutual belief, but not some group entity either. Gilbert is the one who bites that bullet, with joint commitment creating a plural subject plus the obligations and entitlements that come with it. Then there's the empirical wing, where people argue apes coordinate and cooperate instrumentally but only human kids form joint goals and share attention, which sounds like it should settle the philosophy and somehow never does.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want it treated as a hypothesis you're allowed to wreck. First: I don't think the two sides disagree about any concrete case. In every example they both say the same thing about whether it was acting together and about what the agents did; they only disagree about what a successful theory is required to do — whether the analysis has to bottom out in individual psychology, or whether it's permitted to start somewhere collective. That smells less like a factual dispute and more like a dispute about theoretical roles, or maybe two different projects sharing one label: a metaphysics-of-mind project (what states are in whose head) and a normativity project (where the obligations and entitlements come from when you commit with someone). Related: everyone seems to accept two claims that don't obviously sit together — that a we-attitude isn't just a bunch of individual attitudes plus mutual knowledge, and that all the intentionality there is belongs to the individuals — and my guess is that the camps differ mainly in which of those they're willing to bend, which is a pretty deep, hard-to-argue-about starting intuition. I also suspect an asymmetry is doing a lot of the work and keeping the stalemate stable: Bratman owes an explanation of why acting together feels binding on people, while Gilbert owes an explanation of how a plural subject is supposed to be anything other than individual psychologies described at a higher altitude. Each side just waits for the other to pay, so nobody ever has to move.

What I actually want: play the role of a professional philosopher who works on social ontology and philosophy of action, and think carefully before answering. Start by brainstorming the candidate diagnoses explicitly — verbal dispute, substantive but underdetermined by all possible evidence, two different questions under one name, a hidden empirical or formal question, ill-posed, or just genuinely hard — with a line on why each does or doesn't fit, and only then commit to the one or two you think are right. I want a diagnosis argued from what each side actually claims, not a survey of the positions and not a both-sides map with no verdict; if my hypothesis above is wrong or half-right, say which half and why. Then test it: run your diagnosis over two or three concrete cases, including something like my pair-programming example and something developmental or comparative (an infant pointing at something, chimps collaborating to pull a rope for food), and tell me whether the diagnosis predicts any difference at all in what the two camps would say about those cases or what they'd expect to observe. If it never predicts a difference, that's evidence the dispute is idle in a specific way and I want you to say so bluntly rather than hedge. Finally, what would count as progress — be concrete about the kind of thing it'd have to be: a new distinction, a formal result (I keep wondering whether team reasoning and game theory could break the tie, or whether they just relocate it), a developmental or comparative experiment, a change of question — and if your honest answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that too. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I'm not going to look anything up and I'd have no way to check it anyway; just reason from what you know, and feel free to flag anything you're unsure about attributing correctly to a particular person.
