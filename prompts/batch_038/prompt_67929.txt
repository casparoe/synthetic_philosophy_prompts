I am a graduate student in statistics working on probabilistic forecasting, and I am fluent in the whole apparatus: credences as numbers, proper scoring rules, calibration and resolution, Brier and logarithmic scores, priors and updating, and the discipline of being penalised for every claim one is willing to put down in advance. This term I am sitting in on a seminar devoted to Wittgenstein's later remarks on religious belief, and I would like that material brought into my vocabulary rather than the seminar's.

Take the claims he is standardly read as making: that religious belief is not a hypothesis which evidence could confirm or disconfirm; that the believer and the sceptic are not contradicting one another in the way two people who disagree about a matter of fact do, as in his discussion of the Last Judgment; and that what is called belief here is closer to a passionate commitment to a framework by which someone assesses their whole life. Translate each of these, one at a time, as literally as you can, into the terms I actually use: credence, prior and updating, what it is for a belief to be scored at all, and the difference between a well-calibrated forecast and a merely unresponsive one. Do not smuggle in the scoring-rule framework's own assumptions and report them as Wittgenstein's views.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims come out as things I already have names for, such as a credence pinned at one or a prior that no data can move? Which survive only by distortion, and in what does the distortion consist? And which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? End with a verdict on that last category: does my framework have a slot only for how confident a belief is and none for the work it does in a life, so that the residue is its defect, or is Wittgenstein's difference of kind ultimately a difference of degree that a calibrated forecaster's language handles adequately?
