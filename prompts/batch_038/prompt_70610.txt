I am in the second half of a democratic theory seminar that has just spent three weeks on the boundary problem — the all-affected principle, the all-subjected principle, the circularity of letting an existing demos vote on who counts as a demos — and our professor handed out, as a kind of reality check, the recruitment protocols used by the citizens' assemblies that have become common over the past decade: the Irish bodies on constitutional questions, the French and British climate conventions, and a stack of municipal assemblies on housing and transport. The protocol is more or less the same everywhere. The organizers begin from a pre-existing administrative list, usually the electoral register or a national address file, and draw a large random sample of invitees from it. Because response rates are poor, they draw in strata and keep topping up until they hit a target size, and then they hold the resulting body against population benchmarks — sex, age band, highest educational qualification, region, urban versus rural, sometimes ethnicity or disability — and correct it: people drawn from over-represented categories are thanked and released, and additional names are drawn from under-represented ones, so that a person with no post-secondary qualifications ends up with a materially higher chance of serving than a professor does. Nobody is compelled to attend; a declinee is simply replaced, and in the reports I read there is no account of who declined or why. The whole exercise is then defended in a vocabulary of description rather than authorization: the assembly is said to be a microcosm or a miniature of the public, its legitimacy is said to come from the fact that anyone might have been picked, and it is routinely contrasted, favorably, with elected bodies on exactly that ground.

What troubles me is that this practice is supposed to be the answer to the boundary problem, and it seems to me instead to presuppose an answer to it and to hide that answer inside a spreadsheet. The frame is a list the state already maintains for other purposes, so the assembly's claim to speak for the people inherits whatever criterion of belonging produced the register — citizenship, age, sometimes a prohibition on standing for election — and yet the justifying language is about affectedness and about being an ordinary person caught up in a shared fate, which is a much wider class than the register. There is also, I think, a straight internal tension that nobody in class named: the equal-chance idea and the mirror idea pull against each other, since quota correction deliberately makes selection probabilities unequal in order to make the final composition statistically right, and the practice treats the first as dispensable whenever the second requires it. And then there is the treatment of refusal. An election takes willingness to participate as politically meaningful — campaigning, turnout, and consent all depend on it — while an assembly treats a decline as noise to be engineered around, a gap to be filled by the next name on the list. That looks like a substantive claim about what membership in a demos consists in, not a neutral administrative convenience.

So here is what I would like from you. Reconstruct the theory of the demos that this recruitment practice actually presupposes, as precisely as you can, and separate three layers: what it strictly cannot do without, what it merely sits comfortably alongside, and where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that several rival accounts of who belongs would license exactly the same protocol. In particular, tell me whether the practice treats the people as a distribution of attributes to be sampled or as a body of agents with standing to be summoned, and whether the quota machinery commits it to the first. Say which views of inclusion it quietly forecloses — the all-affected principle, the all-subjected principle, any consent- or participation-based account of membership, any view on which belonging is something a person does rather than something a list records — and whether it does so by argument or merely by default. Then address the two frictions I flagged: is the equal-chance/mirror conflict resolvable, or does the practice simply rank the two principles without admitting it; and what does the silent replacement of declinees presuppose about the relationship between willingness and membership? Finally, run the counterfactual I care about most. Suppose a city decided to build an assembly's frame from the all-affected principle rather than from the register — including long-term resident non-citizens, sixteen-year-olds, and, for a climate or infrastructure question, people not yet born and people living elsewhere who will bear the consequences. Explain which of those extensions the sampling technology can absorb and which it cannot, what has to give when the affected population has no list, no addresses, and in some cases no members yet, and whether the assembly would still be able to make the microcosm claim that does all the justificatory work in the current design. I would rather have your best reconstruction with its weak points marked than a survey of what the deliberative democracy literature says about mini-publics.
