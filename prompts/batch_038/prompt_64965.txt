I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about why evidential argument seems to fail in certain public disputes. A philosopher I interviewed offered this picture: hinge propositions — the certainties on which our doubting and inquiring turn, in Wittgenstein's late sense — are like the axioms of a geometry. You cannot prove them from inside the system, nothing compels you to accept them, and swapping in a different axiom set yields another geometry that is just as internally valid. From this she drew a strong practical conclusion: when two parties to a public dispute rest on different hinges, argument between them is a category mistake, and the job of a reporter is to stop adjudicating and start describing frameworks.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the axioms, the theorems, the proof relation, the act of choosing a system, and the standard of validity — and then the disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on her conclusion and those that are merely decorative. I am most interested in what the geometric source quietly imports: a picture of hinges as items one might adopt or decline, of rival frameworks as equally consistent, of justification as a closed deductive structure. Does the category-mistake conclusion survive once those imports are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? And if geometry is the wrong image, is there a better one — including any that Wittgenstein himself reached for in the same context — that preserves the insight about running out of reasons without the relativist payload?
