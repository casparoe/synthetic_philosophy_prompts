I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet and I'm putting together a piece on how contingent big historical outcomes really are — the for-want-of-a-nail business, Cleopatra's nose and all that. The hook is that a complexity group I've been talking to wants to build an ensemble of agent-based models of pre-1914 Europe and just report what fraction of runs produce a general great-power war, which sounds neat except nobody in the room can agree on what a run has to contain in order to count. So I've given up and started asking people for numbers instead, and you're one of my numbers. Here's the proposition, stated as tightly as I can manage: fix the world at the start of 1900 — same distribution of power, same alliances, same mobilization timetables, same technology — and then let it run with the Sarajevo assassination removed as a triggering event (the archduke survives, dies of something else, never visits, whatever). In at least 60% of the resulting trajectories, does a war break out before the end of 1920 between four or more of the six great powers, fought in at least two theaters, with over a million military dead? Give me your actual credence that the answer is yes. A number, not a shrug about counterfactuals being mushy.

Then take it apart for me. I want the sub-questions your number depends on, each with a rough probability and a line or two of justification: how much rides on the structural view (alliances plus mobilization schedules plus naval and army arms racing made a general war near-inevitable once any serious crisis hit) versus the view that July 1914 specifically needed a stack of avoidable human choices; how much rides on the displacement assumption that a war suppressed in 1914 just reappears at another flashpoint a year or three later, and whether that assumption smuggles in the answer; whether base rates from other multipolar systems — Greek city-states, Warring States China, whatever you prefer — actually transfer to industrialized Europe or whether the transfer is decorative; and, the one I care about most, how much of your figure is really about outcome individuation. That last bit came from a philosopher friend who insists contingency isn't a scalar but relative to how finely you describe the outcome: demand that the counterfactual war match the real one grain-for-grain and nearly everything is contingent, describe it loosely as "a big multi-power European war in that decade" and almost nothing is. My proposition above is supposed to be pinned at a middling grain, so tell me how much confidence you'd lose if I tightened it — say, requiring that the war open with an Austro-Russian confrontation in the Balkans and close with the collapse of three empires — and how much you'd gain if I loosened it to "a general European war killing more than 500,000."

Last part: which single sub-question is your estimate most sensitive to, and what would count as evidence that moves it? I'm genuinely curious whether simulation output would move you or whether you'd treat it as a way of dressing up priors you already had — say so plainly if that's your view. And if you had to compress the whole thing into one sentence for a general reader who's never heard of any of this, what would it be?
