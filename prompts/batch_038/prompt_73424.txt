Three things about how rich countries actually tax inherited wealth have been nagging at me and I cant get them to fit into one story. Can you tell me what theory of fair opportunity — or of property, or of persons — this bundle of practices presupposes?

(1) In several places the rate you pay depends on how closely you're related to the dead person. France: a child's top rate is 45%, but a distant relative or an unrelated beneficiary can face 55–60%. Germany's schedule runs from 7% to 50% depending on the family relationship. So the closer the blood tie, the cheaper the transfer.

(2) In the US, when someone dies holding appreciated stock, the unrealised gain just vanishes — the heir's cost basis resets to the market value on the day of death, so decades of appreciation escape income tax permanently, at exactly the moment when the estate tax that's supposed to catch that same wealth exempts all but a tiny sliver of estates. Death is the one event that makes a capital gain tax-free.

(3) If you pay your kid's tuition straight to the school, or their hospital bill straight to the provider, that isn't a gift at any amount; hand them the same money and it's a taxable gift above the annual exclusion (which is $19,000 per donee for 2025). And becuase the exclusion is per recipient, it scales with how many people you spread it across.

My instinct is that these all point at something like "the unit of justice is a life, not a lineage" — the state accepts that you can buy your children advantages while you're alive and die holding them, but it won't let wealth become a standing thing that outlives persons. Except that doesn't explain (1) at all, since an equality-of-opportunity view ought to be indifferent to whether the recipient is your daughter or a neighbour's kid: someone who inherits from a stranger is no less advantaged. And it doesn't explain (3), since paying the bursar directly reproduces advantage exactly as much as handing over cash.

So reconstruct the implicit theory properly. For each of the three, what does the practice *strictly* presuppose as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with — I want that line drawn explicitly, because I suspect a couple of them are philosophically underdetermined and would be licensed by two or three quite different theories. Which parts are in genuine tension and which tensions dissolve once you see that the tax code isn't tracking opportunity at all but something else — donor's freedom, desert, the family as a pre-political unit, or pure administrability? Is there any single principle that generates (1)+(2)+(3) without contradiction, or is the bundle an accretion of lobbying with no theory underneath? And if you actually built a transfer tax out of a luck-egalitarian starting-gate view: which of the three would survive, which would get flipped, and what would the resulting code look like — including the feature that would make it unpassable.
