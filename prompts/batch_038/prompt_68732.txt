my dad had his first heart attack at 54 and i'm 33, so when our company's new wellness program offered a free retinal scan — they photograph the back of your eye and an algorithm spits out a ten-year cardiovascular risk estimate, apparently better than the usual cholesterol-and-blood-pressure calculators — i signed up without thinking. Then i found out we're the pilot group, about 300 of us, and there's an opt-out nobody explained properly: you can ask them to destroy your result unread, or have it sealed and only released if you call back within 60 days. My sister, who's in the same building, told me she's having hers destroyed sight unseen and that i should too. My friend from a decision theory seminar i audit says that's insane and i should obviously keep the result.

He laid his case out as an argument, and i've been staring at it for a week because the conclusion feels wrong but i can't find the crack. Here it is roughly, cleaned up:

(1) The scan is free, the result is reliable, and nothing about getting it changes what treatments exist or what they cost.
(2) Whatever you end up learning, you'll choose rationally afterwards — no rational person is made worse off by having more evidence about the state of the world they're acting in.
(3) So if you choose now to stay ignorant, you'll later be acting on less evidence, and therefore sometimes picking things you wouldn't have picked with the full picture.
(4) So choosing ignorance now can't be rational.
(5) My sister asking them to shred her result unread seems reasonable, even kind of admirable, and definitely not irrational.
(6) If (5) is right then information can have negative value for a rational agent, and it can be rational to pay to not know something.
(7) But the scan costs nothing, so paying to not know means the info is worth less than nothing, which contradicts (2).
(8) So (5) must be wrong: everyone who declines a free, reliable screening is making a mistake, and no clinic should ever offer a sealed-result option.

I do not want to sign (8). But if i drop (5) i'm calling my sister crazy, and if i drop (2) or (3) i'm saying something that sounds like it should be obviously false, since those are the steps everyone in the seminar takes for granted. Something between (4) and (5) smells like a slide to me — maybe (4) doesn't really follow from (2)+(3), or maybe "rational" means two different things across the steps — but i can't name it, and i suspect my friend has an answer ready for whatever i say.

So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost you — like, concretely, what do you have to give up about choice or about evidence? Walk me through how it applies to the scan specifically rather than in the abstract. Then tell me how my friend would push back, and what the honest response is. Last thing: i've heard this is basically the territory of that old result about free information never being harmful for an expected-utility maximizer — can you say what assumptions that result actually needs, and check my case against them one by one? i want to know which of those assumptions the sealed envelope breaks.
