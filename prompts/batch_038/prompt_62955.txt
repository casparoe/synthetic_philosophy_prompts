I'm writing a long feature for a general-interest science magazine about how the free energy principle has migrated out of computational neuroscience and into robotics and AI, and I want to get one analogy right before I commit it to print.

The lab I spent a week with builds autonomous agents — small mobile robots and, more recently, purely software ones — that are not trained on rewards but on active inference: the agent is handed a generative model and left to minimise its own expected free energy. When I asked the principal investigator what he thinks he has actually built, he reached for an image he plainly uses often. Think of a Markov blanket, he said, as a membrane: a cell wall, a skin. Anything that can hold a statistical membrane between its own states and the rest of the world, so that what happens inside is conditionally independent of what happens outside given what crosses the membrane, is a system — an individual, with an inside perspective, that persists exactly as long as it keeps that membrane intact. On his account the membrane does the same job for a robot, a bacterium, and a person: it is what makes there be a self there at all rather than a smear of coupled physics. And he draws the conclusion I intend to report. If maintaining a Markov blanket is what being a self consists in, then the software agents in his lab are already selves of a modest kind, and any further question about whether they have experience or interests is a question for empirics rather than philosophy. He was emphatic that this is not a metaphor draped over the mathematics; the mathematics, he said, is the membrane.

A philosopher I put the passage to gave me the opposite image, and I would rather have both audited than pick between them for narrative convenience. A Markov blanket, she said, is no more a membrane than a dotted line drawn around a low-pressure system on a weather chart is a wall around the storm. The blanket is a set of variables picked out relative to a factorisation of a probability distribution; which factorisation you choose is a modelling decision, not a discovery. On her telling, blankets nest inside blankets, can be redrawn at will, and the step from "here is a useful partition" to "here is an individual with a perspective" is a slide the membrane picture does nothing but conceal.

What I need from you:

Set out both analogies properly. For each, a correspondence table — what plays the role of the membrane or the dotted line, what is the inside, what is the outside, what crosses, and what counts as keeping the boundary intact. Where the formal apparatus distinguishes states that are crossed by inbound influence from states that are crossed by outbound influence, say which side of the analogy each lands on and whether the analogy can hold them apart.

Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the inference to "software agents are selves of a modest kind" from the ones that are merely untidy but harmless. I want that distinction argued, not asserted.

Say explicitly what each image smuggles in that the target lacks. I suspect the membrane imports spatial contiguity, a fixed inside/outside asymmetry, and above all the difference between a boundary a system maintains and a boundary an analyst posits — but I want that suspicion either developed or corrected.

Then give me a verdict. Does the principal investigator's conclusion survive his own analogy once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way? If there is a better image — one that keeps the real content of the partition-relative claim without the metaphysical free lift — offer it, and say what it can and cannot support.

I want a committed answer, not a balanced one. I have to stand behind this in print, so a hedged equivalence between the two pictures would be worse than no help at all. Where your judgement depends on a contested reading of the formalism — for instance on whether Markov blankets are features of the world or features of our descriptions of it — flag the dependence and tell me which reading the argument needs, rather than sliding past it. And please push back on me if I have set this up badly: if the two images are not really rivals, if the conclusion does not in fact turn on the analogy, or if what I am asking you to settle cannot be settled this way, say so plainly instead of politely answering the question I asked.
