I work on distributed systems — replication, consensus, consistency models, split-brain recovery, checkpointing — and I am thoroughly fluent in that vocabulary, though I have no background in philosophy of mind. A colleague summarized Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self for me: a self is not a substance but a high-level self-referential pattern, and such a pattern can be instantiated, partially and with loss of resolution, in several other minds at once — which is why, on his telling, a person can survive the death of their body in something like a distributed fashion. He cited Hofstadter's writing about his wife's death as the motivating case, and offered as an analogy a novel that persists across several translations.

Restate that position in my vocabulary — replicas, quorums, fidelity, divergence, durability, garbage collection — as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in assumptions from either side and calling them translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims map cleanly, which only map with distortion, and which my vocabulary cannot express at all. I particularly want to know whether "the pattern is durable" and "the person survives" come apart under translation, and whether "how many copies exist" even has a determinate answer here. Conclude with a verdict on the residue: is it a genuine blind spot in how I think about persistence, or a sign that the original claim was vaguer than it sounded?
