I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized freight logistics company, and I sit on our Autonomy Review Board: a seven-person standing committee that decides whether a given automated system is cleared to operate without a human in the loop. My training is entirely in engineering — I have no background in philosophy, voting theory, or social choice, so please don't assume I know the vocabulary, and don't assume anyone on the board does either.

We have a problem with a vote that was taken eleven days ago, and the chair has asked me to circulate a written opinion before we reconvene in nine days. I'd like you to draft that opinion for me.

**The charter's certification rule.** Our charter lists three criteria and states that a system is certified if and only if it satisfies all three:

- **C1 (fault tolerance):** the system passes the fault-injection suite at the required severity class, with no unresolved findings rated above "minor."
- **C2 (rollback):** there is a tested path that returns the affected fleet to manual dispatch within 90 seconds of a trigger, including under a realistic control-plane network partition.
- **C3 (operational load):** projected incremental on-call burden fits inside the SRE organization's agreed staffing budget for the coming quarter.

In practice the board votes separately on each criterion and then separately on certification overall. The charter never says which of those votes controls if they come apart. Last week they came apart.

**The ballots, as recorded.** Seats: (1) Platform Engineering lead, (2) SRE lead, (3) independent safety reviewer, who reports to the CTO rather than to the product org, (4) the product manager for the feature, (5) the controls engineer who built it, (6) compliance counsel, (7) me.

| Seat | C1 | C2 | C3 | Certify overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | yes | yes | no | no |
| 2 | yes | no | yes | no |
| 3 | yes | yes | yes | no |
| 4 | no | yes | yes | no |
| 5 | yes | no | yes | no |
| 6 | no | yes | yes | no |
| 7 | yes | yes | yes | yes |

Counted criterion by criterion, C1 carries 5–2, C2 carries 5–2, and C3 carries 6–1, so on the charter's own stated rule the system satisfies all three and should be certified. Counted as a direct vote on certification, it fails 6–1. Both tallies are arithmetically correct on the same set of ballots. The chair wants to know which one is "the decision," and whether we are entitled to change our minds about that now.

**Facts you'll need to reason about the stakes.**

- The system is already live behind a feature flag on 6% of our fleet, under a time-boxed exemption. The charter requires an uncertified system to be pulled from production within 30 days of its review, so we have roughly 19 days if nothing else happens.
- We have committed contractually to bring one customer's regional hub to fully automated dispatch this quarter. Rolling the flag back means manual dispatch at that hub plus a service-credit clause that my director has already told me is expensive but survivable.
- Seat 3 accepts all three criteria but voted no on certification, explaining that she regards C1–C3 as necessary conditions the board agreed to track, not a complete account of what certification means; she thinks the charter is missing a fourth consideration, roughly the erosion of dispatcher skill during long stretches of uneventful automation, and she does not think a system should be certified without it.
- Seat 1 told me privately, after the meeting, that they voted no on C3 even though they believed the on-call burden was in fact within budget, because they wanted the rollout pushed a quarter and voting against C3 was the cheapest way to do it. They asked me not to repeat this. I think it bears directly on whether separate criterion votes can be trusted at all, and I want your advice on whether the memo can use the fact without attributing it to a named seat.
- C3 is not really independent of C2. Two members who voted no on C2 said afterward that their yes on C3 assumed the rollback path works, so their two votes are only jointly coherent on an assumption the board rejected. Nobody re-voted.
- We run roughly forty of these reviews a year, on the same charter language and the same ballot structure.

**What I want the document to do.** It should read as a memo from me to the board, roughly 900 to 1,200 words, and it should:

1. Put the recommended bottom line in the first few sentences: what the board should record as the outcome of last week's vote, what happens to the flag, and by when.
2. Explain why the two counting procedures diverge on these particular ballots, and then explain the general pattern, so I can tell whether this was a one-off bookkeeping accident or something structural that will recur across our other ~39 reviews a year. Reason from the shape of our case; don't lean on authority.
3. Recommend a procedure for this case and defend it. If your answer is that the criterion votes govern, say what that does about Seat 3's objection that the criteria are not sufficient. If your answer is that the certification vote governs, say what the board is supposed to make of the fact that a majority endorsed every stated criterion. Take a position on whether we are entitled to re-open a completed vote: two members say re-opening is moving the goalposts after the fact, and one says a vote whose outcome depends on an unstated choice of counting order was never a determinate vote to begin with.
4. Propose concrete charter language for future reviews. Address at minimum: whether the board should keep voting on criteria separately; what to do when members disagree about how the criteria combine into the decision, as Seat 3 does; whether the board should be required to state the aggregation rule before voting rather than after; and whether the paradox depends on the conjunctive form of our rule or would also bite under a rule like "certification requires any two of the three." Say whether adding a fourth criterion to the charter would make this better or worse.
5. Give the strongest honest case against your recommendation — not a strawman. I expect at least one board member to argue the opposite conclusion in the meeting, and I want to have already understood their best version.

Two constraints. First, no citations, no reading list, no papers or authors I'd have to look up; I can't verify any of it and nobody here would read it. If there is a standard name for the phenomenon, you may mention it once in passing so I know what to call it in the room, but the memo has to stand entirely on its own reasoning. Second, keep it usable: it's going to engineers, a product manager, and one lawyer, and it will be read in about ten minutes before a meeting where we have to actually decide something.
