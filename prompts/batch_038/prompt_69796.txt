I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine and I'm three days out from filing a piece about two labs that published basically the same result three weeks apart, plus the priority fight that's followed. My usual historian-of-science source gave me the framing that's now shaping the whole piece, and it goes like this: what happened here is convergent evolution. Two lineages, same niche, same adaptive problem, and the niche does the designing — so neither lab really "found" anything, they just grew into a shape the field was already going to grow into. Marsupial wolf, placental wolf, that kind of thing. He draws three conclusions off it and my editor likes all three:

(a) the priority fight is meaningless, because asking who got there first is like asking which lineage invented the streamlined body;
(b) individual credit in science is a fiction we should stop feeding, since the field would have coughed up the result without any particular person;
(c) if the niche determines the shape, then you can predict what's coming next — so funders should be picking niches instead of picking people. He really wants (c) as the nut graf.

(c) is the one that makes me queasy and I cant say why, and I don't want to file something where an analogy is doing all the load-bearing and I didn't notice. Can you audit it for me? Lay out the mapping explicitly — what's the niche, what's the lineage, what corresponds to heritable variation, to the selection pressure, to the fitness landscape, to extinction, to deep time — and then where the mapping breaks. I'd especially like the breaks sorted into the ones that actually bear on (a), (b), and (c) versus the ones that are just biologists being picky about their metaphors.

Two spots I want real pressure on. First: are these two labs "independent lineages" in the sense the analogy needs, given that so many so-called simultaneous discoveries involve shared instrumentation, preprints, conference hallways, and people who have literally been in the same room? Second: is "the same result" doing more work than the analogy admits? In morphology there's a known headache where one function gets realized by a whole bunch of structurally different solutions, so whether two things count as the same shape depends on how coarse-grained your description is — and I suspect the priority fight is happening exactly at the fine-grained level this analogy blurs past, which would mean it's evidence *for* the fight mattering rather than against it.

Then tell me which of (a), (b), (c) survive once the disanalogies are on the table and which ones the analogy was carrying the whole way, and for any that don't survive, give me a better comparison to put in its place. I need something I can actually write, not just a reason to kill the section.
