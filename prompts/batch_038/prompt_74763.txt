I work on the incident-response platform at a mid-sized SaaS company, and for the last year I have owned the service that decides, during a multi-incident storm, which pages go out to a limited pool of on-call engineers. Its core is a ranking function: for each open incident we estimate affected users and a severity weight, multiply, and dispatch in descending order of that score. During a regional cloud failure last quarter it ranked a degraded checkout flow affecting roughly 180,000 shoppers above a complete outage of an integration used by about 300 clinic staff, purely on the arithmetic; nobody on the team thought that was obviously wrong, but nobody could articulate why it was right either. My former college roommate, who is now three years into a moral philosophy PhD program, read the design doc over the holidays and sent back an argument that he says demolishes the whole approach — though not in the direction I expected. He framed it as a reductio of Taurek's claim that the numbers should not count, but if it succeeds it also seems to establish something about my ranking function that I am not willing to accept, namely that every non-aggregative term I might add to it is not merely suboptimal but morally impermissible. Please treat yourself as an analytic philosopher who works on the aggregation debate and who is comfortable enough with engineering artifacts to reason about a scoring function directly. Here is his chain:

(1) A moral reason to prevent a harm must belong to some particular person — someone for whom its prevention matters. If a badness is nobody's badness, there is no reason there.
(2) So comparing two options is really comparing two sets of person-specific reasons.
(3) If one set contains every reason in another set, plus additional reasons of the same kind and the same strength, then the first set is stronger.
(4) Therefore, whenever one option prevents a strictly larger number of harms that are individually just as serious as the harms the other prevents, it is supported by strictly stronger reasons.
(5) What an agent ought to do is what the strongest reasons support; where reasons conflict, the stronger set wins outright.
(6) So whenever one option saves strictly more people from equally serious harms, that is the option to take, and nothing else — a lottery, a floor for the worst-off, who the people happen to be, the agent's own commitments — can make doing otherwise the right thing.
(7) And nothing in (1) through (6) depended on the harms being equally serious. Since reasons are just the person-specific weights described in (1), the same reasoning lets us compare any two sets of harms by their total weight, however unequal the individual harms are.
(8) Hence a correct allocation rule maximizes total weight. The scoring function is not a crude proxy that needs fairness terms bolted onto it; every tie-break, priority floor, or chance-giving mechanism added to it is a moral error, and a rule that ever selects the smaller total is impermissible.

What I cannot accept is (8), and in particular its implication for how the rule should treat many small harms against one grave harm.
