I serve as one of nine volunteer commissioners on a county parks and open-space advisory commission in the American Midwest. None of us are academics; the roster is a farmer, two schoolteachers, a landscape contractor, three retirees, and me, and I manage a hardware store. This spring we vote on a plan to convert roughly 600 acres of mowed turf across four county properties into unmanaged meadow: no mowing after June, leaf litter left thick on the ground, and native host plants seeded specifically to support caterpillars and ground beetles. Staff project a ten- to twentyfold increase in insect biomass on those acres, and they treat that number as the measure of success.

For two years I have been reading on my own about whether insects might be conscious, and about the arithmetic that seems to follow. Even if the chance that a given insect has experiences worth caring about is small — I have seen figures tossed around anywhere from one percent to twenty percent — the number of insects is so large that the expected number of sentient individuals swamps every other consideration in the decision, and a wild insect's life looks mostly like starvation, parasitism, and being eaten alive. On that reasoning, a plan whose entire purpose is to produce more insects may be the worst thing this commission could approve. I have been calling it, for my own use, a probability-times-population test, and my worry is that it is not a test at all: it makes every available option look catastrophic and gives a lay body no way to compare them.

Acting as a professional philosopher with genuine expertise in animal welfare ethics and in decision-making under uncertainty, please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the other eight commissioners ahead of the vote. Write in American English, for readers with no philosophy training and no patience for footnotes. Start by showing me your brainstorming — a short list of the two or three cruxes the decision actually turns on — and then write the memo itself, about 900 to 1,100 words, closing on a recommendation the commission can act on: adopt as written, adopt with named modifications and monitoring triggers, or return to staff, together with what would later count as reason to reverse course.

Think carefully and aim to be useful rather than even-handed. The memo should (1) state plainly whether probability-times-population reasoning survives contact with a body like ours, and if not, what ought to take its place; (2) separate how many insect lives the plan creates from whether those lives are worth living, and say which of the two a parks commission is actually positioned to judge; (3) confront the strongest case against me — that invoking invertebrate uncertainty to block a meadow plan is motivated reasoning which, if accepted, would stall routine conservation work indefinitely — and assign it a weight rather than merely acknowledging it; and (4) avoid implying that the biology is settled in either direction.
