I'm in the second half of a political philosophy sequence that has turned into a two-week unit on epistemic democracy, and this week's seminar is on the Condorcet jury theorem and the standard objections to it. I have the framework itself down — the sincerity, competence, and independence conditions, the threshold above one half, the limit result — and I can recite the usual complaints: voters are not independent, real committees deliberate, and competence cannot be measured without already knowing the truth. What I am missing is any sense of how much damage the dependence objection actually does, because every version of it I have encountered is purely qualitative, and the arithmetic seems to matter a great deal here. My TA suggested I try building a case where the numbers are explicit, which is what I want to do below, partly as a rehearsal for a short paper I owe at the end of term.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications and then demonstrate that it meets each of them. Setting: an ordinary standing committee of seven — a hospital review board, a structural engineering panel, an editorial board, a tenure committee, whichever is most natural — deciding a single binary question that has a determinate answer discoverable in principle (a diagnosis that will be confirmed at autopsy, a design that will or will not fail a load test, a manuscript whose central empirical claim is true or false). Every member is sincere, well informed, and free of any stake in the outcome; none is a duplicate of another; and no member communicates with any other before voting, so that whatever correlation exists among the votes cannot be blamed on peer pressure, herding, or deliberation. The correlation must instead arise from a shared feature of their epistemic situation — a common training lineage, a standard reference, a widely used instrument or scoring rule — on which all seven rely and none of them distrusts. Now the payload, which is the part I care about most: tune the case so that each member, polled in isolation and scored against the truth, is right roughly three times in four, while the committee's majority verdict is right barely two times in three, and so that adding further members of exactly the same kind does not push the majority's reliability back up, since it asymptotes somewhere below the accuracy of a single member who had simply ignored the shared input. That is the direction I find hard to build: the competence premise is comfortably satisfied, only independence fails, and yet the aggregate is worse than the individual. Give the scenario in a paragraph or two, then lay out an explicit probability model of it — you may choose plausible values for the reliability of the shared input, the weight members give it, and the accuracy of each member's private evidence — and verify from that model that the three target figures come out as specified. The verdicts have to fall out of the model, not be stipulated into it.

After the case, please do three things. First, identify precisely which structural feature is doing the work; my guess is that it is some interaction between the strength of the members' deference and the fact that the shared input is less reliable than their private evidence, but tell me whether that is right and what happens if the two are swapped. Second, run the removal test: what becomes of the case if the shared input is more trustworthy than any member's private evidence, or if the members are told explicitly how often it misleads, or if you keep the structure but let each of the seven draw on a different input? For each variant, say whether the case collapses back into the textbook theorem or survives in a weaker form. Third, give me the strongest counterattack available to someone who wants to defend the jury theorem and the broader project of epistemic democracy — I have in mind both the charge that my comparison is unfair, since the lone member who beats the committee only beats it by having happened to weight the shared input correctly, which no one can know in advance, and the charge that I have shown only that the independence premise fails here rather than that the theorem is false. Steelman whichever of these bites hardest and say whether it disposes of the case. Then, in two or three sentences: is the mirror image constructible, a committee whose members are each individually worse than a coin flip yet whose majority is highly reliable; and would switching the same seven from voting directly on the conclusion to voting premise by premise on the reasoning leading to it rescue my case, given the correlation structure you built in?
