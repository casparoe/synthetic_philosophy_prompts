So I'm the token philosopher on our city's Automated Decision Systems Review Board — unpaid, meets monthly, mostly we rubber-stamp things. Not this one.

The housing department uses a vendor score to rank applicants for the rental-assistance program, predicting whether someone will still be housed and current on thier share of the rent twelve months out. Two years ago the board renewed the tool subject to a condition somebody drafted in a hurry: the score has to be calibrated within race/ethnicity groups *and* it has to produce equal false-positive and equal true-positive rates across those groups — i.e. among people who would have done fine, equally often wrongly flagged as high risk, and among people who would have struggled, equally often correctly caught.

The department's statistician has now come back and says the condition can't be met: given the actual base rates in the two largest groups, no score does both, and the ones that do are either perfect or worthless. Half the board hears 'the tool is racist, deny the renewal.' The other half hears 'it's just numbers, pick whichever criterion you prefer and stop dressing up a value choice as arithmetic.' Both reactions strike me as missing the point, and I have about nine days to fix that.

Two wrinkles. The vendor has offered a fix: run the score at a strict and a lenient threshold, and for applicants who land in the band between them, assign strict-or-lenient by a logged coin flip whose bias is tuned so that both of our criteria come out satisfied in expectation over the year's cohort. They are very pleased with themselves. I haven't checked the arithmetic and I don't entirely trust them, but I also don't want to wave it away — and I keep flip-flopping between thinking it's a gimmick in a suit and thinking it's the most honest thing in the room, since when there isn't enough to go around and the ranking is contested, drawing lots has a long and respectable pedigree. The other wrinkle is our member from legal aid, who thinks the whole argument sits downstream of a prior problem: 'successful completion' partly measures whether you already had a stable rental history, so the model is trained to predict the very disadvantage the program exists to relieve, and no metric-juggling touches that.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote? I want a real bottom line — renew under the vendor's lottery, renew but pick one criterion and publish what you're trading away, deny and go back to a need-indexed queue, or send it back for retraining on a repaired label — plus reasoning that gets six non-technical people from here to there, and an implementation paragraph with dates. Two pages, bottom line up front.

Three things I care about. First, get the logic right in plain language: exactly what is incompatible with what, and what the results do *not* show. I've had to say out loud more than once that they don't establish that fair prediction is impossible, and I'd rather the memo say it once, well. Second, actually interrogate the coin flip — does it deliver what the vendor claims, and is 'satisfied in expectation across a cohort' the same thing as not wronging the particular person who lost the draw? Its an open question for me, not a settled objection, so treat it as one. Third, don't bury the legal aid point in a footnote: if the label is contaminated, say what follows for the rest of the decision.

No bibliography, nobody on this board reads one — argue it out instead. And be committed, I want something people can vote for, but close with a short section on what would change your mind: which consideration, if the statistician or the lawyer really pressed it, would move you to a different option, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to break.
