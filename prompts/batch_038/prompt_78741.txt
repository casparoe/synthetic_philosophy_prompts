Let verificationism be the principle that every truth is knowable, symbolised (∀p)(p → ◇Kp). Fitch's 1963 result shows that the existence of even one unknown truth collapses verificationism into idealism. Assume p₀ ∧ ¬Kp₀ is such a truth. By verificationism, ◇K(p₀ ∧ ¬Kp₀). Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, ◇(Kp₀ ∧ K¬Kp₀). And since knowledge is factive, K¬Kp₀ entails ¬Kp₀, so the possibility operator is left governing a flat contradiction. Discharging the assumption, no truth is unknown, and classical logic hands the anti-realist the Berkeleyan conclusion she was trying to avoid.

My proposal is that the anti-realist was never committed to reading ◇ as metaphysical possibility. Read it instead as possibility relative to some possible course of inquiry conducted by beings with cognitive capacities broadly like ours, and the crucial step becomes questionable. A course of inquiry is a temporally extended process, and the sentence p₀ ∧ ¬Kp₀ is true only at its earlier stages: what such a course of inquiry would eventually arrive at is not the proposition it set out from. So verificationism on this reading is threatened only by truths that are knowledge-stable — true whether or not anyone ever comes to know them — and the anti-realist can hold that every knowledge-stable truth is knowable while granting that p₀ ∧ ¬Kp₀ is unknowable, without embarrassment, since that proposition was never in the extension of her principle to begin with.

The second half of the move is to argue that this restriction is not the gerrymander its usually accused of being. A restriction is ad hoc when its sole motivation is evading the counterexample. But knowledge-stability is independently motivated: it picks out exactly those propositions whose truth does not turn on our own epistemic states, and those are the only propositions about which an inquiry can be said to have discovered anything. Contrast the familiar move of restricting the principle to decidable statements, which looks unmotivated except by the paradox itself. My restriction is not about decidability at all, it's about stability, and stability is what makes discovery intelligible.

There is also a further reason the anti-realist can stay calm, and I want to develop it in the last section. The derivation requires KK. The step from K¬Kp₀ to ¬Kp₀ looks like plain factivity, but the anti-realist who denies that knowledge is transparent to itself can resist treating the embedded ¬Kp₀ as something the knower is thereby in a position to have knowledge of, and the contradiction never gets assembled.
