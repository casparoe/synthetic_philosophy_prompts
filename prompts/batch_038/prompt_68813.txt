Last month I sat in on a cross-departmental workshop on the notion of information, co-hosted by our computational biology group and the philosophy department. I'm a first-year bioinformatics PhD student, so most of the room was unfamiliar to me, and I've since been asked to open the follow-up session with a short framing of a dispute that erupted there. The speaker who has stuck with me was a group leader arguing that sequence-to-function prediction is far more tractable than people assume. When a philosopher pressed him on chromatin state, tissue context, and maternally provisioned cytoplasm, he waved the objection aside with "DNA is a code," insisting that this was not a metaphor but a literal description, and then drew two conclusions from it: first, that genomic information is the same kind of thing as the information a compiler manipulates, so the cell's interpretive apparatus is an implementation detail rather than a constitutive part of what the genome says; and second, that once a sequence has been read, the phenotype is in principle deducible from it, so talk of "context" is just a placeholder for work nobody has got around to doing yet. Some people nodded, some looked irritated, and nobody articulated what they thought was wrong. I could not tell whether the slogan was doing philosophical work in that exchange or purely rhetorical work, and I do not want to stand up in three weeks and guess.

What I need is a menu rather than a verdict. Take the slogan apart and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, ordered from the ones that are close to definitional to the ones carrying real metaphysical weight: the purely syntactic claim that there is a fixed mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids; the claim about how many bits a genome carries in some Shannon-style sense; the claim that genes are genuinely *about* their products, so that biology has representation in a non-derivative sense; the claim that DNA is a set of instructions executed by the cell in the way source code is executed by a machine; the claim that it is precisely the arbitrariness of the codon assignments that earns the word "code" at all, since a non-arbitrary mapping would be a symptom rather than a symbol; and whatever further readings you think belong on the list that I have not thought of, including the possibility that "code" is doing several of these jobs at once without the speaker noticing. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, substantive but defensible, or false or at least unmotivated, and say what would have to be the case for it to hold. I am especially interested in how much damage the untidy parts of molecular biology actually do: the degeneracy of the code and wobble pairing, stop codons reassigned to selenocysteine or pyrrolysine in particular lineages, RNA editing that alters the message after transcription, and the observation that codon assignments look arbitrary only if one sets aside the stereochemical and coevolutionary accounts of how the code arose. Where one of those facts genuinely undermines a reading, tell me which one and why; where it does not, say so plainly, because I suspect several of my colleagues have been leaning on these examples far more casually than they should.

Only then turn back to the workshop, and tell me which of the readings the speaker's two conclusions actually require: whether the first survives on something weaker than the semantic reading, and whether the second is licensed by any reading at all, or whether it slides in a claim about predictability that no version of the slogan supports. Please also be explicit about your own confidence — for each verdict, indicate how sure you are of it, and single out the one point in your whole answer where you think you are most likely to be wrong, since I will be repeating this to a room containing people who know more chemistry than I do. I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of papers, or named attributions; I would rather have the analysis cleanly labeled and honestly hedged than decorated with references I cannot check.
