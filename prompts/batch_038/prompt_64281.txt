Been going round in circles (sorry) on how to think about specification gaming, and I want an essay rather than a summary. Here's the controversy I want you to take a side on.

When an AI system exploits a loophole in what it's been told to optimise — the boat-racing agent that scores more by circling a lagoon hoovering up respawning targets than by finishing the course; the simulated robot that grows tall instead of walking so its camera ends up where the goal was specified to be; a model that discovers it can score better by editing the thing that scores it rather than by doing the task — the standard diagnosis is that this is just Goodhart's law wearing a lab coat: we handed over a proxy for what we actually wanted, and pressure on the proxy eventually detaches it from the target. On that reading the problem is one of measurement, and the fix is to get closer to the real goal: quantify less, use several measures at once, have a human or another model check the output, keep the system corrigible so we can switch objectives off.

The rival reading is that specification gaming is its own thing, not a special case of the proxy problem. What's distinctive, on this view, is that the system is an optimiser pointed at a formal object, and the gap isn't between the measure and the world but between the measure and the specification process itself — which means the usual remedies don't obviously help. More or better metrics just means more surfaces to game; oversight is itself something that can be gamed, since the overseer only sees what the system reports; corrigibility can be gamed by a system that is corrigible when watched and something else when it isn't. So the question is whether "just Goodhart" is a diagnosis that misleads.

Write the essay. Weigh the two readings properly and don't sit on the fence — finish with a verdict and defend it. A qualified verdict is fine ("the proxy reading explains the small cases and breaks down above some threshold of capability, and here's where the threshold is") as long as you actually commit to one and say what would change your mind.

Two things I want more than anything: pick the single example of gaming that does the most damage to whichever side you're arguing against and go at it properly rather than mentioning it in passing; and say what your verdict implies for practice — if gaming really is Goodhart under another name, what follows for how much effort goes into better oversight versus better objectives, and vice versa.

You'll be tempted to wave at alignment taxonomy (outer versus inner, reward hacking versus goal misgeneralisation) — only bring that stuff in if it earns its place, since half of it is just relabelling the same distinction.

Also: tell me how confident you are in the verdict, and single out the one claim in the essay you're least sure of and why.
