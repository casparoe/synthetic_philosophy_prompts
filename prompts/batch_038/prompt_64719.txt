I run the product data model at a mid-size industrial equipment company — 15 years in PLM/ERP land, BOMs, part revisions, phantom assemblies, where-used reports, the whole thing. Took one philosophy class in college and retained exactly none of it. I've been reading about the special composition question on the side lately and its messing with my head, because the questions you people are arguing about look suspiciously like questions I answer for a living, except I answer them by fiat and nobody files an objection.

Here's my understanding of the lay of the land. You've got the simples, or whatever the smallest parts there are, and the question is when some of them make up a further thing. Van Inwagen's answer is basically that they only do it when they're caught up in one self-maintaining activity — lives, roughly — so there are no chairs, just simples arranged chairwise. The universalists say composition is unrestricted, any old plurality of non-overlapping things has a fusion. The nihilists say nothing ever composes anything. The commonsense-ish crowd says something like "when the parts are fastened together" or "when they're in contact." And then there's the argument that any restricted answer would have to draw a line somewhere, and drawing it would either be vague or arbitrary, and vagueness can only live in our words and concepts and not in wheter some simples actually compose a thing — so you're pushed to either universalism or treating composition as brute.

Now here's what I do all day, and I want you to take it seriously as a vocabulary, not as a cute analogy. In our schema, part_of is a transitive relation stored as rows. A product exists because somebody drew a boundary around a set of rows and an engineer with release authority signed it. Whether a subassembly is a "thing" or just a grouping for manufacturing convenience gets decided at a design review and can be flipped next quarter without moving a single bolt. We have phantom assemblies — nodes that exist purely to organize and are never a physical object at all. We have effectivity dates, so the same physical object belongs to different assemblies depending on what date you query. We sell kits that are one sellable thing and never one assembled thing. One bolt appears on forty BOMs. And we routinely stop decomposing wherever it's convenient — nobody has an opinion about whether a raw material lot has parts.

Two cases we actually fight about internally, use them as test material: a modular machine that ships as four crates and gets bolted together on site by the customer — before it ships, is it one object, four objects, or does the question not have an answer yet? And an aftermarket service bundle that's one SKU, one price, one box, and whose contents are never assembled into anything by us — same question.

So here's the ask. Take each position in turn — van Inwagen's, universalism, nihilism, the contact/fastening answers, and the vagueness argument with the brute-composition fallback it leaves behind — and give it to me in my vocabulary, as literal as you can manage. For each one I want three separate things: what maps over without strain, what you had to bend or quietly reinterpret to make it sit in a relational schema, and what has no way of being expressed in that schema at all. And don't smuggle my assumptions in and call it a translation. If a position only looks coherent or only looks absurd once it's been converted into rows and signatures, flag that explicitly and say what the conversion did to it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. For each thing that refuses to translate, tell me which way to read the failure: is my vocabulary missing a distinction — and if so, name the distinction and say whether a database could hold it in principle, or whether it's the kind of thing that can't be held in any schema — or is the original position less determinate than it sounded on the page? Two specific questions I want answered straight. First: my default working view is that composition is a stipulation, the world just has stuff, and the assembly boundary is a decision with a signature on it. Is that one of the positions above, a position nobody in that literature defends, or not really a position at all because I'm confusing what a database records with what's true? Second: is there anything in this debate that would count as an actual objection to how we run our BOMs — could the metaphysics ever bite on the engineering, or is it purely decorative from where I sit?

One more thing, be honest about the simples business. Our decomposition stops wherever the part number stops. If the positions disagree about whether there are simples at all, tell me which of them are affected by my having no answer, and whether that ignorance is doing any work in the translations above.
