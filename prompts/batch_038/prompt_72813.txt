I'm running a 90-minute philosophy of language seminar, 14 students, mostly seniors who've done Kaplan and one Putnam reading and think they're basically finished with this stuff. I want an in-class game that makes them feel two-dimensional semantics from the inside — specifically the part that isn't just about indexicals: that an ordinary, totally non-context-sensitive term like a natural kind word has two different extensions depending on which kind of variation you're scoring it against, and you can't collapse the two into one.

Here's the shape I think could work, improve on it if you have something better: they commit in writing to a definition/translation for some unfamiliar term, then get scored twice — once in a round where the facts about the world turn out to be different from how they looked when the definitions were written, and once in a round where those facts are held fixed and they're scored against a counterfactual situation instead. The same definitions should do well in one round and badly in the other. If I've got this right, they run into the two axes as two different scoring regimes before anyone says primary intension out loud.

Constraints: no Twin Earth, no vats, no sci-fi framing at all. Half the class has heard the standard setup a dozen times and treats it as a thought experiment instead of a decision, and I want them actually committing under uncertainty with something on the line. Payoffs have to work with points on the board, not real money. Nobody hears any of the vocabulary until the debrief. Also, one of them will say this is just Kaplan's character and content with the labels swapped, and another will say it only gets interesting for words like I and here — build something into the structure that makes both of those hard to maintain by the end, or tell me straight if the game cant do that.
