You are a literary writer who also has serious training in twentieth-century African American intellectual history: someone who can make an argument feel like a room, a hand, a length of unprimed canvas, without letting the fiction collapse into a seminar. Please think carefully before you begin, and show me a little of that thinking. I want a short story, not an essay with characters attached to it.

The occasion is personal, which is why I am asking. I am a painter, thirty-four, and six weeks ago I accepted the largest commission of my life: a three-panel work for the atrium of the converted mill building in the town where my mother's family comes from, to be unveiled on the seventieth anniversary of the walkout of 1955. The arts council's brief describes what it wants in language that has not changed since the ribbon-cutting was scheduled: a work about solidarity, dignity, and a community that lost the strike but never broke. My grandmother, who died in April, told me something the brief does not contain, and my cousin — the chair of the descendants' committee, whose approval the commission formally requires — has told me she intends to make sure it stays out.

Here is the knot, as precisely as I can state it. In the final week of the walkout, three men, one of them my grandmother's brother, signed private agreements with the company: individual payments, a confidentiality clause, and a withdrawal. The line collapsed within days. That same money kept my grandmother's house through the eviction wave that followed, and it is the reason I was able to stay in art school. The company archive, the union's surviving minutes, and the local historical society's files all record only that the walkout ended; the agreements were never filed anywhere public, so the fact exists now in exactly one place, which is my family, and it will not exist anywhere once my cousin and I are dead. Meanwhile my cousin's own argument is not sentimental and not dishonest: she points out that for seventy years the regional story about this town has cast Black workers as strikebreakers and scabs, that the audience at the unveiling will include people who are already looking for a reason to think so, and that a Black artist who paints her own great-uncle signing a private deal is handing that audience a stock image it will gladly generalize. She asks whether I would rather be accurate for the two people who know, or useful for the four hundred who don't.

I want the story to work through the Du Bois position on this, which has become unavoidable for me. In his 1926 essay on the criteria of Negro art, Du Bois argues that for a people held in contempt there is no such thing as art that stays out of the fight: art is propaganda and must be, and the only live questions are what it propagates, whether it is truthful, and whether it is beautiful. The rival position associated with Alain Locke — that binding art to a program stunts it, and that the artist's first obligation is to the work's own demands — should appear in the story too, and not as a strawman.

To keep you from producing something generic, please build the story around three ideas I will name here, and treat them as my coinages rather than as established scholarly terms:

1. The archival remainder: the class of facts that survive only inside families and never inside records, so that the choice about whether to disclose them is a choice about whether they will exist at all.
2. Split valence: a single depicted element — a gesture, a piece of clothing, the angle of a man's shoulders — that reads as dignifying to one audience and as confirming a stereotype to another, which means no criterion of uplift can be applied without first saying whose eyes are doing the evaluating.
3. The fidelity budget: a notion an old teacher of mine used, that a public work can carry only so much unflattering particular before its audience stops seeing its subjects as agents and starts seeing them as specimens, and that the artist's real job is deciding how to spend that budget.

Requirements. Length: 1,700 to 2,200 words. Invent the town, the company, the names, and the specific textures of 1955 and of the present; I care about plausibility, not about matching any real event. Set at least half the story in the present, in and around the mill building, with my stand-in painter at work. Let the argument happen in dialogue and in decisions about composition — what is included, what is cropped, what is pushed to the margin of the middle panel, what is encoded so that only someone who knows will see it — rather than in interior monologue that sounds like a paper. The painter must make an actual choice about the mural by the end, and that choice must cost something specific and land on a person, not merely on an idea. Give my cousin the better of at least one exchange. End on an image or an action, not a thesis, and do not reconcile the two positions.

Output in three parts. First, a short brainstorming section of no more than 250 words: the dramatic situation, what each of the three or four characters wants and fears, and two or three candidate compositional resolutions with what each would cost. Second, the story. Third, an afterword of about 200 words stating which side of the Du Bois–Locke dispute you think the story's own evidence favors, and what the strongest consideration against your verdict is.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not put any words in quotation marks as though quoting Du Bois, Locke, or any other real person; paraphrase their positions in your own prose instead. I cannot verify quotations from here, and an invented one would be worse than none.
