Ok so — pretend you're a philosopher of technology who has actually spent time inside ML labs, someone who reads alignment documents for fun and knows what RLHF and a system prompt actually do in practice, not a pundit who only reads the press releases. Please think really carefully about this one, I'd much rather you take your time than be tidy.

Some background on why I'm asking. I'm about five weeks into a job at a mid-size AI company. My team writes our version of the public "model behavior" document — you know the genre, most of the big labs publish one now. Ours has the standard furniture:

- a short list of broad objectives (assist the developer, assist the end user, benefit humanity, don't blow up the company's reputation)
- a list of hard rules the model must never break, and our doc says explicitly that nobody can override these — not an API customer, not a user, not us
- everything else is a "default," which a developer or user is free to switch off
- and an explicit chain of command for when instructions conflict: platform rules beat the developer's system prompt, which beats the end user's message. Rank is basically determined by which role field the text arrived in.

The document does double duty. It's also the rubric we hand our preference-data contractors ("which of these two responses follows the spec better?"), and their ratings are the training signal. When contractors split, it escalates to a small internal group who make a call, and the call gets written back into the doc as a clarification.

Here's the stuff that's been bugging me since I started, and it's the real question:

1. The justification people give internally for the chain of command is liability, not moral authority. Something like "we're the ones who get sued and regulated, so we get the last word." But the doc itself is published as if it were a statement about what's right for the model to do, and we cite it when we refuse a hospital customer who wants to override a default. So we seem to be claiming a kind of standing we justify with a completely different kind of reason.
2. Our draft has a clause saying the model should treat a user's expressed preferences as the best available evidence about what's good for that user, and a nearby clause saying the model shouldn't lecture people about objectively better choices. And then the hard-rules list does exactly that lecturing, over the top of both clauses, and the contents of that list were picked by roughly nine people in a conference room.
3. Last week my manager asked me to soften a paragraph so that it matches what the model actually does on a certain category of edge case, "so the doc stays honest." Which felt wierd, because the rest of the time the doc is supposed to be what makes the behavior right. Sometimes it's a standard and sometimes it's a report and nobody seems to notice they're switching.

So: what philosophical commitments is this whole practice actually running on? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory — of authority, of what a rule is, of whose preferences count, of what the document itself is — and explain it in plain language, one-line definitions for any technical term you can't avoid. Then:

- Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out just by existing? I'm thinking of things like resolving conflicts by weighing reasons case by case instead of by rank, or getting the model's norms from consent, or from the character of the artifact rather than from a list.
- Is the thing internally consistent? Especially: if the hard rules bind us too, who is sovereign here — is the company a legislator, a trustee, a steward, or something like an interpreter of a text it can't amend? And can a document be both the standard and the description without contradiction, or does that circularity actually do useful work?
- Be really careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Where is it philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same document and the same training pipeline? If your honest answer is that it presupposes no single coherent view and is just a heap of compromises, say that — but then tell me which compromises are load-bearing and which are decorative.
- Finally, and this is the part I most need: what would concretely have to change if we swapped in a rival view? Like if conflicts had to be resolved by weighing reasons instead of checking rank, or if the doc were explicitly descriptive rather than constitutive. What breaks in the contractor rubric, in the escalation process, in what we can publish, in whether the thing is trainable at all? I have to present our doc at an internal review next week and I'd like to know what I'm actually defending.
