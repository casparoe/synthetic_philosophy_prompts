I did chemical kinetics for 38 years, mostly gas-phase oxidation — the unglamorous kind where you can spend a decade nailing down one rate constant and nobody outside your subfield notices. Retired two years ago, and instead of golf I fell down the philosophy-and-AI-risk hole. My wife says this is worse.

Here's the thing that won't let go. When people argue about "takeoff speeds" they argue entirely in metaphors, and I keep thinking they're describing a system I actually spent my life on. An autocatalytic or branching process runs away when the generation term beats the removal term, and the removal term is usually not chemistry at all — its the vessel walls killing your radicals, the cooling jacket, the fact that you can only push so much mass through so much pipe. That's the whole Semenov line of thinking about thermal runaway: whether a thing explodes is a statement about a balance of rates, not about how reactive the material is. So when someone tells me an intelligence explosion is near-certain because "AI will be able to do AI research," I hear "this reaction is exothermic, therefore it will detonate," which is not how any of it works, and I want to know whether the people saying it have ever had to explain a negative result to a funder.

So I'd like a number from you, not a mood.

Proposition: conditional on the loop closing at some point in the next 40 years — by which I mean a deployed AI system, working from the same inputs a human research team gets, can autonomously run frontier AI R&D end to end: propose, implement, train, evaluate, write it up, and hand back a measurably better system, at least as well as a strong human team — the elapsed time between that moment and the first system that clearly outperforms every living human at that work plus the general cognitive tasks around it is under 18 months.

Give me P, then decompose it the way I'd work up a mechanism: separate terms, a number and two or three sentences for each, and say explicitly how you combine them (product of conditionals? something else?). Cut and add terms as you judge, but I want at least these addressed:

- is "the loop closing" a threshold at all, or a long slope some of which is already behind us, in which case my T0 is a fiction;
- what fraction of the remaining progress is bottlenecked by things with real lead times — fabs, power interconnects, data centers, capital, organizational friction — versus by ideas, and whether anyone is actually measuring that ratio;
- whether a large capability jump confers the kind of advantage that lets you jump again, or whether it mostly gets competed or copied away before the second step;
- the selection effect sitting inside my own conditional: the world where the loop closes in 2031 may not look much like the world where it closes in 2055, so conditioning on "within 40 years" might be smuggling in a bias I can't see.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to — where a 10 point shift in a sub-probability moves P the most — and what a person like me could actually watch over the next five years that would update it. My own guess is 0.3 and my daughter, who writes software for a living and considers me a dinosaur, says 0.75; tell me which single term the two of us are most likely disagreeing about.

And if you think my proposition is badly posed, say so plainly and give the number under your preferred version too, with the difference between the two spelled out.
