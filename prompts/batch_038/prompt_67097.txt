I'm a third-year genetics PhD student and my department has roped me into running a ninety-minute session for a PGCE cohort — trainee secondary school teachers — as part of their "understanding your pupils" week. The background, which nobody has said to me out loud but which the programme lead hinted at over email, is that last year a trainee said something on placement about certain children's reading difficulties being "in their genes" and a parent complained. So I'll be stood in front of about thirty-five people who mostly did arts and humanities A-levels, no stats beyond GCSE, and who are I'd guess split between "genes decide everything so there's no point trying to teach it" and "that's all eugenics nonsense, it's environment all the way down". Whiteboard and flip chart only, no projector, and I'm allowed one side of A4 as a handout. I've never taught this, and the last time I tried explaining variance to non-scientists it went horribly — they got stuck on the word itself and I lost twenty minutes to it.

What I want from you isn't an explanation of heritability, I know the content fine, it's a plan for how I should explain it. Specifically: what order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — I keep going back and forth on whether the population-relativity point has to land before or after anyone has seen an actual number, and whether I can even use a number before they understand what a variance is a variance *of*; which single example should carry the whole session, and I mean one, because I'd assumed twin studies but now worry thats the wrong choice since it invites the "well obviously identical twins match, they're the same person" reaction and drags in the equal environments assumption, which I don't have time for; and what that carrying example needs to contain and what it must deliberately leave out. Then the misconceptions. I can already predict "heritable means unfixable", "it's a property of a person, like eye colour", "0.6 heritability means 60 per cent of my height is genes and 40 per cent is food", and the one I actually dread, where somebody asks whether any of it applies to differences between groups of people. Where in the sequence does each of those need heading off, and which one is fatal if it's still alive at minute eighty? I'd also like to use PKU at some point — the completely genetic, completely fixable-with-a-diet case — but I can't tell whether that's a gift or a trap, since someone might come away thinking it proves you just need to find the right chemical fix for everything.

Two more things. I want one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that will actually tell me whether it landed rather than whether they've learned to parrot the approved words back at me — ideally something where the wrong answer is more informative than the right one. And be blunt about what to cut: the programme lead has asked me not to mention IQ at all, and part of me thinks that's cowardly and will make the whole session feel like it's hiding something, so tell me whether to comply or bring it in briefly and, if briefly, at which point in the sequence it does least damage. If you think broad-sense versus narrow-sense heritability has any business in a ninety-minute room full of trainee teachers, say so and say why.
