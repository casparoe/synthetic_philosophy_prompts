I am a postdoc putting together case material for an interdisciplinary seminar on competition and collective action, and I have grown tired of the same four stock examples: the fishery, the emissions negotiation, the race to publish, and — more recently — the race to build and deploy advanced machine-learning systems. I would like to commission a case that breaks the pattern in a specific and, I think, underexplored direction: one in which the Coasean remedy is not the escape from the trap but part of its machinery. The intended audience is a mixed group of economists, lawyers and two philosophers, so the prose of the scenario should be intelligible to someone with no game theory at all, but the incentive structure underneath must be explicit enough that I can check it myself rather than take it on trust. I am not after references or a reading list; the case has to stand on its own.

Please construct a scenario involving a Moloch-type multipolar trap — a configuration in which every participant responds correctly to the incentives the others create, and all end up on a path each regards as worse than an available alternative, with no unilateral escape — subject to all of the following specifications:

1. **Three actors**, roughly symmetric in size, resources and situation, so that the trap cannot be explained by a large player exploiting small ones. Choose a realistic institutional setting; please do not use states, laboratories, fisheries, emissions, advertising platforms or anything involving artificial intelligence.
2. **Full transparency.** Each actor observes the others' choices, costs and resulting payoffs immediately and without error, and this is common knowledge. No private information, no hidden action, no monitoring problem.
3. **No enforceability failure.** The three can and do conclude an explicit, mutually binding agreement, costlessly enforceable, and they comply with its terms perfectly. The agreement is not defective drafting and nobody exploits a loophole.
4. **The harm stays at the table.** Customers or counterparties may exist as background, but the deterioration falls on the three actors themselves; no regulator, court, industry body or public enters to rescue or condemn them.
5. **No psychological escape hatches.** The actors are rational, patient and clear-sighted: no myopia, no bounded rationality, no status-quo bias, no ideology, no misapprehension of their own interests, and no preference change mid-case.
6. **The trap is dynamic.** The equilibrium path involves progressive deterioration rather than a static bad equilibrium, and each stage of the race makes the next stage's temptation larger than the last. The actors foresee the shape of this path correctly.
7. **The bargain is endogenous to the trap.** The outcome under the agreement is worse for all three than the outcome that would have obtained had they never agreed — and each of the three knows this at the moment of signing, and signs anyway, because declining to sign is worse still given that the other two sign.

Alongside the scenario — which should be no longer than about 400 words — please provide: an explicit account of the choice structure and the payoffs at each stage, in tabular or equivalent form, so that every incentive claim in the narrative can be verified rather than asserted; a demonstration, specification by specification, that the case meets items 1 to 7, including the three checks that specification 7 demands (signing is individually rational for each given the others sign; refusing is worse for each given the others sign; all three foresee that the resulting path is worse than the pre-agreement path); an identification of the single structural feature doing the work, together with an account of what happens to the case if that feature is removed — does it collapse into an ordinary prisoner's dilemma, into a coordination problem with several equilibria, or into nothing at all? Finally, give me the strongest attack available to someone who insists this is not a genuine multipolar trap at all — perhaps that the deterioration is really a concealed third-party effect, that the actors are the wrong unit of analysis, or that the agreement's failure is an artefact of the particular numbers chosen — and the best reply to it.

If you conclude that some part of this specification cannot be satisfied in conjunction with the rest, attempt the construction anyway and then say precisely which specification has to be relaxed and why; I would rather learn that from a near-miss than from a case that quietly cheats. Please write the whole response in British English, in spelling and idiom alike.
