I convene a philosophy-of-biology reading group at a regional university, and we record our sessions as a low-budget podcast. Each episode is a forty-five-minute, seminar-style Q&A with the author of a book or dissertation that the group has read in advance, so nobody needs setup questions and nobody will be thanked for a career summary. Next month's guest defends colony-level cognition: she holds that certain eusocial insect colonies are literal cognitive subjects — that the colony, and not merely each of its members, is the thing that remembers, represents, and decides — and in the introduction she leans on the Aunt Hillary material in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach to supply the intuition that you have to raise your level of description before a mind comes into view. She also invokes the parity-style reasoning familiar from extended and embodied cognition: if the causal-functional role is realized, it shouldn't matter what realizes it.

What makes her unusually good to interrogate is that she commits to an explicit criterion rather than to a mood. She says a collection of agents counts as a cognitive subject only when three conditions hold together: (i) the aggregate's behavioral repertoire is not decomposable into the repertoires of the parts plus a fixed interaction schedule; (ii) there is a level of description at which the system's states figure in counterfactual generalizations that the lower-level description cannot deliver at comparable tractability; and (iii) the aggregate preserves those capacities through substantial turnover, redistribution, and even partial loss of its parts. I want questions that use her own conditions as levers. Give me nine to eleven questions, ordered so that the pressure builds, with at least one question on each of the following — and after each of those, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, plus one sentence stating what that reply is, so I know what I am responding to:

- the scope objection: her conditions look equally satisfied by cities, markets, and the global airline network, so ask her why the criterion stops at insects, and press her when she says the human cases fail some condition she has not yet stated.
- the nesting problem: ants have nervous systems, learn individually, and plausibly have representations of their own, whereas neurons do not; ask whether the presence of full-fledged sub-subjects makes the colony a better candidate for subjecthood or a worse one, and whether she can answer without simply inverting the standard intuition.
- condition (ii) and exclusion: if the ant-level description can in principle license the same counterfactuals, then "comparable tractability" sounds like a claim about us rather than about the world; ask her whether her criterion is epistemic, and if it is, what licenses saying the colony is a subject.
- condition (iii) and identity: colonies bud, split, fuse, drift workers into neighboring nests, and adopt foreign brood; ask which of these operations she thinks preserves the subject and whether her answer can avoid being ad hoc.
- the empirical case: nest-site choice in social insects is often explained by quorum-style thresholds whose individual-level implementation is quite well understood; ask what the colony-level attribution adds to that explanation and what it predicts that the threshold account does not.
- experience: ask whether she thinks the colony is a subject of experience, and if she denies it, what principle could separate cognition from experience in a system assembled this way — this is where I expect her to say the question is premature, so build the follow-up to meet exactly that move.
- the analogy: ask what work the Hofstadter passage is doing in her argument, and whether anything survives if the analogy is simply deleted.

Finally, close with one question about what observation or experiment would convince her that the colony-level subject attribution is false, and refuse to let a research-programme answer stand in for it. No warm-up questions, nothing that could be answered by restating her three conditions, and keep each question short enough to be asked aloud in a single breath.
