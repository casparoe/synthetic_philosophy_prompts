So I got talked into joining the board of a small fund about a year ago — we move maybe $400k a year into climate-adjacent stuff — and this quarter we have to actually pick a five-year allocation strategy, and the argument in our Slack has turned into a philosophy problem I didn't know had a name until recently. One of us keeps saying the biggest effects of anything we fund are going to be downstream and utterly unknowable; his favorite move is the butterfly-effect one, that a grant which shifts some researcher's career in 2028 could plausibly be the difference between a good and a bad century, so the entire ranking exercise is theater. Another keeps replying that fine, then just pick whichever option is robust across lots of different stories about the far future — which sounds sensible untill you ask what makes one far-future story count as more relevant than another, and then nobody can answer. I've since learned this is the cluelessness problem and people have been arguing about it for decades, and honestly I care less about who wins than about knowing what a fix would have to look like before I sign off on one.

Which is what I want from you: write the desiderata — the requirements any proposed response to consequentialist cluelessness has to meet to count as an actual solution rather than a restatement of the problem. I want the consideration or the case behind each requirement, not just the list. And be opinionated about which ones are genuinely non-negotiable versus which are just the house style of one camp, because I suspect at least two of the things people demand are overreaching. Candidates for overreach I want you to rule on: the demand that the fix yield determinate, usable guidance for an actual deliberating agent (maybe that's asking the theory to do a job theories don't have); the demand that it not be ad hoc about where it draws the line between consequences that count and consequences that get ignored; and the demand that the fix be distinctively available to consequentialists — my hunch is that cluelessness bites anything whose permissibility conditions reference outcomes at all, so that requirement is either false or it's a feature rather than a bug.

And here's the part I most want, the bit I couldn't find anywhere: my hunch is that cluelessness is really at least three problems in a trench coat, and most fixes only handle one of them. Roughly — (a) plain ignorance, where the probabilities exist and we just don't have them; (b) no well-defined comparison set, where the open question is which possible worlds even count as the relevantly-close ones to rank against; and (c) something worse, where the long-run value of an act might not be a determinate number even for an omniscient observer, because the effects are indefinitely many and each indefinitely small, so the total behaves like a conditionally convergent series whose sum changes depending on the order you add things up in. If (c) is a real category I want it written up as a requirement — something along the lines of: the fix must explain why the far-future total is well-defined, not merely unknown — and if you think it's a confusion, say so and say why, because I've gone back and forth on it for a week. Then flag any subset of your requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable (that's the result I'd find most useful), tell me which single requirement kills the most fixes on the market, and finish with a quick pass of the main families of response against the list: the evidence-relative or subjective-ought move, going rule- or practice-level, restricting comparison to nearby worlds with some default treatment for the rest, swapping expected-value reasoning for robustness or maximin-style rules, and just conceding that agents are massively but blamelessly wrong. Table is fine for that last bit and keep it short. No bibliography, I'll add references myself once I know what I'm looking for — what I actually need is a spec I can hand to five non-philosophers and use to interrogate whatever strategy we end up voting on.
