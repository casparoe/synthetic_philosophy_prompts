Okay so I'm the philosophy person on a working group for a city's capital budget process — the group that's supposed to hand the council a defensible way to compare projects that score well on totally different kinds of goods (affordable housing units, bus travel time, tree canopy, keeping a 1920s building standing). My economist colleague's answer is monetize everything and lean on a value of a statistical life for the rest. My colleague from the preservation commission says you can't put a price on the building and calls that incommensurablity. They keep invoking philosophy at each other and I keep getting asked to referee, and I'd rather not referee anything until I know what I'm supposed to be asking of a theory.

So write me the spec: what a theory of incommensurable values and hard choices actually has to deliver to count as adequate — and note that adequate here means good enough to be operationalized in a decision rule, not just metaphysically respectable. Requirements list, and for each one give me the case or consideration behind it, say whether it's non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly met. If some subset is unsatisfiable together, say so — that's the part I most want to see.

Things I already suspect belong on the list, push back if they don't: it has to explain the difference between a genuinely hard choice and a merely very close call; it has to handle the small-improvement move (offer me a slightly better-paying version of the career I'd otherwise prefer and see if the verdict budges) and say what happens at the end of a long chain of those; it has to keep apart incomparability as a fourth relation from incompleteness in a ranking that's just no verdict yet; it has to say whether what looks like incomparability is vagueness or something else; it has to survive the money-pump complaint about cyclical choice rather than just noting it; it has to say whether a resolution or commitment can settle a hard choice instead of only discovering that it was already settled; it has to explain why regret survives a well-made decision in the tragic cases; and it can't go inflationary (every close call is incomparable) or deflationary (nothing ever is). Add whatever I'm missing — including anything about how incomparability is supposed to transmit upward from sub-comparisons to the whole-life or whole-project comparison, and what a theory should say when two people in the room rank the same options in opposite orders: is the collective verdict incomparability, or just disagreement?

One requirement I need spelled out separately because it's more load-bearing than it probably should be: our draft procedure breaks ties by picking whichever option is cheaper to implement. Is there any theory of incommensurability that licenses that tiebreak, or does every view that admits parity also forbid it?

Then briefly — two or three lines each, no lit review — how the main contenders stack up against the list: straight monism with a single cardinal scale, the parity/fourth-relation view, the vagueness or rough-comparability line, and a multidimensional view that thinks nothing mysterious is going on at all. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates.
