I'm on a six-person forecasting team inside a mid-size reinsurance outfit — we do quarterly probability calls on things like "will the state legislature pass the coastal building code bill this session," and we get scored on them. Two of us, Priya and Marcus, went head to head for about three weeks over one call and I ended up the unwilling referee. Both of them are smart and both read the same packet of documents, so I cant blame it on information asymmetry, and the argument got wierdly philosophical fast. Here's roughly how it went:

1. They'd read the same briefs, sat in on the same two calls with the same outside experts, and still landed at 0.3 and 0.68 on the bill passing. Marcus said that was fine and unremarkable: same evidence, different weighting, priors are priors, and there's nothing to argue about.

2. Priya said that's a cop-out and invoked Aumann. If you share a common prior and you both know each other's posteriors — and know that you know, all the way down — then your posteriors have to be equal. So a disagreement that survives both people knowing the other is rational and fully informed isn't a rational disagreement. Somebody's cheating somewhere.

3. Marcus said the theorem is a trap, because the common knowledge condition basically never obtains in real life: you'd need an infinite chain of "I know that you know that I know," and nobody on our team has that. So the theorem says nothing about the two of them.

4. Priya said fine, drop the theorem, the weaker result does the same work — the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis one where two Bayesians who iteratively announce their posteriors converge on agreement in a finite state space. They had effectively been announcing at each other for three weeks, so they should have converged, and the fact that they hadn't is itself the finding.

5. Marcus said that result assumes a common prior too, so it's the same assumption with extra steps, and the assumption is exactly what's in dispute. He said the Harsanyi line — that any difference in beliefs has to be traced back to a difference in information — can't apply to priors at all, since a prior is by definition what you have before the information arrives. Using it to police priors is begging the question.

6. Priya said no, it's a coherence constraint, not question-begging. If you think your prior is rationally permissible for you and mine is permissible for me, then before either of us has any evidence one of us should already think the other is irrational — but neither of us thinks that, we each rate the other as competent. So we're jointly committed to there being one right prior, with one of us nearer it. She added the escape hatch: you don't have to assert the common prior as a fact about the world, you just build a bigger model with a prior over types, and our "different priors" gets redescribed as each of us being unsure which type we are. Nothing is lost.

7. Marcus said that's precisely why it's empty. You can always cook up a prior over types to represent any pair of beliefs you like, so the assumption can never be violated — it's a modeling convention dressed as a claim, and it can't tell Priya anything she didn't already know. He also said their real disagreement isn't about textbook priors anyway: a lot of what people call a prior is smuggled-in other stuff — risk attitude, how costly a false positive is, how much you trust the model class, how much compute you're willing to burn — and there's no reason those should match between two people with identical data. And half the time they don't even share a state space, since he's thinking about coalition dynamics in the senate and she's thinking about the lobbying money, and you can't put a common prior over hypotheses the other person hasn't articulated.

8. Priya conceded some of that and retreated to a weaker claim, which she said is the one that matters: whatever priors do, once each of us knows the other is honest, competent, and fully informed, the bare fact of the disagreement is evidence, and I should update toward you. Marcus agreed with that immediately. Then he said it proves his point — updating toward someone because they dissent and are smart is just ordinary Bayesian updating on new evidence, nothing to do with common priors, and two people can rationally do it by different amounts, since the weight you give the other person's dissent is itself a prior-level judgment. Which left them roughly where they started: 0.41 and 0.6.

Flag anything both of them are getting wrong or just not seeing. If some part of the fight turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which part and point at where the words are doing the work. And practically, since we get scored: end with what a team like ours should actually do when two people land far apart and neither will move — if the answer is anything.
