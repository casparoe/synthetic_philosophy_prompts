I'm on the nine-person awards and nomenclature committee of a professional society for structural engineers, about 4,000 members, and in October we have to rule on a petition about the Harroway Medal — our top prize, named after one of the founders, who in 1831 shot and killed a rival in a formal duel over a published quarrel about whose bridge design was sound, and who afterwards defended the practice in a pamphlet.

Two things are going on and I only care about the second one. The surface fight is easy: the petitioners, mostly early-career members, want the medal renamed; the old guard says a name is just a name and we have bigger fish to fry. The interesting part is what one petitioner, a member who teaches history, put in her cover note. She says we've been evaluating this the wrong way — as a question about whether Harroway was a good man — when it's really a question about mechanism. She sorts the ways a norm like dueling dies into three: (a) refutation, people get argued out of it; (b) displacement, the practice loses its social job and simply stops being worth doing; (c) recoding, the practice keeps its job but flips sign in the honor ledger, so what used to buy prestige now buys shame. Her claim is that a renaming decision only counts for something if it's an instance of one of those, and that renaming a medal is at best recoding-by-proxy — theater — unless it attaches to something still live. And she thinks there is something live: our own dispute culture. Members who believe they've been scooped or trashed in review are informally expected to escalate — public denunciations at the annual meeting, priority claims, defend-your-work-in-person-or-forfeit-it — and the ones who do this hardest are the ones who get elected to society leadership. She calls that a duel culture with the pistols swapped out for proceedings, and says the medal is its totem.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before we meet. It has to come down somewhere — rename, keep, keep-with-a-condition, re-scope what the medal is actually awarded for, refer it elsewhere, whatever — and it needs a date attached, because the bylaws give us 90 days to answer a petition and we're on day 60. Complication you have to work with: the original endowment deed requires the award to carry Harroway's name in perpetuity, and counsel says the donor's heirs could challenge a straight renaming, so "just rename it" may not be an available move even if it's the right one.

The reasons should be about the mechanism, not about Harroway's character. Specifically: can a naming decision genuinely be an instance of refutation, displacement, or recoding, or is she stretching those categories when she applies them to a piece of institutional signage? Is the live-practice claim real or a category slide — is an informal professional escalation culture the same kind of thing as a codified honor duel, and what would make it the same kind of thing versus merely analogous? And treat the old guard's "it's just a name" line as possibly stronger than it sounds, since if names don't carry honor then her whole framing collapses, and if they do then the deed clause becomes a serious obstacle rather than a technicality.

One more thing to handle honestly: the treasurer thinks the petition is partly factional — two of the signatories' mentors were passed over for the medal, and the society is also mid-campaign for younger members, so there are interests in play that have nothing to do with honor or argument. Say whether that observation discredits the petition or is just true and irrelevant, or both.

For what it's worth I read a popular book years ago arguing that dueling in Britain and footbinding in China ended because they became dishonorable rather than because anyone won an argument, so I'm already pre-disposed to side with the petitioner. Push back on me where I'm wrong. And write the strongest case against your recommendation at full strength, in the voice of someone who could actually vote no — two of the nine will, and they aren't stupid.
