Write a short story of roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words whose engine is Aumann's agreement theorem — the 1976 result, proved in the paper Agreeing to Disagree, that two rational agents who begin from a common prior cannot remain at odds about the probability of some event if their respective probabilities are common knowledge between them: in that case the numbers must be equal. Two further features of the result should shape the story. First, mere mutual knowledge is not enough — each knowing the other's number, and each knowing that the other knows it, to any finite depth, still leaves room for a gap. Second, an honest, repeated exchange of numbers between agents who genuinely started from the same place does converge, and converges to agreement. Persistent disagreement, therefore, is not a mood or a temperament; on these conditions it is a finding.

The premise is as follows. Two people who have pooled everything — you may make them a long marriage, or two siblings who inherited and still run the same concern — have for roughly a decade each kept a private estimate, held as a single number, of one binary factual question about an absent third person: whether that person is alive, whether they will come home, whether they did the thing they are said to have done. Choose the question, but it must be a matter of fact that evidence could in principle settle, not a matter of interpretation. Every scrap of evidence has been shared between them: correspondence read aloud, calls taken on speaker, the same lawyer, the same reports. And yet, once a year, on some fixed occasion, the two compare numbers, and the numbers stay stubbornly far apart — something like three-quarters against something like one-tenth — and the gap does not close.

The narrator is the one who eventually understands that the gap is itself information: two people who began in the same place and have shared everything since cannot still be that far apart unless one of them is not telling the whole truth about something.

Requirements.

The narrator should first attempt the obvious remedy — say the numbers aloud, plainly and repeatedly, in conditions where nothing can be missed or misheard, so that each knows and knows that the other knows. The gap must survive even this, and it is that survival which forces the second and worse diagnosis.

The story must contain, on its surface, one failed announcement: a number spoken and heard, and the hearing acknowledged, where the loop nevertheless closes no further than that. Let the reader identify from what happens in the scene — not from explanation — the rung at which the chain stops.

Beneath this, the true explanation must be that the two never did begin in the same place. One of them received, once, long ago, something the other never received, and has never been able to say so. Make this small, physical and datable: a page, an object, a sentence overheard through a wall, something seen from a car window on a particular afternoon. It must retroactively account for both the size and the persistence of the gap.

Include at least one piece of genuinely new evidence, received by both at the same time and honestly weighed by both, and show the two numbers move in the same direction by visibly different amounts.

Do not name the theorem, do not cite the paper, and do not allow the vocabulary of priors, posteriors, updating, common knowledge, or Bayes into either the dialogue or the narration; the mathematics must appear only as behavior. One character may try to articulate the reasoning in ordinary language exactly once, and what they say must be either incomplete or aimed at the wrong target. The narrator's inference should be right in form, right about who, and wrong about what.

The story must end in agreement. The two numbers must finally meet, and the meeting must be the worst thing that happens to either of them.

Keep it in scene: no academic setting, no conference, no character whose profession is the study of belief; no dreams, no messages from the dead, no twist delivered in the last line. Treat the annual exchange of numbers with the gravity of a rite, and do not let the story wink at its own cleverness.
