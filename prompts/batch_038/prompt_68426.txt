'The genome is a code in the strictest sense, and it is worth being precise about what that means. DNA is written in a four-letter alphabet, and the cell reads it by means of a cipher that maps three-letter codons onto amino acids — a cipher with start signals, stop signals, and the same kind of built-in redundancy that engineers deliberately introduce into error-correcting transmission schemes. This is no more a metaphor than Morse code is a metaphor. Because the cipher is very nearly the same in a bacterium and in a person, a human gene can be dropped into a plasmid and read correctly, and that shows the specificity lies in the instructions rather than in the machinery doing the reading. Nor are those instructions sparse. When a large consortium set out to catalogue the biochemical activity of the human genome, it reported that roughly eighty percent of the sequence takes part in some biochemical event in some cell type, which retires the older picture of the genome as a handful of genes scattered through inert filler. It also explains what identical twins share and adopted siblings do not: a body of information sufficient to specify a developmental outcome, which is just what the heritability numbers have always implied. To call DNA a program is therefore not preformationism in disguise. It is a literal description of a molecule that specifies its own products and, through them, the organism that grows around them.'
