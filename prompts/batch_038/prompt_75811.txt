I have no academic training in philosophy or mathematics — this is all self-directed reading over the past couple of years — and I have arrived at a question I cannot settle on my own.

The setting is logical uncertainty. As I understand it, a reasoner with finite computational resources cannot be logically omniscient, and so it is at least coherent for it to hold degrees of belief about mathematical sentences it has not yet settled. The proposal I have in mind asks for a book of probabilities over the sentences of arithmetic, revised over time as proofs come in, subject to a constraint roughly of this shape: no trading strategy that is computable within the reasoner's own resource budget can systematically extract unbounded profit from that book — not on sentences whose truth is eventually fixed by proof, and not on empirical regularities in the reasoning itself, such as the digit frequencies of π. A sequence of probability books meeting that constraint is what is meant by a logical inductor. Such a reasoner assigns Goldbach's conjecture some middling probability, assigns a long-unproved but true sentence a probability noticeably below one, gives some contradictions a small nonzero probability early on, and converges only in the limit.

Now the historical part. Gottlob Frege strikes me as the natural adversary of this entire picture. He insisted that logic is concerned with truths and not with the mental goings-on of anyone who entertains them; he demanded proofs that are gapless, with every step fully evident; and he treated a thought as timelessly true or false, with nothing in between. Yet Frege also lived through something close to a personal refutation: Russell's letter showed him that a principle at the base of his system was inconsistent, and his response, as I read it, was to concede the difficulty in the afterword to the second volume, sketch a restriction that might repair it, and carry on treating his account of number as essentially sound rather than abandoning the project.

So: what would Frege have made of logical induction, had he encountered it? I would like an answer to each of these, and I am especially interested in the places where my framing is probably wrong.

- Would he file the inductor's credences under logic or under psychology, and what would hang on that classification for him, given that the criterion is stated in terms of what a bounded agent can compute?
- Would he regard a credence of 0.4 in Goldbach's conjecture as a fact about the thought, a fact about the reasoner's grasp of the thought, or simply a conflation of the two?
- Is a market price that converges on the truth in the limit any better in his eyes than one that does not, or does the intermediacy already disqualify it?
- Would his own conduct after Russell's letter count, by his own standards, as an instance of the very thing he would reject — a reasoner revising the standing of a principle without having proved anything about it?
- Is there anything here he would welcome? My hunch is that he might accept the limit behavior and reject everything before it, and that he would find the no-exploitation argument circular because it is parasitic on the notion of an agent's abilities. Tell me where that hunch is naive.

Please distinguish, as you go, which parts of your answer are grounded in things Frege actually maintained and which are extrapolation from his general commitments.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
