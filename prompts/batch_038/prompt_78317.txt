I help run a community science circle out of our public library: about fourteen adults, none with any physics training, one ninety-minute evening a month. I've been reading about Bell's theorem on my own for a few months, and what I want the group to feel isn't the mathematics but the specific bite of it — that two people who agreed on a plan beforehand, however elaborate, and who then cannot signal to each other, are capped in how well they can answer separately posed random questions, and that the world apparently does better than that cap.

Design a session in which the group runs into this on their own, before anyone hears the words Bell, locality, or hidden variable. Materials are limited to paper, coins or dice, and some way of keeping pairs out of each other's earshot. Give me the round structure, exactly what a pair is permitted to agree on in advance, how each round is scored, and a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money. I want most of the session spent on them inventing ever cleverer pre-arranged codes and hitting the same ceiling, and I want them to leave having established that ceiling for themselves rather than being told about it.

Then the debrief: map what happened onto the real result, and explain why the ceiling binds any pre-agreed strategy and not merely a lack of imagination. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — above all, how I'm meant to convey the side that beats the ceiling when nothing in the room is entangled — and about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. One regular, a retired engineer, is certain that any such limit falls to a clever enough code; arrange things so that he is the one who finds the wall.
