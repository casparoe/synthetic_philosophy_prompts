I wrote down the bit that seemed to matter and it went roughly like this:

"For decades physicists have argued about whether the wavefunction is a physical object in the world or just a convenient package for our ignorance. Harrigan and Spekkens turned that vague dispute into a precise one. Call a model ψ-ontic if the complete physical state of a system fixes exactly which quantum state it's in, and ψ-epistemic if two different quantum states can both be attached to one and the same physical state — the way two different states of knowledge about a coin in a box can describe one and the same physical situation. Then in 2012 Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph proved that ψ-epistemic models cannot reproduce quantum mechanics, as long as you accept that two systems prepared independently of each other really do have independent physical states. The trick: take two qubits, each prepared in either |0⟩ or |+⟩, so there are four possible preparation pairs. There is a joint measurement on the pair with four outcomes, and quantum mechanics assigns probability zero to each outcome for exactly one of those four pairs. But if |0⟩ and |+⟩ overlap, then with nonzero chance both systems end up in a physical state that is compatible with all four pairs at once, and then every outcome is forbidden — yet the detector still has to click. Contradiction. So the quantum state is part of the furniture of the world rather than part of our description of it. The result gets compared to Bell's theorem a lot: assume nothing beyond a mild locality-style independence for separately prepared systems, and the wavefunction turns out to be real. That takes QBism off the table, since QBism says the quantum state is just an agent's personal betting odds, and it leaves the genuinely realist interpretations — Everett, Bohm — as the survivors."
