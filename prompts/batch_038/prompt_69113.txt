I teach a grad seminar on the metaphysics of medicine and I'm stuck on the section about the legal definition of death. The model act most US states have adopted — the Uniform Determination of Death Act, approved in 1981 — says an individual is dead if they have sustained either "irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions" or "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem," with the determination made in accordance with accepted medical standards. Two prongs, disjoined. Everybody in my department assumes they're just two routes to the same verdict. I want a case that shows they aren't.

So: build me one ICU scenario, one paragraph, in which the two prongs come apart in the direction nobody ever constructs — the circulatory prong delivers "dead" and the whole-brain prong delivers "alive," about the same patient at the same moment, and the patient is awake, oriented, and in the middle of an argument with the attending about exactly that. Don't give me the reverse (whole brain destroyed, heart beating on a ventilator for months, family insisting they're not dead); that one's easy and I already have it.

Constraints, and please treat them as hard:

- Current ICU technology only: ECMO, ventricular assist devices, total artificial hearts, ventilators. No uploading, no cryonics, no synthetic blood, nothing a transplant surgeon would roll their eyes at. If some detail would make an intensivist say "that patient wouldn't be sitting up and talking," flag it yourself rather than letting me discover it in class.
- Nobody makes a mistake. The determination is being made by a competent clinician following accepted standards, and both prongs are read honestly. The verdicts have to come out of the text of the prongs, not out of a stipulation about what "irreversible" means — a case that quietly assumes its conclusion is worse for me than no case at all. But the scenario should force the question of what "irreversible" means rather than settle it in advance, so when you report the verdicts, tell me which reading of that word you needed and mark it as load-bearing.
- Make the patient a plausible transplant candidate. That's what makes irreversibility bite: their own organs are gone for good, irreversably, and yet they are booked to receive new ones.

Then the part I actually want: a second version of the same case, identical down to the physiology, with exactly one thing changed — whether anyone is standing by, equipped and authorized, to restore flow. I have in mind the argument that what the standard really trades on isn't irreversibility but permanence: a cessation counts as irreversible because we've decided not to undo it. Build the pair so that decision is the only difference, then show me which prong's verdict flips and why. If neither flips, say so plainly and tell me why my requested pair doesn't do the work I wanted — don't paper over it.

After the two scenarios, run the audit:

- apply each prong to each version, state the verdict and the reason, in a form I could drop straight onto a slide;
- say what the disjunction is doing here — whether a standard that declares someone dead on either prong can keep both prongs once a case like this exists, or whether one of them has to be demoted to a rule of evidence about how we detect death;
- identify the single feature of your case that's doing all the work, then ablate it: take the pump away and let the native heart beat; take away the patient's capacity to argue; take away the transplant listing. Which of those collapses the case back into something uninteresting, and which ones don't matter?
- give me the strongest attack from someone who thinks the whole-brain prong is the only real criterion and that the circulatory prong is just legacy drafting. I expect it to run through the claim that "circulatory function" means the work of moving blood around the body, not the work of the heart — in which case your prong-1 verdict never gets off the ground. State that objection at full strength, then tell me whether it saves the standard or merely relocates the problem onto donation-after-circulatory-death protocols, where death is declared after a few minutes of absent flow and the organs are then used in ways that demonstrate the flow could have been restored.

No bibliography, no reading list, im not after a lit review — just the cases and the reasoning. And if you need a clinical detail you're not certain about, mark it uncertain instead of inventing a study to cover it.
