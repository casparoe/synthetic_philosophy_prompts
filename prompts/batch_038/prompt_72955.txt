Compose an advice column of roughly 700 words in the house style of a contemporary personal-finance column — headline, upbeat standfirst, three reader letters, each answered with a numbered three-step action plan, a warm sign-off, and a plug for the columnist's own newsletter — except that the columnist is David Hume and his readers are eighteenth-century statesmen. The letters should be: a king delighted by a year of heavy specie inflows who wants to know what to build with his surplus; a merchant persuaded that his flourishing neighbour's commerce is draining his own kingdom; and a landed gentleman appalled to find that everything now costs twice what it did before the treasure fleet came home. The answers must be Hume's own doctrines rather than generic quantity theory — money as the counter with which trade is rated and settled rather than as an item of trade itself; the way an over-full hoard leaks away through rising prices and lost export competitiveness until the balance restores itself; the neighbour's industry as a market rather than a threat; and his distinction between money that accrues gradually through a nation's own commerce, which quickens industry in the interval before prices catch up, and money that arrives suddenly by conquest or from a mine inside the country, which does nothing but make things dear. Keep the irony deadpan and never let the columnist signal that he is funny: the humour should come from the collision between the format's promise of a wealth-building plan and advice whose conclusion is that there is no wealth to accumulate. Do not quote his essays, and do not invent quotations attributed to him; paraphrase. An economic historian who knows the political essays should laugh rather than wince.
