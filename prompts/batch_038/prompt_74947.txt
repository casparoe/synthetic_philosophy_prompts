I TA an upper-level metaphysics seminar, 14 students, and next week is our 100-minute session on the special composition question. They've already done two weeks on vagueness and the sorites, so they walk in thinking they know exactly what's coming: a heap, add one grain, uh oh, contradiction. Last year I ran a version of this where I just put objects on the table and asked "how many things are here?" and it flopped hard — within ten minutes three of them had said it's all just convention so whatever we decide goes, and the one nihilist in the corner spent the rest of the hour smug. This year I want a real game where the pressure comes from the structure instead of from me talking, and where "it's just conventional" doesn't let anyone off the hook. Setting: one long session, four tables (groups of 4/4/3/3), about $30 for hardware-store props, the room's projector is unreliable so please don't make anything depend on slides until the very end, and I don't want to say the phrase "special composition question" out loud until the debrief.

Here's the mechanic I want you to build out and improve on. Every student gets a 30-item answer sheet of photographied setups arranged along several smooth physical continua — for instance two magnets held a hair apart, magnets just touching, magnets taped, magnets epoxied, two magnets melted into a single lump; or marbles loose in a jar, jar with the lid on, jar shaken so they're wedged, marbles glued into a rigid sculpture; or a rope coiled on the floor, rope tied in a knot, rope spliced end to end, rope woven into a mat. For each item they write one integer: how many material objects are in the picture. No "it depends," because I need to be able to audit them. Two sealed conditions: half the tables answer all 30 cases first and only afterwards write down the general rule that's supposed to explain their answers; the other half write the rule first, seal it in an envelope, then answer the cases. Then they swap sheets with another table and audit for mismatches between stated rule and actual answers, and the group with the fewest mismatches wins (small prize, coffee card or whatever). My hope is that rules-first students discover their own principle drags them somewhere they didn't intend, cases-first students discover no rule they can state matches their answers, and both get compared out loud. Please give me: the full prop and continuum list (aim for 3 continua of about 10 items, with the integer answers you expect each family to provoke and a note on where you expect the class to fracture), scoring and audit rules detailed enough that a TA who's never heard from me could run them cold, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes, and the debrief — specifically the ordered sequence of questions that gets them from "my rule didn't fit my answers" to seeing why someone would say everything composes, or nothing does, or only living things do, plus where the epistemicist option lands (there IS a sharp cutoff, you just can't find it) and why it feels so bad at that table. Also tell me the two or three things I'll have to say myself because no activity can make them feel it.

And be straight with me about failure modes: what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run. I'm bracing for students gaming the audit, one group answering "exactly one" on all 30 or "exactly thirty" on all 30 just to make the sheet self-consistent, someone insisting the word "thing" is doing all the work and refusing to engage, the two sealed conditions finishing at wildly different times, and the kid who won't write an integer at all. Say how to handle each one in the moment without blowing the reveal.

Start with the activity plan itself — props, rules, timeline — with no preface, no summary of what you're about to give me, and no closing commentary after the failure-mode section.
