I sit on the governance-design team of a regional transit authority that is piloting something futarchic: an elected board would define and maintain a single measure of rider-weighted access, and conditional prediction markets would choose among five capital projects by comparing the board's metric conditional on each project against the metric conditional on building nothing. At the design review I raised a worry — once traders hold large positions in contracts written against one particular definition of the metric, the board loses its freedom to revise that definition, because the market has by then created an organized constituency for the current values, and "voting on values" stops being a live democratic choice rather than a ceremony. Our consultant waved this away with Robin Hanson's slogan for futarchy, that we should vote on values but bet on beliefs, as though the division of labor were itself the answer: values are the part the public decides, beliefs are only forecasts, so nothing has been taken from the electorate.

Can you take that slogan apart? I want the distinct precise theses it could be expressing laid out as a menu — paying attention to what "values" has to mean in each (terminal ends, or the operational proxy the board writes down), what "beliefs" has to mean (individual credences, a clearing price, or a payout an authority settles), and what the betting is supposed to accomplish that voting supposedly cannot. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, or whether it is false or simply unsupported. Then identify which reading the consultant actually needs in order to dismiss my objection, and whether any of them delivers it. Don't give me a balanced survey and leave the choice to me: commit to the reading you think best rescues the slogan, or to the conclusion that none does, and argue for it.
