Two years ago I relocated from Philadelphia to Cologne for work, and within a month my payslip began carrying a deduction I had never authorized. Because the registration form I completed at the citizens' office listed me as Roman Catholic — a baptism from infancy that had occupied roughly no part of my adult thinking — the Land was withholding church tax on the archdiocese's behalf, at nine percent of my income tax liability. Extracting myself required a formal declaration before a state office, a fee, and, as the clerk took some time to explain, the forfeiture of a Catholic funeral and of eligibility for employment in the diocese's own hospitals and schools. I moved from finding this apparatus absurd to finding it interesting, and I have now spent more hours on comparative church-state finance than is entirely respectable. The consequence is that I have been invited to present a paper at a workshop on law and religion at the American law school where I am currently a visiting researcher, and I need to prepare for the question period rather than for the paper.

The thesis is deliberately uncomfortable in an American room, and I would like you to treat it as something worth attacking rather than as something to be handled gently. Call it attributable establishment. A scheme of state-religion entanglement is objectionable on grounds of conscience — as distinct from grounds of fiscal fairness — only to the extent that it makes a citizen's standing depend on a religious affiliation that the citizen cannot herself vary. Measured against that criterion, I argue, the German settlement ranks ahead of the American one. Germany grants recognized religious bodies the status of corporations under public law, a right carried over from the Weimar constitution into the Basic Law, collects the church tax as a surcharge on income tax for a handling fee, and permits exit by formal declaration at a known and finite price. The United States funds religion pervasively but invisibly, through property-tax exemption, faith-based service contracting, legislative and military and prison chaplaincies, and voucher programs, and it gives the individual citizen no instrument whatever for varying her own exposure. My argument runs in three steps. First, the harm in establishment is not that money reaches religion but that the citizen cannot say which religion her money reaches, or reach not. Second, the German system is more transparent and more individually variable than the American, since a German taxpayer can unilaterally reduce her contribution to zero and knows precisely what that costs her in civil and ecclesial terms. Third, therefore the familiar American objection to the church tax on grounds of compelled support is self-undermining: it condemns a regime with a costly exit and acquits a regime with no exit at all.

Here is the room as I understand it. Eight or nine American First Amendment scholars, two of them senior, both of whom began publishing when the tripartite framework of the early seventies was still live and who have since written critically about the Court's turn to historical practice and tradition as the operative test; a comparative constitutionalist who works on Germany and knows the machinery far better than I do, including the Länder variation in the surcharge rate, in the authority before which the declaration of withdrawal must be made, and in the fees charged, and who will notice immediately if I have described any of it loosely; a specialist on French laïcité who will want to press the point that recognition is itself a hierarchy, and who can invoke the surviving concordat regime in Alsace-Moselle, where the state still pays the clergy of the recognized faiths and the 1905 separation law does not reach; and a church historian of the Church of England, who is the person I am most apprehensive about and whom I would much rather have you anticipate than discover in the room. There is no philosopher of religion present, and I do not want the objections a philosophy seminar would raise about the intrinsic value of non-identification or the difference between harm and wrong. I want theirs, in their idiom.

So: give me the ten or so questions this audience will actually put to me, in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them and not the way I would. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, a candid note on whether that reply is load-bearing or merely presentable, and an indication of where it is softest. Tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks so that the concessions do not have to be extracted under questioning, and tell me which one or two objections have enough force to sink the paper if I handle them badly. End with the question I will be hoping nobody asks, and the honest answer to it. I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or pointers to the literature — I have read what I can reach and citations will do me no good in the room; I want your own assessment of the argument and of the people who will attack it. Finally, please tell me how confident you are in your ranking of these objections and in the substantive claims you make about how each of these regimes actually operates, and single out the point you are least sure of, especially any place where you suspect you may be reconstructing a legal or institutional detail from a general sense of the shape of these systems rather than from anything you would stand behind if the Germanist in the room pushed on it.
