I am preparing a deliberately heretical paper on conditionals, and I want you to construct the strongest argument for its central claim. The claim is that English has only one conditional construction, governed by a single semantic clause, and that the familiar contrast between the indicative *if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did* and its subjunctive counterpart *if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have* is generated not by two distinct connectives but by a rule that shifts which information state supplies the facts held fixed in evaluating the antecedent: the indicative is assessed against the speaker's actual information state, the subjunctive against a state from which the antecedent's negation has been removed by supposition.

Please defend this thesis directly. Show that it reproduces the standard data — including the fact that assertability for indicatives appears to track conditional probability while counterfactuals resist any comparable probabilistic reading — without positing a semantic ambiguity in the conditional itself, and show why the rival two-connective account needs the extra machinery that your view dispenses with.

Then confront the embedding data usually taken to be fatal to deflationary accounts: your conditional embedded inside another conditional, under negation, under a probability operator, and as the antecedent of a question. Explain whether the information-state rule survives being embedded, or whether at some of these points it must be revised, and say which.

Finally, name what the view costs. Which entrenched intuitions about indicatives — regarding contraposition, modus ponens, or the acceptability of conditionals with antecedents known to be false — must it surrender, and where would a competent referee press hardest? I want the argument to be honest about its own weak points rather than triumphant about its strong ones.
