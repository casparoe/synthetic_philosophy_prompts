For the past fourteen months I have been serving on the governance working group of a research institute with roughly three hundred voting members, and our single substantive charge has been to recommend a rule for electing three at-large seats to the board of trustees. Our chair is a retired statistician who keeps a laminated copy of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on the table in front of him, and for a while I assumed the result would settle our deliberations in a sentence or two: with three or more alternatives, any rule that can actually reach every candidate and is not a dictatorship or an imposition can be pushed around by voters misreporting what they prefer, so strategy-proofness is simply unavailable and we should choose among imperfect options on other grounds. It has not settled anything. What it has done instead is scramble my sense of which particular behaviors are objectionable, because I keep finding that the theorem's blanket verdict does not line up with how I actually judge cases. So I would like to run the exercise in the other direction: I will report my verdicts, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here they are, in the order they came up. Under plurality, when a widely liked third candidate draws support away from a viable rival and the candidate almost everyone ranks last wins, I think the outcome is defective but that the people who put the third candidate first did nothing wrong at all; they reported exactly what they thought. When a bloc of about sixty members — call them the applied-program people — meets beforehand, agrees publicly to abandon their true favorite for the more viable candidate, and votes accordingly, I do not think they have done anything wrong either; I would call that ordinary politics and I would say so in the newsletter. But when that same bloc instead enrolls eighty colleagues who have no interest in the election, hands each of them a card saying how to mark the ballot, and many of those eighty then vote sincerely for what the card says, I think something genuinely bad has happened — and I cannot locate the badness in misreporting, since most of them are reporting honestly. In a twelve-person committee with cyclic preferences where the chair simply chooses the order of the pairwise contests and everyone votes sincerely in every one of them, I think the chair has done something much worse than the bloc in the previous case did, though again nobody has misrepresented a single preference. When a candidate under instant-runoff gains forty additional first-place rankings from people who would otherwise have put him second and loses the seat as a result, I find that worse than the coordinated bloc's behavior and I cannot name anyone who acted badly. Under range voting, if everyone awards their maximum to their favorite and zero to all the rest, I feel the outcome is degraded relative to what the same electorate would have produced with honest scores, yet I do not feel the scorers did anything wrong; and I would rather live with range voting plus a handful of honest holdouts than with plurality plus universal sincerity, which I realize is close to incoherent. A rule that draws one ballot at random and elects that person's first choice is, as far as I can tell, immune to every complaint above, and I find I cannot bring myself to recommend it — the objection that surfaces for me is not manipulability but something more like the absence of a collective judgment. Finally, when I picture our institute's preferences as genuinely single-peaked along the one axis that structures our politics, how much weight to give applied work versus basic work, nearly all of these anxieties dissolve, including my anxiety about the chair, which surprises me, because the chair in that world is still choosing the agenda.

The closest thing I have to a principle is this: an act of voting is objectionable when it makes the result less responsive to what everyone sincerely wants than the result would otherwise have been, and a rule is objectionable when it is not robust against such acts. But the chair case, the non-monotonicity case, and the random-ballot case all seem to break it, since in the first two everyone reports sincerely and I still think something has gone badly wrong, and in the third the rule is maximally robust and I still want to reject it. So I would like the following from you. Give me the principle, or the short list of competing candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a rule I have never encountered; say which case forced each clause of it into the formulation; tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them you think I ought to abandon rather than preserve at the cost of a baroque theory. Then apply whatever you land on to three situations I have not listed: a faction that fields two nearly identical nominees in order to split the opposition under plurality; a donor who funds a campaign that sincerely changes what members prefer before any ballot is cast; and quadratic voting, where the intensity a voter expresses can be purchased. I also want to know whether the principle you end up with is about manipulation in the theorem's sense at all, or whether I have been using one word for two distinct complaints, one about the conduct of voters and one about the design of rules, and if so which of my cases belong to which. You do not need to cite anything or point me toward the literature — I want the principle itself, argued for in your own terms. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. State your recommended principle in your opening sentences, before you work back through the cases, since our group intends to read the reply aloud at the spring meeting.
