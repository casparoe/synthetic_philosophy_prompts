**The Attention Budget: Why Care Ethics Needs a Principle Exactly Where It Claims Not To**

Care ethics makes a distinctive epistemological claim: moral knowledge starts in attention to a particular other, not in subsuming a case under a rule. On Noddings's account, caring is built out of engrossment — a receptive, non-selective attention to the one cared-for — plus motivational displacement, where the carer's own projects get reorganized around the needs she has taken in. Tronto's first phase of care, caring about, is likewise a matter of noticing that there is something to meet. The whole thing is pitched against principle-first ethics on the ground that rules screen off exactly the particularity that makes an act a caring one rather than a merely efficient one.

Here's the problem I want to press. Engrossment is a capacity with finite bandwith, and it doesn't divide without loss. You cannot be fully receptive to seven people at once. Anyone who has done care work knows this: the ward, the caseload, the person on the phone and the person in the doorway. So the carer faces a question the phenomenology of engrossment never raises, because engrossment is always described as already directed at someone: *whom do I attend to now?* Call this the attention budget.

The argument:

1. Care consists essentially in engrossment plus motivational displacement toward a particular other.
2. Engrossment is exclusive in its exercise: attending fully to A precludes attending fully to B at the same time.
3. Carers are always situated among more than one needy particular.
4. So the carer must allocate attention before, or at least independently of, engrossment — the allocation cannot be the output of an engrossment that hasn't happened yet.
5. Allocating attention across competing claimants requires a comparative judgment about whose needs are more pressing.
6. A comparative judgment that governs a class of cases over time is a principle.
7. Therefore care, at the point where it is scarce, is governed by principles — the thing it was supposed to have displaced.

Two escapes are available and I think both fail. First: allocation can itself be a way of caring, and Tronto's later phases (including the institutional and the collective one) are meant to handle distribution, so the principle shows up inside the practice rather than hovering above it. But that concedes my conclusion — if the practice has to be organized by distributive criteria, then care ethics at scale is a theory of just allocation with a phenomenology of attention bolted on. Second: attention can be serial rather than divided, so there is no comparison, just one encounter then the next. This relocates the problem instead of solving it. Deciding the order *is* the comparison, and deciding that the person in the doorway can wait is a judgment about her made in her absence, which is precisely what engrossment was never supposed to be.

Three things I'm not sure of, and I want to know which are fatal.

- Maybe this isn't a problem for care ethics specifically. A virtue ethicist with phronesis, or a Murdochian talking about just and loving attention, has the same budget, and neither of them claimed to abolish principles. If so I've made a general point about attention under scarcity and dressed it up as an internal critique, which is a stick rather than an argument.
- Step 6 may be doing all the work. A one-off ranking of these particulars, made in this situation, might be a perception and not a principle at all. I only get 6 if I assume that anything that ends up governing repeated allocations is thereby a rule, and that assumption is exactly what a care ethicist rejects.
- Step 4 may be too quick. Perhaps there is a standing background attunement — you notice the doorway because you are already diffusely receptive — so allocation is downstream of a generalized engrossment rather than prior to it. That would make 2 false as stated, since diffuse receptivity isn't exclusive even though focused engrossment is. If that's right the whole thing collapses, and I suspect it might be.
