I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on legal self-reference, and I've also been roped into drafting the governance charter for a small interdisciplinary institute at my university — about forty members, an elected council, one amendment clause. Here's my problem. The draft says the charter can be amended by a two-thirds vote, amendment clause included, plus a single entrenched provision (nobody gets expelled without a hearing) that only a unanimous vote can remove. A faculty member on the comittee — political theorist, very practical, allergic to my scruples — says I'm manufacturing a puzzle out of nothing, and his line is that this is just like a football league changing the offside rule mid-season: games change their own rules all the time, nobody thinks there's a paradox, and the new rule governs the next match. A logician on the same committee goes the other way and says it's pure bootstrapping: a rule that authorizes its own replacement is a rule hauling itself up by its own bootstraps, and the entrenched clause is worse still, since a unanimity requirement that gets satisfied unanimously has destroyed the very thing that authorized the change.

Can you audit both analogies for me? I want the mapping made explicit — what in the league/season/match picture stands in for the charter, the council, the two-thirds, the entrenchment, and "the next match," and likewise what plays what role in the bootstraps picture. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten the conclusion each analogy is being used to reach, and the ones that are surface noise I should stop losing sleep over. My hunch is that the football one quietly imports something about who's standing outside the game, and that the bootstrap one assumes the authorizing rule has to still exist at the moment it gets used — but I could be wrong about both, and I'd rather you tell me.

Two further things I want pulled into the same audit. First, I've been reading around parliamentary sovereignty and that distinction between a legislature that can bind its successors and one that cant — continuing vs self-embracing, I think? — and I can't tell whether it's even available to a forty-person institute, since there's arguably always somebody above us (the university, the law of the land). Does either analogy still work if we aren't sovereign at all, or does the nesting dissolve the puzzle entirely? Second: if we replace enough of the charter, including the clause that made us who we are, is it the same institute afterwards, or did one end and another begin — and do these analogies have anything to say about that, or do they just run out?

Bottom line I'm after: does the practical conclusion survive (we write the clause this way and get on with it), does the logician's worry survive, and if neither analogy is worth keeping, what's a better one? I need something I can say out loud at the next committee meeting, not a technical lecture.
