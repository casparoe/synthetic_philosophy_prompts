Here it is:

> Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle says that a rational agent's present confidence in a claim should match the confidence she expects to have in it later. If a year from now, once I've read the file and talked to the people involved, I expect to be 90 percent sure the intervention worked, then I should be 90 percent sure of it now. This is no mere recommendation; it can be proven. An agent who violates Reflection is vulnerable to a Dutch book — a package of bets she is bound to accept and bound to lose money on — so violating it is a flat-out failure of rationality. Notice what the principle amounts to. Your future self is an expert on the question you have only started to think about, and Reflection is just the demand that you defer to that expert now. Rationality requires you to treat your own future opinions as authoritative.
>
> That is impressive until you see it cannot be true as stated. Suppose I know that next week I will have been through a conversion experience, or dosed with something that leaves me certain the earth is flat. Reflection then instructs me to be certain of that today. Van Fraassen's way out is to restrict Reflection to cases where the future self is better informed than the present one. But the restriction is unprincipled, and worse, it empties the principle out: read that way, Reflection says only that you should trust your future self when your future self is trustworthy, which is not a discovery about probability but a tautology.
