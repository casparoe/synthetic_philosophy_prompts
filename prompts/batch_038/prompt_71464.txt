I'm in week three of a political philosophy seminar on legitimacy and my prof dropped a Pascal line on me in section and I still haven't recovered. I'd been arguing that an international criminal court can have genuine authority over a great power even though it has no way of enforcing its rulings against one, because the authority comes from the justice of the decision rather than from enforcement capacity. He said something like "that's a lovely thought," and then quoted the *Pensées* at me — the bit about "La justice sans la force est impuissante ; la force sans la justice est tyrannique" — plus the follow-on idea in the same fragment that since we couldn't make what is just strong, we made what is strong just, so that the just and the strong would be the same thing. Everybody nodded like the matter was closed and I sat there unable to object, mainly because I couldn't tell what claim he had actually made. I have to write a response paper on it due friday, so I'd rather not just concede the point.

Can you lay out the menu of precise claims that one sentence could be standing for? I want each reading spelled out with its subject and quantifiers fixed: whose justice (the court's? natural law's? whatever the legislator happens to say?), which force (military? police? the general capacity to make people comply? economic?), and what kind of connection is being asserted — causal, conceptual, normative, or historical. Sort them roughly into a descriptive claim about when people in fact comply; a claim about the conditions under which anyone has an obligation to obey; a genetic claim about where legal authority actually came from; a straight might-makes-right normative claim; something about what "justice" even means when applied to positive law; and the epistemic one Pascal seems to lean on in the same fragment, that justice is always disputable while force is obvious and easy to recognise. For each: is it a platitude nobody denies, a substantive but defensible thesis, or just false — and, the part I care about most, would it license dismissing my arguement? If a reading is true but irrelevant to what I was claiming, say so explicitly, because I suspect at least one of them is.

I'd also like some sense of which readings are plausibly Pascal's own and which are the slogan-as-shibboleth that people repeat without the text. The surrounding fragments have him mocking the idea that a river can mark the boundary of the just ("Plaisante justice qu'une rivière borne !") and saying truth on this side of the Pyrenees is error on the other, and complaining that nothing is just in itself by reason alone since everything shifts with time and custom — which doesn't sound like a man who thinks enforced custom *is* justice. So: is the line better read as a description of a political order that has lost contact with real justice, rather than as a claim that force creates justice? Does it matter that he's a Christian writing about a fallen world? Then give me two or three concrete cases that separate the readings — say, a strong state that voluntarily complies with an adverse ruling it could have ignored, or a law everyone agrees is unjust that is nevertheless reliably enforced and obeyed, or a just decision nobody has the power to implement — and tell me what each case shows and which readings it kills. Finally flag any pair of readings that turn out to be the same claim in different clothes, and any that are compatible so I don't accidentally attack a straw man.
