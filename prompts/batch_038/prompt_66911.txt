My housemate Tom and his girlfriend Alice had a row at dinner last night and roped me in as referee, which I now regret. It was about our old friend Joe.

Joe has been going on loudly and constantly about his brother's decision to sell the family house to a developer rather than split it with the rest of them — greed, betrayal, all of it. The catch is that Joe himself hasn't spoken to his own daughter in four years.

(1) Alice said Joe's criticism carries no weight at all: he's got no standing to lecture anyone about how you treat family when he's done far worse.
(2) Tom said that's a straight tu quoque. Whether Joe's brother acted greedily is one question; whether Joe is a hypocrite is another, and you can't settle the first by pointing at the second.
(3) Alice said she never disputed the truth of what Joe said — she was disputing his right to say it, and those come apart: a criticism can be correct and still not be his to make.
(4) Tom said fine, but then she owes an account of what makes a criticism yours to make, and the only candiate he can see is that the blamer's own fault has to match the one he's blaming — and sulking at your brother is nothing like abandoning your child.
(5) Alice said that actually proves her point, not his: the faults don't need to be identical, just close enough that the blamer has forfeited a right to be heard, which is exactly why 'you did it too' sometimes lands and sometimes doesn't.
(6) Tom said there's your problem — 'close enough' is now doing all the work, and on her view anyone can shut down any criticism by trawling the critic's life for a vaguely analogous blemish, so nobody ever has standing to blame anybody.
(7) Alice said so be it, maybe that's true, and the reason it sounds bleak is only that we've muddled blame up with moral judgement, which survives all of this.

Could you go through the exchange and tell me who's right at each step rather than just who wins overall? And I'd like a view on whether (7) is really available to her or whether it costs her the thing she spent six moves defending.

Please use these sections, in this order:
- "Where the row actually is" — about 100 words on what the two of them disagree about underneath the moves.
- "Move by move" — a rating for each of the seven (clearly wrong / partly right / clearly right) with two or three sentences of justification each, roughly 400 words total.
- "The hinge" — about 150 words on the single step where the argument turns, and what has to be true for it to go through.
- "Verdict and blind spots" — about 150 words: who's more right overall, and what neither of them thought of.
