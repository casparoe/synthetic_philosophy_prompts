I am a high school senior writing an extended essay in the philosophy of science on Imre Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. I have the standard vocabulary under control — hard core, protective belt, negative and positive heuristic, theoretically and empirically progressive problemshifts, degenerating programmes — and my faculty advisor has instructed me not to spend the essay re-defining those terms. What I cannot yet get straight are three puzzles that I suspect are one puzzle wearing three disguises. I would like a clear explanation of each, followed by an account of how they hang together.

First. Lakatos separates a theoretically progressive shift, in which a new version of a programme has excess empirical content over its predecessor, from an empirically progressive one, in which some of that excess is corroborated. These evidently can come apart. Please explain, using an example of your own invention rather than a famous historical episode (I want the logic, not the history), what a theoretically progressive shift that never gets corroborated looks like from the inside, and what a corroborated shift that involves no theoretical progress would look like. Then answer this: in that second case, why is the appraisal anything more than naive falsificationism with a waiting period attached? What is left of the difference between progress and mere survival?

Second, and this is the one I find hardest. Suppose programme A absorbs an anomaly by importing an auxiliary hypothesis from rival programme B, and the resulting prediction is confirmed. Whose progress is that? Does A score empirical progress, because its belt delivered a corroborated novel fact, or does the fact that the belt was patched with borrowed material show that A is degenerating while B, which actually generated the auxiliary, is progressive? Explain what in Lakatos's criteria could settle the question, and if nothing does, say so plainly. It seems to me that if a protective belt can be rented, then the belt is not what individuates a programme after all, and I cannot see what else in the framework would be doing that work.

Third. Lakatos concedes that a programme's status can only be appraised retrospectively, and that a young programme carrying a pile of anomalies may be rationally tenable because it might turn progressive later. Please explain what keeps this from being a licence to disregard counter-evidence indefinitely. In particular, I want to understand whether the concession governs what an individual scientist is permitted to believe, or only what a philosopher of science is permitted to pronounce — and what difference that makes to the researcher who has to decide, this year, where to spend the next ten.

Two requests about form. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or page references: I cannot verify anything you name, and my advisor has already supplied the texts. And where you think the standard Lakatosian answer is evasive or unstable, please say so directly rather than reconstructing the most charitable version of it and stopping there.
