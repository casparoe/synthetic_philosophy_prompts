I spent something like three hours last night arguing with an old friend of mine, who teaches history at a high school, about whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, and I came away genuinely unsure what I believe, so I would like your help sorting it out. Please approach this as a philosopher of history who is equally at home with the general metaphysics of causal explanation and with the disputes actual historians have about these matters, and think carefully before answering: I would rather see you sketch two or three candidate principles and reject one or two than have you commit to the first thing that occurs to you. His position is that every major outcome is structurally overdetermined — a defeated, hyperinflated, resentful Germany with a long antisemitic tradition was going to produce a revanchist dictatorship of one flavor or another, and whoever ends up in the seat does what the seat demands. He also thinks the whole counterfactual enterprise is unfalsifiable wish-fulfillment, along the lines of the objections Richard Evans presses, and he says I am simply re-litigating the intentionalist side of a quarrel he considers settled decades ago. My position is that he is right about the occurrence of the regime and wrong about its character. To pin this down we spent part of the evening building a small taxonomy, and that is what I most want you to attack: for any person P and event E, we ask separately whether P was necessary for E's occurrence at all; whether P affected only its timing; whether P fixed its form, meaning the specific character it had as opposed to the generic kind of thing it was; and whether P affected its aftermath, meaning the sequence of later events with E itself held fixed.

Here are my verdicts, which I think are fairly stable, and I want the principle that generates them. Hitler: an authoritarian revanchist Germany was not avoidable, but the war of annihilation in the East and the decision to murder European Jewry were — so occurrence no, form emphatically yes. Stalin: the industrialization drive and the collectivization would have happened under any Bolshevik successor, but the scale of the purges in 1937 and 1938 depended on him personally. Napoleon: the revolution's legal and administrative settlement was coming regardless; the Russian campaign and the Continental System were not. Temujin: he matters about as much as Hitler does, even though my friend assures me the steppe was every bit as ripe for unification as Germany was for dictatorship. Edison: does not matter at all, since Swan had a working lamp and a British patent within months; the same goes, I think, for Newton and Leibniz on the calculus, although I have a nagging sense that Leibniz's notation mattered not for the discovery but for the next two centuries of mathematics, which my taxonomy has no slot for. Szilard: the bomb was overdetermined once fission was public knowledge, but I keep wanting to say that the idea he reportedly had while waiting at a traffic light in Bloomsbury in September 1933 changed the timing — a weapon in 1945 rather than 1947 — without changing the occurrence. Bill Gates: does not matter; someone would have written the interpreters and licensed the operating system.

The pair I cannot reconcile is Hitler and Stalin, and I would like you to concentrate your fire there. Under my own taxonomy they come out with the same four answers — occurrence no, timing marginal, form yes, aftermath yes — and yet my gut treats them very differently: I would say the Holocaust had no counterfactual substitute, whereas I would happily grant that a terror of roughly that magnitude did, on the grounds that any paranoid successor purges rivals. My friend says that is special pleading, that I have decided in advance which regime I find more inexplicable and then read a metaphysics off that reaction, and I have no answer for him. So: state the principle, or the short list of candidate principles, that best captures the verdicts above — precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh case without further instruction — and say for each clause which of my cases forced you to include it. Then tell me what it delivers for two cases I have not raised; I have been mulling over Mao and the Cultural Revolution, and whether the networked web needed Berners-Lee, but choose your own. Finally, and most importantly, tell me honestly whether any principle simple enough to be worth having can accommodate both the Hitler and the Stalin verdicts, or whether one of them has to go. If you think the real problem is that occurrence and form are the wrong axes altogether, and that what distinguishes the cases is something like the probability of E absent P, the number of available substitutes, or the degree to which the machinery of the state was already pointed at the outcome, then say so and rebuild the taxonomy from that instead of patching mine. I am writing this up for a reading group run out of a British university, so please compose your answer in British English.
