I have just been made responsible for the anti-corruption annex of a maternal-health procurement programme that a European funder runs through district health offices here, and two analogies are being used against each other in our working group. I need to know which one can bear weight before I draft the clause.

Our district medical officer argues that refusing a gift is an insult and that the whole question is being misdescribed. A basket of produce, an envelope at a wedding, a cousin hired as a night guard — these, he says, are like bringing wine to a dinner: a gesture that marks a relationship, not a transaction that prices one. On that picture the funder's rulebook is imposing a market model where no market exists, and the charge of cultural imperialism is supposed to follow: what counts as corrupt is set by whose norms get treated as the default. The funder's head of integrity answers with a different image. Every gift is a down payment. Money is fungible, obligation accrues silently, and when the ambulance tender comes up the relationship amortises itself. On her picture the wine analogy is a euphemism, and the annex should treat any transfer above a nominal threshold as a prohibited payment regardless of stated intent.

Please audit both analogies rather than splitting the difference. Set out the correspondences explicitly — what plays the wine, the host, the occasion, the obligation to reciprocate; what plays the down payment, the loan, the interest, the collateral — and then say where each mapping breaks. I want the disanalogies sorted into those that actually matter for writing a rule and those that are merely rhetorical, with attention to the temporal structure of the exchange, whether the official has genuine discretion over what is being asked for, whether the gift buys something the giver was already entitled to, who ultimately bears the cost, and whether the parties would want the exchange publicised. Say plainly which features of each source domain the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks, and whether either can carry the conclusion it is being used for: that the practice is benign, or that it must be prohibited by threshold alone.

Then commit. Tell me which analogy you think survives contact with these details, what your reasons are, and — if neither does — what third analogy describes such transfers better than wine or down payment. I would rather have a definite recommendation about how to draft the clause, with the argument for it, than a balanced menu of considerations left for me to weigh.
