I'm doing a masters module on democratic innovation and I've spent the last fortnight going round in circles on sortition — Fishkin's three goods (statistical representativeness, deliberation, non-dominance), Pitkin on descriptive representation and the 'mirror' idea, Landemore's cognitive-diversity argument, plus the obvious Condorcet jury theorem gloss that people put on randomly selected bodies. My flatmate is convinced the Irish Citizens' Assembly basically settles it in favour of sortition; I'm not. So I want the episode analysed properly against the theory rather than either of us waving at it. Here are the facts I've assembled, and please use them rather than trusting your recall of the details: the Constitutional Convention of 2012–14 was a chair nominated by government, 33 politicians chosen by the parties and 66 randomly selected citizens, sitting for about fifteen months, and its marriage-equality recommendation went on to a successful referendum. The Citizens' Assembly set up in 2016 deliberately dropped the politicians — 99 randomly selected citizens plus a chair who was a sitting Supreme Court judge, members picked by a polling firm off the electoral register (an Act was passed to allow the register to be used), stratified by gender, age, location and social class, expenses only. Its remit came from the government programme and a Dáil resolution, and included not just the Eighth Amendment but fixed-term parliaments and how referendums are held.

Now the awkward bits. Of the original 99, seventeen withdrew before the first working meeting and were immediately replaced; another eleven withdrew before the final abortion meeting and their replacements didn't take part until the assembly moved to its next topic; and in January 2018 seven replacement members had to be removed when it emerged they'd been recruited through the acquaintances of an employee of the polling firm rather than randomly. On abortion the assembly worked from November 2016 to April 2017 with expert and stakeholder presentations, and in the final ballot, of 87 valid votes, 64% backed termination without restriction as to reasons — and within that 64%, the twelve-week limit only carried 48%, so twelve weeks was a plurality of a subgroup, not a majority of the body. The report then went to a 21-member Oireachtas committee which re-ran hearings from September to December 2017, kept twelve weeks but dropped socio-economic grounds after that, and the referendum in May 2018 carried at 66.4% on 64.1% turnout. Pro-repeal campaigners said at the time that the government had cynically created the assembly to dodge a decision it didn't want to own.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, in both directions. First: who are the players under the theory, what counts as the design and what counts as the output — is the output the assembly's recommendation, the committee's report, or the referendum result, and does it matter which one you pick? Second: run each theoretical claim against the facts. Descriptive representation supposedly needs random selection from the whole eligible population and no self-selection — does the body survive seventeen plus eleven withdrawals with replacements, and the non-random replacements scandal, or does the theory need an extra condition about attrition being uncorrelated with views? Non-dominance was secured by excluding politicians, but the chair was a judge, the secretariat was civil service, the agenda was set by government and the expert lists were curated — where does dominance come back in? And on the epistemic side: small-table deliberation over five weekends is designed to destroy the independence Condorcet needs, so what's actually carrying the weight — diversity-trumps-ability, or just the descriptive claim? Third: tell me which features the theory explains well, which it misdescribes, and where it's simply silent rather than making me read silence as support. I especially want the Convention/Assembly contrast pressed: the mixed body with 33 career politicians in it produced an output that got adopted, and the pure sortition body produced an output that had to be re-legitimised by 21 politicians and then by a national campaign the members had no standing in. Does that show mini-publics work by substituting for elected politicians, or only ever by lubricating them — and does the theory have any resources to answer the cynicism charge, or has it been qualified into immunity? Finally, land it: is the Irish sequence a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case sortition theory never anticipated, and which single prediction would a theorist writing in 2016 have got most confidently wrong? Also, what does it say about a randomly selected body being handed the fixed-term-parliaments question — i.e. citizens deliberating on the tenure of the people whose job they're supposedly not doing. No reading lists or citations please, I can't chase them down anyway; just flag anywhere you're arguement-sure about a fact I haven't given you.
