The chapter is meant to run about twenty-five minutes as a talk at a small philosophy-of-mind workshop in the spring, and my two designated commenters could not be less alike: one is a cognitive neuroscientist who does sleep work and has told me, politely, that she finds conceptual analysis of dream reports "quaint," and the other is a Wittgensteinian who I expect will say that my central proposal is itself a piece of the very confusion I am trying to diagnose. **Draft: What the Sleeping Brain Contributes**

*1. The two positions.* Malcolm's *Dreaming* (first published 1959, corrected in 1962) opens with an argument that the ordinary first-person claim to be asleep, made in the present tense, cannot express a genuine judgment, since there is no evidence on the basis of which one could arrive at it: any attempt to supply one either lands us in wakefulness or produces something that is not a claim at all. He then turns the same machinery on dreaming. If the only thing that could make it the case that someone dreamt is the impression he has on waking, then the idea of a dream as an occurrence during sleep — an occurrence logically independent of that impression — has no clear sense; and if that idea has no clear sense, so too do the ideas that a dream has a determinate location and a determinate duration in physical time. Malcolm was aware of the sleep-laboratory work of his day and discussed it, including the studies of Dement and Kleitman, and his reply was in essence that physiological correlations do not supply criteria for what the dream was. [?]

Dennett's 1976 paper in the *Philosophical Review* takes the challenge more seriously than most of the subsequent literature did. Rather than dismissing Malcolm, he asks what would actually have to be surrendered if the default picture — dreams as experiences occurring during sleep and recalled afterward — were mistaken. His answer, as I read it, is that the load-bearing commitment is not that something happens during sleep but that whatever happens has a determinate content, in a determinate order, at determinate times, prior to and independently of its being reported. Give that up and the question of when the dream happened loses its grip rather than acquiring a surprising answer. In later work with Kinsbourne he generalizes the point into a contrast between two models of content-fixing, one on which the brain retrospectively edits a record and one on which there was never a single record to edit, only multiple simultaneous drafts; and the dream that is cut short by the very noise that seems to have caused it becomes his stock illustration.

*2. A shared assumption.* My claim is that both men are playing on the same board, and that the board is the problem. Each assumes that a dream report stands to whatever happened during sleep in a correspondence relation: the report either matches an antecedent episode or it does not. Malcolm notices that matching can never be checked and withdraws the episode; Dennett notices that if matching cannot be settled in advance then the episode has no determinate content until it is reported, and relocates content-fixing. But matching is only one mode in which a report can be answerable to a process. A weather vane is answerable to the wind without copying it. An improvisation is answerable to a score without being a transcription of it. A caricature is answerable to a face precisely by refusing to match it. So I propose replacing the single question — does the report match an episode? — with two questions: what is the report answerable to, and in what mode? I want to treat copy, translation, elaboration, and invention as points on a continuum rather than as an exhaustive dichotomy, and to allow that a single report may sit at different points on that continuum for different parts of its content. [?]

The second and more speculative claim is that even the answerability question is misdirected if we assume, without argument, that whatever constrains the report constrains its imagery. Dream reports ordinarily deliver two things at once, and the debate has treated them as a single deliverable: first, imagistic and narrative content; second, the felt pastness and ownership attaching to that content — the sense that this happened, to me, just now, while I was asleep. I shall call the second the "pastness tag." My hypothesis is that the pastness tag is what the sleeping brain reliably contributes, and that the imagery may be very largely composed at the moment of reporting without the report's thereby being a confabulation in any interesting sense. Call this the "retrospective-seeming hypothesis."

*3. A design.* Take three groups. Group A sleeps normally. Group B is woken and prompted after a delay. Group C is kept awake all night and prompted at the same clock times, matched for fatigue and suggestibility as far as that is possible. Within each group, half the subjects receive a neutral prompt on waking and half receive a leading prompt that names a concrete object — say, a lighthouse — before they are asked what they were just experiencing. Measure two things separately: the rate at which the named object appears in the ensuing report, and a cluster of temporal-ownership markers, namely confidence that the episode was experienced rather than imagined, spontaneous use of the past tense and of first-person ownership, and the pattern of response when the subject is told, falsely, that the report was invented for him.

The three positions predict different patterns. On Malcolm's account there is no relevant difference between A and C: in both cases the claim that the subject dreamt is fixed by the waking impression, so the leading prompt should be equally effective in both, and the only differences will be physiological. On Dennett's account the leading prompt should be less effective in A than in C, because in A there is a process to answer to and it resists. On the retrospective-seeming hypothesis, suggestibility with respect to imagery is equal across A and C — the sleeping brain is not contributing imagery — but the temporal-ownership markers diverge sharply, with A showing higher felt pastness, more ownership, and a characteristic refusal to revise under challenge. That last prediction is what separates my view from Dennett's, and I think it is falsifiable. [?]

*4. Payoff, and a worry.* If it came out that way, the answer to the question whether dreams are experiences would be yes, but not where the debate was looking. There is a real and datable episode — the seeming-at-waking — and its date is the date of the report, which vindicates Malcolm's insistence that we have been misplacing the phenomenon; and the first-order question remains unsettled in principle, which vindicates Dennett's insistence that the default picture's demand for determinate antecedent content is the commitment to surrender. The worry I cannot yet answer is whether the pastness tag is a distinction with an empirical handle at all, or whether my confidence and ownership measures are just an elaborate proxy for demand characteristics — in which case group C will contaminate group A and the whole design collapses. A second worry, which I expect from my Wittgensteinian commenter, is that talk of modes of answerability simply redescribes the matching relation in vaguer language and buys nothing but a feeling of having escaped a dilemma.
