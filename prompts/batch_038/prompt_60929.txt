I've been in a reading group on cooperation between AI systems for a few months now and I've drifted into a position I want stress-tested. The position: program equilibrium basically dissolves the puzzle of cooperation in the one-shot prisoner's dilemma. The setup as I understand it — stop me if I've garbled it — is that instead of picking a strategy, each player hands in a program, and each program is run with the opponent's source code as its input and outputs an action. Once you allow that, mutual cooperation stops looking like a miracle. There's the code-comparison bot that cooperates iff the opponent's source is literally identical to its own and defects otherwise, which is an equilibrium because any deviator just gets defected on and so can't do better than mutual defection. There's the proof-searching bot that cooperates if it can prove the opponent will cooperate against it, where Lob's theorem is what makes two copies of it actually bottom out in cooperation instead of an infinite regress. And there's the simulation-based one that cooperates with small probability epsilon to guarantee halting and otherwise runs the opponent's program on itself. Then the part I find most striking is the folk theorem: the payoff pairs achievable in program equilibrium are exactly the feasible and individually rational ones — the same characterization you get from the repeated-game folk theorems. So my view is that the standard gloom about defection being the only rational outcome in a one-shot PD is a artifact of artificially restricting the strategy space, and that once agents are mutually transparent about their own decision procedures the old result just doesn't get a grip.

What would count against this? I want the whole map of exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, not a list of generic worries. Some candidates I've half-thought of, feel free to tell me they're weak: does the folk theorem buy its punch by blowing up the action space until anything goes, so that you'd get the same characterization from some much dumber device (a correlating signal, a commitment mechanism, just letting each player submit a contingent plan) — and if so has program equilibrium collapsed into a relabeling of Nash equilibrium in a bigger game rather than a new solution concept? The equilibrium definition quantifies over every possible deviating program, which smells like unbounded compute for searching, proving, and simulating; if you cap proof length or runtime, do the cooperative equilibria survive, or does mutual defection crawl back? The epsilon-randomization trick to avoid nontermination: does that quietly change the game, and is a profile that only halts in expectation still an equilibrium in the sense that matters? The brittleness objection — a single extra whitespace character and the code-comparison bots both defect — defenders say the proof-based and simulation-based versions fix this, but is robustness actually achieved or just relocated, and is there any argument that you can't have robustness together with cooperating against a wide class of opponents rather than near-copies? Also: does the framework predict that agents cooperate mainly with agents they can verify are like them, and would that parochialism count against using it as a general theory of cooperation? And what would an experiment that could actually bear on it look like — say handing two systems each other's source and watching — versus a failure that gets absorbed as "you just submitted the wrong program"? Is there any behavior profile the view rules out, and if it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, is that a problem here given it's a theorem-backed concept rather than an empirical theory?

Two things about how you answer. Tell me which of these objections people who actually work in this area would themselves concede as counting against them versus wave off, since I suspect the ranking looks different from the inside than it does from my reading group. And please keep what's proved separate from what's genuinely contested — e.g. I can tell there's disagreement about whether the folk theorem is the interesting result or whether the robustness work is, and about how much of the cooperation story survives once you take computational limits seriously. Don't dress up a contested reading as the consensus view, and flag the places where you're reconstructing an argument rather than reporting a settled result. Personally I'd be most moved by anything showing that cooperation here rests on idealizations I can't buy for real deployed agents, because that's what I actually care about.
