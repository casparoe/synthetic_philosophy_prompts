Ok so, background: I've read the sequences and basically every rationalist blog since the Overcoming Bias days, but my day job is private equity. Operating partner, "value creation" at portfolio companies, which in practice means I live inside EBITDA bridges, headcount plans, multiple expansion, WACC, IRR/MOIC, sale-leasebacks, and the recurring argument with management about whether a plant is a cost line or a moat. That's my native vocabulary and I'm fluent in it, not dabbler-fluent — I build the models myself.

Here's the ask. Take Marx on exploitation and alienation — the actual claims, not the bumper-sticker version — and restate them in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report.

Things I specifically want you to try translating:

- The surplus value argument. My working read, which I suspect is the standard econ-flavored lay read, is that Marx is claiming workers get paid less than the value they add — less than a competitive market would clear at — and that if you just paid people their marginal product the complaint evaporates. Tell me plainly whether that's right, because I have a nagging feeling it isn't. If it isn't, restate what the claim actually is in my terms and see whether it survives. My whole framework assumes that in equilibrium each input gets its marginal contribution and the residual goes to whoever holds the residual claim, so I want to know whether that assumption is what's doing the work in my dismissal of him.
- The labor theory of value underneath it. My memory is that the analytic Marxists — Cohen? Roemer? — more or less conceded the LTV isn't load-bearing for the moral complaint, and that Roemer rebuilt exploitation out of unequal ownership of productive assets plus some kind of exit test, where a coalition counts as exploited if it would do better walking away with its per-capita share of the alienable assets, and that he could generate exploitation in toy models with no labor market at all. If that's roughly right, translate *that* version into my vocabulary. My hunch is it maps onto my world much better than Marx's own account does, and I want to know why, and what the mapping costs.
- Alienation, all four-ish aspects from the early manuscripts: the product, the activity itself, species-being, other people. Be concrete about which of these have any counterpart at all in a language whose only nouns for human work are headcount, retention, engagement score, key-man risk, and culture.

Then the part I actually care about. Sort everything into (a) translates cleanly, (b) translates only if you bend something, (c) cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. For each (b) name the bend — I'm especially suspicious that "value" gets quietly swapped between embodied-labor and willingness-to-pay-at-exit somewhere in the middle, which would make the whole dispute look resolvable when it isn't. For each (c), take a side: is my framework missing a real fact about the world, or was Marx gesturing at something that dissolves once you try to state it precisely? And run it backwards once too — is there anything in my vocabulary that Marx has no term for but arguably should have? I can imagine the risk-bearing and deferred-consumption story being that thing, but I don't trust my own judgment here.

One failure mode to avoid: translating by smuggling my framework's premises in and calling it translation. Rendering exploitation as "paying below market" would assume away the thing under dispute. I'd rather you tell me a claim is inexpressible than launder it into something I already agree with. Also seperate out, as best you can, which parts of the residue are Marx's fault and which are mine. Don't be diplomatic — "your industry literally cannot represent this fact" and "this is just confused Hegelianism" are both useful answers; a both-sides shrug is not.
