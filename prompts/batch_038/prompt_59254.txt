I chair the scoring subcommittee of a mid-size forecasting tournament (~4,000 registered forecasters, run out of a research group, questions resolved by an independent team that doesn't talk to us about substance), and we have a fight to settle before the season opens in three weeks. This year's question set has a lot of logically interlocking questions and people have started submitting numbers that can't all be right. Concrete cases in front of us right now: (a) Q12 asks whether the annual mean anomaly for 2031 will be above 1.5C on our baseline and Q13 asks the same for 2.0C — Q13 can only happen if Q12 does, and one forecaster has 0.58 and 0.71 respectively; (b) Q31 asks whether the treaty gets ratified by at least 40 signatories by 31 Dec, Q32 whether it gets ratified by at least 40 signatories *including* a specific country, and someone has 0.44 on Q31 and 0.52 on Q32; (c) Q7 and Q19 turned out, once the resolution team wrote up their criteria, to be the same question in different words, and we have submissions scattered between 0.2 and 0.8 across the two. Half the committee wants a coherence rule that automatically flags any submission set violating the obvious probability constraints, then either bounces it back for revision or docks the forecaster's score. Their argument is the betting one: with numbers like those, someone could build you a package of wagers at your own stated prices that loses money come what may, so you're strictly worse off than a forecaster who bothered to fix their numbers, and we shouldn't be handing out points for that. The other half says this is unfair — nobody's beliefs come pre-packaged with all their consequences visible, a person working through 300 questions over a weekend can't be expected to have mapped which entails which, and if we police this we end up punishing the people who think hard about the hard questions instead of the people who are just wrong.

I lean toward the second group but I dont have a clean position, mostly because the duplicate case (Q7/Q19) feels different to me from the 1.5C/2.0C case and I can't articulate why, which is annoying since that one is coming up no matter what we decide. Two other complications. Our one computer scientist on the committee says the pairwise checks are trivial but that screening a whole assignment over 300 interlocking questions for joint consistency is a nasty combinatorial problem — she keeps saying "intractable" and won't commit to a number of pairs we could realistically screen with the two part-time scorers we have. And some of the alleged entailments aren't strict: one pair only entails if you accept a particular definition of "recession" that the resolution team may or may not use when they write the criteria, and I genuinely don't know whether a rule should reach out and touch those or leave them alone.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the full committee before Thursday's meeting? I need a real bottom line up front — adopt the coherence rule, reject it, or adopt a narrowed version — plus an operational procedure the scoring team could actually run: what gets checked, any tolerance band, what happens to a flagged submission before resolution, and what happens when a violation is only discovered after the questions have already resolved and points have been paid out. Engage the betting argument on its merits rather than swatting it, and give me the strongest case for whichever side you end up rejecting, because I'll get asked. I'd also like an explicit line on the duplicate-question case and on the not-strictly-entailing case, and if your recommendation turns on some distinction between those and the clean nested ones, say so plainly so I can defend it in the room. About 700–900 words is right.
