Last month I sat in on a small two-day workshop about how autonomous decision systems belonging to different organizations should interact — mostly people from AI governance and a couple of economists. I'm a program officer at a funder, so I'm not a game theorist, I was there because we might end up funding some of this work. On the second afternoon the conversation landed on safe Pareto improvements: roughly, the idea that two agents who would otherwise end up in some mutually bad non-cooperative outcome (a capability race, a bidding war, a shared-resource collapse) can each commit in advance, before they know the details of the game they'll actually play, to act as if they shared some combined utility function — with the "safety" being that each of them expects, by its own beliefs, to do no worse than it expected to do in the default. The running example was two labs that have each delegated a chunk of their scheduling and compute-allocation decisions to an optimizer, where the default looks like a race. Two of the participants, who I'll call Marta and Devin, went back and forth for a while and I genuinely couldnt tell who was winning. I've written their moves as close to how I remember them as I can, but treat all the wording as mine rather than theirs.

1. Marta opened by saying the word "safe" is doing something sneaky: safety is defined relative to each agent's own beliefs about the default, so it's bookkeeping rather than a guarantee, and the whole scheme is only as trustworthy as those baseline beliefs are.
2. Devin said that's too weak to be an objection — every expected-utility claim is relative to someone's probabilities, and nobody says buying insurance is "unsafe" because the judgment used your own credences. The point of committing before you learn the details is that neither side can tailor the deal to private information, so the ex ante comparison is precisely the right one to make.
3. Marta's reply was that the baseline isn't private information, it's a posture. An agent that credibly makes its default behavior awful — a genuine racer, a burn-it-down optimizer — gets a better deal inside the compromise, since the compromise is scored against that awful default. So SPIs don't prevent precommitting to be a monster; the safety constraint ratifies it and pays it a premium.
4. Devin gave two answers. First, the commitment happens behind a kind of veil: you don't yet know which game you'll be in or what role you'll have, so making your default monstrous is a global disposition that costs you in every interaction where you're the exposed party, and you're choosing before you know. Second, even if someone inflates their baseline, the other party can condition on it — refuse to compute the deal against a default estimated from anything other than publicly observable behavior.
5. Marta said the veil move only works if your utility function is also up for grabs, which it isn't: you're not afraid of being the other lab, you're only afraid of the games you happen to lose. And the conditioning move just relocates the dispute, because now the two sides disagree about what counts as a reasonable default — which is bargaining again. Which is her real complaint: SPI doesn't eliminate bargaining, it hides it. You still have to pick the combination rule — sum of utilities, equal proportional gains, something Nash-flavored — and that choice is the entire distributional fight, moved somewhere nobody has to argue about it out loud.
6. Devin conceded part of that but said it overreaches. If you pick a rule stated purely in terms of each agent's own gains relative to its own default — say each gets the same fraction of its maximum available gain — then the rule is unaffected by rescaling either agent's utility function, so you don't need interpersonal comparisons at all, and there's a principled reason to prefer it to "maximize the sum," which does require them. Once the rule is principled, the residual disagreement is small enough to just split.
7. Marta said the scale-invariance is a trick of presentation: the arbitrariness hasn't gone away, it's just moved into "why equal fractions?", and any monotone rule can be dressed up as principled after the fact. In real negotiations the party with the most at stake refuses any rule that doesn't favor it, and no amount of invariance changes that. Devin started to answer and then we went to dinner.
