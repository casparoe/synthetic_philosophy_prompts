I am in my final year of secondary school and I run our school's philosophy society, which this term is holding a formal debate on the resolution that moral judgments are not beliefs. I drew the affirmative, so I have to defend expressivism against a classmate who is a sharp cognitivist and has already told me that he intends to attack the compositionality of my semantics rather than rehearse the usual complaints about booing and hurrahing. To prepare, I would like to watch a genuine expert handle a hard case, so please play a character I will call Professor Vance: a senior metaethicist, thirty years in the field, a committed plan-expressivist who regards the embedding problem as a technical difficulty with a technical solution rather than a refutation. Speak in the first person and stay in character until I write "step out." You may concede details and you may revise the view in front of me, but you never concede the core thesis that moral sentences do not express beliefs and are not truth-apt in any robust sense. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, since this is meant to feel like a conversation in your office rather than a paper; do not cite or name books. If any clause of the view below strikes you as ambiguous, resolve it in whichever way best serves your position and tell me that you have done so.

Here is the version of the view I have reconstructed for us, partly from Gibbard's planning semantics and partly from the higher-order-attitude treatment of negation that I associate with Schroeder. Call it compositional plan-expressivism, with four clauses. (A) Base clause: "Lying is wrong" expresses a plan-like, non-cognitive state — being against lying, in the sense of planning to refrain from lying oneself and to discourage it in others, where planning is a state whose function is to settle a practical question rather than to represent how things are. (B) Compositionality: for every well-formed complex built out of such sentences, the semantics assigns a definite state, fixed by the logical form and by the states assigned to the parts, whether or not the complex is ever asserted. (C) Negation: "Lying is not wrong" does not express the mere absence of being against lying; it expresses a higher-order state, roughly being in favour of leaving lying unregulated by one's plans, and the two are inconsistent not because their contents contradict but because a planner cannot at once settle the question of lying and be in favour of its remaining unsettled. (D) Conditionals: "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" expresses the higher-order state of being against the combination of being against lying with not being against recruiting your brother; an argument is valid when accepting the premises and rejecting the conclusion lands the agent in a combination of states that the consistency requirement in clause (C) rules out, which is how modus ponens goes through here without any moral truth conditions.

My opening move, Professor. Clause (B) owes you a state for the sentence "Either lying is wrong or it rained in this town last night," and I do not think you can supply one. The recipe in clause (D) always yields being against, or being in favour of, some combination of plan-like states, but the second disjunct expresses an ordinary belief about the weather, and no plan of mine can settle whether it rained: being against the combination of not being against lying with not believing that it rained is not a practical stance at all, not something anyone could occupy by deciding anything, and it has no obvious way of being inconsistent with whatever state you assign to the negation of the whole disjunction. Nor is the case idle, since "It did not rain in this town last night; either lying is wrong or it rained in this town last night; therefore lying is wrong" is a valid inference, and your account of validity is supposed to be a general account of what makes inferences valid. Let me head off one escape in advance: this is not merely a problem for a speaker who does not already know the weather, because clause (B) is a claim about sentence types and not about the doxastic situation of whoever accepts them; and if your answer is instead that the higher-order state may take as its object a description of any states whatsoever, plans and beliefs alike, combined by negation and disjunction and assessed for consistency, then I want to know what is left of your denial of representational content, since that logic of state-descriptions looks exactly like the propositional content you said you were avoiding. So tell me which of the four clauses you give up. I want a committed answer, not a survey of the ways an expressivist might respond: take one line, argue for it in your own voice, and if you judge that one clause must be revised to save the others, say plainly which one and what the theory loses by the revision.
