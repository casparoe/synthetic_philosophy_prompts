I co-teach a first-year seminar on the logic of scientific inference, and every autumn the same thing happens: a cluster of students arrives having read a short online summary of the raven paradox and treats the matter as closed — a green apple confirms that all ravens are black, so there is nothing paradoxical, next topic. What I want instead is an instrument that sorts the seminar by what its members would actually say about cases, so that before we spend three weeks on the literature I can see which resolution each of them is already committed to. Please write a decision tree: ten diagnostic questions, each with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. The starting point for every student is the shared setup — a universal hypothesis about ravens, its contrapositive, and the claim that an observation satisfying either formulation confirms the hypothesis.

On the questions: none of them may name a technical condition or a school of thought. No question may ask whether the reader accepts the equivalence condition, or Nicod's criterion, or Bayesian confirmation theory, or severe testing; each must instead present a concrete scenario and ask for a verdict or a choice, phrased so that a student who has never encountered the resulting position can answer it and thereby be sorted. Please build the following scenarios into the set: an object drawn at random from a barrel of a million items of which two hundred are ravens, which turns out to be a pebble; a white shoe found in a room the reader has already searched for ravens and found none; an albino raven; a chemistry hypothesis of my own devising — say, that every substance containing lithium colors a flame crimson — tested by holding a lump of quartz known to contain no lithium into a colorless flame that stays colorless; a case in which the objects shown to the reader are selected by an assistant who is trying to refute the hypothesis and deliberately hands over non-ravens; a case in which the reader sets out from the beginning to test the contrapositive formulation rather than the raven formulation; and one control question using a contrived predicate of the grue type, included specifically to detect students who are running the raven paradox together with the new riddle of induction. The questions should jointly separate, at minimum, these positions: accepting the conclusion and blaming the appearance of paradox on background knowledge we tacitly carry along; accepting the conclusion while insisting the evidential weight is real but minute; holding that the weight of the pebble is not fixed until it is conjoined with everything else the inquirer already knows, so that the question does not have an answer relative to the observation alone; keeping confirmation by instances but refusing to let a hypothesis inherit confirmation from observations that satisfy only its contrapositive; restricting instance confirmation to certain projectible predicates or natural kinds; holding that treating confirmation as a positive evidential relation is the wrong enterprise altogether, since what matters is whether a hypothesis has survived a demanding test; and the critical line on which the quantitative solution smuggles in assumptions about frequencies or about how the object came to be observed that no real inquirer has, so that the paradox is relocated rather than dissolved.

On the tree and the key: build it from the logical space before building it from the roster of positions — lay out the questions and the space of answer combinations they generate, then locate the actual views within that space. Where a combination is unoccupied, say so and diagnose why: incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves your instrument cannot reliably separate, flag them explicitly and say what further question would be needed; I expect the first two leaves above to be hard to pull apart, and I suspect the third may collapse into the first under pressure, so tell me whether you agree rather than papering over it. Give a leaf a proper name only where you are confident both of the attribution and of the view's content; otherwise describe it functionally and mark it unnamed instead of guessing at who held it. Please also keep the handout honest about the state of the field: separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this problem from what remains contested, do not present a minority resolution as though it were the standard one, and note anywhere that the design of the instrument itself reflects a contested reading of the paradox rather than a neutral one. American English, and no bibliography or list of further reading — the students will be given the primary texts separately, and I do not want anything in the handout that I cannot vouch for.
