I'm in the third week of a seminar on the political economy of famine, and the thing that has the whole group arguing is not the case material but an analogy. Our guest lecturer, who spent a decade running field operations for a large relief organisation, told us that a famine is a natural disaster and should be handled the way an earthquake is handled: you assess needs, you move food, you stay out of politics, and you leave the attribution of blame to historians afterwards. He said this quite deliberately, as a professional credo, and he used it to license at least three claims — that the famine is nobody's fault in any operational sense, that the correct remedy is to restore the aggregate supply of food to the affected area, and that humanitarian action must be blame-free in order to be effective at all. Our tutor pushed back in the following session with a rival analogy of her own: a famine is not an earthquake but a crime, and treating it as a disaster is a category error, because a crime has perpetrators, victims, and a scene that can be reconstructed, whereas a disaster has only casualties. I have been asked to write the seminar paper on this exchange, and I have a third analogy in my own draft that I am not sure is any better — that a famine is closest to a bank run, where the fear of shortage produces the shortage, where the people who die are those who were already outside the queue, and where the remedy is not a shipment of grain but a credible commitment that changes what everyone expects.

The cases we have read are not neutral between these pictures, and I want the audit to take them seriously rather than treating the analogies as free-floating. In the Bengal famine of 1943, the standard supply-side story is complicated: there was a cyclone, tidal flooding, and a rice crop disease in the preceding season, and their weight relative to everything else is still disputed, but there was also wartime inflation that destroyed the real wages of labourers paid in cash rather than in kind, the denial measures that stripped coastal districts of boats and rice stocks against an expected Japanese landing, the loss of Burmese imports, a Foodstuffs Scheme that privileged the army, war industries, and civil servants, price controls whose collapse produced hoarding and speculation, inter-provincial trade barriers, and a refusal to divert shipping. In Ireland in the late 1840s the trigger really was exogenous — a plant disease — yet food kept leaving the country and the relief works paid wages below what food then cost. In the Great Leap Forward the harvest fell, but the procurement quotas were set against reported yields that local cadres had an overwhelming incentive to inflate, criticism of the policy got the critic purged rather than the policy corrected, and grain continued to be exported through the worst years. Sen's entitlement analysis, which our reading was built around, is supposed to explain why the people who starved in each case were identifiable social groups — landless labourers, tenants, pastoralists, anyone whose claim on food ran through a wage or through the market rather than through their own harvest — and why the aggregate food supply figures often do not show what the mortality figures do.

So: audit the three analogies against each other and against those cases. For each one, give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the shock, the damaged structure, the affected population, the rescuers, the thing to be restored, and the assumption of exogeneity — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusions being drawn from the merely picturesque ones. I suspect, for instance, that "a famine is slow and an earthquake is fast" matters much less than "a famine selects its victims by social position while an earthquake selects them by geography," but I would like that argued rather than asserted. Tell me what each analogy quietly imports that the target lacks: whether the disaster picture smuggles in the idea that the harm is a shortage of stuff rather than a failure in who is entitled to it, whether the crime picture smuggles in an intentionality that most famine deaths do not have, and whether my bank-run picture smuggles in a rational, self-interested actor who can be reassured by a credible signal, when the people who starve are precisely those for whom no signal will restore a claim. Then say which of the lecturer's three conclusions survive the disanalogies and which do not, whether any of the analogies is right for a restricted purpose — the first seventy-two hours of a response, say — even if it is wrong as a justification, and what a better analogy would be if there is one. If you think the honest answer is that no single analogy carries the argument and that the lecturer and my tutor are simply making different claims about different questions, I would rather hear that defended than see a table of correspondences built to flatter one side.
