I write about science and its history for a general-interest magazine, and last month I sat in on a workshop exchange I was unable to referee. Two historians argued about whether non-human things — a pathogen, an eruption, a shift in rainfall — can legitimately be called historical actors, or whether they are at most causes. Call them A and B. I want the exchange adjudicated before I attempt to describe it in print.

A opened by saying that agency requires behaviour that can be read as responsive to reasons; since *Yersinia pestis* has no reasons, calling it an actor is a category mistake dressed up as boldness, and there is nothing deficient about being a cause.

B replied that this standard is too strong for actual historical practice, since historians routinely treat chartered companies, treasuries, grain markets and anonymous crowds as actors without supposing that any of them has a single mind. If unified intention is the price of admission, most of social history is left with no agents at all.

A answered that those cases differ in kind because their agency is derived: it is borrowed from the people who compose them and can in principle be traced back to particular human decisions. A bacterium's contribution is borrowed from nobody.

B accepted the distinction but said it was doing covert work, since derived agency is granted generously wherever it flatters human centrality and withheld elsewhere. B then offered a criterion of their own, which they called interpretive uptake: a non-human thing functions as a historical actor when contemporaries had to interpret it and build institutions around that interpretation. On this view the quarantine rules, the lazarettos and the penitential processions that plague produced are precisely the uptake that makes the pathogen an actor, in the only sense that can explain institutional change.

A said this collapses. On B's criterion anything people misread becomes an actor — a comet, miasma, a wrathful God — so agency turns out to be a property of beliefs about things rather than of things. That may be an interesting thesis about meaning, A conceded, but it is not a thesis about causation, and B had been sliding between the two throughout.

B replied that A was defending the wrong side of the ledger, and added that the environmental factor supplies parameters, and parameters underdetermine outcomes: the same eruption produces famine in one polity and new forms of poor relief or market organisation in another, which shows the shock is not determinative in any strong sense.

A's last move was that this concedes too much. If outcomes are settled by institutions, the eruption explains only the timing, and B has written a book about institutions with a natural disaster in the title; whereas if the eruption does settle outcomes, B has reinstated the environmental determinism the field spent fifty years discrediting. Either way, A said, B owes an account of what explanatory work is left over.

Please adjudicate move by move rather than in the round. For each of the seven moves — A's opening, B's reply about aggregates, A's derived-agency counter, B's interpretive-uptake criterion, A's collapse objection, B's parameters point, A's closing dilemma — give a rating, a justification, and the strongest reply available to the other side that the move failed to pre-empt. Then tell me who was more right overall and about which question, and identify anything neither of them reached for; I suspect there is a third position in this space that the exchange crowded out.

Return a JSON object with exactly these fields: `moves`, an array of seven objects each with `move` (1–7), `speaker` ("A" or "B"), `claim` (one sentence stating what the move asserts), `rating` (one of "clearly mistaken", "mostly mistaken", "mixed", "mostly right", "clearly right"), `justification` (two or three sentences), `strongest_unmade_reply` (the best counter available to the other side that this move does not already answer); `overall_verdict`, three to five sentences; `both_missed`, the distinction or position neither articulated; and `what_would_change_my_mind`, saying which move, defended differently, would flip your rating and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail. Write all prose content in British English.
