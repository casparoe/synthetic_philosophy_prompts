Right, bit of an odd request but bear with me. I write a column on science and ideas for a UK magazine — mostly physics and AI these days — but every August I end up filing something about Hiroshima, and this year I want to do it as an invented interview rather than another thinkpiece about body counts. The person I want to talk to is Elizabeth Anscombe, in Oxford in 1956, a few days before the university votes on whether to give Harry Truman an honorary degree: the fight she really did make against it. I know she died decades ago, that's rather the point of the exercise — I want her arguments in her own register, not my summary of them, because my summaries always come out sounding like a columnist who's read the Wikipedia page.

So: play Anscombe, first person, and stay in it until I type "stop". Ground rules. You're a Catholic philosopher and a Wittgenstein pupil, ferocious in argument and not especially fussed about being liked, but you are not a crank or a pacifist caricature — you think wars can sometimes be fought, you just think there are things a man may never choose to do in order to win one. You may draw on anything you'd have had by 1956: Aquinas, the just war tradition, what you were working through on intention and practical reasoning. Do not put anything in quotation marks unless you are certain it is verbatim — paraphrase your own views instead, and if I ask about something you couldn't know, ask me back the way she would. Concede the side points freely: you'll happily grant that an invasion of Japan would have been appalling, that the casualty arithmetic might well have come out in Truman's favour, that plenty of Allied bombing before August 1945 was worse than people now admit. What you never concede is the main thing — that a calculation about consequences can make it permissible to choose the killing of the innocent as your means. Two or three paragraphs a turn, British English throughout (spellings, idiom, the lot), and don't narrate your feelings at me or step out of frame to explain what you're doing rhetorically.

My opening, then: Professor Anscombe, you say the line between murder and tragic necessity runs through the intention, not the outcome. But that distinction looks like it's doing all the work and none of the checking. Truman can describe himself exactly as you'd want a virtuous man described — he intended to end the war, the deaths in those cities were something he foresaw and accepted with regret, not something he aimed at. And if you answer that the whole point of the weapon was to horrify a population, then you're making a claim about Japanese morale and about what the bomb causally did, which is the same empirical, consequentialist reckoning you say has no bussiness deciding these questions. So which is it — do I get to fix the description of my own intention by my sincerity, or does the world fix it by what my action was actually for? And if the second, haven't you smuggled the consequences back in through the back door?
