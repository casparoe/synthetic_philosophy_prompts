I'm the only analyst at a six-person food-crisis advocacy outfit. Maybe twice a month somebody forwards me a deteriorating situation — a regional price spike, a bad harvest report, mortality numbers trickling out of a clinic network — and asks the same two things: is this heading toward famine, and are we allowed to call it man-made? Right now I answer on gut feel and it isn't defensible. Our comms lead isnt an economist and she's the one who actually files, so whatever you give me has to be runnable by her in about an hour, using only what we realistically get: market and wage price series, migration reports, one or two nutrition surveys, government procurement or relief announcements, and how much press the situation is getting.

Build the procedure on the entitlement approach — Sen's basic point that people starve when they lose command over food, not only when food stops existing — but aim it at a gap the severity classifications leave open. The technical phase thresholds tell you how bad things are; they say nothing about why, and why is the part our wording has to survive. Our counsel's rule is that we can only print man-made if we can name a decision by an identifiable actor that made things worse than they'd otherwise have been. So the procedure has to hold three levels apart that our bulletins currently smear together: the trigger (blight, drought, war), the transmission mechanism (who lost access and through what — wages, prices, land, movement restrictions), and the response failure (what authorities did or refused to do afterwards). Responsibility gets located at each level on its own terms, and the procedure has to be able to return entitlement-driven-but-nobody-blameworthy and availability-driven-but-still-a-political-crime as two distinct outcomes rather than collapsing them.

Then run it on three cases: Bengal 1943; Ireland 1845-49, where I want the poor-law rule barring workhouse relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre treated as a real step and not a footnote; and the Great Leap Forward famine. For at least one, give me the answer I dont want — I expect that's Bengal, since the procedure should have to concede that historians still argue there was a genuine aggregate shortfall that year, not merely a distributional one.

Mark which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, say what it can't settle at all, and say where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because the question is values or legal exposure rather than fact. It also needs a branch for can't-tell-yet — the cases with almost no mortality data — including what we're permitted to publish in that state. No reading list, no citations.
