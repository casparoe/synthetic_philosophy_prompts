I manage a platform engineering group, and I wrote a short written exercise on project estimation for our internal delivery track. Twenty-two engineers sat it; I need to grade all of them consistently, and since my background is software rather than decision theory or philosophy, I want an independent read on one answer before I fix my scoring key. I have already scored this one myself, but I am deliberately not telling you how, because I want your assessment to be uncontaminated by mine. Please treat the exercise as self-contained: candidates were not permitted to consult outside sources, and I am not interested in whether they cite the right people or papers. Grade the reasoning.

---

**Handout given to candidates**

Our group is about to migrate the remaining 30 internal services from the legacy billing pipeline to the new one. The following facts were supplied:

- Over the past five years this company has completed 14 migrations of broadly comparable shape. Elapsed calendar time, in months: 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13, 18, 21.
- Five of those 14 depended on a third-party vendor's API at some point in the migration. Those five took 10, 11, 13, 18 and 21 months. The other nine took 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 7 and 8 months.
- The upcoming migration has no third-party dependency.
- The team lead has produced a bottom-up estimate of **4 months**, built by decomposing the work into 60-odd tasks, sizing each from the team's own knowledge of the codebase, and summing. He describes the plan as containing no padding.
- Nine of the 14 historical migrations ran over whatever their own bottom-up estimate had been at the equivalent stage; none of the 14 finished meaningfully early relative to its plan.

**The question**

(a) *[30 points]* State what the inside view and the outside view each recommend as an estimate for this migration, and say how the two should be combined. Give a number and defend it.

(b) *[30 points]* The team lead objects as follows: "Every one of those fourteen projects was hit by something nobody saw coming. My bottom-up plan already accounts for the specific work in front of us, so the historical numbers simply don't apply here." Evaluate the objection.

(c) *[40 points]* A colleague proposes that, in order to avoid overconfidence, the team should publish a 90% interval that is "as wide as we can honestly make it," on the grounds that a forecast is modest to the extent that it leaves room to be wrong. Evaluate the proposal.

---

**Candidate answer, reproduced in full**

(a) The inside view is what the team lead did: take this particular migration, break it into its actual tasks, size each one from what we know about this codebase, and add them up. It yields 4 months. The outside view ignores the specifics and treats this migration as one more member of a class of past migrations, then asks what the class did: the fourteen ran from 3 to 21 months with a mean of about seven. The outside view is the one to start from, because the inside view is where optimism lives. A task decomposition only ever contains the work you thought of; it never contains the waiting on another team, the coordination overhead, or the thing that blocks you for three weeks and appears nowhere in the breakdown. The handout's own numbers make the point: nine of fourteen overran their plan and not one came in early, so the direction of the error is not in doubt even where its size is. I would narrow the reference class to the nine migrations that, like ours, had no vendor dependency, since those are the ones that genuinely resemble this job; they run from 3 to 8 months, which makes the lead's 4 months optimistic but not absurd. Combining the two: anchor on the outside view, treat the bottom-up figure as the no-surprises floor rather than as an estimate, and adjust only mildly. I would give **6 months**.

(b) The lead has the reasoning backwards, though his conclusion is closer than he thinks. The historical figures are not a set of unusually unlucky projects against which ours can be judged exceptional; they are the record of what happens when each project is hit by something nobody saw coming. The surprises are already inside the base rate — that is what makes it a base rate rather than a plan. Since the distribution of past outcomes already contains every variety of surprise that past projects encountered, and since we cannot know in advance which surprise will be ours, the bottom-up decomposition carries no information that the base rate does not already contain. The estimate should therefore be the base rate itself, the median of the class, which is what I gave in (a), and the decomposition should be set aside. The 18- and 21-month projects look like outliers, but both were vendor-dependent, and in any case regression to the mean tells us that after runs that extreme, later projects come back toward the middle; we should not inflate our estimate to hedge against a repeat of them.

(c) The colleague is right, and I would push further. Overconfidence is the dominant failure mode in this kind of estimation, so the way to be modest is to make sure the published interval is wide enough that it really will contain the outcome. A 90% interval that misses is worse than no interval at all, because it is a false assurance that other people will build commitments on top of; if you are in doubt, widen. The test of a modest interval is whether the truth falls inside it, and the sharper an interval is, the more modest you would have to be to defend it, so sharpness and modesty trade off directly. Modesty also has a behavioural side: do not commit publicly to the point estimate. Publish 6 months, plan the roadmap against 12, and decline to be pinned to a date. That, in practice, is what epistemic modesty amounts to here.

**Grading bands** (I use these across the whole batch): A = 85–100, B = 70–84, C = 55–69, D = 40–54, F = below 40. The overall score out of 100 is the sum of the three part scores.

---

**Deliverable**

Return a single JSON object, no prose outside it, with exactly these fields:

- `overall_score`: integer, 0–100; must equal the sum of `points_earned` across `part_scores`.
- `grade`: string, one of "A", "B", "C", "D", "F", per the bands above.
- `one_line_assessment`: string, at most 25 words, stating the central strength and the central weakness.
- `part_scores`: array of three objects, one per part, each with `part` (string, "(a)", "(b)" or "(c)"), `points_available` (integer), `points_earned` (number), `justification` (string, at most 60 words).
- `findings`: array of objects, one per distinct claim or inferential move in the answer that bears on the grade — expect somewhere between eight and fourteen. Each object has: `id` (string, e.g. "A1", "B2", "C3"); `part` (string); `student_claim` (string, at most 25 words, your paraphrase, not a quotation); `assessment` (string, one of "sound", "partly sound", "unsound", "unsupported leap", "correct but incomplete"); `why` (string, at most 60 words); `severity` (string, one of "none", "minor", "moderate", "major"); `conspicuousness` (string, one of "obvious on first read" or "easy to overlook"); `comment_to_student` (string, second person, at most 40 words, written so I can paste it into the feedback sheet).
- `internal_consistency`: array of objects, each with `first_id`, `second_id` (both referring to `findings` ids) and `tension` (string, at most 50 words, describing how the two claims sit with each other). Include an entry for every place where a later part of the answer relies on something an earlier part denied, glossed over, or used differently; use an empty array only if there is genuinely nothing.
- `statistical_handling`: array of objects, each with `quantity` (string, e.g. "mean of the fourteen", "median of the nine"), `what_the_answer_says` (string), `what_the_handout_supports` (string), `consequence_for_the_estimate` (string, at most 40 words). Cover every summary statistic the answer invokes, and note whether the answer keeps the reference class and the statistic consistently paired across the three parts.
- `credit_deserved`: array of strings, at most 40 words each: things the answer gets right, or partly right, that a grader in a hurry might fail to award.
- `grade_inflators`: array of strings, at most 40 words each: features of the answer that make it read better than it argues — fluency, confident tone, a well-chosen example carrying an unsupported inference, and so on.
- `uncertain_calls`: array of objects, each with `id` (a `findings` id) and `why_uncertain` (string, at most 40 words): any finding where you are not confident of your assessment on the material given.
- `what_would_move_the_band`: string, at most 80 words: the specific changes that would raise this answer one band, and the ones that would drop it one band.
- `reusable_rubric`: array of five to seven objects, each with `criterion` (string), `weight` (integer, summing to 100), and `what_full_credit_looks_like` (string, at most 40 words). This is for grading the other twenty-one answers consistently, so the criteria should be general to the three parts of the question rather than tailored to this one response.

Two constraints on the assessment. First, do not penalise the answer for omitting references, terminology, or names it was not asked for and had no access to; the question asks for reasoning about the supplied figures and the supplied proposals, and that is the standard. Second, where a claim is defensible on the handout as far as the handout goes, say so in `why` rather than treating it as an error because a more elaborate treatment exists.
