So last night I got into a three-hour arguement with my friend Priya that we never actually settled, and now I've decided to turn it into an exam question, which I know is a bad way to write an exam, but here we are. I'm TA-ing a philosophy of medicine seminar that's roughly half philosophy majors and half public-health people, and I'm writing the take-home final. They've done the usual three weeks — Boorse's biostatistical account, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction thing, then a normativist week with Nordenfelt and some Canguilhem — so the one thing I absolutely cannot have is papers that line those three up, note that each has a problem, and conclude that a hybrid view is probably best. I want a question where knowing the labels buys you nothing.

Here's the case I want it built around (I invented the biomarker on purpose so nobody can answer from clinical knowledge). A national screening program measures a marker, call it T, in asymptomatic adults at age 40. T isn't hooked up to anything the person can feel: nobody with high T feels ill, and there's no syndrome attached to it. Right now the lab flags a result as abnormal if it falls outside the middle 95% of the reference population, where the reference population is just whoever happened to get tested in the same region and seemed fine. A committee is proposing to move the flag somewhere else entirely: to the level of T above which a cheap, well-tolerated intervention measurably reduces later cardiovascular events — which turns out to sit well inside the current "normal" range, so a lot of people would flip from normal to flagged overnight. Two side fights came up that I want folded in. One, the distribution of T is lower in women, and a committee member argued that setting a separate female range would launder a widespread mild deficiency into normality — the female average is depressed by diet and blood loss, so calling it typical isn't a value-free statistical fact. Two, Priya insisted the whole thing is just about where to aim an expensive intervention, that thresholds are policy knobs turned by cost-effectiveness, and that "is high T a disease" has a settled answer that has nothing to do with any of it: either the part is performing below species-typical efficiency or it isn't. I said if that's right then disease status can't change when someone invents a drug, but the flag obviously does change, and she said I was conflating a metaphysical question with a decision rule and then wouldn't say anything more specific, which is precisely the move I want the students to have to make — or explicitly refuse to make — on their own.

Could you write the whole thing for me? What I need: (1) the question itself, in real final-exam wording, with the case compressed into a paragraph the students can work from, sized for about two hours and roughly 1200 words of answer; (2) a model answer, about 800 words, that actually argues a line rather than surveying positions; (3) a marking scheme out of 25 organized around five must-hit moves with the marks for each, plus a one-line note per move on how students typically fumble it; and (4) three or four sketches of the recycled-summary papers that should land at a C, saying what each does instead of answering. The marks should go to whoever can say what the committee's decision does and doesn't commit it to about what disease is — including whether a treatability threshold makes disease depend on which drugs happen to exist, and whether the sex-differentiated range problem is really an objection to statistical accounts of disease or to something narrower than that. No bibliography or reading list in the model answer; if it wants to gesture at a theorist, just dropping the name is fine.
