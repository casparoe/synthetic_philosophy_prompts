Two things landed on me in the same week and I can't stop mashing them together. I'm in a seminar on moral responsibility where we've been reading on standing to blame, and separately my brother got offered a promotion to a job where he'd be the one signing off on other people's expense reports — and I happen to know he's been padding his own for years, because he learned how from me. We did it together at a job I had six or seven years back. I stopped, and I've been quietly annoyed at myself about it ever since, but I never gave any money back or told anyone. He hasn't stopped. So I want to say something to him, just the two of us, before he takes the job. Trying to figure out whether I'm actually entitled to, I wrote down what I believe about blame in general, and now I can't tell if the list is coherent.

Here it is. (1) Whether blame is correct depends only on the target — they did it, they knew, they're responsible. Nothing about me enters into whether the blame is apt. So "you're one to talk" is never an answer to an accusation, it's just a change of subject. (2) Relatedly, I don't think standing to blame is a real property. Nobody loses the right to blame anybody. There can be pragmatic reasons to keep quiet — you'll be less persuasive, you'll poison the relationship — but that's all there is. (3) That said, I think hypocritical blame is genuinely bad, in some cases worse than blaming someone who didn't do the thing at all, because when I invoke a rule against you that I don't accept for myself I'm not treating you as my equal, and that's a wrong to you rather than just a flaw in my character. (4) But I don't think the cure is confession; someone who announces their own identical screwups in order to earn the right to criticize you is doing a piece of moral theater and that's its own little wrong. What matters is whether you really hold yourself to the standard, and doing that silently costs you nothing — a person who is privately appalled at their own conduct hasn't forfeited anything. (5) I also think busybodies do wrong: someone spotlessly honest who keeps tabs on strangers' private conduct and comments on it accurately is doing something objectionable, so truth plus faultlessness isn't enough. (6) What licenses blame for me is relationship, not virtue — family, close friends, people you're in a shared project with. Strangers don't get a say no matter how good they are. (7) And the verdicts on my own case: I think I should talk to my brother, privately; I think my mother would be wrong to bring it up at Christmas even though she doesnt know about my part in it and is scrupulously honest herself; and, the one I keep circling, I think if my brother turns it back on me and says "you taught me and you never gave the money back," he'd be doing something wrong — not just being ineffective, wrong.

Is this a package that hangs together? Some of it is obviously pulling against some other part but I honestly cant tell whcih tensions are real and which ones just look bad from the outside, and I'd rather not be told everything is equally broken. So treat it as an audit, not an essay: which pairs genuinely conflict, ranked by severity; which pairs only look like they conflict and why; and for each real one, the cheapest revision and what I lose by making it. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. And please be specific about (7) — is there anything in (1) through (6) that can actually deliver the verdict that my brother's turning-it-back-on-me is wrong, or that my mother's bringing it up is wrong? I have a feeling I'm smuggling something in, I just don't know what.
