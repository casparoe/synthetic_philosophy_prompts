A colleague of mine who does neuroengineering and a friend who teaches philosophy of mind argued after a department talk, and I was the only witness. Neither conceded anything, and they've asked me to get an outside verdict. Here is the exchange as closely as I can reproduce it.

(1) My colleague opened with Libet. The readiness potential begins roughly 550 milliseconds before a self-initiated wrist flexion, while subjects report the conscious urge to move only about 200 milliseconds beforehand. Neural preparation is therefore already underway when the subject first feels she decides, so the conscious decision cannot be what initiates the movement; it is a late report of a process that has already run. Free will as conscious initiation is empirically refuted.

(2) My friend said this over-reads a trivial task on two counts. Libet himself did not draw that conclusion: he proposed that consciousness retains a veto, and later imaging work identifies a patch of dorsal fronto-median cortex that is more active when people prepare a movement and then deliberately cancel it. And the reported time of the urge is not a measurement but a retrospective judgment made while watching a rotating clock, where attending to the clock distorts both the judgment and the movement.

(3) My colleague replied that the veto is no refuge: a veto is itself an action, so it will have its own unconscious preparation, and relocating freedom to an earlier neural event does not make that event free. The clock objection, he added, cuts against her — an unreliable report of the urge is likelier to be too late than too early, which pushes consciousness further downstream.

(4) My friend answered that the live alternative is the stochastic accumulator model, on which the readiness potential reflects noisy premotor activity drifting toward a threshold, making the early ramp largely an artifact of averaging backward from movement onset; there is no discrete decision at 550 milliseconds for consciousness to have missed. The strongest version of his evidence — decoding which button a subject will press up to ten seconds before she reports deciding — succeeds only modestly better than chance, which shows a bias, not a settled outcome.

(5) His last move: on that model, whether she moves at a given moment is fixed by which fluctuations happen to cross threshold, so what is left of authorship if her reasons merely tilt the odds? Her answer: that is all authorship could ever be, since weighing reasons just is evidence accumulation, and a freedom requiring an uncaused conscious spark would be randomness rather than control.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five steps from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating, say which of the two is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them left out.
