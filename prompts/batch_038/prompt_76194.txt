Question as set: "Explain the combination problem for constitutive panpsychism. Distinguish at least two distinct forms the problem can take, and assess one proposed response to it."

Student's answer (very lightly compressed, otherwise their wording):

Panpsychism says mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous: the basic entities posited by physics have, over and above their structural/dispositional properties, intrinsic phenomenal properties. Constitutive panpsychism adds that the consciousness of organisms like us is constituted by — built out of, not merely caused by — the consciousness of those micro-entities. The combination problem asks how that constitution is supposed to work. James pressed the objection against the mind-stuff theorists of his day: a hundred separate feelings, however tightly heaped together, do not thereby become one new single feeling, becuase each is still owned by its own subject and private to it; so the panpsychist owes an account of how micro-experiences fuse into the unified consciousness of a person. Goff usefully splits this into two versions. The quality combination problem: how do micro-qualities — a tiny bit of reddish feel, say — sum into the rich unified character of my experience of a tomato? The subject combination problem: how can several distinct subjects literally add up to one further subject? In the neuroscience of consciousness the same puzzle goes by the name of the binding problem, so panpsychists at least have company.

Two points about scope. First, the problem bites only this constitutive variety. On panprotopsychism the micro-level carries protophenomenal rather than phenomenal properties, which collectively give rise to experience; nothing is being asked to merge with anything, since there are no micro-subjects in the picture, so the objection loses its grip. Second, if one goes the other way to cosmopsychism — the universe as a whole is the one fundamental subject, and our minds are aspects or modifications of it — combination is simply reversed: the cosmic subject never had to be assembled out of parts, so the objection evaporates.

As for a response, Goff's phenomenal bonding does the work. Micro-experiences stand in a special relation such that, when they are bonded, there exists a further subject whose experience is their sum. Since the bonding relation is itself taken as a fundamental feature of reality, this answers the objection without abandoning constitutive panpsychism. And in any case the combination problem is only an explanatory gap — we just can't see how the constitution is meant to go — which puts panpsychism in exactly the same boat as physicalism, which also can't show how matter comes to feel like anything. So it gives no reason to prefer one view over the other and isn't a distinctively damaging charge.
