I'm about two hundred pages into a family novel — one rust-belt town, five generations, and I've been recycling two first names down the line the way García Márquez recycles José Arcadio and Aureliano. My workshop says that's just Solitude cosplay, and honestly they might be right, so before I decide whether to keep the trick I want to know what the original is actually doing with it.

Can you read One Hundred Years of Solitude through the argument about whether generations are real kinds? Bring the usual toolkit to bear — Mannheim's generational location and his idea of fresh contact, Ortega y Gasset's overlapping generations on roughly a fifteen-year rhythm, Strauss and Howe's tidy twenty-year brackets cycling through four turnings, the demographers' complaint that you cant seperate cohort effects from period and age effects, plus my own pet name for the disease: the horoscope problem, treating a birth-year bracket as if it had hidden causal structure like a star sign.

What I'm after:

- Does Macondo have cohorts at all, or only kinship generations? Almost nobody in that book shares a birth year with anybody else in a way that seems to matter. Point me at the places where you do get a real cohort — the banana plantation workers, the colonel's officers, whoever — versus the places where "generation" just means a rung on a family tree, and say whether the novel knows the difference or slides between them on purpose.
- What's the metaphysics of the name-recycling? Is the book asserting that the Aurelianos and the José Arcadios really do come out with different temperaments, i.e. cashing in a generational essence — or staging that belief as a family superstition and letting us watch it fail? And whose head does the belief live in, Úrsula's or the narrator's? That's the crux for me, because in my draft the pattern is asserted by a grandmother and contradicted by the plot.
- Same question for the pig's tail: the family believes in a hereditary mark and, spoilers aside, it shows up. Does that make the book an essentialist about lineages, or is it just mocking essentialism with a straight face?
- Melquíades' parchments — if the whole line is pre-written, is "generation" here a real kind for a totally non-sociological reason? Does the fatalism rescue the reading or wreck it?
- And tell me where I'm imposing. Where does the novel simply refuse to be about cohorts, where is the solitude-and-incest reading the only one that fits, and what does the book understand about generations that the philosophy papers dont? I'd really like one stealable thing for chapter fourteen.

No reading list or citations, please — no library card, no time, and I only want your read on the book itself.
