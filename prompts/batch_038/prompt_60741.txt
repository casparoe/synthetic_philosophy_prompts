Below are my considered verdicts about persistence through time. I would like to know what principle they add up to, or, if no single principle can carry them all, what small set of candidate principles comes closest. Please state each candidate precisely enough that I could apply it to cases I have not thought of, identify which of my verdicts motivates each clause, and say plainly which verdicts it cannot accommodate.

1. A candle that was straight in the morning and bent by the evening is one and the same candle, and it was literally straight. I resist the idea that this is really a claim about one of its temporal parts having a shape the whole lacks.
2. The ship whose planks are replaced one at a time is the original ship, and the ship later reassembled from the discarded planks is also the original ship. I will not rank these two verdicts, though I know they cannot both be literally true.
3. When Tibbles the cat loses her tail, the tailless remainder just is Tibbles, and it is the whole cat. I deny that a cat-minus-its-tail was already sitting where Tibbles was before the accident, made of exactly the same matter and differing from Tibbles only in what it could survive.
4. Yet a statue shaped from a lump of clay is not that lump. The lump could survive being pressed into a ball; the statue could not. That difference in survival conditions seems to me to put two things in one place.
5. In fission, where both hemispheres are successfully transplanted and both resulting people carry on my memories and character, neither is identical to me; nevertheless I was not thereby destroyed, and how many of me there are afterwards strikes me as a shallow question.
6. My five-year-old self is gone in a way the matter that composed him is not, and I take that to be a worldly fact rather than a fact about my concepts; but when I say that I learned to read, the subject of that sentence is me, not some vanished predecessor.
7. It is vague in reality, and not merely in our talk, exactly when a scattered heap of planks first constitutes a ship.

Verdicts 3 and 4 look to me like the same argument run in opposite directions, and I cannot hold both without arbitrariness. Give me the principle, or the competing candidates; apply each to two cases I have not raised, such as a brain gradually replaced by synthetic neurons and two ships that swap one plank per day with each other; and tell me which of my verdicts must be given up to buy the simplest coherent account.
