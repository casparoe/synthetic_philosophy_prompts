I have no academic training in philosophy or physics. I left school early, and what I know of this subject I have taught myself over the last fifteen years, mostly by reading and, more usefully, by arguing. For eight months I have been exchanging long emails with three friends in a small correspondence group of our own, and we are deadlocked on a question about chaos, determinism, and predictability. We have recently begun to suspect that a good part of the deadlock is that we are not actually stating the same claims when we say we disagree. I would like you to write the document we should have produced at the beginning: the joint statement of a proper adversarial collaboration between the two sides.

Please work in the role of a professional philosopher of science who is comfortable with the technical content of dynamical systems (sensitive dependence, Lyapunov exponents, hyperbolicity, the behaviour of numerical error in long integrations) and who has real experience drafting such joint statements between parties who each believe the other is being either trivial or confused. Think carefully before drafting. Begin with a clearly marked brainstorming section that is not part of the signed document: list, for each camp, the concessions it could make without surrendering its core claim, and the formulations it would refuse to sign as slanted, empty, or a caricature. Only then write the document.

Here is the dispute, stated as evenly as I can manage. Where I have stated something badly, correct it openly rather than quietly rewriting me.

Camp A, whom I will call the Exactists, hold that chaos is a property of equations and not of anyone's knowledge. A deterministic chaotic system has one and only one future for any exact initial condition, and an intelligence given those initial conditions exactly, together with the exact laws, would predict that future indefinitely far, chaotic or not. Sensitive dependence means that our errors multiply, so our forecasts decay; that is a fact about the crudeness of our instruments and our arithmetic, and nothing else. They are happy to concede that no human being and no actual computer can supply exact initial data. They deny that anything about determinism follows from that concession. Several of them also appeal to the classical shadowing results for hyperbolic systems as evidence that even a round-off-riddled numerical integration tracks something real rather than nothing at all. They accuse Camp B of sliding from "no physical agent can do this" to "this cannot be done," and of smuggling in a verificationist criterion disguised as a discovery about the world.

Camp B, whom I will call the Finitists, hold that predictability in principle means something only if some possible agent could in principle carry it out. An initial condition specified to infinitely many digits is not a physical possibility: storing and transmitting it would require more information than any bounded physical region can hold, and any measuring device is itself part of the deterministic world it measures, so exact data about its surroundings cannot be handed to it from outside. On their view the ideal intelligence of Camp A is therefore not an idealisation of a real capacity but a piece of metaphysics with no empirical content, and the claim that chaos imposes no limit in principle is not false so much as empty. Some of them add a further claim: even granted exact data and exact laws, there may be no shortcut, so that the only way to obtain the state at some distant time is to run the dynamics for that length of time, which for a predictor living inside the system is not prediction but patience. They accuse Camp A of defending a tautology and dressing it as a theorem.

Both camps have agreed in advance to treat the following three cases as bearing on the dispute, and both have promised not to dismiss any of them as beside the point.

Case one, which we call the Billiard Oracle. We simulate a point particle moving at constant speed inside a square box containing one fixed circular obstacle in the middle, reflecting elastically off both wall and obstacle. Two integrations are run from initial conditions that differ only far out in the digits: one carried at ordinary machine precision, one at a hundred thousand decimal places. We want an answer to a qualitative question about the far future, for instance on which side of the obstacle the particle passes at the millionth collision. Our own rough reasoning is that the separation of nearby trajectories is multiplied by roughly the same factor at each bounce, so the number of correct digits required grows by about the same amount per collision, and hence roughly linearly with the number of collisions we want to reach. The Exactists say that a large enough digit budget settles the question and that the answer is already fixed. The Finitists say that no finite procedure terminates with a settled answer, that each doubling of precision buys only a bounded number of further collisions, and that in any physical realisation of the billiard there is no fact of the matter about the hundred-thousandth digit of the initial condition, so the mathematical question does not correspond to any physical one.

Case two, which we call the Predictor in the Box. A computer sits inside the box with the particle and must print out the state of the system at some later time; the computer is itself part of what is being simulated, and it is also asked to print its own later output. Both camps accept that there is a diagonal-style obstacle to that last request. They disagree about its reach: the Exactists say it is a curiosity of self-reference that disappears the moment the target is anything other than the predictor's own future output, while the Finitists say that every predictor worth the name is embedded, so the curiosity is the normal case and the supposed outside view of the ideal intelligence is not a coherent alternative to it.

Case three, which we call the Distant Pebble. This is my own argument, and I do not know whether it is any good. Suppose a pebble on some far-off moon is displaced by a millimetre. My claim is that, given what we know about how disturbances propagate and how fast neighbouring trajectories separate, the subsequent motion of air molecules here would diverge from what it would otherwise have been within weeks, so that no available evidence about the past can ever be fine-grained enough to fix the future weather. The Exactists say this is just the definition of sensitive dependence restated, and that it is neutral between the camps. The Finitists say it shows that our best possible evidence is always a coarse-graining, so that the exact initial condition Camp A hands to its ideal intelligence is not a notion of evidence anyone could possess.

Now the deliverable. I want a single document, roughly fifteen hundred to twenty-five hundred words, in the following order.

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign: about what the mathematics of chaotic systems does and does not establish, about the status of numerical simulation, about what determinism is and is not committed to, and about what would count as a successful prediction. For each claim add one sentence explaining why it is not merely something both sides believed before they sat down.

2. A table of the disagreements that survive. Columns: the issue at stake; the formulation the Exactists would sign, in their own idiom; the formulation the Finitists would sign, in their own idiom; and whether the gap between those two formulations is substantive or merely a difference in how the words in principle, possible, and predict are being used. Each side's wording must be one that side would accept without wincing; that is the standard I will be checking.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each crux give a heading, a one-paragraph statement of the sub-question, the case or argument from the three above (or a better one you supply) that both camps accept as bearing on it, and each camp's prediction recorded in advance. The predictions must be concrete enough that somebody could later point to a result and say that one of them was wrong; do not write predictions that both camps would be happy with. I expect at least one crux to concern whether shadowing delivers what the Exactists want from it, since I have a vague sense that shadowing guarantees closeness to some true orbit rather than to the orbit that was intended, and that it may fail beyond a finite time in the physically interesting cases. If that sense of mine is wrong or muddled, say so plainly.

4. Two short sections stating, for each camp, what it would count as evidence or a decisive argument against its own position. If one camp cannot name any, say that this is itself a finding and say what it implies about the collaboration.

5. A closing section in which you state which parts of the dispute you judge to be verbal, which parts you judge to be genuine, and what is left standing once the verbal parts are set aside.

Two constraints on how you write it. First, do not adjudicate: do not tell me who is right, and do not let a preference leak into the wording of the agreed claims, because all four of us will be reading it looking for a thumb on the scale. Second, do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, and do not attribute any of these positions to a named philosopher, physicist, or textbook. I cannot check attributions and I would rather have none than have plausible-sounding ones. You may use standard technical vocabulary freely; you may not invent quotations. If you think one of my three cases is technically confused as I have described it, say what is confused and state what the corrected case does to the disagreement.
