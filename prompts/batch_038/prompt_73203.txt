I coordinate an annual two-day ethics workshop for a research funder's review committee, and the room has split into two camps that have spent the past year talking past each other. The first camp thinks in terms of gains from trade: where two parties weight the same values differently, an arrangement that lets each advance what it cares about more at the cost of what it cares about less is permissible and often obligatory, and the refusal to trade is a self-indulgent purity that leaves everyone worse off by their own lights. Its members are friendly to offsetting schemes — I run a program that a minority of the community regards as a serious wrong, I earmark an equivalent sum to that minority's own cause, and the wrong is not excused but it is compensated. The second camp holds that a good many values are not fungible: that converting the question of whether something may be done into the question of what it costs degrades the value itself; that the supposed mutual benefit is always measured in the currencies of the two traders, which is exactly what is in dispute; that the party bearing the cost of a trade is systematically not a party at the table — an animal, a person not yet born, a community that cannot sign; and that offsetting licenses the wrong it pays for, because the reliable psychological effect of having purchased absolution is to keep doing the thing.

The pilot proposal in front of us is concrete, and I would like both camps to engage with it rather than with abstractions. The funder would permit an investigator to run a program that a minority of the review community regards as a grave wrong, provided the investigator earmarks a sum for that minority's highest-priority cause, with the exchange rate set not by the funder but by an arbiter drawn from the minority itself. Trades settle annually, the ledger is public, nothing is coerced and nothing is concealed, and the minority's cause receives money it would not otherwise have had. Note that the minority is at the table and sets its own price, which blunts one standard objection but not obviously the others.

What I want is the document these two camps should have produced: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please include (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about what a trade is, about whose consent could matter and when, about what a public offsetting ledger actually does to the behavior it prices — one sentence per claim, each with the case or consideration behind it named inside that sentence; (2) a table of the disagreements that survive, no more than six rows, with columns for the disagreement stated neutrally, the formulation the trade camp would sign, the formulation the non-fungibility camp would sign, and your judgment of whether that row is substantive or merely verbal — and if you conclude that some part of the year-long argument was about the word trade, say which part and why; (3) at most four cruxes, meaning sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side, each paired with a case or an experiment both camps agree would bear on it and with each side's prediction written down in advance of the result; and (4) a closing paragraph in which each camp states, in its own preferred vocabulary, what would count as evidence against its own position.

Do not referee the dispute and do not write it as a dialogue; the deliverable is a text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Do not cite studies, name researchers, or supply references — where you need empirical support, describe the finding in general terms and say what would have to be true for it to obtain. Keep the whole thing under 1,000 words, and hold the agreed claims to one sentence each. The constraint is deliberate: the exercise fails if the parties retreat to formulations so weak that nothing is at stake, so prefer fewer, sharper agreements to a long list of platitudes.
