Our historiography department is running a methods seminar for advanced students on where legitimate historical revisionism stops and denialism begins, and I want the core handout to be a decision tree that sorts a contested work rather than a defense of any particular orthodoxy. Please build it to the following specifications.

Every node must be a procedural or epistemic test that a reader can apply to a text without already knowing which way the verdict goes: for instance, whether some identifiable body of evidence is treated as capable of overturning the conclusion; whether the standard of proof demanded of opposing accounts matches the one applied to the sources the author favors; whether silence or gaps in the surviving record are converted into positive evidence; whether the strongest available version of the opposing scholarship is engaged. No node may turn on whether the conclusion is congenial, or on the author's stated politics. Aim for five to seven nodes and six to nine leaves, about a page before the case section.

Build the space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate actual positions in it. At least two leaves must be ones where method and truth come apart: a work that is procedurally sound but simply wrong, and a work that reaches a true or defensible conclusion by disqualifying means. Say what the seminar should make of each. Note which combinations of answers nobody occupies and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your nodes cannot in practice separate.

Finally, run these four cases through the tree, reporting for each the leaf it reaches and the single node at which the classification becomes decisive:

1. A scholar argues that because no signed central order authorizing the killings survives, the atrocities were improvised by local authorities, and therefore cannot properly be described as a centrally directed genocide.
2. A scholar documents a genuine and serious sourcing error in a widely read book about a massacre, then infers from that error alone that the massacre itself was invented.
3. A scholar argues that structural features missing from the surviving ruins show the facility could not have operated as witnesses describe, and treats the entire testimonial corpus as the product of a coordinated interested conspiracy.
4. A scholar revises an orthodox death estimate downward by roughly a factor of four using newly opened population records, applies the same evidentiary standard to sources on both sides, and answers the strongest existing case for the higher figure point by point.

Please organize the response in these sections, in this order: "The tests" (roughly 350 words, defining each node's criterion and saying what in a text counts as answering it); "The tree" (the tree proper, with nodes phrased as questions and labeled branches); "Leaf notes" (one or two sentences per leaf on its most awkward commitment); "Empty and blurred cells" (about 200 words covering the unoccupied combinations and the leaves the tree cannot separate); and "Case verdicts" (about 250 words).
