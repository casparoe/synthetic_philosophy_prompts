I build measurement systems for a living. For the past year I have been the engineer responsible for a "positive experience" score in a consumer audio product — short guided sessions, focus music, wind-down tracks — which we report back to individual users weekly and, in aggregate, to two clinical partners who evaluate our programs. We needed a definition of the target before we could defend the number, so I went to the hedonist literature on well-being, and I have been stuck there ever since, because the standard objection to hedonism strikes me as either devastating or trivially answerable and I cannot tell which.

The objection, as I have reconstructed it: pleasures are wildly heterogeneous, and nobody can isolate a single felt ingredient shared by all of them. Consider my own working list, which I assembled to stress-test our score — biting into a very ripe peach; a headache lifting; hearing a bassline land exactly where you predicted it would; peeling a label off a bottle in one unbroken strip; deleting a feature branch after it merges; the smell of rain on hot asphalt; schadenfreude; being right in an argument you were losing; the first full yawn after a long flight; a cold pool on a hot day; a joke landing in a room you were nervous in. Hedonism about well-being is supposed to say that all of these consist in one and the same thing, and that this one thing is what makes them good for the person. The critics say there is no such thing to point at. The defenders say the critics are looking in the wrong place.

From what I have read, the responses fall into roughly four camps, and I cannot tell whether they contradict each other:

1. There is a common hedonic tone or a single dimension along which experiences are more or less pleasant; the heterogeneity lies in the objects and the modalities, not in the pleasantness itself.
2. Pleasures form a family-resemblance kind with no shared essence, and the theory needs only the family, not an essence.
3. The relational or attitudinal move: what makes an experience pleasant is not how it feels but a certain relation to desire — roughly, wanting it to continue, for its own sake, while it is happening. Sidgwick's characterization of pleasures as feelings that appear desirable seems to belong here, as does Feldman's shift to being pleased that something is the case, which appears not to require a feel at all.
4. Dissolution: the objection presupposes a picture of feelings as inspectable inner objects, and once that picture goes, the demand to identify a common ingredient was misplaced from the start.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and probably too tidy: this is two questions wearing one name. Question A is phenomenological — is there a single felt dimension present in all pleasant experiences, discoverable by attending to them? Question B is motivational — is there a single relation to desire that all pleasant experiences bear? Camp 1 answers A and claims to answer B; camp 3 answers B and claims to answer A; camp 2 is a claim about natural kinds and semantics that does not sit on either axis; camp 4 is a claim about a picture of mind that both sides are using without noticing. Arguments fail to move anyone because they cross the questions: the critics' lists of cases are evidence about what introspection can deliver, which is A, but get offered as refutations of B, while the relational accounts secure B partly by definition and then present that as a discovery about A. On this reading the dispute is partly verbal (turning on "feel" and "quality"), partly two-questions-under-one-heading, with a genuine substantive core left over that neither camp is currently addressing. But I may simply be wrong: there may be one real question here, and one side may just be failing to find its answer.
