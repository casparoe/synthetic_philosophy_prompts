My flatmate and his brother got into it at dinner about the no-trade theorem and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Ravi said the theorem basically proves stock speculation is a scam: if everyone's Bayesian, started from a common prior, and knows everyone else is rational, nobody should ever trade on private information — so the "prices contain dispersed wisdom" story is a myth, and every trade that actually happens involves somebody being wrong or being had. Dev said that's a gross overreach, since the theorem only rules out trade driven by differing beliefs; heaps of real trading comes from different risk appetites, hedging, or someone needing cash because they got laid off, and none of that requires anyone to be irrational. Ravi said fine, subtract all the hedgers and liquidity traders and what's left over is exactly the speculative trade the theorem says cant happen — which means markets structurally depend on irrational people, because if prices already reflected everything nobody would bother paying to find anything out. Dev said the real load-bearing assumption is the common prior: priors aren't the kind of thing you can be rational or irrational about, so two perfectly reasonable people can just start from different ones, and trading on that difference is rational and the price still aggregates something. Ravi's comeback was that if you let priors float free like that the theorem goes empty, and Dev has quietly given up on treating the market price as a shared public object at all. Dev's last move: even granting a common prior, the thing only unravels if the offer to trade itself leaks what the offerer knows — and in an anonymous limit-order book where nobody knows who's on the other side or why they're there, it doesn't leak.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each stage rather than just picking a winner? And is there something neither of them touched that actually decides it?
