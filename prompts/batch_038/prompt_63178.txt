I'm running a twelve-student senior seminar on the limits of knowledge, and this week we derive the knowability paradox: from the assumptions that knowledge is factive, that knowledge distributes over conjunction, that every truth is possibly known, and that at least one truth is in fact unknown, a contradiction follows in a handful of steps that need no exotic modal machinery. My students have done a term of modal logic but none of the specialist literature, and they have taken to announcing which "solution" they prefer before they can say which of the four assumptions it costs them anything to surrender. So instead of a lecture I want to hand them a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree titled something like 'which response to the knowability paradox do you actually hold?', built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of famous names.

Specifications. Ten to fourteen nodes, seven to nine leaves, formatted as numbered nodes with explicit if-yes/if-no pointers so it fits on a single printed sheet, plus a one-line scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. Every node must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the debate, which means each one has to pose a concrete case or a yes/no commitment about a case rather than a slogan or a term of art: what to say about the exact number of blades of grass on a particular lawn at some moment in the distant past; what to say about the sentence on a card in a sealed envelope which asserts, in effect, that no one knows what is written on that card; whether a truth that would be destroyed by coming to be known still counts as a truth that could be known; whether the possibility in the claim that every truth is possibly known should be read as possibility for creatures like us, for an ideally placed community of inquirers with unlimited time, or as bare metaphysical possibility; and whether knowing a conjunction ever licenses knowing each of its conjuncts in the sense that matters here. The branches should turn on which of the four assumptions the student's answers commit them to dropping, restricting, or re-reading, plus whatever further assumptions you judge belong on the tree — I would expect at least a node on whether the target proposition can be known as such while remaining true, and one that tests the claim, made by at least one of my students, that this whole business is just the liar paradox with extra steps. Each leaf gets a descriptive label saying what the position actually does — which assumption it gives up or narrows, and what that costs — together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Attach the standard name of a view only where you are confident such a name exists; where you are not, describe the position and flag the uncertainty rather than supplying an attribution.

Two jobs the tree must do that I doubt an encyclopedia summary would. First, since the four assumptions are jointly inconsistent, many answer patterns cannot correspond to any coherent position at all: build in explicit inconsistency-detector nodes that catch those patterns and route the student to a display of the derivation in three or four lines, naming the precise pair of commitments that collide, instead of quietly herding them into the nearest leaf. Second, after the tree, give me the analysis of the space it carves up. For every combination of rejected or restricted assumptions that your tree does not lead anywhere, say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or simply because nobody has thought to occupy it. And flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate — my own suspicion is that restricting the knowability principle to propositions whose being known is consistent, and re-reading the modal operator so that what is possibly known is that actually such-and-such, deliver identical verdicts on every case I have been able to think of; if you agree, tell me whether the difference is merely notational or reflects a substantive disagreement about what could be known, and if you disagree, give me the case that separates them. Finally, keep the whole thing self-contained: no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, nothing that would send a student off to look something up; and wherever a leaf's viability appears to depend on the strength of the modal logic in play, say so on the leaf itself — I want them to see that no position gets to hide behind an axiom.
