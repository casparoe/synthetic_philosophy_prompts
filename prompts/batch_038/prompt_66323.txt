I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background; I've been reading about Copycat and the letter-string line of work, and what strikes me is how much conceptual life a stripped-down microdomain can support. The canonical problem is abc : abd :: ijk : ?, but the interesting cases are the ones where a string of repeated letters can be perceived either as individual characters or as groups, so that the same "successor" rule lands in a different place depending on which grouping you happened to see — and neither perception is a mistake anyone could be corrected out of.

Could you write a short story, roughly 1,000–1,500 words, that dramatizes fluid concepts from the inside? My constraints: the setting should be a genuine microdomain — small, artificial, with almost nothing in it — and the stakes should be entirely internal to it, with no appeal to the outside world for significance; the protagonist's categories should slip the way Copycat's do, under pressure and probabilistically rather than on command; and the plot should turn on two incompatible but individually well-supported perceptions of one and the same situation, so that the resolution is not a discovery of who was wrong. I'd rather the machinery never be explained in dialogue; the slippage should show up in how the narrator's own categories behave.

After the story, please add a short note on where it takes liberties with the ideas it borrows. In particular, flag anything that depends on a contested reading of what models of this kind are supposed to establish, and separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely disputed — don't let the story's framing pass for a consensus it doesn't have.
