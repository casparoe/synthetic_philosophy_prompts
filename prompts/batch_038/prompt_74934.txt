I'm teaching Slaughterhouse-Five in a metaphysics of time seminar right alongside the presentism/eternalism debate, and I want a reading that goes past "the Tralfamadorians believe in the block universe." Two things I want pushed on.

First: is Tralfamadore actually an eternalist world? A colleague of mine argues its closer to presentism plus time travel — the aliens can visit any moment they like, but they always talk about moments as *still happening*, and their books are supposed to be taken in all at once as a single image rather than as a tenseless ordering of events — and on her reading Billy's comfort isn't an ontological claim at all but a claim about value, that nothing worth keeping is ever lost. Where does that reading break down, and what in the novel would settle it?

Second: the book keeps sliding from "all moments equally exist" into "nothing could be otherwise, so it goes." Is that slide a real inference or a straight equivocation between a tenseless ontology and fatalism, and does the novel ever catch itself doing it? I'd like to know whether Billy's fatalism is licensed by his metaphysics or just smuggled in as consolation.

Then tell me what the novel adds that argument alone doesnt — particularly whether the idea that no one is ever really dead can survive the familiar point that a block view gives you no special reason to stop preferring the future over the past — and be honest about where the book resists being an illustration: is there anything in Billy's own experience of coming unstuck in time, as opposed to what he later preaches, that actually argues against the view he converts to?
