I do clearance research for docs and biopics, and there's a pattern in how these projects get made that I've never seen anyone step back and describe philosophically. Here's roughly how it goes on every job I've worked.

Before picture lock, everything goes through a lawyer — and if we want errors-and-omissions insurance, through the insurer's reviewer too — who sorts every scene and line into two piles: stuff about living people, and stuff about dead people. The rules applied to the two piles are totally different. Living people can't be defamed, even by implication, so a real person who might recognize himself gets renamed, turned into a composite, or cut; anything we can't source gets softened or dropped; the film ends with a card saying characters and events have been dramatized. The dead pile basically gets waved through, because in both the US and the UK you can't defame a corpse — the claim dies with the person. So we can put invented dialogue in a dead woman's mouth, compress three people into one, move an incident ten years, imply she did something she didn't, and the clearance note says its fine.

Except the dead aren't unregulated, they're just regulated by a different lever. Their actual words are usually still under copyright, held by an estate or an archive, so if we want a real sentence from a real letter on screen we need a license, and licenses come with informal conditions: the family wants to read the script, wants the portrayal to be 'fair', wants grandma's affair left out. The practical outcome on my jobs is that we can freely invent what a dead person said, but we can't quote a documented sentence she actually wrote without permission from people who may be using that permission to shape how she comes across.

Then there's a historical case I keep thinking about, which looks like the same machinery running backwards. Elizabeth Gaskell's Life of Charlotte Brontë came out in 1857, and within months the first edition was pulled after complaints of slander from people who were alive and recognized themselves: the headmaster and teachers of the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge, which Brontë had fictionalized as Lowood in Jane Eyre (one of them, the Rev. William Carus Wilson, published a pamphlet refuting Gaskell's account), plus Brontë's husband, some of her reviewers, and a woman connected to her brother. Smith, Elder brought out second and third editions with the offending material cut or softened, all within about five months of the first. Gaskell had been asked to write the book by Brontë's father as a tribute, and her evidence was mostly Brontë's letters to Ellen Nussey plus interviews with people who'd known her, like her friend Mary Taylor. So the constraint there wasn't about the dead subject at all — it was about living complainants — and the effect was that the record of a dead woman's life got edited down to make things comfortable for the people still around to complain. I'm going from memory on the details, so don't treat any of that as gospel.

What I want isn't a verdict on whether any of this is right. I want the implicit theory dug out:

1. What view of what we owe the dead does clearance practice actually presuppose — that a person's reputation is a legally enforceable interest that expires at death, that what survives a person is a bundle of copyrights and archive permissions their heirs control, or the thing we say out loud at festivals, that we owe the dead person herself fairness and respect? Those pull in different directions and I can't tell which one the machinery is built on.
2. What rival accounts does it quietly rule out — that the dead can still be harmed, that fidelity to who someone actually was is a debt owed to them rather than to their estate, that the public has a claim on the record that outweighs a family's comfort?
3. Where is the practice underdetermined, so that two incompatible theories license exactly the same behavior?
4. Is the two-pile split a straight inconsistency, or can one coherent view generate both halves plus the inversion where the invented line is free and the real letter costs money?
5. My working hypothesis, which may be wrong: the practice doesn't presuppose any moral view of the dead at all — it's a liability and rights-clearance schedule we've dressed up as ethics, and the respect language is decoration. If that's right, say so. But then explain why the notes keep reaching for moral words.
6. Run your reconstruction over the Gaskell case and tell me whether 1857 presupposed the same theory or a different one. My intuition is that a system that censors the dead to protect the living and a system that ignores the dead to protect the living are morally the same shape, but I can't argue for it.
7. If we replaced the presupposed theory with one where the obligation runs to the dead person rather than to whoever can sue or whoever owns the letters, what would concretely change in how these films get made — which steps of clearance would have to be rebuilt, and what would we lose?
