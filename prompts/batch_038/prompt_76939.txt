I'm the retiring chair of our faculty senate. Back in 1998 I drafted the clause saying any change to the tenure rules needs a two-thirds vote plus a twelve-month delay before it takes effect, and I wrote it for one reason: I didn't trust us to legislate in a panic. Tomorrow the senate votes on whether to strike the clause, and by tradition whoever wrote it gives the opening remarks — ten minutes, to the same room that wants it gone, plus two trustees and the college's lawyer sitting in the back. Four junior faculty in the room had a contract vote frozen last year by my own rule, and they know it.

Write the remarks, about 1200 words. They should make the case for the clause that I actually believe, but they have to take seriously the objection I can't answer in my own head: the senate of 1998 and the senate of tomorrow share almost no members, so who exactly is being held to a promise they never made? Deal with that without turning it into a seminar — these are historians and chemists, not political theorists, and the trustees are there to decide if I'm senile. No Ulysses-and-the-mast bit: half the room has heard it and the other half will look it up and feel talked down to. And I cant claim the delay has cost people nothing, because it has, so don't.
