I was raised in a conservative evangelical church, and for about two years now — since my father died, mainly — I have found that I cannot pray in the way I used to: I speak, and there is no sense of anyone on the other end. Rather than leave the matter unresolved, I joined an online reading group attached to a parish, and I have been doing the reading seriously even though my formal education stops at an introductory philosophy course and a degree in supply-chain analytics. Last month our group leader circulated a chapter from a book of responses to the main arguments for atheism, written by a professional philosopher who is also a practicing Christian; I am supposed to lead the discussion of it next Tuesday. Its section on divine hiddenness runs as follows:

"Schellenberg's hiddenness argument says that if God exists and is perfectly loving, he would make sure that anyone who is not actively resisting him believes that he exists, since you cannot be open to a personal relationship with someone you do not think is there. That is a demanding claim, and it means that every sincere atheist is evidence against God. But the argument confuses a goal with a precondition: God does not want belief for its own sake, and in any case he gave us freedom, so he cannot simply produce belief in us without producing it by force, and a belief produced by force would be worth nothing. Nor does the argument have the material it needs, because it depends on nonbelief that is genuinely nonresistant, and much unbelief is nothing of the kind: there are people who are furious at God, and people who see perfectly well what believing would require of them and are not prepared to do it. Finally, once we allow what any theist must allow — that God may have reasons for remaining hidden that we cannot grasp — the argument loses its grip altogether, since it depends on our being able to see that no such reason exists. In any case the argument is not a new one: it is the problem of evil in a quieter register, with the absence of God's presence taking the place of suffering as the bad state of affairs a good God would have prevented."

I would like an accuracy audit of this passage that I can actually bring to the meeting. Please take it claim by claim — split a sentence into two units if it is doing two things — and for each unit say whether a careful defender of the hiddenness argument would accept it, whether it is a fair simplification, or whether it misrepresents the argument. Where a unit distorts, give me the accurate version, and identify which of these it is doing: overstating a thesis that is actually restricted in scope; substituting an everyday notion for a technical one the argument needs; crediting the chapter with a distinction the argument already draws; or answering a stronger version of the argument than the one its author presses. Then evaluate the chapter's four responses on their own terms — the free-will response, the claim that much unbelief is resistant, the appeal to reasons we cannot grasp, and the assimilation to the problem of evil — and tell me for each whether it engages the argument as stated or only a neighbor of it, and how much force it has if it does engage. I suspect, though I could be wrong, that the genuinely contested ground here is the factual question of whether there are people who are open to God and nevertheless do not believe, and I would like to know whether the chapter's silence about that ground is itself a defect or a reasonable concession. Please do not treat the chapter as acting in bad faith: what I want to know is whether it is competent but loose, or whether the looseness is load-bearing.

Since I will be asked about this in front of people who trust the book, I also want to know where your audit is most likely to be wrong.
