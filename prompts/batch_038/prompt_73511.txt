I direct a research group in the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of biology, and this term I am chairing an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have spent the better part of a decade talking past one another over the question Hofstadter dramatises in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* through the ant colony Aunt Hillary: whether a colony can be a subject of experience at all, its mental life standing to the individual ants as a person's stands to their neurons.

The first defends what she calls the bounded-subject view. A subject of experience, on her account, requires a single spatially contiguous system which is self-monitoring and whose parts exchange information fast enough to be bound into one perspective at a time. A colony is self-organising and remarkably good at solving problems, she grants, but nothing it does is evidence of a point of view; the intuition that there might be one simply confuses adaptive coordination with consciousness. The second defends a functional pluralism: nothing in the concept of a subject requires a skull, a nervous system, or a particular transmission speed, and colonies exhibit the very marks we accept in animals — division of labour, homeostatic regulation, flexible collective decision-making, and information retained across seasons in the trail network and in the architecture of the nest. On his account, the bounded-subject view merely re-describes the mammalian brain as the criterion of mentality and then congratulates itself on a discovery.

Before our first session I intend to place a draft joint statement in front of them, so that they argue about the wording rather than about the agenda. Please draft it, in British English, in four parts.

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. Make these substantive rather than diplomatic: claims about what colonies demonstrably do; about what the notions of a subject, of experience, and of representation commit us to; about what the two of them agree would count as finding a mind and as failing to find one; and about which familiar framings of the question — Block's thought experiment in which a whole national population simulates a brain, the vocabulary of panpsychist inflation, appeals to a hard problem — they both regard as unhelpful here. Where a claim is acceptable to one party only under a stipulation, record the stipulation.

2. A table of the residual disagreements. For each row, state the disagreement precisely, in wording each party would accept for its own side, and mark whether it is a disagreement about the world, about the concept of a subject, or merely about words. I expect at least one row to be diagnosed as verbal — identify it and explain why — and at least one to be of a kind that no further evidence could settle.

3. The cruxes: those sub-questions whose resolution would actually move one of the parties. My own hunches, which you are free to revise or replace, are (i) whether colony-level processes are too slow to be bound into a unified perspective, and what the temporal grain of a moment of experience must therefore be; (ii) whether the colony has anything capable of playing the role an animal's body schema plays, as against functional structure ascribable only from the third person; (iii) whether its retention of information is genuinely colony-level, or nothing over and above the habits of individual ants together with the layout of the nest; and (iv) whether there is any fact of the matter about what the colony believes independently of a charitable interpreter.

4. For each crux, a test, case, or argument that both parties accept as bearing on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance and in a form that admits of being wrong — if X is observed, A concedes Y. At least two of these should be studies one could in principle run on a real colony: for example, a bisection design in which a colony is divided and each half tested for information only the whole had access to; or a task whose solution requires carrying a trace across an interval longer than the lifespan of any individual ant. Say, for each, what observation would be equivocal.

Close with a short section in which each party states what would count as evidence against their own view, whether either believes anything would in fact move the other, and whether they agree that the outcome bears on whether a colony has interests that can be harmed as a whole, over and above the harms suffered by its members. Do not include references, a bibliography, or a reading list: everything in the document should be reconstructible from the arguments it contains.
