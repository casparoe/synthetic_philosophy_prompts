I have published four novels, and since the spring I have sat as one of three outside members on the editorial board of a literary quarterly: small, venerable, two paid staff, an endowment that loses a little water every year, and something in the neighborhood of four hundred unsolicited manuscripts arriving each month. Nine months ago, without telling the board, the managing editor began using a language model to draft the reply to every declined submission. The old reply was a printed paragraph of thanks. The new ones run two or three hundred words about the particular story — where its pressure sits, what it appears to want, the sentence at which a reader's confidence begins to slip. By every measure we have, they are better than anything this magazine has ever sent anyone. Writers rate the exchange helpful sixty-one percent of the time, against twelve percent under the form note; submissions are up; one woman wrote back four thousand words of argument and then sent us something new; two writers have since published stories whose titles are lifted, apparently affectionately, from a line in their own rejection.

No one has accused the letters of being wrong, and I am no longer certain that counts as a defense. The system does not tire, does not lose patience, and can assemble in thirty seconds a more careful reading of a story than any of us could manage in an afternoon; it has been fed, so far as we can determine, every issue the magazine has printed since it began. The person on the receiving end has no way to check what it says about her own work and no realistic way to argue with it. When one writer asked point-blank whether a human being had read her story, the reply said that her story had been read with great care and that the reader wanted her to know exactly which paragraph had begun to lose him — true in every particular, and when she forwarded it to me she said she had felt handled rather than answered. The board is now split three ways and I have been asked to write us out of it. The poet who chairs our finance committee wants disclosure on the first line, in plain words, before anything else is said. The managing editor counters that a disclosed letter is a discounted letter, that the writers who most need the reading will file it as machine noise and the whole benefit evaporates, and that we would be surrendering something real for a formality that alters no sentence. A third member wants to go further: extend the system to our acceptance letters, and to the personal notes we send the handful of writers we hope to keep.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate before the board meets on the fourteenth. I need a recommendation it can vote on rather than a survey of considerations — whether and how the letters disclose what composed them, what they may and may not claim about who has read a manuscript, whether they may answer a writer who answers them, how long they may go on pressing a point against someone who disagrees, and whether any of this should be extended to acceptances. Give me rules an overworked managing editor could apply on a Tuesday without consulting anyone, and tell me what to do with the four hundred replies already queued for this month. Then make the strongest honest case for the position you are rejecting, the managing editor's above all, since I find hers the harder to answer, and say plainly where you think she wins. Two things I would ask you to avoid: the vocabulary of a compliance document, and any conclusion that amounts to striking a balance. If the right answer is that we should stop sending them, say so, and tell me what we lose by it.
