Six weeks ago the county board stuck me on the Cedar Basin Regional Water Authority as the at-large member — I'm a hydrologist, not a lawyer or an economist, and I'm pretty sure I got the seat only because I'm the one person in the room with no stake on either side. Now I'm chairing the finance subcommittee and I have to circulate a draft memo to the full board before our November 14th meeting, and I'm in over my head. Background: the city of Fairview (pop. ~90k) and Sanitary District No. 4 (~11k, mostly unincorporated, failing lagoons and septic) are going to build a $180M advanced treatment plant together. Staff's numbers say Fairview upgrading on its own would run $140M, and the district's best realistic standalone fallback is $95M — so cooperating saves $55M, and the whole fight is how to divide that. Two formulas are on the table for the intergovernmental agreement. "Rule N" is the Nash bargaining idea, maximize the product of each side's savings relative to its fallback; with our numbers staff say it just lands on a straight 50/50 split of the $55M, so the city pays $112.5M and the district pays $67.5M, which works out to roughly $220/year per city household versus roughly $1,490/year per district household — about 0.3% of city median income ($78k) against 3.6% of district median income ($41k). "Rule K" is the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution, where you take the point on the frontier at which both sides have made the same proportional progress toward their own best case. Run in plain dollars, staff say Rule K gives the identical answer to Rule N; run with dollars weighted for ability to pay, it comes out around city $126M / district $54M, i.e. about $1,190 per district household, 2.9%.

The board has been arguing about this for four months and I'm now fairly convinced they're arguing about three different things at once without noticing. Prof. Ruth Ledbetter, a retired economist and one of the city's appointees, says Rule K is the principled one because a party should never end up worse off just because its own outside options improved — which is live for us, since the state may hand the district a $30M clean-water grant in the spring that by statute can only be spent on the district's standalone project, and the district's counsel has been quoting that "your better options can hurt you" line at literally every meeting. Our staff economist says that with a frontier as flat as ours the paradox can't actually bite, which is exactly why the two formulas coincide in raw dollars, and that the moment we start weighting dollars by ability to pay we've stopped doing bargaining theory and started doing distributive justice with the lights off. Meanwhile Duane, the rancher who holds the district's seat, keeps saying the district would honestly rather keep violating its permit and eat the fines, because the state always ends up taking over districts like theirs anyway — so the $95M fallback may be a number nobody in the room believes and nobody will ever have to test. And there's a third wrinkle: an industrial park wants to interconnect in 2029, and somebody told me Rule K doesn't extend cleanly once there's a third party, which matters because whatever we adopt is supposed to be a standing rule for the life of the agreement, not a one-off split.

So: draft the memo I'll put in front of the board. I want the recommendation up front — adopt Rule K, adopt Rule N, some hybrid, or scrap both and allocate by cost causation (flow and pollutant load) with a bargaining rule applied only to the genuinely joint residual — and then the reasoning that gets us there, with the actual dollar and per-household figures under whatever you recommend. Two pages max, plain English; the board includes a rancher and two councilmembers who will not sit through notation, so any technical term has to be defined inline the first time it shows up. The memo has to take a position on whether the axiom fight is substantive or just a disguised fight about the metric, and if it's the metric, say straight out whether any bargaining axiom can settle that question or whether we have to own a distributive choice in public. It also needs to tell me plainly whether "better outside options can make you worse off" can actually happen in our setup or whether Ledbetter is right that it can't; what a standing rule should do when one side's fallback point is arguably a bluff; how the rule behaves once the industrial park shows up as a third party in 2029; and — this is the part I'm most nervous about — the strongest honest case for whichever side loses under my recommendation, argued as well as you can manage rather than strawmanned, because I'm going to have to read that section aloud in a room where both sides already think I'm the other side's plant. No bibliography, no citations, no suggested further reading — I just need something I can actually defend on the 14th.
