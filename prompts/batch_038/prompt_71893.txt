I've been keeping a running list, sort of in the spirit of the specks-versus-torture argument everyone's read a dozen times, except mine aren't thought experiments — they're decisions I or people around me actually faced, and my gut verdicts on them turn out to be a total mess. I've argued the abstract version of this since the old LW days and always said the same thing: sure, a big enough number of specks beats fifty years of torture, that's just arithmetic, grow up. But now that I write down what I actually believe about specific cases, that answer keeps not showing up where I'd expect it, and I can't tell whether my verdicts are incoherent or whether they're pointing at a principle I haven't been able to state. Here's the list:

1. A friend runs push notifications at a big consumer app. She can ship a change that lifts engagement by making the app mildly more annoying — call it one extra unwanted interruption per user per week, irritating in the moment, forgotten ten seconds later, across ~200 million users. She asked if it's obviously fine. I said I thought it wasn't, and that I'd rather she not ship it. But if you told me the same change prevented one person from being paralyzed, I'd ship it myself and not lose sleep.
2. Someone I know works on post-op pain protocols and asked how a million hours of level-2 nausea should be weighed against one case of level-10 pain. I said trade it, obviously. But a million hours of level-0.5 nausea — the kind you only notice if someone asks you to check — I said doesn't count at all, at any number of hours. So there's a cliff somewhere and I can't say where it is.
3. One spam email costs me maybe four seconds. I think a spam operation that sends ten billion of them is doing something genuinely wrong. But I don't think it's a million times more wrong than one that sends ten thousand. Diminishing returns feel right here in a way they don't feel right in case 1, and I don't know why.
4. A speck-level harm to a chicken. I think enough chicken-specks outweigh one human-speck, which sounds deranged to me when I type it, but I don't want to retract it.
5. If the harm is a nanosecond of visual flicker that leaves literally no memory trace and no behavioral effect, I think its weight is exactly zero, not small-but-nonzero. If it's the same flicker that the person later remembers, it's nonzero. So whether a harm counts depends on whether it's retrievable, which I'm aware sounds arbitrary.
6. A thousand people each getting one migraine: worse than one person getting a thousand migraine-days, I'd say. But flip it to a thousand people each losing a year of life versus one person losing a thousand years — stipulate it's possible — and now I say the concentrated version is worse. My view on distribution flips with severity and I have no account of why.
7. Give one person in a village of 500 a week of real suffering, or give all 500 a mild flu-like day: I take the flu day, every time, no hesitation. But 3^^^3 strangers getting specks versus one stranger getting tortured: I take the specks. So the number I flip at is somewhere between 500 and 3^^^3, and I'm pretty sure it isn't really about the number — something about identifiability, or about it being a community where they'll all know who got the week. I've read enough about scope insensitivity to know I'm supposed to call that a bias, but the village case doesn't feel like a bias, it feels like it's tracking something.
8. The one I keep going back and forth on: my own life. Coffee, flights, cheap electronics — every purchase scatters tiny harms I'll never see. I think I'm obliged to cut down, up to maybe 5% of my income and no further, and I think someone who obsessively optimizes every speck they cause is making a mistake rather than being virtuous. So there's a "good enough" threshold on my side of the ledger too.

My current ad hoc rule, which I'm not attached to: harms add linearly as long as each one clears a floor of "noticed and remembered," below which they're exactly zero; and above the floor, the summing gets discounted when the harms fall on people who don't know each other and won't compare notes. Test that against the list and tell me honestly what's left. Please don't summarize the standard moves at me — I know the labels for aggregation, the spectrum argument, the person-affecting restriction, all of it. I want to know what actually survives when you try to build a rule that fits these eight cases, which clauses you'd need, and which of my verdicts has to go. Format it like this: **Bottom line** — three or four sentences up front, your actual answer to whether these are reconcilable. **What the list commits me to** — ~200 words naming the structural constraints the eight verdicts jointly impose and which pairs are in direct conflict rather than merely awkward. **Candidate principles** — three of them, ~150 words each, each stated as a precise rule with explicit clauses, and for every clause say which of my cases it exists to handle. **Where each one breaks** — ~100 words per principle, including at least one case it gets wrong that isn't on my list. **The verdict you'd drop** — ~200 words picking one of my eight and arguing I should give it up. **Three predictions** — ~100 words total giving your preferred principle's verdict on an A/B test that adds two seconds of load time for 500 million users in order to fund a large donation, on a policy that gives every citizen a 1% chance of a painful-but-brief procedure to spare one person a decade of agony, and on whether the spam operator in case 3 should send ten billion emails or one hundred billion.
