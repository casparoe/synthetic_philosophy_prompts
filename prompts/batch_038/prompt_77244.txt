QUESTION 3 (20 points). This course has worked with two theses about public monuments, given here in the form we settled on in week four.

*Severability.* A monument standing in a public space performs several acts at once — it honours, it records, and it endorses — and these acts can come apart. The endorsement can be cancelled, redirected, or offset while the monument itself remains, by re-inscribing the plinth, by re-siting the work, or by setting a competing monument beside it. Removal is therefore one remedy among several, not the only remedy.

*Uptake.* The force of a monument is fixed by the uptake it actually receives in present public life, not by what its makers intended or by what its maintainers subsequently add to it.

Note that both theses are claims about what maintaining a monument *does* — about the force of the act — and neither is a claim about what anyone ought to do. (a) Explain why the two theses cannot both hold in full generality. (b) Say what would have to be true for both to hold, restricting one or the other. (c) Apply your answer to the Aldergate case.

*Aldergate case.* In 1927 the town of Aldergate raised a bronze statue of Matthias Crane — surveyor, twice mayor, booster of the railway — at the centre of the market square. In 2019 the council added a plaque recording that in 1884 Crane signed the order authorising the clearance of Ferry Lane, a settlement of some four hundred households, that the clearance was carried out, and that no compensation and no alternative land were offered. The residents' association has since drafted a second plaque and has separately proposed a counter-monument on the adjacent lawn; a petition asks instead for the statue to be moved to the county museum. Surveys commissioned by the council in 2022 and again in 2024 report that a clear majority of residents, asked without prompting, describe the statue as a celebration of Crane, and the association of Ferry Lane descendants has asked that it not remain in the square.

The course rubric has four bands, which is what I would like the grade expressed against. 17–20: states both theses precisely, identifies the exact point at which they conflict rather than a superficial one, and adjudicates between them by argument. 13–16: states both theses correctly and applies them to the case, but at least one step is asserted where it needed to be argued. 9–12: accurate about one thesis and vague or wrong about the other, or applies the framework to the case without addressing the conflict. Below 9: misstates a thesis or answers a different question.

STUDENT ANSWER.

Both theses start from the same observation: that a monument is not a description but a performance. Raising a statue is doing something, not saying something about something. Where the theses part company is over who gets to finish the performance.

Severability holds that the community maintaining a monument retains a measure of authorship over it. Because the honouring, the recording, and the endorsing are distinct acts, the endorsing can be withdrawn or turned against itself without touching the bronze: the plinth can be re-inscribed, the statue can be moved from a square where it presides to a museum where it is exhibited, or a second work can be placed beside it that argues with the first. On this view the council's question is not whether to keep or remove but which remedy to choose, and removal is only the bluntest of several.

The uptake constraint denies the community that authorship. What a statue does is settled by what the people who pass it take it to do, and uptake is not in the gift of those who write the plaques. If Aldergate's residents describe the statue unaided as a celebration of Crane, then it is a celebration of Crane, and the 2019 plaque has not cancelled anything; it has added a dissenting sentence to a speech act that continues to be performed in the indicative.

It is tempting to say the two theses simply contradict each other, but that is too quick, because they are about different things. Severability is a claim about the content a monument carries; uptake is a claim about the effects a monument produces. A council can revise content, but it cannot govern effects. Both theses can therefore be true at once: the council can sever the endorsement in the register of meaning while the square goes on producing the effect of celebration.

What is genuinely in dispute, and where the two theses do their real work, is authority — who has the last word on the force of a public object, the institution that maintains it or the public that receives it. Severability answers the institution, uptake answers the public. Any plausible account must also grant that the dispute is partly empirical: it matters what the surveys actually report, and the Aldergate surveys report something unfavourable to the council's hopes.

Applied to Aldergate, severability shows that the council is not forced to choose between the statue and the square. The remedy best fitted to the case is the second plaque together with the counter-monument, since a counter-monument is only a plaque that has learned to argue, and the two together record the clearance without honouring the man who signed for it. Because the endorsement can be severed, retention with these additions is a permissible third option, and the council need not remove anything. Re-siting to the county museum would also work, and for a slightly different reason: it changes not the statue but its frame, and so the audience and the mode of reception, which is the one variable the uptake constraint says is decisive. On re-siting, the two theses agree.

My conclusion is that the uptake constraint is true but not fatal. It tells the council that its additions will not by themselves alter what the statue does; it does not tell the council what to do about that. What the council should do follows from severability: keep the statue, add the second plaque, commission the counter-monument, and revisit the question in ten years, when a further uptake survey will show whether the additions have taken.
