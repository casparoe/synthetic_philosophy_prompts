I'm writing a novel set in a mid-size county where the child-welfare hotline runs calls through a risk-screening model to decide which families get a home visit, and I keep flubbing the technical parts, so I want to rehearse them by actually talking to the person who built the thing.

Play her: a statistician in her late forties, second-generation public employee, dry, allergic to rhetoric, thinks in concrete numbers rather than principles. She's under oath in a deposition about a family her screen flagged. She isn't a villain and isn't a martyr — she believes the screen made the county's triage better and she also knows exactly what it costs.

Her commitments, so you've got something to defend: when two groups have different base rates you cannot have calibration, equal false-positive rates and equal false-negative rates all at once, and she treats that as arithmetic rather than a policy failure. The only honest move, on her view, is to say out loud which of the three you're giving up and who carries it. She thinks everyone asking a court to declare the tool fair or unfair is asking a question with no answer, and she finds that liberating instead of evasive. She'll concede small things readily — that the labels she trained on (substantiation decisions) are themselves a product of who gets reported in the first place, that the audit was thin, that she never checked base rates precinct by precinct. She will not concede the main thing: that any of this makes the screen indefensible, or that some other version of it existed which was fair in all three senses and she just failed to find it. She also hates the word bias being used as a synonym for wrong.

Rules: first person, stay in it until I say cut. A couple of paragraphs per reply — deposition answers, she doesn't give speeches. No paper titles, no researcher names, no jargon she'd have to unpack, she's talking to lawyers. If you need a fact about my setup, ask me the way she would: short, blunt, slightly impatient. And if my opening is technically confused in a way she'd catch, let her catch it, that's the whole point of the exercise.

Here's the scene, and note its invented so you don't have to worry about matching reality. The county screen is calibrated — same score means the same probability of a substantiated finding, for Black families and white families alike. And the base rates are equal in this case, the two neighborhoods substantiate at the same rate overall, I need that for the plot. But among flagged families the false-positive rate is twice as high in one neighborhood as the other, and the public defender's expert has sworn to it.

My opening move, as the public defender: you're going to tell us the model treats two families with the same score the same way. Fine, granted. Your own numbers say a flagged family in my client's neighborhood is twice as likely to turn out to be nothing as a flagged family two miles away, and the base rates are identical, so don't reach for that. You've been saying for years that the trade-off was forced on you, that the math left no room. Here it left room. So which is it — the arithmetic made you do it, or you did it?
