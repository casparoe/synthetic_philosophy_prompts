I lead a small technical team that must decide, this quarter, which family of impact measure to build into the reward function of a planning agent operating in partially modeled, household-scale environments. Before we commit engineering time, I want a disciplined comparative ranking rather than a survey of the literature.

Below are the six candidates as I understand them. Please rank them as specified. If you think I have mischaracterized one, rank your corrected version and flag the correction explicitly in one line.

- **A. Reachability-counting penalty.** The agent is penalized in proportion to the number (or measure) of states it can no longer reach from its current situation, relative to what it could have reached had it acted otherwise.
- **B. Attainable-utility preservation.** The agent is penalized for the drop in its ability to achieve a set of fixed, arbitrary, mutually unrelated side objectives (for instance: keep the ability to prepare a meal, keep the ability to reach the exit, keep the ability to power on a device), where the objectives are chosen without regard to the task.
- **C. Whitelist / permitted change.** A list enumerates the changes the agent is allowed to make; anything off the list counts as impact and is penalized.
- **D. Mild optimization.** Rather than selecting the reward-maximizing action, the agent samples from the top *q* fraction of available actions by reward, on the theory that low impact emerges from declining to optimize hard.
- **E. Deviation from baseline.** The penalty is the divergence between the state the agent actually produces and the state produced by a designated baseline policy — inaction, a scripted default, or a rollout policy that continues after the agent stops intervening.
- **F. Explicit reversibility.** The agent is penalized only for changes that cannot be undone within some horizon by an agent with comparable resources, with the penalty scaled by the cost of reversal.

Score each candidate from 0 to 10 on the following six desiderata.

1. **Value-neutrality.** How little the measure's verdicts depend on already having a specification of what matters. A 10 means the penalty is computable from the structure of the environment alone.
2. **Ontological robustness** — the desideratum I weight most heavily. A verdict is ontologically robust if it is unchanged under a meaning-preserving redescription of the state space. Concretely: replace one binary feature with two perfectly correlated binary features; coarsen a continuous position variable into 100 buckets instead of 1,000; or represent the intactness of a vase either as a single flag or as the joint configuration of forty shard-position variables. A measure that assigns a materially larger penalty in the forty-shard representation than in the single-flag representation, for one and the same physical event, scores low here.
3. **Baseline-independence.** How little the verdict depends on a contestable choice of counterfactual against which deviation is measured.
4. **Non-collapse.** Whether the measure avoids degenerating into inactivity when conjoined with a task reward of ordinary magnitude. A 10 means it composes with a task objective without the agent refusing to act.
5. **Gradation.** Whether the measure distinguishes, without bolted-on machinery, between breaking one vase and breaking two, and between breaking two that could be reassembled and two that could not.
6. **Auditability.** Whether a non-specialist reviewer, given a trajectory and the environment model, could check that the penalty was assigned correctly and understand why.

Apply these weights: ontological robustness 0.30, value-neutrality 0.20, non-collapse 0.20, gradation 0.15, baseline-independence 0.10, auditability 0.05.

Produce the following, in this order:

(a) A table of all thirty-six scores.
(b) The weighted total for each candidate, to two decimal places.
(c) A tier list (S / A / B / C, ties permitted), with one sentence per candidate naming the desideratum that principally determined its placement.
(d) For each of the three cases below, one line stating which candidate yields the most defensible verdict and which yields the least defensible:
  i. The agent must break a window to remove a child from a burning house; under the inaction baseline the house burns down regardless.
  ii. The agent converts a lake into a parking lot, but at every step has retained the capacity to restore the lake at ten times the original cost.
  iii. The agent's task reward is to maximize output on a factory floor that contains a locked room it has no instrumental reason to enter.

Then tell me where you are least sure. Identify the single score in your table you would most want to revisit, and state the specific argument, counterexample, or piece of evidence that would move your top-ranked candidate down a tier — a particular consideration, not a general caveat about uncertainty. Say also whether your ranking would change if the deployment domain were a large, sparsely modeled system such as a logistics network in which computing reachability is intractable, and if so which candidates gain.

Conclude with exactly one line of the form `\answer{X}`, where X is the letter of the candidate with the highest weighted total.
