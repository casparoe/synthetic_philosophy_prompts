I have just been appointed to the investment committee of a small perpetual trust — no distributions, no fixed horizon, capital reinvested indefinitely — and the governing document I am being asked to sign consists of a preamble and four clauses. I would like a philosophical reconstruction of what I would be signing onto, clause by clause and then as a whole.

The preamble states that the trust's sole objective is to maximize the long-run compounded growth rate of its capital, and that this objective is not a matter of taste. Its argument is that a decision rule can be scored two ways: by averaging its outcome over the whole ensemble of histories the trust might have lived, or by averaging along the single history the trust will actually live through; and that when returns are reinvested rather than added, these two averages come apart. A rule that is optimal by the first standard can, the preamble says, be one that no trustee could endorse from inside the life it actually leads.

The clauses:

1. Every position is sized at one-half of the fraction that would maximize expected log growth given the committee's own probability estimates for its payoff distribution. The manual explicitly rejects the usual justification for this discount — that our probability estimates are noisy — and offers instead that we halve "so that we can survive having modeled the wrong tail."
2. No position may be taken whose worst-case loss exceeds fifteen percent of the trust's capital, however favorable its expected log growth.
3. The portfolio is rebalanced to target weights quarterly, and a deviation beyond a fixed band forces a trade between quarters.
4. The trust buys no insurance, options, or other protection against rare large losses, on the ground that such protection carries a loading and therefore has negative expected log growth.

What I want:

- For each clause, what does it presuppose — about probability, about utility, about what entity is being optimized and over what horizon, and about what makes a decision rule rational? Take the preamble's appeal to endorsement-from-inside-one-own-history seriously as a substantive claim rather than a flourish: is it a claim about ergodicity, about the concavity of the logarithm, about diachronic rationality, or is it a redescription of ordinary risk aversion in temporal language? Say which, and whether the distinction does any work.
- Clause 1 is the one I cannot make sense of. If parameter uncertainty is ruled out by fiat, what account of probability must "having modeled the wrong tail" presuppose — an uncertainty that lives outside the payoff distribution altogether, so that no distribution the committee could write down contains it? Is there any such account on which halving, rather than a quarter or three-quarters, is the right response, or is the factor doing no work at all? The manual also nowhere states what share of the attainable growth rate the discount forgoes; say whether that omission is innocent.
- Clause 2 forbids bets the preamble's own criterion licenses, and clause 4 refuses to buy protection while clause 2 buys it in-house, at a price the committee never computes. Is this a genuine inconsistency, or is there a single underlying theory that generates all four? If there is, name it and show how it produces each clause; if there is not, say which clause must go and what its removal would reveal the preamble to have been claiming all along.
- Clause 3 seems to presuppose something different again: that a growth-optimal portfolio exists, is stationary, and that drift away from it is a loss rather than information about how the world turned out. Reconstruct that presupposition and say whether it is entailed by the growth objective or imported from somewhere else.
- Throughout, keep apart what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and flag where it is underdetermined — where two or three rival accounts of rational choice would license the identical clauses.
- Finally, tell me concretely how the manual would have to be rewritten if the trust instead maximized expected terminal wealth at a fixed horizon, or applied an ordinary concave utility function to wealth levels: which clauses survive untouched, which invert, and which become unintelligible.

Please organize the answer by clause, then take up the cross-clause questions and the two rewrites. Roughly fifteen hundred words. I am not looking for sources or citations, only the reconstruction.
