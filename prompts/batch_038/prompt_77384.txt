Third-year welfare econ PhD student here, and our reading group keeps running into something I can't find a word for.

Setup: a committee ranks three proposals. We elicit cardinal utilities twice — once as dollars of annual benefit, once on a 0–10 how-much-do-you-care scale. Everyone's ordinal ranking of the three is identical in both rounds, and in both rounds the winner is preferred by literally every member, so Pareto is satisfied in exactly the same way. But sum the first round's numbers and proposal A wins; sum the second round's and proposal B wins. Nothing about anybody's preferences changed — only the units we happened to ask in. Same thing if you just double one member's scale: the social ranking moves.

The standard reply in the room is "well, utilities aren't interpersonally comparable," but that isn't quite what's biting us, because nobody there doubts comparison is possible in principle. What's biting us is that the answer tracks the questionnaire. And Harsanyi is supposed to get you summing out of Pareto plus vNM rationality, which makes it look like the units got fixed somewhere along the way; I just cant point to where. Then someone says the impartial-observer construction settles it, and the objector says fine — but then the theorem is really about the observer's own scale and the members' preferences did no work.

So does this have a name? I want to know what the nearest established concepts are, how what I described differs from each of them — including from ordinary scale complaints about index numbers, and from Arrow-type results, which are about ordinal profiles anyway — and which one actually fits best. If nothing fits, coin me a term with a real definition and tell me where its edges are: at what point does this stop being a technical caveat about representations and start being an objection to the whole program? A bare label won't help me, I need the carving.
