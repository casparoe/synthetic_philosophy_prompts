Our forecasting team has been asked to estimate the probability that a newly created independent anti-corruption agency in a mid-income democracy still has its statutory powers intact ten years after founding. The difficulty we have run into is not the data but the selection of the class. Four candidate reference classes, each with an adequate sample size and each defended by a different colleague, yield the following historical survival rates:

- all independent agencies of any kind created in democracies since 1945: 0.71
- all anti-corruption bodies created anywhere, regardless of regime type: 0.34
- all independent agencies created in the same world region within the last thirty years: 0.52
- agencies whose enabling statute passed a legislature in which the sponsoring coalition held a working majority: 0.85

Each class is defined by genuine similarities to our target case, no class is a subset of another, and the spread between the most optimistic and the most pessimistic exceeds 0.5.

Against that background, I would like your credence in the following claim, which I will call the Insolvability Thesis:

There is no general rule for selecting the correct reference class for a single-case probability that (a) can be applied by someone who does not already know the outcome or its causes, and (b) is not merely a restatement of the instruction to use the class defined by the features relevant to the outcome. A rule counts as a solution only if it picks out one of the four classes above, or some fifth class, as correct in this case on grounds available in advance.

I want a number, but more than that I want the derivation exposed rather than buried. Please assign a rough probability to each of the following four sub-claims, justify each in one or two sentences, and show explicitly how they combine into your overall figure:

1. That the classical instruction to use the narrowest reference class for which adequate statistics exist determines a unique class here, rather than trading narrowness against sample size in a way that admits no principled stopping point.
2. That a relevance-based rule, on which the right class is the one defined by all and only the features statistically relevant to the outcome, can be applied without antecedent causal knowledge, given that the available cases number in the tens or low hundreds.
3. That the subjectivist dissolution succeeds: that the single-case frequency was never well-posed, so the spread above reflects legitimate differences in information and prior rather than error by anyone, and nothing remains for a solution to the reference class problem to accomplish.
4. That current practice, meaning learned similarity metrics, hierarchical models that partially pool across several classes at once, and regressions that weight the features instead of choosing among classes, constitutes a genuine solution rather than a relocation of the problem into the choice of features, priors, and pooling structure, which is itself a disguised reference-class selection.

Then answer three further questions: which of these four terms your final number is most sensitive to; which sub-claim you hold least confidently; and what specific argument or result would move your credence furthest and in which direction.

Finally, a colleague in quantitative social science puts about 0.2 on the Insolvability Thesis, while I put about 0.75. Using your own decomposition, identify the term on which the two of us are most likely diverging, and say which of us is probably operating with a weaker notion of what would count as a solution.
