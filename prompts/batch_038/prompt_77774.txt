I belong to a small reading group that has spent the last several weeks on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual worlds — whether a house built over a decade inside a persistent game is a real object, whether destroying it is a real harm, whether the answer changes if the item is traded for money on an exchange outside the game. The discussion has stalled in a familiar rut: one side says virtual objects are simply digital objects and therefore fully real, so the harms are fully real too; the other says the whole thing is play-acting and the only genuine loss is of time and attention. At our next meeting I have been given fifteen minutes to defend a position that neither side likes, and I want to get the argument right before I walk in. The position is this: a player can be genuinely wronged by the destruction or appropriation of their virtual possessions even in circumstances where they are not harmed at all, and once that is granted, the metaphysical question of whether virtual objects are real turns out to be doing no ethical work whatsoever — it is a question the moral analysis can afford to leave entirely open.

To make the argument bite, please build it around two cases I have designed to sever the link between destruction and setback, rather than around the intuitive ones everyone already has. First case: a player's long-built in-game house is razed by another player; the operating company, whose terms the player accepted, restores the house from a server backup within the hour, structurally identical and with its contents intact, at no cost and with no lost playtime. Second case: a rare item is stolen from a player's inventory, used by the thief for a week, and then returned undamaged, and the game's economy is large enough that the item's market price never moved. In both, the standard counterfactual account of harm seems to deliver the verdict that the player is no worse off than they would otherwise have been, and the standard temporal-comparison account likewise finds no lasting deficit. Argue, nonetheless, that something was done to that player that they have standing to object to, and identify precisely what that something is if it is not a setback to their interests. I want the argument to say what grounds the entitlement being violated: labor expended, recognition by the community of players, the conventions internal to the game, or something else — and I want it to confront the obvious deflationary reply that the end-user license agreement grants only a revocable license, so the player never owned anything and cannot have been wronged in respect of property.

Please also handle three objections explicitly rather than waving at them. The felt-distress objection: the player was shaken and their evening was ruined, so there is a harm after all, located in their mental states. The identity objection: the restored house is a numerical replica and not the very thing built over ten years, so the counterfactual comparison was misdescribed and there is in fact an irrecoverable loss. And the collapse objection: if the account is supposed to cover permanent destruction too — the item deleted beyond recovery, or the servers shut down for good — then it will have to admit a setback there, at which point it looks like the harm view with extra steps. I would like to know where your account can absorb each objection, where it has to concede ground, and what the residual difference is between a wrong that involves harm and one that does not. Finally, tell me the single strongest case you can make against the position I have asked you to defend, since that is the one my group will press me on. Argue from the structure of the concepts and the cases; I am not asking for a survey of who has said what, and I would rather you reason it through than gesture at the literature.
