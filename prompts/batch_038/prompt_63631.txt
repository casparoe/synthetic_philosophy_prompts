I am the grader for a take-home question in an upper-level philosophy of social science course. The question was worth 25 points; below is the question and one student's answer, reproduced as submitted.

**Question.** Popper holds that historicism trades on a confusion between trends and laws. Explain that distinction, and explain why, on Popper's account, it blocks the project of a theoretical history — a social science that forecasts the future course of history in the way physics forecasts an eclipse. Then assess this objection: Popper's argument turns on our inability to predict the future growth of our own knowledge, but a forecasting system standing outside the epistemic community it models, or one vastly better informed than we are, need not share that limitation; so the argument at most shows the limits of our predictive capacities, not the impossibility of historical prediction.

**Student's answer.** "Popper defines historicism as an approach to the social sciences that takes historical prediction to be its principal aim and holds that the aim can be met by uncovering the laws or trends underlying historical development. The trend/law distinction is the hinge of his critique. A trend is a claim about a pattern observed over some period under some conditions — rising productivity in a particular industrial economy, say — and such a claim is singular and tied to those conditions; a law is universal, and combines with initial conditions to yield predictions. Historicists mistake the first for the second, so their forecasts are extrapolations carrying no guarantee of continuation. This is why Popper thinks the project fails in principle rather than merely in practice: a theoretical history would have to be built on laws of succession, and what the historicist actually possesses are conditional statements about trends, which belong to a different logical category. The deeper objection is that historicist doctrines are unfalsifiable — they can absorb any counterexample by appending auxiliary claims about stages not yet reached or about the dialectic working through contrary appearances — and it is this that makes them unscientific.

On the objection concerning a predictor outside the system, Popper has a ready reply in the Oedipus effect: a social prediction, once made, alters the situation it predicts, so any such forecast is self-defeating. A super-forecaster faces the same difficulty and a worse one, since its own activity would have to enter its model. The objection therefore fails.

Overall, then, Popper is right that no laws of historical development can be had at all, which leaves the social sciences not with explanation but with the interpretation of unique historical processes."
