I am drafting a paper on the equalisandum debate for a political philosophy workshop, and the argument I have built to organise it now seems to prove far too much. It runs as follows.

(1) A metric of distributive justice is correct only if it measures what ultimately makes people's lives go well or badly; anything that merely serves that end is the wrong thing to equalise, since equal quantities of a means yield unequal quantities of the end whenever conversion rates differ between people.
(2) Primary goods and resources are means of that kind: the wheelchair user, the person living with a chronic illness, the sole carer, and the able-bodied single adult in a temperate climate do not get the same out of the same income. So by (1) resources are the wrong metric.
(3) Capabilities are means of the same kind: two people with identical capability sets achieve very different functionings, and identical functionings do not produce identical lives — the ascetic who fasts by choice and the person who goes hungry by necessity, the person who relishes a repetitive routine and the person it crushes. So by (1) capabilities are the wrong metric as well.
(4) Functionings are means again, this time to welfare, since what a given condition contributes to a life depends on the person's desires and dispositions. So by (1) functionings are the wrong metric too.
(5) Yet welfare cannot be the metric: it is hostage to Dworkin's Louis, who has cultivated a taste for rare eggs and old claret, and to preferences adapted to deprivation, and an egalitarianism that pays for the first while ignoring the second is not egalitarianism at all.
(6) Therefore (1) is false, no metric is correct, and the search for the right equalisandum is either empty or interminable.

I want to hold on to (2) and to (5). Which step should I cut, why did it look unobjectionable in isolation, and what does cutting it cost the position I would like to end up with?
