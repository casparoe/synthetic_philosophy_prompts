I sit on the Initiative Review Commission of a state I'll call Meridian. Treat Meridian as a jurisdiction you know nothing about beyond what I state here, and do not substitute any real state's law for the rules I'm giving you.

Fifteen years ago Meridian adopted a constitutional initiative process drafted explicitly on the Swiss federal model: petitioners must collect a fixed number of signatures within an eighteen-month window, and measures that qualify go to the voters, who must approve both a popular majority and a majority of the state's counties. The legislature may attach a counter-proposal to a qualified measure; when both texts are approved, a third preference question asks voters which of the two is to take effect.

My Commission is a gatekeeping body only. It may withhold certification on exactly four grounds: (a) the measure conflicts with a short closed list of fundamental rights that are entrenched above the reach of amendment; (b) it lacks unity of subject, meaning it puts two or more distinct proposals to voters who might accept one and reject the other; (c) it cannot be implemented as written, because it is impossible to execute or internally contradictory in operation; (d) it is inconsistent with Article I, section 2 of the state constitution, which reserves to the people "the continuing power to alter or abolish their government." We may not assess a measure's merits, wisdom, or likely effects, and we must publish written reasons for any decision. Certification is due thirty days after filing; our hearing is in ten.

The case is Measure 214, styled by its sponsors as the Forethought Act. As filed, it does four things. Section 1 entrenches a fiscal rule: the state may not issue general-obligation debt, and each budget must be structurally balanced. Section 2 provides that the rule may be repealed or amended only by a two-thirds vote of each legislative house together with a majority of voters in a referendum held no earlier than nineteen years after enactment, and that this referendum requires sixty percent for repeal; the drafter's stated reason for nineteen years is that it is roughly one generation. Section 3 bars the placement on any future ballot of an initiative that conflicts with Section 1 or Section 2, and designates this Commission as the sole and final judge of what counts as a conflict, with its determinations unreviewable by any court. Section 4 is a precedence clause: Measure 214 prevails over any other constitutional or statutory provision, whether enacted before or after it, including any counter-proposal adopted by the legislature.

The legislature has attached a counter-proposal. It achieves the same fiscal objective through an annually renewable statute plus an independent citizen audit panel, with no entrenchment and no supermajority requirement. Under the charter it takes effect only if Measure 214 passes and voters prefer it on the third question.

The Commission is split two to two, and I hold the deciding vote. Two colleagues hold that Section 2 and Section 3 fall squarely under ground (d): a majority that disables future majorities from reconsidering its choice, and then installs an unreviewable arbiter of what may even be voted on, has not exercised the continuing power to alter government but attempted to end it. The other two hold that ground (d) cannot bear that weight, on pain of self-defeat: the constitution is itself entrenched, Article I's amendment rules are themselves hard to change, and if a sovereign people may never bind themselves then no constitution binds anyone, which would make ground (d) invalidate the very document it comes from. They add that entrenchment of a debt rule is unremarkable compared with the entrenched rights already listed under ground (a). Everyone agrees Section 4 creates a puzzle nobody has solved: if Measure 214 prevails over any provision including the counter-proposal, is the preference question a nullity, and does that make the measure unimplementable under ground (c), or is the contradiction merely apparent? The sponsors have offered, at the hearing, to amend Section 4 so that the measure yields to the counter-proposal wherever voters prefer it, and Measure 214 also contains an express non-severability clause declaring Sections 1 through 4 a single indivisible proposal.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate to the other four commissioners before the hearing, roughly 1,200 words, with headings, and a bottom line they can act on: certify, refuse to certify, or certify subject to stated conditions, together with our recommended ballot title and the exact wording of the preference question. It must resolve the Section 4 puzzle rather than flag it; it must say whether the non-severability clause means we cannot strike Sections 2 through 4 while keeping Section 1, and what the sponsors' offered amendment does to that question; and it must give the strongest version of the losing side's case in terms that colleague could recognize and reply to.

Two further requirements. First, cite nothing and invent nothing: no case names, no statutory references, no scholarship, no foreign jurisdictions whose rules you would have to state exactly. Reason from the charter provisions I have set out and from arguments you can state in your own voice; where it helps to note how other systems handle a problem of this kind, describe the practice generically. Second, mark the epistemic status of your reasoning as you go. Distinguish what you take to be settled — what follows from the text I have given you, and what people who work on constitutional amendment and referendum design would regard as common ground — from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position, in either direction, as though it were the consensus. Where the right answer turns on a theoretical dispute with no agreed resolution, say so plainly, identify which of the two sides the dispute favors, and explain what would change if it were settled.
