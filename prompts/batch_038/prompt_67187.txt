"In the drug case Taurek discusses, he denies that we should simply save the greater number. Explain his reasoning, explain the procedure he proposes instead, and say how Scanlon's contractualist account responds to him. End with one sentence on whether Taurek thinks the numbers never count or only sometimes."

the student's answer:

"Taurek starts from the fact that you can't compare one person's happiness against another's, so there's no way of saying that five lives saved is better than one. When he asks whether five deaths are five times as bad as one his answer is no, because there is no single subject who loses five lives — the losses belong to five separate people, so the sum doesn't correspond to anything real. Since no claim outweighs David's, each person has an equally strong claim to be saved, and it follows that we should flip a coin, that being the only way to treat equal claims equally. He does allow the numbers count sometimes, in cases where you already have a special obligation to the people at risk. Scanlon arrives at the same place by a different route: instead of adding up goods he asks which principles no one could reasonably reject, and a principle that settles the choice by a coin flip is one each person could accept, so Scanlon also endorses giving everyone an equal chance. Both are non-utilitarians who refuse to let the numbers do the moral work."
