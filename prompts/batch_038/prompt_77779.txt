I'm one of five members on my state's board of pardons and we take up a petition next month that's got the panel split down the middle, so I want to circulate something in writing beforehand.

Facts, as far as they matter. Petitioner is serving 14 years for an armed robbery at a check-cashing place. During discovery on an unrelated appeal last year a confidential-informant file surfaced that had been withheld at trial. It shows the CI was wearing a body wire with a panic function — a two-second hold that would have put a surveillance team inside the lobby within roughly 40 seconds — and that the handler's written standing order was to trigger it at the very first sign the petitioner was getting cold feet, because the department wanted the arrest made at the counter with the weapon visible rather than out on the street. There are also transcript excerpts of the CI reporting in real time that the petitioner never wavered: no hesitation, no second thoughts, no questions. The panic button was never used. Nobody stepped in. What happened, happened entirely on the petitioner's own initiative, start to finish.

His counsel's argument — and the one two of my colleagues find persuasive — goes like this. The state engineered a situation in which that robbery was guarenteed no matter what the petitioner did. Had he changed his mind at the door, he'd have ended up in handcuffs anyway, with the same intent on the record and the same plan known to the police an hour ahead of time. So he could not have done otherwise, the alternatives were removed by the state's own machinery, and you can't hold a man fully responsible for something that was fixed in advance. Commutation to time served, or at minimum resentencing.

The rest of us think that's sophistry but I can't yet say why in a way that isn't just "he wasn't compelled, end of story." He wasn't. Nobody made him do anything. But I can feel that there's something his lawyer is getting at that I don't have a clean answer to, and I'd rather the memo face it than wave it off. Two things in particular worry me. One: some of us will say he did still have alternatives — he could have hesitated, could have been taken at the door instead of at the counter, the outcome was fixed but the route to it wasn't — and I want the memo to say whether that kind of leftover alternative is worth anything, because to me it sounds like a technicality that dodges the real question. Two: even if he deserves exactly what he got, there may be a separate point about whether the state is in a position to be the one handing it out, having built the machine that made it inevitable. I don't know if that's any good, but I want it developed at its strongest and then either adopted or rejected with reasons.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. A recommendation — grant, deny, or grant in part — with the reasoning laid out so the two members on the other side can actually argue with it rather than just being outvoted. Beyond the two worries above I need it to give us a line we could apply to the next petition of this shape without the rule collapsing into either "surveillance never counts for anything" or "any operation with a failsafe entitles you to commutation," because in basically every sting and every monitored transaction the police could have prevented the offense and chose not to, and if his argument works I can't see where it stops. Say plainly whether the absence of alternatives does any work on blameworthiness here, and if it doesn't, explain what the intuition that it does is actually latching onto and why that thing is attached to the wrong variable.

One of my kids tells me this is a famous thought experiment in the free will literature, something about a neurosurgeon who would have flipped a switch but never had to. Fine. But no member of this board has heard of it and the memo should not read like a philosophy seminar — no jargon, no name-dropping, no bibliography, just argument we can act on. Four pages max.
