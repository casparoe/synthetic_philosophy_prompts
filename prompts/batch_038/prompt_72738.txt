I am trying to fix the standard before I look at any candidates, in the domain of whether video-game characters matter morally — whether there can be direct duties to, or wrongs against, non-player characters, playable characters, and the fictional persons a game presents. Fix the purpose as follows: I want a specification demanding enough to serve as a shared standard between people who think there is a substantive question here and people who think the appearance of one is an artefact of confusing fiction-directed attitudes with moral concern. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of who holds what; I want the reasoning.

Deliverable: the requirements an adequate theory in this area must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and tag it explicitly as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (saying which camp, and what that camp gains by insisting on it). At minimum, address the following — my list is not exhaustive, and I would rather you add requirements I have missed than pad the ones I have named.

1. **Target.** What is the theory a theory of? If it relocates the question — from the character as patient to the player's character, to the fiction, or to the practice of designing and playing — it owes an account of why the intuition that there is a direct duty is not simply a mistake, and it must state its relocation policy up front rather than by stealth.

2. **The case battery.** The theory must deliver verdicts on, or explain why no verdict is owed for: (i) sustained, gratuitous cruelty toward a richly authored single-player character with a biography and voiced reactions; (ii) the same behaviour toward a procedurally generated character with no memory, no biography, and no persistence across sessions; (iii) reloading a save afterwards, so that neither physically nor fictionally does anything remain of the act; (iv) irreversible death under permadeath rules, where loss does accumulate; (v) an avatar controlled by another person in multiplayer; (vi) a character modelled on, voiced by, or representing a real person or the victims of a real atrocity; (vii) a game whose own fiction entails that the character suffers; (viii) the same character across millions of copies and playthroughs — one patient wronged many times, many patients, or none; (ix) an unplayed game or a shut-down server, so that nothing is instantiated at all; (x) a game whose fiction has the character consenting to, or wanting, the harm.

3. **Distinctions it may not collapse.** Having moral status and how much; being wronged and an act's being wrong; nonexistent, fictitious, and instantiated; a direct duty and an indirect duty that happens to have the same content; and, above all, "this matters" and "this matters to the player."

4. **Constraints from outside the topic.** It cannot require anyone to believe that a current game character is phenomenally conscious; it should be consistent with the familiar puzzle that people have genuine feelings toward characters they take not to exist; and it should not treat reading about a torture and playing it as obviously equivalent, or obviously inequivalent, without saying which and why.

5. **Agency and design.** Whether and why the fact that the player acts, rather than merely witnesses, makes a moral difference — and something about the obligations of designers and publishers, not only of players.

6. **Joint unsatisfiability.** Identify any subset of these requirements that cannot all be met, and argue for it rather than merely suspecting it. My own suspicion is that the following trio fails: the wrong must be a wrong *to the character* rather than a defect in the player; fictional characters are not the sort of thing that can be wronged; and the theory must explain why many people find (i) more troubling than (ii). Tell me whether that trio is genuinely inconsistent, or whether I have mis-described one of its members — and if some requirement must go, which one you would drop and what the theory loses with it.

7. **Most exclusionary requirement.** Which single requirement on your list knocks out the largest number of otherwise plausible candidates.

8. **Brief scoring.** How the views one actually encounters — an indirect-duty view on the Kantian model, a virtue- or character-based view, a contractualist view keyed to who could be a party, a make-believe-based view, and a view granting narrative persons some genuine but slight status — fare against the list. Keep this short; the weight belongs on the criteria.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work seriously on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position — including one you favour — as though it were the consensus. Where you label a requirement a camp prejudice, name the camp and say what is at stake for it.
