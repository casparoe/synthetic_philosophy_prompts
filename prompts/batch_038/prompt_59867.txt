I'm auditing a philosophy of religion seminar and we're doing a unit on middle knowledge, and before I write my response paper I want to figure out what I actually believe by going case by case instead of picking a label first. Here's where I land, running on one thought experiment about a person I'll call Alice:

1. If God puts Alice in circumstances where she's handed an unmarked envelope of cash and told nobody will ever find out, she'd freely pocket it. I think God knows that before he decides anything about creating, and I think his creating that world doesn't make him blameworthy for the theft — Alice is, entirely on her own.

2. But suppose God has two worlds on offer that are equally good in every other respect, and in one Alice pockets the envelope and in the other she doesn't. If he picks the first, I think that's a bad choice on his part — not the theft, but the choosing. I can feel this starting to eat into 1 and I don't know how.

3. "If Socrates had been handed the envelope, he'd have pocketed it" — where Socrates is never created at all. I think this just isn't true or false. There's no fact of the matter, nothing to make it so.

4. "If Alice had been handed the envelope on Tuesday instead of Wednesday, she'd have pocketed it" — a real person, a circumstance that will never actually occur. This feels straightforwardly true to me, and I cannot say what makes it different from 3 that isn't just restating my gut.

5. God weakly actualizes the theft: he arranges the setup knowing the conditional, but nothing he does is even part of what brings her choice about. I still want to call this manipulation. If you engineered a situation knowing precisely what it would produce, you'd be responsible for the product, and God's knowing it precisely makes things worse for him, not better. This is the one my friend in the seminar says I have to give up, since 5 contradicts 1 by construction.

6. "If God had been in Alice's shoes, he'd have left the envelope." True, and God knows it trivially. Which makes me suspect the whole grounding problem only bites for created wills and doesn't generalize — but I can't tell if that's an escape hatch or a confession that the objection was never very deep to begin with.

So: give me your best single principle that captures as many of these as possible — is it Molinism, open theism, something Báñezian, or something you'd have to build bespoke? And say plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Two cases I haven't listed that I want it to rule on: (a) is "if Alice were handed the envelope she'd have about a 60% chance of pocketing it" something God knows, and does dealing in chances rather than certainties change verdict 5? and (b) a person whose counterfactuals are such that every world God could create containing her involves at least one free sin of hers — does creating her wrong her, wrong nobody, or something else?

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion. Item 1 is the principle stated in one sentence (or, if you think no single principle survives, say that and use items 2–4 for the three nearest misses). Items 2–4 are each one clause of it plus which of my numbered cases forces that clause. Item 5 is the verdict or verdicts I have to give up, plus one-line answers to (a) and (b).
