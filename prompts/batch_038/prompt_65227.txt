Last week I tried to explain to my brother-in-law why I think most confident takes about the stock market are overconfident, and he cut me off with "if you're so smart, why aren't you rich?" He uses it on everyone: weather forecasters, his accountant, anybody who says housing is overpriced. He's not rich himself, which I found funny at the time and have been chewing on since, because by his own rule that ought to tell us something about him.

Anyway, I keep thinking that line is doing several different jobs at once and that some of them are pretty weak, but I cant seperate them cleanly on my own. Could you lay out the different precise claims that question could be making, from the boring ones to the ambitious ones, and for each say what it has to assume in order to be true and whether it would actually license him ending the conversation? The ones I've sort of identified so far:

- a plain statistical claim about how intelligence and income line up, which I suspect is true-ish, boring, and gets him nothing;
- some kind of rule about evidence, like a theory of markets that can't be cashed out in profit isn't worth believing — I don't know what would have to hold for that to be a good rule rather than just a gotcha;
- something about arbitrage, where the idea is that if an edge were real somebody would already have grabbed it, so its still being there proves it isn't one. My half remembered version of this is that if everyone knows everyone else is rational, nobody would ever trade anything, which can't be right because people trade all day, so I'm obviously mangling whatever the real result says — can you say what it actually says and what it actually needs;
- a version where being smart buys you less risk instead of more money, so the question is comparing two things that aren't the same currency.

Please include readings I haven't thought of too. And here's one of my own that I want you to attack rather than just nod at: the line seems like it can only ever be aimed at people who already had the money and the nerve to act, so it can't touch somebody who was right but never placed a bet — which would make it useless exactly where he uses it, on armchair people like me. Or am I just building myself a dodge? Same question about the fact that he isn't rich: does that actually count against him, or is there a reading where the line only applies to people claiming to have found an edge, and he's just a guy who runs a landscaping company?

One more thing. I don't want references or a reading list, I just want to know which parts of all this are settled among people who study markets for a living and which parts are genuinely argued about, so please don't hand me the contested stuff as if it were the consensus. I'd like to know where I'd get shouted down and where I'd just be repeating what everybody already thinks.
