I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and this year I am adding a session built around John Carpenter's 1982 film The Thing — the Carpenter version specifically, not the 1951 adaptation and not Campbell's novella. The students will already have done the standard week on definitions of life: the NASA-style gloss of life as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution, the familiar parade of borderline cases (viruses, prions, mitochondria and other endosymbionts, mules, sterile worker castes, biofilms, slime molds), and the problem of biological individuality. What I do not want is another session in which the film gets waved at as an illustration of the category of life breaking down. I want a rigorous reading that treats the film as raising difficulties the standard cases do not, and that is equally willing to say where the biological lens is simply a bad fit.

The features I want pressed are these. The organism does not merely sit near a boundary of the definition; it propagates by assimilating and then imitating hosts rather than by descent, it appears to have no body plan of its own, and its parts may each qualify as organisms in their own right — which is exactly the conjecture Macready voices before the hot-wire test on the men's blood samples, and on which the success of that test depends. Meanwhile the station biologist, Blair, reaches his conclusions about the organism by modeling how fast it could spread, and then acts on them by destroying the vehicles and the radio. And the ending refuses to settle whether either of the two surviving men is human.

Please write a reading of the film through the definition-of-life literature, working from your own understanding of both. I do not want references of any kind, and I do not want verbatim quotation from the film or from any philosopher: describe scenes and positions in your own words.

Structure the response in these six sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** (about 120 words). State your overall verdict up front: whether the film genuinely engages the problem of defining life or is only usefully pressed into that service, and which single feature of it does the most philosophical work.

2. **The inventory** (about 300 words). Take a standard set of criteria — self-maintenance and metabolism, response to stimuli, growth, reproduction, heredity and variation, homeostasis, individuality — and score the organism against each. Run the scoring at three levels: the intact imitated body, the assimilating colony, and the single cell. Wherever the verdict changes with the level, say so explicitly, and say what that implies about whether life is a property of things or of levels of organization.

3. **Cells as organisms: the blood test** (about 350 words). Set out what the hot-wire test presupposes — that a detached sample of the organism's blood behaves as an autonomous defender of itself, while an ordinary human sample does not — and then assess it. Is Macready's conjecture a screenwriting convenience, or a substantive claim about self-preservation as a property of parts rather than wholes? Address the tension the test depends on, namely imitation that is perfect at the level of form and chemistry but imperfect at the level of the imitated part's own interests: is that coherent, and what does the literature on biological individuality make of it, given the way biologists treat cells, colonies, and social insects as rival candidates for individualhood?

4. **Imitation without descent** (about 350 words). The criteria we teach are built for entities related by common ancestry, where reproduction and heredity do the individuating work; this organism instead propagates by converting hosts into copies. What does that do to a definition framed in terms of Darwinian evolution — does the creature refute it, expose it as silently restricted to a lineage-shaped world, or fall outside its intended scope altogether? Bring in the duplicate-style thought experiments about entities that satisfy every functional criterion for membership in a kind while lacking the history the kind is normally taken to require, and say which answer the film itself endorses, functional or historical, judging by how its characters attempt to verify humanity and by what the unresolved ending declines to resolve.

5. **Where the lens fails** (about 250 words). Be honest about how much of the film is about testimony, trust, and the epistemology of suspicion rather than about the metaphysics of life. Identify the scenes where the biological reading earns its keep and those where it is imposed at a cost, and say what is lost by the imposition — including whether Blair's modeling-and-sabotage sequence is better read as a scene about containment decisions and their justification than as a scene about definitions.

6. **What the film contributes** (about 200 words). Say what, if anything, the film gives the philosophy of biology that an argument or a tabletop thought experiment cannot — for instance, a case in which the criteria come apart not along a spectrum of marginal instances but within a single entity that is at once organism, colony, parasite, and copy — and what follows from that case being presented as horror rather than as a puzzle.

Aim for roughly 1,500 words in total.
