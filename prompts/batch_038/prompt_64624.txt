I was a hydrologist for thirty-eight years, the last twenty of them directing a state water-resources agency, and I have taken up political philosophy in retirement with rather more leisure than is good for me. What has held my attention is the difficulty democratic theory appears to have in saying who constitutes the electorate for a given decision — the problem, as I gather it is called, of the boundary of the demos. Two criteria recur in what I have read. The first would enfranchise all whose interests are affected by the decision; the second would enfranchise all who are subject to the authority that makes it. In the examples I have encountered, the comparison almost always runs in one direction: the affected-interests criterion is the more generous, sweeping in resident non-citizens, foreigners who live downstream of a policy, and generations not yet born, while the subjection criterion holds the line at those who actually live under the deciding authority's rules.

I want a case that runs the other way, because I suspect the standard examples are chosen rather than found. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

1. Domain and realism. The decision must be a water-allocation or river-management decision of a kind that genuinely occurs in a federal system with interstate compacts or basin authorities, and it must be set in the present day. No science fiction, no idealised agents, no stipulated moral psychologies — and, if you please, no climate change, no unborn generations, no non-human animals or artificial agents, and no extraterritorial jurisdiction of the military-bases-and-embassies variety. I spent my career inside these institutions and I will notice if the plumbing does not work.

2. Three groups. The case must contain (A) a substantial, identifiable class of persons who are subject to the deciding body's rules but whose interests are not affected by this particular decision; (B) a substantial, identifiable class whose interests are deeply affected by this decision but who are not subject to the deciding body's rules; and (C) the ordinary electorate, who are both. Groups A and B must each be more than a handful of people, and each must be picked out without borrowing the vocabulary of either criterion to do the picking.

3. Direction of the divergence. On a decision-specific reading of affected interests, group A must come in under the subjection criterion and be excluded by the affected-interests criterion, while group B does the reverse. The affected-interests criterion must be the more restrictive of the two in your case.

4. Neutrality of description. The scenario itself must be a single paragraph written in purely descriptive terms: no ought, no right to, no entitled, no legitimate, no wrongly excluded. I want a theorist who rejects the affected-interests criterion outright to be able to accept every sentence of the description without swallowing any of your conclusions.

5. No cheating by redescription. State for group A what their actual stake in the decision is and why that stake fails the affected-interests test, and for group B what authority does govern them and why it is not the deciding authority. If either statement can only be made by fudging, say so plainly rather than fudging.

6. A live question. The deciding body must be one whose franchise could conceivably be redrawn for this decision without the body ceasing to exist — a commission with allocated seats, for instance. Otherwise the question of who should vote is moot and the case proves nothing.

Having set out the scenario, please also supply the following.

7. The derivations. For each of the two criteria and each of the three groups, the verdict together with the clause of the criterion that produces it, laid out so that I can check whether the verdicts are genuinely generated by the criteria or merely written into the facts.

8. The scope test. Both criteria are ambiguous between application to a single decision and application to the institution as a whole. Re-run the derivations on the institution-wide reading and tell me whether the divergence survives. This is the part I most expect to break, and I would rather it broke visibly.

9. The load-bearing feature. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and state what remains when it is removed: does the case collapse into agreement between the two criteria, or does it turn into a different problem altogether?

10. The best attack. Give me at least two moves a defender of the subjection criterion would make against your own case — a broadened account of what it is to be subject to coercive rules, a denial that group B's interests are political in the relevant sense, or something better than these.

11. The circularity. In your case, who would have to decide whether to enlarge the electorate, and does the case offer any route out of the familiar difficulty that an existing electorate cannot coherently be asked to vote itself out of existence?

Finally, and this matters to me as someone trained to distrust a clean result: tell me how confident you are that you have actually met specifications 1 through 6 rather than quietly bent one of them, name the constraint you think is under the greatest strain, and single out the one claim in your answer of which you are least sure. If the case can only be built by relaxing something, tell me what you relaxed and what the cost is. I would far rather have an honest construction than a tidy one.
