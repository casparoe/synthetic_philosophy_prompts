I'm teaching a 75-minute session in an upper-level seminar that is split roughly evenly between philosophy majors and cognitive science majors. The topic is the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, and the way that distinction structures global workspace theory. The students have met the hard problem and the "what is it like" formulation, so they arrive disposed to treat consciousness as a single mysterious property; none of them has read Block, and they will have no idea why anyone would want two concepts where they think one will do. There is a whiteboard and nothing else: no slides, no video clips, no live demonstrations requiring equipment beyond markers.

Please give me a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Specifically:

1. The order of presentation. What has to be established before the distinction can be stated at all, and what depends on what, such that the distinction arrives as a solution to a difficulty the students are already feeling rather than as a terminological decree. I want the dependency structure made explicit, with the reason each step must precede the next.

2. The carrying case. I want the whole session built on one experimental paradigm, and I want you to choose it and defend the choice against the obvious alternatives — change blindness, backward-masked priming, partial report of a briefly presented letter array, and the attentional blink are the candidates I have in mind. Say what features the chosen paradigm must have in order to pull the distinction apart, and what it must lack, since a paradigm that is too suggestive of a simple two-store picture will do damage later.

3. The whiteboard. Describe what I actually draw or write at each stage — the arrangement of boxes and arrows, or whatever you prefer — and identify which single drawing will have to stay up and be returned to for the rest of the hour.

4. The misconceptions, in the order they will form. I expect at least four: that access consciousness just means verbal report, so that the theory is only about what people say; that phenomenal consciousness means an ineffable raw feel and is therefore not a fit subject for theorizing; that the distinction licenses a two-box picture in which some content sits in the inaccessible box and other content in the accessible one; and that global workspace theory is either trivially true or simply irrelevant to phenomenal consciousness. Tell me at which precise point in the sequence each should be headed off, and by what move.

5. Where the session has to concede rather than gloss. There is at least one place where the honest position is that the framework does not settle the question — most obviously over whether there is phenomenology in unattended or locally processed content. Give me a sentence I can say aloud that names the difficulty without either dissolving it or letting it swallow the hour.

6. The closing check. One question, posed in the final five minutes, whose answers will discriminate the students who have grasped the distinction from those who have merely acquired the vocabulary. Tell me what a good answer sounds like and what a fluent-sounding bad answer sounds like, since the bad answers here are the ones that use all the right terms.

7. What to leave out, and what to say instead. I will be tempted to bring in higher-order thought accounts, first-order representationalism, recurrent processing as a rival, the dispute over whether animals and infants have the relevant architectures, and the challenge from illusion and transparency arguments. Tell me which of these to cut, and give me the sentence that lets me cut it in front of the class without seeming to be dodging it.
