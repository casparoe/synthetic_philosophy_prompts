I am drafting a position paper on the ethics of price controls and want to fix one claim precisely enough that a number is meaningful rather than evasive. The set-up is as follows.

**Policy R.** A city imposes a ceiling on the rent of every existing tenancy, set at a fixed percentage below the prevailing market level. New construction is exempt: units first let after the ceiling takes effect may be priced freely, and nothing in the policy restricts conversion, demolition, or the withdrawal of units from the rental market. Paired with it, the city funds from general revenues a transfer to any household that can be identified as worse off under R than it would have been in its absence — in practice, chiefly households priced out of the tightened market, and those displaced by conversion or withdrawal.

**Proposition.** Policy R, so paired with that compensating transfer, is morally permissible even granting the empirical premise that the ceiling reduces the quantity and quality of rental housing available to low-income households over a ten-year horizon.

Give me your credence that this proposition is true, and then give me the decomposition that produces it. At a minimum, break the question out along these lines, though you should add, drop, or reorder terms where you think my framing is wrong:

1. Whether compensating the identifiable losers can in principle convert an otherwise impermissible redistribution into a permissible one, or whether the permissibility of R turns on something the transfer cannot touch — the manner in which the loss is imposed, the standing of the parties, the difference between redirecting a burden and creating one.
2. Whether the compensating transfer as specified is one a real city could actually administer, and what happens to the proposition if it could not: does the permissibility claim survive the failure of its own institutional precondition, or was it hostage to that precondition all along?
3. How much of the disvalue in the empirical premise attaches to statistical, unnameable losers — the household that never finds the unit it would have found — rather than to assignable individuals, and whether that distinction carries independent moral weight here or merely epistemic weight.
4. Your probability that the empirical premise is itself correct, given what you take the evidence on supply response, maintenance, and tenant mobility to show, and how that uncertainty should enter the moral calculation rather than being bracketed by the stipulation.

Assign each term a number with one or two sentences of justification, show explicitly how you combine them into the final credence, and flag any term you suspect I have double-counted or mis-specified. Then identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive — the one where a modest shift in your estimate would move the answer furthest — and state what single piece of evidence or argument would produce that shift, and whether it would work by changing a term's value or by changing what the proposition means.

Two closing notes. I am not asking for references or a literature survey; I want the reasoning behind the numbers, and I would rather have a well-argued estimate than a hedged catalogue of positions. Finally, state in one or two sentences whether your answer would change if Policy R were replaced by a statutory minimum wage set at the same proportional distance from the market-clearing wage, with the same compensation scheme for those the evidence identifies as excluded from employment — and if it would change, say which term does the work.
