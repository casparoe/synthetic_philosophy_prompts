I convene a small reading group in development studies — mostly graduate students, two of them in economic history — and we have spent an entire term on the question of whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, and I have come away convinced that the disagreement is not the one people think they are having. Let me set out what each side in our group actually says, because the diagnosis I want from you has to engage those claims rather than the slogans. On one side, people point to Bengal in 1943 and note that the wartime administration confiscated boats and moved rice out of coastal districts as a scorched-earth measure against a feared Japanese landing, that the Bengal Chamber of Commerce designed a scheme guaranteeing preferential food supplies to designated priority classes of war workers and civil servants, that inter-provincial trade barriers were erected, that price controls produced hoarding and then a black market, and that the war cabinet declined to divert shipping; they then say that whatever the cyclone and the brown-spot rice disease did to the autumn crop, the mortality was the product of those choices. The same people point to Ireland in the 1840s — grain and livestock kept leaving the ports while the potato failed, and the relief administration was run on a doctrine of providential correction and non-interference with trade — and to the Great Leap Forward, where procurement quotas were raised, cities were closed to inflows of peasants, grain continued to be exported, and the defense minister's private letter criticizing the exaggeration of output was answered at Lushan by his purge and an anti-rightist campaign that made honest reporting career-ending. On the other side, people insist that all of this rides on top of a physical shortfall, that entitlement language obscures the fact that a blighted potato field or a storm-damaged aman crop contains fewer calories than it did before, that the statistical evidence about how much rice was actually available in Bengal in 1943 is contested and the shortfall may have been larger than the famous analysis suggested, and that for most of history no state had the transport, the telegraph, or the fiscal capacity to prevent a famine even if it had wanted to — so that calling those events crimes imports a modern standard of feasible rescue backwards. Both camps cite the same economist, sometimes for opposite propositions: the entitlement analysis for the first camp, the claim that China's catastrophe persisted for years because there was no free press or opposition to correct the false output reports for the second camp, where it functions as a story about information rather than about malice. A third, quieter position in our group holds that the availability-versus-entitlement dichotomy was a false one to begin with, since almost every serious famine involves both a physical shock and a collapse in particular groups' claims on food, and that what has really changed over the last century is not causation but the technology of response.

Nobody in the group has moved an inch, and I do not think the impasse is about the facts, since both sides accept the same chronologies. My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis I want you to test, not as a conclusion — is that the two framings differ in which background conditions they hold fixed when they construct the counterfactual: the natural-disaster grammar asks what would have happened holding policy constant, the political-crime grammar asks what would have happened holding the weather and the blight constant, and each therefore reports a true but non-comparable proposition about an overdetermined event. A related hypothesis is that the phrase "famine was caused by" is doing two jobs at once, a mechanism job (what physiological and economic process killed these people) and an agency job (who had the capacity and the standing obligation to stop it), and that the camps are answering the second while arguing as though they were answering the first. A third thought, which may be the most concrete, concerns phases: in Bengal more than half the famine-related deaths came in 1944, after a record harvest had arrived and the food crisis had abated, in an epidemic tail of malaria, cholera and smallpox among a displaced, destitute population — and no account of who commanded how much rice in the autumn of 1943 explains that tail, yet the tail is where most of the dying happened. Connected to this is the modern classification apparatus: the technical thresholds for declaring a famine — households facing extreme shortage, acute child malnutrition prevalence, a crude death rate per ten thousand per day — are deliberately non-attributive, describing a state of affairs while withholding any judgment about its origin, and I cannot tell whether that is a post-entitlement advance in rigor or the old natural-disaster grammar reincarnated inside a supposedly political framework.

So: diagnose the disagreement for me. Tell me whether it is substantive, verbal, a matter of two questions sheltering under one heading, a hidden empirical question about which nobody yet has decisive data, or a bedrock moral and political commitment about the reach of obligation across time that no finding could touch — and support the diagnosis by saying what each side is actually committed to, not by restating the camps. Then tell me, given that diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a change in the unit of analysis (event, phase, population group), a measurement convention, a comparative case design that could separate physical shortfall from failed response, or a reformulation of the question itself — or an argument that the debate should be expected to stay stuck, and why that would not be a failure. I would also like your view on a question I keep dodging in the group: whether the natural-disaster framing has been doing ideological work rather than explanatory work, given that the colonial famine codes turned a catastrophe into an administrative checklist of distress signals and relief works with nobody to blame, and whether the political-crime framing has a symmetrical weakness of its own — I suspect it flatters the present by assuming that any state with our logistics could have acted differently, and it may also depend on an agent whose knowledge and capacity the sources cannot establish. Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. State your diagnosis plainly in your opening paragraphs before you develop the argument for it, and keep the whole thing argumentative rather than survey-like — I have read the surveys.
