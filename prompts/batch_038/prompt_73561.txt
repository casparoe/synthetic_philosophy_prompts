I've got a 30 minute invited talk at a theater studies conference in the spring. Audience is mostly dramaturgs and people who make site-specific and immersive work, plus two Rousseau specialists: a historian who'll insist that everything in the Letter to d'Alembert is really about Geneva's pastors and the Encyclopédie article, and a French lit person who knows Rousseau's own plays and libretti cold and won't let me get away with ignoreing them.

My thesis: the Letter isn't a moralist's complaint about corrupting content on stage. It's an argument that spectatorship is a structure that outsources civic presence — you get to feel and discharge your obligations to your fellow citizens vicariously, in the dark, without ever being called on. So the festival at the end isn't a rival spectacle but an attempt to abolish the audiance altogether. The practitioners will want to know whether that licenses what they make or condemns it.

Give me a prep sheet: the eightish objections I'll actually get in that room, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they'd do. For each, a reply I can deliver in about 90 seconds, what I should concede up front, and where the reply is thinnest. Then tell me the question I should be dreading — I assume it's the one about Rousseau writing plays while arguing against them, but I suspect there's a worse one.
