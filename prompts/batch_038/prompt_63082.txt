So I'm the grad student rep on the editorial board of our department's open-access journal and we've got a mess I have to write up before Thursday. Board is split 3-3, and apparently my memo is going to be the document everyone argues from, so it actually needs to land somewhere rather than just summarise the fight.

Background: last spring we published an audit paper where a team of six re-ran 62 empirical findings drawn from our own back catalogue (mostly articles we published ourselves, awkwardly). Eleven failed to replicate. Four of those eleven were marginal — the audit's own tables show the replication p-values landing between .06 and .09 against a .05 threshold the team fixed in advance, and there's no multiple-comparison correction anywhere in the paper. The auditors also put a short note up front saying roughly that with 62 tests run at these standards some individual verdicts are likely wrong even where the procedure was followed correctly, and that the note wasn't meant to weaken any particular verdict.

Now three of the original authors — all three from the marginal group — have filed a formal complaint demanding full retraction of the audit. Their argument isn't "you got mine wrong." It's: the paper says of itself that at least one of its verdicts is probably false, so it isn't asserting any of them; a paper that disclaims its own contents can't be making 62 claims; and by publishing it the board endorsed all 62, including ones the authors themselves flagged as likely erroneous. One of them put it to me as: if you can't tell me which of your verdicts is the bad one, you can't stand behind any of them. I dont think that's nonsense and two of the faculty members agree with her.

Our bylaws only give us three remedies: retract in full, publish an expression of concern, or dismiss the complaint. Seperately, the editor wants to know whether we should adopt a house rule about prefaces like the auditors' — require them, ban them, or leave it to authors — and that needs a two-thirds vote, so if I want it I have to sell it in the same document.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate? It should pick a remedy, say what happens and by when (next issue goes to press in six weeks), and give reasons the three complainants could actually argue with instead of procedural hand-waving. I want their strongest version taken seriously and then answered, not strawmanned — including whether "some of these are probably wrong" does to the other 61 what they say it does. And on the house rule I need something an editor could apply to the next submission that turns up with a similar note, not a vibe. Don't re-derive the formal epistemology for me, I've read it — tell me what it commits us to institutionally.
