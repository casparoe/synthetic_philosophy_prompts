I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections for something like fifteen years, and the problem of induction is the one topic on which my views have refused to converge. What follows is the set of verdicts I actually hold about when an inductive inference is licensed. Individually I am confident in each. Jointly I cannot get them under one principle, and I would like to know whether that is my failure or the subject's.

1. Ten thousand white swans, all observed in Europe, confirm "the next swan I encounter in Europe is white" a great deal and "all swans are white" only a little. When black swans turn up in Australia, I do not think the earlier inference was shown to have been unjustified — only mis-scoped.

2. Observed green emeralds confirm "all emeralds are green" and, I insist, do not confirm "all emeralds are grue" (grue meaning green before some fixed future date and blue afterward). The only thing I can offer is that green is the more natural predicate, and I am not satisfied that "natural" is doing any work beyond restating the verdict I wanted.

3. A classifier scoring 99% on a held-out set drawn from the same pipeline as its training data is warranted for deployment.

4. The identical classifier, with the identical score, moved to a different hospital's imaging setup, is not warranted until revalidated there. Nothing about the data has changed; what has changed is my belief about which population it will face. I cannot see how that belief enters the account except as a premise about the world's regularity — precisely the sort of premise I have been trying to avoid needing.

5. A learning method that has outperformed every rival on every public benchmark for twenty years is good evidence that it will outperform the next rival too. The no-free-lunch results — that averaged across all possible target functions every algorithm does equally badly — strike me as true and as irrelevant here, but I cannot explain the irrelevance without sounding as though I am changing the subject.

6. Twenty years of a stock picker's outperformance leaves me far less confident than twenty years of sunrises, and not because the record is shorter or noisier. The difference I want to point to is survivorship bias, backfitting, and the absence of any mechanism — but mechanism-talk looks like metaphysics I have not earned.

7. I accept Hume's point that no inductive conclusion follows demonstratively from its premises, and I reject the leveling that seems to go with it: expecting the sun to rise is not on a par with expecting it to arrive late, or expecting bread to nourish and arsenic to nourish equally. Something must explain why some non-demonstrative inferences are so much better than others.

My working formulation is: an inductive inference is warranted to the degree that sample and target are known to be produced by the same generating process, and no-free-lunch is silent about warrant because it averages over target functions rather than asking which one is operative. My suspicion is that this is the problem of induction with extra steps, since "known to be produced by the same process" quietly assumes the uniformity I meant to derive.

Please give me a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen. For each clause, say which of the numbered verdicts it exists to handle. Then run it on two or three cases of your own choosing and report what it delivers. Be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which one you would advise me to give up. And tell me where you land on the question underneath all of this: whether no-free-lunch is a genuine constraint on induction or a theorem about a quantity nobody has any reason to care about. If the principle that saves every verdict and the principle that is simple enough to actually use come apart, say which you would take, and what it costs.
