I spent forty-one years in a national metrology institute, most of them on the arithmetic of measurement uncertainty, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with rather more seriousness than my former colleagues think appropriate. In the course of reading about what the moderns call coherence arguments for the probability calculus, I came across a pamphlet of about a hundred and thirty pages, printed in Edinburgh in the 1890s by a man who appears to have made his living in life assurance and wrote privately on the logic of assent. It is not indexed anywhere I can find, the copy I am working from is a poor scan of a single surviving volume, and I am confident you will not have encountered it, so I ask you to resist any temptation to identify the author, place him in a tradition, or supply context you do not have; work only from the text I give you. What I want is help with a single passage in the third chapter, which my reading group of three (a former colleague, my daughter, and myself) has now argued about for two evenings without agreement.

The passage, in the pamphlet's own spelling and punctuation, runs as follows:

> It is objected by some, that the odds a man offers upon a contingent matter are but an outward show, and no true measure of the assent within him; for he may name a price to oblige a friend, or to win a wager he cares nothing for. Yet let him be questioned in earnest, and by one who has no interest to serve; and let it be understood that the stake he lays down shall be forfeit upon either hand alike, and that the matter shall be determined by an umpire indifferent to both: then I say his odds, so given, are the very quantity of his belief, and nothing else can be. Suppose now that he assign to three propositions, of which the third is but the disjunction of the first two, odds such as, being translated into the language of the ledger, do not sum aright. Then a dealer, more cunning than he, may so frame two wagers that he shall receive his money upon both hands and owe him nothing upon any; which is an absurdity, no man consenting to be ruined by the arithmetic of his own opinions. It follows that the ledger of the mind must close; and if it do not, the fault lies not in the uncertainty of the world, but in the inconsistency of the bookkeeper.

My questions are these. First, is the middle of this an argument or a stipulation: the opening sentence reads to me as though he is dismissing an objection by fiat (that odds are not belief) merely by describing ideal conditions under which he declares them identical, and I cannot tell whether any reasoning is being offered at all before he reaches the case of the three propositions. Second, and this is where my group splits, what is the precise content of the "two wagers" construction? He sets up a man whose odds on three propositions fail to sum, and then claims a dealer can frame two wagers that secure a guaranteed profit; my daughter thinks the number two is a slip and the intended book must have three legs, while my former colleague thinks two legs suffice because the disjunction leg can be replicated by a combination of the other two, and I cannot decide which reconstruction, if either, actually delivers a loss on every branch. Third, what is he assuming about stakes and about the relation between money and satisfaction, since I notice he says nothing about the size of the wagers or the bettor's wealth, and in my own trade we were always obliged to ask whether a measuring instrument behaves linearly across its range. Fourth, does the closing claim — that a failure to sum is an inconsistency in the bookkeeper rather than a fact about the world's uncertainty — commit him to the view that incoherent degrees of belief cannot exist, or only that they are criticisable, and is there any way to tell from this passage alone? Finally, I should be grateful for your view on whether the passage anticipates, in however rough a form, the standard synchronic coherence result, or whether it is closer to the later diachronic arguments about how one ought to revise when the umpire's decision comes in, since the reference to an "umpire indifferent to both" leaves me unsure which of the two he has in mind. Please be explicit about where the text underdetermines your reading rather than smoothing it over, and state any auxiliary assumption you must add to make the reasoning valid.
