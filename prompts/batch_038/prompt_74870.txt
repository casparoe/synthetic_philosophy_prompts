THE QUESTION (10 points, in class, 25 min, no notes):

Nils spent nine years as a boatwright. Given a plank and a hull he can tell by eye and hand whether the plank will take the curve, he rejects the ones that won't, and he can train an apprentice to make the same calls. Asked what he's going on — what makes a plank right for a given bend — he has nothing better than two or three sayings he got from his own teacher.

Priya is a materials engineer who has never touched a boat. She can state and derive exactly which grain orientations and elastic moduli let a plank bend to a given radius, and her predictions from the numbers generalise in exactly the way Nils's calls do. She has never attempted the work and could not do it with her hands to save her life.

Two stipulations. Neither of them is infallible: Nils miscalls maybe one plank in fifty, and Priya's derivations come out wrong about as often. And both of their judgments generalise — Nils is right about species and weather he's never met, Priya's predictions are right about the same range.

(a) On Ryle's account, what does Nils have that Priya lacks, and what does Priya have that Nils lacks? Say what in the account does the work in your answer, not just which way you're voting. (6)
(b) Ryle thinks the intellectualist account of intelligence generates a regress. Set out the regress, and say whether it has any bite on Priya. (4)

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

(a) Nils has knowledge how and Priya has knowledge that. For Ryle that isn't a ranking — knowing how isn't the second-rate cousin, it's the intellectualist who thinks every intelligent act has to be preceded by thinking of a rule, and Ryle is reversing that. Nils's knowledge is a disposition, a settled habit of getting the call right that was built up over nine years of practice, and it shows up in what he does rather than in anything he can state. Priya has the facts about grain and moduli, but facts are inert if you can't do anything with them and she can't do anything with them in the shed.

The stipulations make this awkward, since neither of them is infallible. So it can't be that Nils knows how just because he never gets it wrong, because he does get it wrong one time in fifty. I think the answer is that his hit rate is still way above chance, and more importantly it generalises: he's right about species he's never seen, so his disposition isn't a list of remembered cases, it projects. Priya's knowledge projects the same way, so she really does have knowledge that on Ryle's view, it just doesn't amount to knowledge how, because knowledge how has to be cashed out in performance and she can't perform.

(b) The regress goes like this. If acting intelligently required first contemplating a proposition telling you how to act, then that act of contemplating would itself have to be done intelligently, which would need another proposition contemplated first, and so on forever, so you'd never get going. It's basically the same argument as Wittgenstein's rule-following one, where no interpretation of a rule fixes its application, so the intellectualist is stuck either way.

Does it bite on Priya? Not right now, because she isn't performing anything and the regress is about performance. But if she did try, she'd have to consult her propositions before she could start, and consulting them is itself something she'd have to know how to do, so she'd never get off the ground. Which shows Ryle's main point, that knowing that is really just a kind of knowing how in disguise.
