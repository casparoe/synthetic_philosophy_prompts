I run a small podcast on philosophy of psychiatry — mixed audience, maybe half clinicians, half grad students, and they know the jargon but they dont have patience for it being waved around. Next week's guest is a philosopher who's built her career on Hacking's looping effects and has pushed them somewhere fairly specific. Her view, roughly: psychiatric categories are interactive kinds in Hacking's sense; each one has what she calls a settling time, a stretch in which the classification is chasing its own tail because people change under it; and the reason biomarker research in psychiatry keeps coming up empty isn't that the disorders aren't biological but that the field keeps re-cutting its categories before they ever settle. She also has a paper arguing that when a diagnosis gets taken up by an online patient community and starts shaping the symptoms people report back to their doctors, that's evidence the loop is running — not evidence the condition is progressive.

Two things bug me about her. First, whenever anyone asks whether looping effects actually show that psychiatric kinds aren't real, she says the looping thesis is ontologically neutral, all it says is that the target moves, and she glides straight on. Second, Hacking himself never drew the anti-realist conclusion she's drawing from his machinery, and as far as I can tell she's never really said why she gets to go further than he did.

So write me eight to ten questions that turn on the actual content of the view. I want at least one each on:

- whether interactivity is distinctive of human kinds at all. She'll reach for the usual line that quarks don't change their behavior when you reclassify them, but what about climate models that change emissions policy, credit scores that change the spending they predict, the placebo-controlled trial subject who's been told what's being tested? Ask her where she draws the line and what does the work.
- the unfalsifiability worry. If every category she looks at turns out to have looped, what would a psychiatric kind that failed to loop look like, and how is she supposed to tell a genuine looping effect apart from ordinary conceptual revision under new evidence?
- direction and ownership of the loop. Patient communities, insurers' billing codes, pharma's trial designs, epidemiologists' screening instruments — these pull in different directions and on different timescales. If she can't say which one is driving, does the settling-time idea have any content?
- the ontologically neutral deflection, in three stages that make it progressively harder to keep using, ending on what evidence would count against her stronger claim rather than against the weaker one.
- diagnostic drift and the autism prevalence figures. Looping, broadened criteria, better ascertainment, changed incentives to get a diagnosis — she needs to be able to separate those for her argument to do any work. Can she, and if not what's left?
- how her view differs from Giddens's double hermeneutic, and whether the difference is more than branding.
- what her position costs her elsewhere. If categories never settle, can she still say a treatment worked, or that a prevalence estimate from 1995 and one from 2020 are measuring the same thing? Clinicians in my audience will want to know whether she's left them any ground to stand on.
- the 1973 declassification of homosexuality as a case she has to handle. Was that a loop closing, or a correction — and can she tell those apart from inside her own framework?

For every question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not a generic "and why is that?" And for the two hardest ones, a third-level rejoinder in case the follow-up gets deflected too. Keep each question short enough to say out loud, one idea per question, and cut anything she could answer by pointing at a paper she's already written or with "that's an empirical matter." No career questions, no what drew you to this, no softball openers. Finally tell me which single question I should hold onto if we run out of time, and which one to drop if she's being unusually forthcoming and the conversation is going well.
