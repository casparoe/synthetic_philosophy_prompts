I audit a graduate reading group in decision theory at a nearby university — I'm a data analyst by trade rather than a student, but they let me sit in — and last week the two regulars, Marta (philosophy) and Julian (economics), spent ninety minutes on money pumps and did not converge. The case they argued over is Julian's, and the details matter, so let me state it exactly. An agent has a strict cycle over three objects: a first pressing of a certain record R, a signed first edition N, and a numbered print P, with R preferred to N, N preferred to P, and P preferred to R. She begins holding R and ten dollars, and she prefers more money to less, other things equal. A trader makes three offers in a fixed order, each offer made only if she accepted the previous one: give up R and take N for a fee of twenty-five cents; give up N and take P for twenty-five cents; give up P and take R back for twenty-five cents. The fees are trivial next to how much she cares about the differences between the items, so each trade, fee included, leaves her with something she prefers to what she surrendered. She accepts all three and ends up holding R and $9.25 — the item she started with, seventy-five cents poorer — and the cycle can be run again for as long as the trader is willing. They asked me to referee afterward and I could not. This is the exchange as I wrote it down; the numbering is mine.

(1) Julian: The pump settles the question. This is not about taste or about having expensive preferences. A strict cycle makes you liable to a guaranteed loss you could have avoided, and because the loss can be repeated indefinitely the exposure is unbounded. A theory that licenses such a cycle is not describing rationality, whatever else it is describing.

(2) Marta: You have slid from choices to sequences. At each of the three nodes she takes the option her preferences rank highest among the options actually on offer. If rationality is a property of choices made in light of preferences, every one of her choices is rational, and all the pump shows is that rational choices can compose into an outcome she would have preferred not to reach. That is a fact about the world's offer structure, not a demonstration that her ordering is defective.

(3) Julian: That reply survives only by insisting she is blind, and she needn't be. Give her foresight. At the first offer she can see that accepting puts her on a path ending at R with $9.25, while declining ends the sequence and leaves her at R with $10, and she prefers the second. So declining is required by her own preferences applied to the outcomes she can actually reach. I have imported no norm; I have simply let her use the preferences you say she has. Removing the blindness adds nothing to the theory — it removes an artificial restriction.

(4) Marta: It removes one artificial restriction by installing a larger one. Your verdict holds only because the second and third offers are conditional on her accepting the first, which means that whether the first trade is permissible depends on what will be offered later. The identical trade at the identical price is irrational inside your sequence and perfectly harmless if the trader never intends to make the third offer; had the trader laid all three offers on the table at once, she would keep R, decline everything, and there would be no pump. A defect that appears and disappears with the format in which options are presented is a defect of presentation. Note too that if the fees were zero there would be no loss at all, which tells you where your objection really comes from. And separately: foresight is not a preference. You have assumed a capacity to form and act on plans, and then called the absence of that capacity irrational. Being poor at something is not being incoherent.

(5) Julian: The zero-fee case cuts against you rather than for you. With no fee, each of the three trades is a strict improvement by her own lights, and the three together leave her exactly where she began — three gains that add up to nothing. That is not an ordering behaving sensibly; that is an ordering that cannot keep its own books. Add any positive fee, however small, and the loss returns, guaranteed and avoidable. Nothing in your objection turns on magnitude, so you cannot retreat to the claim that the loss can be made arbitrarily small: a guaranteed loss of a quarter and a guaranteed loss of a dollar are the same kind of thing, and an argument that switches verdict at exactly zero is an argument about arithmetic, not about reasons.

(6) Marta: But "guaranteed" was never a property of the ordering. Exploitability is a joint property of a preference structure and a fee schedule, and the fee schedule belongs to the trader. If the same ordering is innocent in one market and exploitable in another, what the pump detects cannot be a defect in the ordering; at most it detects that some orderings are expensive to hold, and expense is not irrationality. Consider also where real cycles come from. A colleague of mine is choosing among three job offers, and her pairwise verdicts track different dimensions each time — salary and commute for the first pair, colleagues and location for the second, prestige and stability for the third — so she cycles. Nobody thinks she is confused; each verdict is a genuine evaluation. If your pump condemns her, it is condemning ordinary multidimensional deliberation.

(7) Julian: Then look at what she does when offers are actually made. If she sees the cycle and declines to trade, she is not cyclically preferring in the only sense that could matter: there is a ranking somewhere in her that governs behaviour under offers, and what you called a cycle is a set of pairwise verdicts that never get integrated into a preference at all. If she does not see it and accepts, she pays for a round trip that she knows will deliver nothing — and people in exactly that situation do get drained, by renegotiations, by plan-switching, by salespeople who walk them round a loop. Preferences are whatever governs choice when choice is on the table. Anything that never governs choice is not a preference, and its cyclicity is nobody's problem.

(8) Marta: That is a definition doing the work of an argument. If "preference" is fixed by trading behaviour, then no pattern of judgement can count as a cyclic preference, and the pump cannot be evidence for transitivity, because the transitivity was built into the term before the argument began. What remains is that cycles are costly, which supports at most an instrumental requirement: if you do not want to be drained, do not hold strict cycles. Requirements of that shape prove too much — by the same reasoning I should cultivate whichever tastes happen to be cheapest for traders to satisfy — and we do not call a person irrational for having expensive tastes.

(9) Julian: Even granting that, it is the only requirement of its kind decision theory has ever produced. A theory that tells me how to bet given my preferences while having nothing whatsoever to say about which preferences to hold omits the only part of it that could ever function as advice, and the pump is the one argument that connects the axioms of choice to the life you end up with.

Then tell me who was more right overall, and whether this disagreement is the kind that a sharper formulation would dissolve or the kind that reflects a real fault line about what a theory of rationality is supposed to do. Finally, tell me what neither of them raised: there were two or three things I wanted to say at the time and did not, and I suspect they matter more than most of what was said. As you go, please mark explicitly which parts of your adjudication are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested. I would much rather be told that a given move is one that experts still fight about than have a disputed position handed to me as though it were the textbook answer.
