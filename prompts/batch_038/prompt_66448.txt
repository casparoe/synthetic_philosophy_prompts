I never studied philosophy. I've come to this material on my own, mostly through summaries and arguments that turn up online, and I've spent an unreasonable amount of time on Parfit's hitchhiker — the stranded traveller who can only get a ride if the driver can tell he's genuinely disposed to pay up later, so that it's in his interest to swallow a promise-keeping pill even though, once he's safe in town, paying would no longer serve him at all. What grips me isn't that case by itself but the family of cases sitting around it: the toxin that pays you for forming an intention you already know you'll never carry out; the commander who is better off having lost the ability to recall his bombers; Ulysses having himself lashed to the mast while his crew keeps their ears plugged with wax; and the ordinary person who resolves in January to become someone who doesn't check his phone, where nobody is watching and nothing hangs on it.

On my own I've tried to build a test for telling these apart. My thought is that a scheme for making yourself irrational only pays to the extent that three conditions hold:

1. There has to be a reader — someone or something other than you that can detect your disposition, whether a driver, an adversary, a predictor, or a being who inspects intentions directly. Without a reader, the disposition buys nothing.
2. Your later self has to be genuinely unable to opt out at the moment of action, whether because you've been physically bound, chemically altered, or because backing down would cost you something you cannot dodge.
3. Faking it has to be hard — pretending to be the sort of person who pays, who retaliates, or who drinks must be detectably worse than actually being that sort of person.

I don't know whether these are the right conditions, whether they secretly collapse into one another, or whether the second is doing all the work and the others are decoration. Sorting that out is part of what I want from you.

So the task. Take the five cases: the hitchhiker, the toxin, the commander who cannot recall his bombers, Ulysses at the mast, and the unobserved January resolution. For each, rate on a scale of 0 to 10 how strongly each of my three conditions is satisfied, then give a single overall score for how reliably the irrational move actually pays off there. Rank the five by that overall score, best first, with a sentence or two defending each placement.

Three things I want to be explicit about. First, don't simply accept my framework: tell me where it misfires, and if a condition should be dropped, split, or replaced, redo the scoring under the corrected framework and say which of the two rankings you would stand behind. Second, say for which of the five the difference between bringing the change about with a pill and bringing it about through years of deliberate character-building would shift your score, and why it wouldn't shift the others. Third, after all of that, name the single case that in your view does the real philosophical work of showing that irrationality can be rational, and say whether the remaining four are variants of it or genuinely different phenomena. I would rather have a firm answer on that than a survey of the options.
