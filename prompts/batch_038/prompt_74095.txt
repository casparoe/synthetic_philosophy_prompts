So I run a monthly reading group with a mix of physics grad students and two people who just like the genre, and this month's theme was "probability in a world where everything happens." It turned into a shouting match. One of the physicists swore the Deutsch/Wallace decision-theory route basically settles it — any rational agent in a branching universe is forced to bet in proportion to squared amplitude, done, and Zurek's envariance argument points the same way. One of the philosophers said that's just hiding the answer inside whatever axioms about rational preference you picked, and that you can run the same trick to derive branch-counting if you start from different axioms. A third guy said forget all of it, count the branches, each one is a person, and got absolutely piled on for assuming there even is a well-defined number of branches to count — which I thought was actually the most interesting five minutes of the night, because decoherence only ever gives you a fuzzy, approximate decomposition, and the Tally position needs it to be crisp. Nobody budged. I think the argument would land better as a story than as a fourth round of the same three positions.

So here's what I want: a short story, somewhere around 1,500–2,500 words, set in a place where branching isn't a metaphysical claim anybody argues about, it's just infrastructure, like plumbing. My seed, use it or bend it: a habitat called the Loom, where the Office of Apportionment employs clerks whose job is to set the amplitude schedule before any controlled split — a clinical trial, a ship launch, a sentencing. Everything downstream is allocated by weight: inheritance, votes, rations, jail time. There's a legal fiction called the Sufficient Weight Doctrine, under which branches that come out below some tiny amplitude threshold simply aren't persons for census purposes — nobody downstream of them has standing, and the people who live in them are referred to, not kindly, as "the thin." There's a heretical movement, the Tally, that wants one branch one share regardless of amplitude, which would quietly transfer almost everything from the heavy branches to the thin ones, so the metaphysics has a price tag attached and everybody knows it. Before a split, citizens file a sealed instruction addressed to whichever copy of them survives, and the clerks audit these for coherence — which is where I'd like someone to run into the wall head-on, because what does "I hope it's me in the heavy one" even mean when both of them are you and both of them get the letter. The plot engine: a clerk has to schedule a three-way split at one-half, one-quarter, one-quarter for a trial that kills in some continuations, and discovers that the Doctrine's threshold means somebody she loves is scheduled to come out thin — under the Born reading she's a rounding error, under the Tally reading she's a full third of the outcome, and the clerk is the one holding the pen. Feel free to also give her a clean exact tie somewhere, a fifty-fifty she has to break with nothing in the theory to break it with, because I want that moment to be as uncomfortable as the weighted one.

A few things I care about: the story should stage at least two of the positions — the decision-theoretic derivation, the self-locating-uncertainty move, naive counting, some variant of "just care about all your future selves and stop asking which one you'll be" — without turning anyone into a strawman or a mouthpiece, and it should hit the circularity worry at least in passing, the fact that the only way anybody on the Loom ever checks the schedules is by looking at frequencies across lots of repeated splits, which kind of assumes the thing under dispute. No named real physicists, no papers cited, no lecture about Hilbert space — one line of amplitude-squared notation is the most math I want, and the characters should treat all of this the way we treat tax law, with boredom and resentment. Don't resolve the philosophy with a speech; let the institutions and the deadline and the audit do the work, and let people be wrong in ways that make sense from where they're standing. End on the clerk's decision, whatever she picks, and make the reader genuinely unsure whether it was the rational one — I don't want a story that secretly takes my side, I want one that would make the physicist and the philosopher both leave the room arguing. Oh, and dont sweat the mechanism of the splitter, hand-wave it like an elevator, the hardware is not the point.
