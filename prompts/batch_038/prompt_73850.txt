I'm giving a talk at a forecasting-practitioners meetup next month — the room is people who run prediction markets and in-house forecasting programs, a couple of Brier-score nerds, one psychologist who's published on training people to think in base rates, and a Bayesian statistician who does hierarchical models for demand forecasting. No philosophers, no decision theorists.

My thesis: the reference class problem isn't a solvable technical puzzle but a standing limit on the whole "take the outside view" program. Because any reference class gets carved out by a causal story about which features of the case matter, the outside view always borrows its authority from the inside view — there's no principled stopping point between the class so narrow it has n=3 and the class so broad it stops being informative. My argument, roughly: (1) for any case you can construct a broader and a narrower class containing it, and the data almost never forces the choice; (2) when you press forecasters who say they just used the base rate, they can always produce a post-hoc story for why that class was the right one; (3) so what gets sold as statistical discipline is model choice in a lab coat. Positive proposal: forecast from an explicit weighted portfolio of candidate classes and argue about the weights out loud, rather than pretending one class was handed to you by the data.
