I'm putting in for one semester of release time from our internal faculty fund — two pages maximum, and the readers are a chemist, a medievalist, and an assistant dean, so it has to read like a real project rather than a puzzle. The idea has been sitting in a notebook since a graduate seminar I co-taught in the fall, where we spent two sessions on Gödel's modal ontological argument and I ended up more confused then the students did. Here's the half-formed thing. Everyone teaches the same apparatus: a positivity predicate on properties, a handful of axioms (exactly one of a property and its negation is positive; positivity is closed under necessary entailment; positivity is a necessary matter; necessary existence is itself positive), a definition of a God-like individual as one having all the positive properties, and then S5 to convert the possibility premise into the conclusion. The pressure points are all well rehearsed too. My hunch is that nearly all of the trouble traces back to something nobody interrogates: the apparatus is Boolean. Positivity is a monadic predicate on properties, so complements come for free with it, and it's precisely the exclusivity axiom — a property is positive iff its negation isn't — that generates the objection about positivity having to be world-relative (Hájek's, I think, but don't worry about the attribution), and that forces the rigidity axiom to carry so much weight. What I want to do is rebuild the thing on a comparative footing instead: start not with "φ is positive" but with a weak ordering over property-pairs — it's better to have φ than not to have φ, or better to have φ than ψ — and ask what survives. The immediate problem is obvious and it's the interesting part: a comparative notion doesn't hand you the set of all the positive properties, so "God-like" can't be defined the old way and has to become some sort of maximal element, which raises whether maximality across properties that aren't pairwise comparable is even a coherent condition, and whether a conclusion you can actually derive would say anything a theist would recognize.

There's a second, smaller strand I want folded in rather than tacked on. I vaguely remember that when people encoded the argument in a proof assistant, the axioms as Gödel actually wrote them came out inconsistent and had to be patched, and the versions in circulation now are the emended ones — Anderson's and Fitting's are the two I know of. I have a doctoral student who works in Isabelle and has agreed to give me a couple of hours a week, so the proposal should include a modest machine-checked component whose purpose is narrow: to locate the point at which the comparative axioms stop being strong enough to yield the possibility premise, or fail to yield a maximal individual at all. I'd rather be upfront in the document that this is a logic-and-conceptual-analysis project and not a contribution to theology, partly because the chemist on the committee will be looking for something with a determinate answer.

So: write me the two pages. I need the question stated precisely enough that a reader could tell whether it has a determinate answer; a real account of why the standard repairs — weakening or dropping the exclusivity axiom, relativizing positivity to worlds, or just glossing positivity informally as "better to have than not" while keeping the Boolean syntax — leave my question open rather than answering it; what I would actually do month by month across a single semester, with the formal and philosophical strands interleaved rather than run sequentially, and an explanation of why that sequencing and not the obvious alternative of doing the metalogic first and the interpretation afterwards; what a result would look like in each of the two or three outcomes I I can imagine, including the unglamorous one where the reconstruction is consistent but too weak to prove anything; and what would count as the project failing. My own suspicion is that it fails if the comparative ordering turns out to be so shot through with incomparabilities that the maximality clause is vacuously satisfied or unsatisfiable, but argue with me about that if you think I'm wrong. Finish with an honest scope statement — one semester, one long paper plus a formal artifact, no book — and no citations or bibliography, please, I'll add those myself.
