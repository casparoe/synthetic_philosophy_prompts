I teach a senior seminar on transitional justice — 16 students, one 80-minute session near the end of term. They've already done the reading on the South African amnesty process, so I don't need anything that explains what a truth commission is or why the truth-for-amnesty trade was contested. I want an activity that makes them feel one specific thing that I don't think the readings ever spell out plainly: under an individualized amnesty-for-full-disclosure deal, the record a commision ends up with isn't shaped by what victims need acknowledged, it's shaped by each perpetrator's own private estimate of their prosecution risk. So the official truth systematically over-represents exactly the people who were most likely to be caught anyway, and the ones with the best lawyers and the least exposure never have to say anything at all — they're neither convicted nor cleared, they just drop out of the story. Ideally the students run into this through the rules, not through me announcing it.

So design it. I want the roles and the card content (some private information about prosecution risk, something for the victims, something for the panel that has to decide), the exact procedure and timing so it fits in 80 minutes including debrief, how applicants are incentivized when I can't offer anything but participation credit, what I'd have to prep the night before, and whether the thing still works if three people are absent. Then the debrief: I want it to walk backwards from what actually got recorded to why, and to surface the second twist that I think they'll only half-see on their own — that the non-applicant who was never prosecuted is left in a worse public position than the guy who did get amnesty. And be straight with me about what predictably goes wrong the first time it runs and how to handle it, plus which part of the real thing the game can't reproduce. My guess is something about the weight of actually testifying in front of the person you harmed, but tell me if its something else.
