The exam question, which students answered by hand in fifty minutes without notes, was this: Explain why, on Fukuyama's account in "The End of History?", liberal democracy no longer faces a systematic ideological competitor. Say what role the struggle for recognition plays in that account, and then consider the following objection: the thesis cannot function as a prediction about the future, since any state that remains non-liberal can simply be redescribed as not yet having arrived at the terminus. Does Fukuyama have a response to this?

The rubric I have been given allocates fifty points to accurate exposition of the argument, twenty-five points to the handling of the objection, and twenty-five points to precision in attribution and terminology, for a total of one hundred. The course median on this question so far is 74. Please calibrate your judgment to second-year undergraduates writing under time pressure rather than to a specialist article.

Here is the student's answer in full.

Fukuyama's claim is that history, understood in the Hegelian sense as a directional process driven by contradiction, has reached its terminus in liberal democracy, because no rival regime any longer offers a coherent universal alternative. Monarchy and empire were discredited before the twentieth century; fascism was destroyed militarily; and communism exhausted itself both because it could not match the productive efficiency of market economies and because it failed to satisfy the desire to be recognized as a person of equal dignity. The essay's most original move is to read that second failure through Alexandre Kojève's account of Hegel, on which the motor of history is not material scarcity but the struggle for recognition between master and slave. Liberal democracy is distinctive in granting recognition universally and reciprocally, through equal rights, and so it settles a demand that earlier regimes could only partially satisfy.

The economic track of the argument therefore matters less than it appears to. Fukuyama is not simply a modernization theorist: he denies that market capitalism as such produces democracy, pointing out that capitalist economies have flourished under authoritarian rule, and he holds that prosperity alone cannot explain why people would want a regime of equal rights. What growth does is drain the material grievances that made radical alternatives attractive, so that the demand for recognition can operate unimpeded.

Two consequences follow. First, because that demand is finally satisfied, wars whose cause is the craving for prestige disappear; once all states are liberal democracies, and Fukuyama takes the advanced industrial world to be already in that position, there is no motive left for large-scale war among them, which is what makes the end of history also the end of the state of war. Second, Fukuyama is not naively celebratory about the outcome: he worries that the resulting condition resembles Tocqueville's last man, a comfortably self-regarding creature with no great projects left, and that frustration in the spirited part of the soul may eventually drive people to restart history for its own sake. That worry, however, does nothing to meet the objection posed in the question. If any surviving non-liberal state can be reclassified as merely pre-historical, the thesis is compatible with every possible state of the world, and so it is not a prediction at all but an unfalsifiable article of faith.
