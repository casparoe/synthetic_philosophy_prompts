ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on how these chatbots actually get trained, and I think there's something philosophically loaded buried in the machinery that nobody points out. This is for an independent study thing at my school — I'm not in college — so please explain as you go but don't dumb down the actual ideas.

Here's the setup as I understand it, correct me where I've got it wrong. They show a human two different model responses to the same prompt, the human picks the better one, and they collect an enormous pile of those head-to-head picks. Then they train a separate "reward model" to predict which one a human would pick, and that reward model spits out ONE number. Then they train the real chatbot to make that number as big as possible, except they also throw in a penalty term that stops it drifting too far from the model it started as, so it doesn't degenerate into spamming whatever technically scores high.

Seperately, people write down a list of principles — be honest, don't help with dangerous stuff, refuse this whole category of request no matter what — and they have the model critique its own drafts against the list, and those critiques get turned into more of the same head-to-head comparisons, which get folded back into the same single-number reward.

So: what theory of right and wrong does that whole pipeline presuppose? Not what the finished model says when you ask it about ethics — I mean, if you treated the pipeline itself as an agent making moral decisions, is it a utilitarian, a deontologist, or something that doesn't have a name? Here's what I noticed and can't reconcile:

- maximizing one scalar looks exactly like "add up the good and push it as high as it goes," which is the utilitarian shape. but a single number seems to have no room in it for "you may not do this, no matter how much good the rest of it produces." a constraint isn't a low score, it's a different kind of thing.
- but the humans doing the comparisons don't seem to be ranking by total good either. they're doing stuff like "this one was dishonest so it's worse," even when the dishonest answer was way more useful. so the DATA tastes deontological and the machinery that eats the data tastes utilitarian. what happens to the deontological part when it gets compressed into one number — does it survive as a shape, or is it gone?
- the flat refusal categories look like genuine side constraints, but they were learned through the exact same compare-and-score route as everything else. so aren't they just preferences with a really big weight on them? what would the difference even be, in the machinery, between a rule and a huge weight?
- and the drift penalty. is that a constraint on HOW the model may chase the good, or is it just one more quantity being added into the total?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Then split it for me: which parts does the practice STRICTLY presuppose, versus which parts is it merely compatible with, because I have this nagging feeling that a lot of what looks like deontology here is really just an artifact of how the training works and not a moral commitment at all. I want to know which bits are load-bearing. Also tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where two different moral theories would generate this exact same pipeline and you could never tell them apart from the outside.

Then the part I actually care about most: if you wanted to build it so it was deontological in its STRUCTURE and not just in its outputs — so some things are genuinely off the table instead of merely expensive — what would have to change mechanically? Does the single reward number have to go? Do the principles have to live outside the scoring entirely, like checked before or after instead of fed into it? Give me at least one concrete alternative design and be honest about what it costs: does the model get worse at normal helpful stuff, do you lose the ability to handle weird cases the rule-writers never thought of, does it break the ability to make trade-offs at all?
