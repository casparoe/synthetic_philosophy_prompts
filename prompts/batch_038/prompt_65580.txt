I write literary fiction, mostly tight third person, one POV per section, and I have a tic in my process that I've started to suspect isn't just a tic: I don't let anything happen off the page that matters. If two characters have the conversation that ends the marriage, somebody is in the room rendering it or it didn't happen. Meanwhile I keep these dossiers — thirty pages of backstory on a guy who appears twice, his childhood, his divorce, what he ate the morning his father died — none of which will ever be rendered.

Someone at a party told me Berkeley is doing my job, or Im doing his. Things exist only insofar as they're perceived. The room keeps existing when I leave because God keeps perceiving it. And his master argument, as I've got it secondhand, is that you can't form a conception of a thing existing unconceived, so nothing can.

So translate his idealism into my craft vocabulary, piece by piece, and then give me the residue report. Specifically:

- esse est percipi, where percipi means rendered-by-a-POV. Who is Berkeley's finite spirit in my shop — the POV character, the narrator, the implied author? Pick, and say what the pick costs you.
- the master argument. My dossiers look like a flat counterexample: I conceive of unconceived stuff constantly, in a Word document, for a living. Is that a refutation, or does it just show that the conceiving he means isn't the conceiving I mean? Be precise about which sense of conceive is doing the work in each.
- God as perpetual perceiver. Try each candidate in turn — the reader, the finished manuscript, the author, the whole book grasped at once as an object outside time — and tell me which one breaks first and how badly.
- Berkeley sorts the world into passive ideas and active spirits. In fiction it looks reversed to me: my characters seem to be the agents and the sentences the inert material they're made of. Does that inversion matter, or is it just a way of talking?
- his answer to the obvious objection, that idealism collapses the real and the imaginary into the same stuff, is something about order, vividness, coherence, and ideas not being up to our will. I draw exactly that line — what happened versus what a character believes happened — and I draw it with focalization and tense and register, which is to say with style. Is he drawing it with style too? Can the analogy hold here?

Flag anywhere you catch yourself smuggling a craft assumption in and calling it a translation.

Then the part I actually care about: revision. When I cut chapter four in draft six, thier events never happened. My character's dead brother is alive again, retroactively, and nobody in the book notices, because in the published version there was never a brother who died. Berkeley's God cannot unperceive anything. Tell me whether that gap is a defect in my vocabulary, a defect in his, or the spot where the whole analogy was prettier than it was useful.

End with your call. After the translation, is there anything left standing that a novelist genuinely can't express — and if so, does the leftover make Berkeley look confused or make him look deep? I'd rather have three honest sentences on that than a survey. And dont quote him at me; I've never read him, so paraphrase, and mark the places where you're paraphrasing loose.
