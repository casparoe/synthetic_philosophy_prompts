The question set was as follows.

*In 1956 the University of Oxford conferred an honorary degree on Harry Truman. G. E. M. Anscombe opposed the conferral publicly and unsuccessfully, and later published her account of the matter as the pamphlet* Mr. Truman's Degree. *She maintained that the decision to use the atomic bomb was murder.*
*(a) Reconstruct her argument and identify what she takes to be the decisive feature of the decision.*
*(b) Explain how she meets the defence that the bombing saved more lives than it cost.*
*(c) Does her position commit her to pacifism?*
*Support your answer by reference to the pamphlet and to "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958). One question only.*

The answer submitted, complete and uncorrected, reads:

---

Anscombe's objection is not that the bombings were unwise, or disproportionate, or productive of suffering on a monstrous scale. It is that they were murder, and she means the word as strictly as it can be meant. In "Modern Moral Philosophy" she argues that from Sidgwick onward English moral philosophy had quietly lost the resources for saying that anything at all is simply forbidden, so that the only surviving vocabulary was one of outcomes; her use of *murder* is an attempt to recover the older vocabulary, in which some descriptions of action carry their verdict with them. The decisive premise of the pamphlet is correspondingly narrow: one may never choose to kill innocent people as a means to one's own ends. The inhabitants of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were innocent in her sense. They were not fighting the Allies and, for the most part, were not supplying the means of fighting; and their deaths were not incidental to the destruction of some military objective but were the very mechanism by which the weapon was to work, since Japan was to be brought to surrender *because* these people had been killed. That is what makes the case different in kind, and not merely in degree, from an ordinary act of war.

She refuses the descriptions that would soften the act. Against the claim that Truman only signed an order, while the killing was done by aircrew, physicists, and a bureaucracy, she holds that what a person does is not limited to the movements of his own body, and that a man who orders a killing has killed; intention, on her account, is not an inner event detachable from what the agent set out to bring about. Against the objection that if killing the innocent is murder then it would be murder to kill enemy soldiers in their sleep, since at that moment they threaten no one, she accepts the bite and locates permissibility in role rather than in the victim's state of mind at the instant: the attacker may be attacked, the non-combatant may not. The technical instrument at work here is the doctrine of double effect, which she adapts from the Catholic moral tradition. An effect merely foreseen is not thereby intended; on this account the bomber who destroys a munitions works while foreseeing that people nearby will die has done nothing wrong, whereas Truman, who needed the deaths, has murdered. She adds a further charge against the Allies' demand for unconditional surrender: a demand that can only be met by atrocity forms part of the wrongdoing, not merely its occasion.

On the defence that the bomb saved lives, Anscombe's first move is to dispute the arithmetic. The casualty figures offered for an invasion of the home islands were settled after the fact and were never agreed; the blockade and the Soviet entry had already brought Japan to the point of looking for terms. What the defenders call a calculation is therefore not a fact about 1945 but a rhetorical device. Her second move is to deny the authority of any such calculation: even if the numbers were as claimed, they could not license what is forbidden absolutely. In practice the two moves come apart, and the second is doing less work in the pamphlet than its prominence suggests. What Anscombe objects to, chiefly, is that Truman's defenders cannot establish their premise. Had the calculation been certain — had it been demonstrable in advance that the bomb would save more lives than it cost — the decision would have been a tragic necessity rather than a crime, and the objection would have been that the tragedy was avoidable, not that it was murder.

The consequence of this absolutism is that Anscombe is committed to pacifism, and she does not resist the commitment. If it is always murder to kill the innocent as a means, and if no modern war can be conducted without doing precisely that — the blockade, the bombing of cities, the bomb — then no modern war can be conducted at all. A nation that cannot fight without murdering had better not fight. This is why her protest is not a narrow point about one weapon but a rejection of the idea of a just war; it is why she reads the prohibition she takes to be common to the Jewish and Christian moral traditions as leaving no room whatever for statecraft; and it is why the target of the pamphlet is as much Oxford as Truman, the university having chosen to honour the whole conduct of the Allied war.

---
