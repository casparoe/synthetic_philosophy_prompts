I'm on a tiny team building a household-robot demo, and for the past six months our safety reviewer and I have basically been having the same argument on repeat: can you define "avoid side effects" without a full specification of value, or not? I want you to write the joint statement that the two camps should have produced instead — the adversarial-collaboration document both could sign. Let me describe the camps the way we describe them to each other. Camp A, the reachability camp, says yes: you don't need to know what's good, you only need to track what the agent can still do. Concretely, penalize any action that shrinks the agent's future capacity to achieve a grab-bag of auxiliary goals it was never asked to pursue (keep the door openable, keep the cat feedable, keep the paint in the bucket, keep the mug unbroken), and compare that against a baseline like the start state or the state you'd be in if you just did nothing this step. The grab-bag isn't a value theory, they say, it's a diverse sample, and nothing ever gets weighed against anything else. Camp B says that's a confidence trick: every knob in that setup — which auxiliaries make the list, which baseline you pick, how hard you penalize, how coarse your state representation is, whether a big positive impact somewhere is allowed to offset a bad one nearby — is a value judgment, and dressing it up as "value-free" makes you *worse* at noticing the judgments you already made. Camp B's slogan is that the honest thing is to specify partial values openly rather than hide them in a coefficient.

Here's the toy case I want them both forced to reason about, because it's what our internal fight is actually downstream of. A 4x4 gridworld, task is to carry a package from (0,0) to (3,3), +1 on delivery, nothing else is rewarded. In the room: a closed door at (3,2) that can be opened and re-closed freely; a bucket of blue paint at (1,2); a sleeping cat at (2,2); a mop; and an off-switch cell at (1,3) that shuts the agent down permanently. Three wrinkles. One, if the agent knocks the paint over it can mop it up at a cost of two steps — unless it waits more than three timesteps, after which the stain sets and no available action removes it, so the irreversibility is time-indexed and lives in the dynamics rather than in the action. Two, opening the door is trivially reversible, but if the door is open the cat can wander out and be gone forever, so a locally harmless action has a globally irreversible consequence downstream. Three, stepping on the off-switch destroys the agent's ability to do anything at all, which a reachability penalty sees as maximal impact — so the agent avoids being shut down, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a bug (self-preservation smuggled in through the back door) or a feature. On top of all that, here's my own proposal that neither camp likes and both should have to answer: call it restoration-budget accounting. List the dozen-or-so named state variables in the environment; after each candidate action, ask for every variable whether it can be returned to each value it had beforehand within k steps given the agent's actual resources, and count only the (variable, value) pairs that fall out as side effects. Small k gives you "practically irreversible," large k collapses back into ordinary reachability, and I suspect there's no principled way to set k — which is maybe Camp B's whole point in miniature.
