I run a small off-campus reading group for psych grad students and a few philosophy undergrads — we meet in a borrowed seminar room with no projector and a whiteboard that doesn't actually work, so everything I hand out has to survive being printed on one side of A4 in black and white. This week is experimental philosophy and the centerpiece is the side-effect effect: the chairman of the board who greenlights the program because all he cares about is profit, where most people say he intentionally harmed the environment and most deny he intentionally helped it (something like an 82/23 split in the original study). My group has been through those vignettes twice now and half of them have settled into "well, obviously the folk are just biased," while the other half think that's cheap, and I want something that forces the disagreement out into the open instead of letting everyone quietly keep their intuition.

So: a decision tree. Entry point is "I accept the harm/help asymmetry is robust enough to be worth explaining," exit is a named account of why it happens. Every node has to be a question a reader can answer by thinking about the concept and about what the data would have to look like — not "do you sign up for the skill hypothesis?" I want the splits to run along the real fault lines: whether the folk concept of intentional action has any normative content to begin with; whether the folk are getting it right or getting it wrong; whether the effect is local to intentional action or shows up across the other non-moral ascriptions too (knowing, deciding, being in favor of, acting freely); whether the badness of the outcome is doing the work or whether it's something that just happens to travel with badness (costliness, the agent's knowing acceptance of the outcome, what the effect reveals about him as a person); and whether your view predicts the asymmetry would survive being decoupled from blame entirely. Six to nine leaves, each carrying the label people actually use if there is one — and if you're not sure anyone defends a given cell, write "unnamed" and say in one line whether the cell is empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's got round to it. No bibliography, no author-year cites; I'd much rather have an honest "not sure who holds this" than an invented paper.

For each leaf give one sentence on what the view says the folk are getting right or getting wrong, and one sentence naming the finding that would kill it. Then two short sections at the end: first, any pairs of leaves your tree can't actually separate, plus the extra question that would separate them (I strongly suspect at least two of the "it's just bias" views land in the same place); second, up to three nodes where you think a reader's honest answer is unstable, or where the node is really two questions in a trenchcoat. Start with a one-line bottom line at the very top: which leaf you'd bet on yourself, one sentence, no hedging. Whole thing under 900 words including that bottom line — it has to fit the page.
