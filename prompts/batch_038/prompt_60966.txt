I am the works manager of a four-person architectural carving and joinery shop that does most of its work on Victorian churches, and three weeks ago I was given sole sign-off on a specification for the first time. The job is the north aisle of a parish church where a small fire did more damage than the smoke: twelve foliate capitals are spalled or gone, the softwood reredos and screen are scorched, and the porch ceiling has to come down and go back up. The architect's specification, which I am now expected to price, defend to the parish, and execute, asks us to do the following six things:

1. Replace the twelve capitals by casting them from a mould taken off the one surviving original, with only light hand-finishing afterwards.
2. Grain the new softwood reredos so that it reads as oak, and, in one bay, so that it reads as veined marble.
3. Rebuild the porch ceiling as a decorative ribbed vault hung from a steel frame, the stone ribs carrying nothing.
4. Chemically treat the new limestone so that its colour matches the soot-darkened fabric it is being set into.
5. Gild the lettering of the aisle memorial.
6. Where a capital is to be carved by hand rather than cast, carve it as an exact facsimile of the lost original, working from photographs and from the surviving capital.

The parish genuinely cannot afford all six done the way I was trained to do them, and I need to know which of them I am right to refuse and which I am merely being precious about. A retired master carver — someone I respect, and someone whose own livelihood was hand work, which I should flag as a possible bias in his advice — pressed on me the second chapter of John Ruskin's *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* (1849), "The Lamp of Truth." I have been reading it at the kitchen table and I am stuck. Here is the passage, copied out of an online text of the chapter, from §VI into the start of §VII; it breaks off where my copy does:

> VI. Architectural Deceits are broadly to be considered under three heads:
>
> 1st. The suggestion of a mode of structure or support, other than the true one; as in pendants of late Gothic roofs.
>
> 2nd. The painting of surfaces to represent some other material than that of which they actually consist (as in the marbling of wood), or the deceptive representation of sculptured ornament upon them.
>
> 3rd. The use of cast or machine-made ornaments of any kind.
>
> Now, it may be broadly stated, that architecture will be noble exactly in the degree in which all these false expedients are avoided. Nevertheless, there are certain degrees of them, which, owing to their frequent usage, or to other causes, have so far lost the nature of deceit as to be admissible; as, for instance, gilding, which is in architecture no deceit, because it is therein not understood for gold; while in jewellery it is a deceit, because it is so understood, and therefore altogether to be reprehended. So that there arise, in the application of the strict rules of right, many exceptions and niceties of conscience; which let us as briefly as possible examine.
>
> VII. 1st. Structural Deceits. I have limited these to the determined and purposed suggestion of a mode of support other than the true one. The architect is not bound to exhibit structure; nor are we to complain of him for concealing it, any more than we should regret that the outer surface of the human frame conceal much of its anatomy; nevertheless, that building will generally be the noblest, which to an intelligent eye discovers the great secrets of its structure, as an animal form does, although from a careless observer they may be concealed. In the vaulting of a Gothic roof it is no deceit to throw the strength into the ribs of it, and make the intermediate vault a mere shell. […]

Please help me understand what Ruskin is actually committed to here. Specifically:

1. The gilding concession. Gilding is admitted in architecture but condemned in jewellery, and the stated difference is that in architecture it "is therein not understood for gold." Is the criterion what actual viewers believe, what the practice invites them to believe, or something about the standing conventions of a craft? And if that criterion really is doing the work, does it not license things Ruskin plainly does not want licensed — above all the cast ornament, which by now nobody mistakes for hand cutting?
2. The third head. Why is a cast or machine-made ornament a species of *deceit* at all? A cast can be perfectly visible as a cast. Locate the lie for me: is it in the object, in the inference the object invites about how it was made, in a claim about the maker's labour, or somewhere else entirely?
3. Concealment versus suggestion. §VII says the architect is not bound to exhibit structure and that concealment is no more to be complained of than skin hiding anatomy, yet condemns "the determined and purposed suggestion" of a false support. Where exactly is that line, and what is the word "purposed" adding? If Ruskin needs an intention condition in the first head, is that consistent with the third head, where nobody seems to intend to fool anybody?
4. The intelligent eye. The rib-and-shell vault looks structural and is not, yet is no deceit, because an intelligent observer would presume the ribs carry it — while a careless observer may be left deceived. Whose eye is the standard, and how much weight is that idealized spectator bearing? Does it save the chapter, or does it quietly relocate the whole question from the building to the audience?
5. Value. "Architecture will be noble exactly in the degree in which all these false expedients are avoided." Is truthfulness for Ruskin a *constituent* of architectural value or an independent *constraint* on it — that is, can a deceptive building be beautiful and merely forfeit nobility, or does the deception spoil the beauty itself? Tell me which reading the quoted sentences support and what turns on the answer.
6. The question I care most about. Does Ruskin's objection make a building's worth depend on its actual history of making, so that a flawless, fully documented cast is second-rate even when every viewer is correctly informed about it; or does it depend only on the beliefs the building produces in viewers? People arguing about forgeries and facsimiles today split along exactly this line, and my impression is that this chapter is trying to hold both at once. Tell me whether it can, and if it cannot, which half the gilding example gives away.
