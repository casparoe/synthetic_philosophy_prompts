I am convening a half-day session at a workshop on the foundations of autonomous agency. Rather than two talks, I have persuaded the two invited speakers to produce a co-signed joint statement, and I would like you to draft it for them.

The first speaker works in the situation-calculus tradition of planning. Her position: the frame problem, in the sense in which AI originally posed it, is a representational bookkeeping difficulty with a known family of solutions — successor-state axioms, occlusion-based formalisms, nonmonotonic treatments of the circumscription and default-logic kind — and the residual difficulties are matters of scale, elaboration tolerance, and engineering. What philosophers have made of the term since, she thinks, is a different question wearing the same name.

The second speaker is a philosopher of mind and cognitive science. His position: those formalisms do not solve the problem so much as inherit it. They require a fixed, finitely enumerated vocabulary of fluents and actions, supplied from outside by someone who has already decided what can change and what cannot; and they license inertia — whatever is not mentioned stays the same — which no finite agent embedded in an unstructured environment is entitled to assume. On his view the deep problem is the determination of relevance, it is holistic rather than merely combinatorial, and it applies to any architecture that mediates action through a representation, whatever logic that representation is written in.

To stop the statement from floating free of anything decidable, both have agreed to fix their cruxes to one concrete system, described here. Take this description as given; do not invent further results for it.

**The Kitchen Case.** A lab has built a mobile manipulator, K, whose domain model is not hand-authored. At each decision point, a large language model receives a natural-language scene description and the current goal, and is asked to (i) list the state variables relevant to the next few actions, (ii) propose preconditions and effects for each available action, and (iii) rank the listed variables by likelihood of changing on their own. The output is compiled on the fly into a small classical planning problem with successor-state-style axioms, solved by an off-the-shelf planner, and the first action is executed. After each action a perceptual check tests a handful of predicted invariants; on failure, the domain model is regenerated from scratch. K was run for two hundred episodes in each of two settings: an instrumented lab kitchen with a fixed object inventory, and an occupied apartment kitchen whose residents move things around between episodes. The lab reports roughly 90 percent task success in the first setting and 45 percent in the second, and classifies most of the failures in the second setting as cases in which the model omitted a relevant variable.

Write the joint statement in the first-person plural, as a document both speakers could actually put their names to, in the following sections and order:

1. **Shared ground** (7–10 numbered claims, 350–450 words). Claims both can sign without caveat — including what both agree the original technical difficulty consisted in, what both agree the existing formal solutions genuinely achieve, what both agree the Kitchen Case shows, and what both agree no currently implemented system does. Each claim must be specific enough that a reader can imagine one of the parties being tempted to refuse it.

2. **Where we still differ** (table, roughly 400 words). Columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; the planner's own formulation of her side, in wording she would sign; the philosopher's formulation of his, in wording he would sign; and a verdict — substantive, partly terminological, or wholly terminological — with one line of justification.

3. **Terminology** (about 200 words). An agreed stipulation of how the statement will use the frame problem, the qualification problem, relevance, and open world, plus an explicit note of which senses each party rejects and why. Use this section to settle, once and for all, how much of section 2 was an argument about words.

4. **Cruxes** (three or four, 700–800 words total). For each: the sub-question, stated so that both parties accept that resolving it would move at least one of them; the feature of the Kitchen Case, or the analysis or experiment performed on it, that both accept as bearing on it; the planner's prediction, written in advance and falsifiable; the philosopher's prediction, likewise; and a note on which of them has conceded more if the prediction fails. At least one crux should concern whether the failure taxonomy the lab itself supplies — omission of a relevant variable — is a description of an engineering defect or a restatement of the philosophical problem.

5. **What would change our minds** (about 200 words). For each party, two observations or arguments that would count against their own position, specified tightly enough that a third party could recognize them had they occurred.

6. **Signature note** (about 100 words). A closing paragraph stating what the collaboration resolved, what it relocated, and what it left open.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus: describe positions and results rather than referring to them. Second, neither party may be a strawman — if you judge that one side's standard line is overstated or simply wrong, say so inside the relevant section and give the weakened version that side could actually sign, rather than reproducing the strong version for symmetry's sake. Aim for roughly 1,800 words in total.
