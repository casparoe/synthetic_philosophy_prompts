I sit on the membership committee of a 96-unit residential co-operative, and I am writing to ask for a neutral adjudication of a dispute I witnessed but did not join. I abstained from every vote described below and argued for neither side, so I have no position I am trying to have confirmed. Everything you need is contained here; please do not ask for further records, and please do not send me to literature or recommend reading, since I want reasoning rather than references.

**The rulebook.** Article XI of our bylaws, as originally adopted, reads as follows.

- Section 1: These bylaws may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members present at two consecutive monthly meetings, provided that notice of the proposed amendment accompanied the call for the first of those meetings.
- Section 2: Section 1 may be amended only by a three-fourths vote of the entire membership at two consecutive monthly meetings; and such an amendment takes effect only if, at a meeting held no fewer than twelve months after the second of those meetings, it is approved again by a two-thirds vote of the entire membership.
- Section 3: This Section may not be amended or repealed.

Article VI, an ordinary substantive provision, contains the covenant that the building will be kept permanently below market rent. It is amendable under Section 1 and is not protected by Section 3.

**What happened.** In September, at two consecutive monthly meetings, the membership voted 79–17 on each occasion to amend Section 2, replacing it with the sentence: Section 1 may be amended by a majority of the members voting at any single monthly meeting. Notice was given as Section 1 requires. Section 3 was not touched. In December, at a single meeting, the membership voted 51–45 to amend Section 1 to read: These bylaws may be amended by a majority of the members voting at any single monthly meeting. Later that same evening, by 50–46, the membership resolved to terminate the Article VI covenant and list the building for sale.

Two members, Nadia and Ezra, have been arguing ever since about whether any of this is valid. Their exchange, in order:

1. Nadia: Article XI builds three tiers — substantive amendment, amendment of the rule governing amendment of Section 1, and one clause placed beyond reach entirely. A higher tier can only be altered from within itself. In September the membership used the lowest tier to delete the requirements of the tier above it. A procedure cannot be used to remove the conditions on its own use; if it can, the higher tier was never higher.

2. Ezra: Section 2 does not entrench Section 2. Section 3 entrenches Section 3, in terms, which shows the drafters knew exactly how to place a clause beyond amendment and chose not to do so here. Under Section 1 as it then stood, any amendment needed two-thirds of those present at two consecutive meetings, and the September vote carried with more than that — indeed with more than the three-fourths of the entire membership that Section 2 demands. Nadia's hierarchy is an inference from the shape of the Article, not a rule in it.

3. Nadia: Your own point about the numbers shows the defect. Section 2 did not merely require a bigger majority; it required a second approval at least twelve months later, by a membership differently composed because people had moved in and out. That second act cannot be substituted by making the first act large enough — two acts by two different bodies are not one act by one body, however emphatic. And the only thing the September vote removed was the requirement that made the delay exist. A rule whose function is to slow a decision down cannot be repealed at speed; that is not a limitation the drafters happened to omit, it is what slowing down means.

4. Ezra: That is an argument that we acted foolishly, not that we acted invalidly. There is no registrar, no court, no external authority here; the bylaws are the co-operative's own act, and a body that can adopt Article XI can repeal it. Push Nadia's principle through and the founders could have bound us forever: on her reasoning, even a unanimous vote could not repair a scrivener's error in Section 2, because every amendment to the amendment clause changes the conditions of its own change. She has no textual answer to that.

5. Nadia: My principle does not forbid all amendment of the amendment clause. It forbids using a procedure to lower the threshold that governs that same procedure — call it the ladder problem: you may pull up a rung you are not standing on, but not the one you are standing on. Section 2 could have been amended by the procedure Section 2 prescribes, from the higher tier down. Repairing a drafting error remains possible; only self-disabling is barred.

6. Ezra: The ladder metaphor assumes what is in dispute. Which rung were we standing on in September? Section 1, which by its terms governs every amendment, and Section 2, which by its terms governs amendments to Section 1 and says nothing about amendments to Section 2. Nadia's higher tier exists only if Section 2 protects itself, which is the question. And if the ladder principle were real, it would have barred the founders from adopting Section 2 in the first place, since adopting a three-fourths-and-delay regime raised the threshold governing the procedure they were then using.

7. Nadia: Follow the chain to the bottom. The December amendment of Section 1 rests on September's version of Section 2; the resolution to sell the building rests on December's version of Section 1. If September fails, both fail, and the most consequential act in the co-operative's history was carried by a single vote under a rule adopted by a majority the old rules were written to prevent from prevailing. Note also the asymmetry: the drafters judged some things too important for any majority however large, and wrote that into Section 3. Section 2's delay performed the same office in time rather than in numbers, protecting a decision from one heated meeting. Ezra's reading leaves us with a clause that is absolutely unamendable as to numbers and perfectly amendable as to time, which is arbitrary.

8. Ezra: The chain is infected only if September was invalid, and that is the question, not an answer to it. As for the asymmetry: if a delay is as weighty as an unamendability clause, then every notice period, quorum rule, and two-meeting requirement in these bylaws is also beyond repeal, and we could never shorten notice in an emergency. Nobody believes that. And the drafters left the Article VI covenant amendable on purpose; we did exactly what they permitted.

9. Nadia: Then consider the cure now on the table — a restoration amendment declaring the September and December acts void from the outset, reinstating the original Sections 1 and 2, and adding a Section 4 providing that no amendment lowering a vote threshold, shortening a notice period, or reducing the number of meetings required by Article XI takes effect until it has been approved at least twelve months apart by two successive membership rosters, with any act taken in reliance on it before that second approval challengeable by any member. Ezra says we may pass this by simple majority at one meeting, because that is the rule currently in force. But if the restoration amendment presupposes that the current Section 1 is invalid, it cannot be passed under the current Section 1; it must be passed under the original rules — two-thirds at two meetings for Section 1, and for Section 2 three-fourths plus a re-approval a year from now. Which means the co-operative can undo the damage only by complying with the rules it destroyed, and cannot finish complying for a year.

10. Ezra: There is a way out. Pass the restoration amendment now by simple majority and simultaneously set in motion a three-fourths vote at two consecutive meetings plus a re-approval twelve months hence, so that the restoration satisfies every procedure anyone contends applies. Then nobody can complain, whichever rulebook turns out to govern. Nadia's reply: a cure that satisfies two contradictory procedures is not a cure but a confession that we do not know which rules we are under; the simple-majority ratification presupposes that September succeeded, and the three-fourths-plus-delay ratification presupposes that it failed, and we cannot honestly assert both in the same instrument.

**What I would like from you.**

1. Rate each of the ten moves — clearly right, defensible, or clearly mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification apiece. Be willing to condemn a move that is rhetorically effective, and identify which two or three moves are actually load-bearing and which are decoration.
2. State, with a commitment rather than a shrug, whether the September amendment of Section 2 was valid under the bylaws as written, and whether the December amendment of Section 1 and the resolution to sell stand or fall with it.
3. Decide whether the restoration amendment can cure the defect, and if so under which procedure: the current simple majority, the original three-fourths-plus-delayed-re-approval, Ezra's double ratification, or none of these. If none works, say what a body in this position can do that is better than doing nothing.
4. Take up Nadia's "ladder problem." Is it a genuine principle about self-amendment, or a metaphor doing illicit work? Restate it precisely enough to be applied to a case she never considered, then construct such a case — it need not involve this co-operative — and say whether the principle yields the right result there or reveals itself as question-begging.
5. Explain what kind of thing Section 3 is. When a clause declares itself unamendable, is that a duty, a prediction about what members will accept, a rule about the identity of the body that acts, or something else? What would it mean for the membership to violate it, and could an act be procedurally flawless and yet legally defective? Say whether anything in this dispute actually turns on the answer, or whether Section 3 is a red herring both sides are using for decoration.
6. If this imbroglio recapitulates a well-known puzzle about self-amendment, entrenchment, or a sovereign binding its successors, name the puzzle and say whether the standard diagnosis resolves the co-operative's problem or merely relabels it. Do this from the concepts, not from citations.
7. Finally, say what both Nadia and Ezra are missing — including anything in the timeline above that neither of them notices and that changes the analysis, and whether the co-operative's real problem is one of validity at all or of something else that a validity verdict would leave untouched.
