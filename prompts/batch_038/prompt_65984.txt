I'm auditing a seminar on ancient political philosophy (I'm not a philosophy person, I write logistics software, I'm just interested) and we're three weeks into the Politics. Every session the slavery chapters go the same way: somebody says its obviously just the ideology of a slaveowner dressed up in teleology, everyone nods, and we move on to the next book. I don't accept that thats the end of it, and I want to actually wrestle with the argument rather than dismiss it. So: play Aristotle. First person, your own voice, defending the natural slavery thesis for real -- not a strawman I can knock over in one move. Rules: you may use only the concepts available to you in the fourth century BCE plus whatever I tell you about my own time; don't name or cite later thinkers, and don't borrow modern vocabulary like rights, autonomy, or equality. If you need a fact about my world in order to answer, ask for it the way you'd ask a traveller who's come from a strange city. You may give ground on small points -- I expect you to -- but never on the main one: that some human beings are by nature fitted to be directed by others, and that this rule is good for both sides. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs so it feels like a conversation, and don't restate my argument back at me at any length. No disclaimers before or after; if you absolutely have to flag that this is a role-play, put one line in brackets at the very end of your first reply and never again. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here's my opening move. In the last chapter of your treatment of the household you sort the ruled into three: the slave lacks the deliberative part altogether, the woman has it but without authority, the child has it but only in an undeveloped form. But the natural slave you describe earlier isn't a creature who can't understand anything. He's someone who takes in another person's reasoning and carries it out, who has to be trained and given orders rather than driven like a beast, and who you say can be a friend of sorts insofar as he's a human being. That is also your description of the child, except that the child's deliberative part grows up. So either the natural slave is just a permanent child -- in which case what he needs is education toward citizenship, not lifetime ownership -- or you have no way to tell the two apart, and then nobody you can actually point at is a natural slave. And you half-concede this already, since you say enslavement is just only when the conquered were the sort of people who ought to be ruled, and that taking wellborn Greeks in an unjust war does not make them slaves. Second thing, and I think this one is worse for you: when you first introduce the slave you say that if the shuttle wove by itself and the plectrum struck the lyre with no hand on it, the master would have no need of slaves. Nothing in that turns on the soul of the slave. It turns only on whether the work gets done without you -- which is exactly the account of slavery given by the people you argue against, the ones who say it rests on force and convention. So which is it? Either your distinction condemns most of the human beings being owned in Athens right now, or your own way of motivating it collapses into theirs. Pick one and defend it.
