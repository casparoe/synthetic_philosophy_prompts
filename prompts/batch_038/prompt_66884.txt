I am writing a novel whose central consciousness is Arthur Smith Woodward — the palaeontologist at the Natural History Museum who, from 1912 onward, made the Piltdown finds his life's work — and the whole book depends on my being able to render his belief honestly rather than as farce. Before I can write him, I need a rigorous epistemic case analysis, and I have decided the analysis should be run through Hume: specifically Section X of the Enquiry, both the general account of testimony in Part I and the argument about miracles in Part II.

Here are the facts as I shall use them, so that you need not go hunting for any. In 1912 Charles Dawson, a Sussex solicitor and prolific amateur antiquarian, brought Woodward fragments of a human-like cranium and an ape-like jaw with curiously worn, human-seeming teeth, said to have come from a gravel pit at Piltdown; the find was announced publicly that December and named for Dawson. A second cache of fragments turned up nearby in 1915. The composite was accepted for roughly forty years, in large part because it confirmed a "brain first" expectation then dominant in British anthropology; an anatomist dissenting within a few years of the discovery argued that the jaw was simply an ape's, and Raymond Dart's Australopithecus child from southern Africa was dismissed in 1925 largely for contradicting it. The exposure came in 1953, by way of chemical dating of bone and microscopic examination that revealed filed teeth and deliberate staining. Who forged the material remains disputed; your analysis must not depend on that question.

What I want, then, is the mapping done carefully and in both directions.

First, sort the evidence: which elements of the case are testimony in Hume's sense, and which are objects offered for interpretation? State the proposition that Dawson's word was supposed to establish, as distinct from the far larger proposition the community inferred from the bones, and say whether Hume's calculus has any term for the second.

Second, apply his conditions on witnesses. Dawson was a man of some standing with no apparent pecuniary interest and much reputation to lose; the announcement was public, unhurried, made among the learned in a country of learning. Very nearly every safeguard Hume requires seems satisfied here, and the testimony was nonetheless false. Say precisely what that costs him, and what it costs him as against the ordinary run of miracle reports he despised.

Third — and be blunt about this — nothing at Piltdown violated a law of nature. A forgery is human artifice, not a miracle, on Hume's own definition of a miracle as "a violation of the laws of nature." So tell me whether Part II has any purchase on this episode at all, or whether Part I is doing all the work; and if it is, say what that shows about how the two parts of Section X hang together.

Fourth, take his sociology seriously and then break it. He holds that such reports abound among ignorant peoples and reach the learned at second hand, and that the love of the marvellous is what carries them. Piltdown inverts the pattern: the credulous are the experts, and the vulgar are barely present. Identify what plays the role of the love of wonder here — national prestige, a preferred picture of human origins, the satisfaction of an answer to continental discoveries — and say where Hume's psychology stretches to cover it and where it snaps.

Fifth, account for the failure of contrary instances to accumulate. Taung was rejected, the dissenting anatomist was set aside, and the find survived for four decades without Hume's machinery of diminishing contrary experience ever being triggered. Is there a place in his framework for the fact that a witness's credibility was being assessed by the very hypothesis under test? If not, state what would have to be added, and whether adding it preserves the theory or replaces it.

Finally, give me the two things I most need as a novelist. One: a timeline of epistemic standing — 1913, 1927, 1953 — on which you tell me when, if ever, Woodward's continued belief ceased to be defensible by Hume's own lights, given that no new evidence arrived until the chemical tests did; and remember that those tests did the work Hume assigns to reason and contrary experience, so say what the arrival of a detection technology does to his account. Two: an explicit list of the places where Hume is simply silent about this case. Do not force him to speak. Mark those silences, and tell me whether Piltdown ultimately confirms his warnings, embarrasses him by locating the failure in the wrong people, or exceeds a two-part structure that never contemplated a falsehood which is neither a miracle nor a report from the vulgar.
