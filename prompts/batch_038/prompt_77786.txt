I should explain my circumstances before putting my question, since they bear on why I am asking it. I retired four years ago after thirty-six years in applied geophysics, most of them spent on the unglamorous business of predicting how fluid moves through fractured rock. Since then I have taken up philosophy properly, rather than in the desultory way one does between grants, and I have joined a small reading group in my town. One of its members is a former colleague who turned to the history and philosophy of science after his own retirement, and he has been pressing an analogy on me for several months now. I keep hearing it elsewhere as well, which makes me suspect it is doing more work than it can carry.

The analogy is that science proceeds by trial-and-error elimination in just the way natural selection does, and that Popper himself endorsed the comparison: problem, tentative theory, elimination of error, new problem; and, if I recall a remark of his correctly, the difference between an amoeba and Einstein is only that Einstein is willing to let his theories die in his place. I have not checked either attribution and I am not asking you to. My friend maps the two domains onto each other with some care. Conjectures are the heritable variation. Refutations are the selective event. The published literature and the training of graduate students are the inheritance mechanism. The world, as interrogated by instruments, is the environment. Corroboration is fitness. Competing research programmes are competing lineages. The consensus of a field at a given time is the population. From this he draws three conclusions: that demarcation is a matter of degree rather than kind, since the astrologer too ventures guesses and is loosely sifted by experience, so the hunt for a criterion separating science from non-science is a pseudo-problem; that selection can never justify, so survival is no warrant for truth and the falsificationist's own criterion enjoys no privileged standing; and that the whole question of whether science is rational therefore dissolves, since we have described a natural process rather than a normative one.

My unease is not abstract. In my own field, the standard way of representing flow through a fracture network rested on an idealization that everyone used and nobody defended in print. It was never falsified by an experiment. Over about two decades it was patched with adjustable parameters, so that a failed prediction was absorbed by retuning rather than by rejection, and the model was simply moved to the domain where it still worked. It fell out of use within roughly five years of the retirement of the handful of people who controlled the journals and the panels, and I could not point you to a decisive new measurement that killed it. On my friend's analogy that is just selection doing its work. But from where I sat it looked much more like the death of the organisms than the death of their theories, which is precisely what Popper says separates Einstein from the amoeba. I do not know whether that observation rescues the analogy or undoes it, and I should like your judgement on that point in particular.

What I want is an audit rather than a summary of either Popper or Darwin. Set out the correspondences explicitly, taking each pairing in turn, and then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the three conclusions from the ones that are merely picturesque and can be conceded without cost. Say what features of natural selection the analogy quietly imports into the target domain that the target lacks. I have some candidates in mind but no confidence that they are the right ones: that variation in evolution is not aimed at a problem, whereas Popper's schema begins with one; that biology has no inheritance of acquired characteristics, whereas a refuted theory hands its lessons to the next conjecture, which makes the process Lamarckian in a way selection is not; and that survival means something very different when the selector is a colleague deciding whether to cite you than when it is a winter deciding whether an organism lives. Please also tell me whether the analogy is being used descriptively or prescriptively at each step, since the first conclusion seems to need the descriptive reading and the third the normative one. Then state plainly whether each of the three conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether the analogy was carrying them all along. If it fails, say whether a better one is available. My own suspicion is that something from iterative engineering design, where a specification is deliberately varied against a known failure criterion, would do more work; but I do not know whether it brings distortions of its own.

One request about form. Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables. When you lay out the correspondences, take them one after another in sentences rather than in columns. I am not looking for references or a reading list, and I would rather have your reasoning than your sources.
