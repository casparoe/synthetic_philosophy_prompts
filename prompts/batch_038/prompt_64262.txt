so i'm drafting a paper on deferance to objective chance and I've ended up with five candidate principles on my desk and I cant work out which of them is worth defending. Rank them for me. Score each out of ten, but on one dimension only: how well it dodges the self-undermining problem — the one where the chance function assigns chances to chance propositions themselves, so a straight-up Principal Principle tells you to defer to a chance about a chance and ends up pulling against itself — without ceasing to be recognisable deference to chance at all. I don't want the standard objections recited back at me; I want an ordering with a reason attached to each item.

Here they are, in my own shorthand. A is the proposition to be believed, H is the total non-chance history up to the present, x and y are reals in the unit interval.

1. Plain PP: whenever the proposition that the chance of A is x is included in my evidence, my credence in A given that evidence should be x, provided the remainder of my evidence is admissible.

2. Historical PP: my credence in A, given that the chance of A conditional on H is x, should be x, where H is the actual non-chance history. Chance propositions are never allowed into the conditioning part.

3. Recursion-blocked PP: as in 1, except the principle applies only to chance propositions whose subject matter contains no chance facts and no facts about my own credences. Chance may be about anything, but deference is only owed where it cannot come to be owed about deference itself.

4. Weighted PP: my credence in A should be w times x plus one-minus-w times the credence in A I'd have had if I'd never heard of chance, where w is my degree of belief that the chance fact in question is a genuine chance fact rather than a hypothesis somebody has merely asserted to me. w is explicitly not itself a chance.

5. Second-order PP: I defer to the chance of the chance. My credence in A, given that the chance that the chance of A is x is y, should be y, with first-order deference recovered as the limiting case where y is one.

For each of the five, say where it lands and why in a couple of sentences, then give the ordering from best to worst and tell me which one you would actually stand up and defend in front of a hostile audience. On top of that: which of my five is a real principle from the literature wearing a slightly diffrent hat, and which is genuinely something I've invented? And close with a single number between 0 and 1 for how likely you think your winner is to survive a determined referee.
