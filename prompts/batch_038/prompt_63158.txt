Build me a thought experiment about a dynasty that learns the theory of its own decline and then acts on it. Specifications: the dynasty follows Ibn Khaldun's pattern — group solidarity (asabiyyah) forged by shared hardship, spent across three or four generations of settled comfort — and in its second generation it acquires an accurate written account of that cycle and institutes a deliberate austerity regimen for its heirs. Engineer the case so that a linear, cumulative view of history (one on which recorded experience lets a society permanently escape a trap it can foresee) predicts the regimen succeeds, while the Khaldunian account predicts it fails, and the failure must come from inside the theory rather than from corrupt rulers, a watered-down program, or bad luck. Hold the external conditions fixed: no invasion, no plague, no climate shock, no succession crisis; the only variable is the dynasty's self-knowledge. The account of the cycle must arrive as an ordinary chronicle — no prophecy, no magic, no time travel. One paragraph for the scenario.

Then do the work. Give each view's prediction and the mechanism that produces it; identify the feature of the case doing the work; say what happens to the divergence if the rulers keep the regimen but stop believing the theory that motivated it; and state the strongest reply available to the linear view, in particular whether it can absorb the case by treating the Khaldunian diagnosis as simply more accumulated knowledge of the kind it credits. Add a short note on whether Polybius's constitutional cycle, which runs on the succession of regimes rather than on the character of a generation, is vulnerable in the same way.

Finish by saying what you take the case to establish about cyclical versus linear historiography, how confident you are in the mechanism you have attributed to Ibn Khaldun, and which step of it you are least sure of.
