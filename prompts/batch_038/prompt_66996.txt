ok so before I read one more defense or attack of middle knowledge I want to back up and write the spec first, because half the arguments in this area are people disagreeing about what the job even is. So: write the set of requirements that ANY account of providence has to meet to count as adequate — Molinism is one candidate sitting the test, not the assumed winner. The point is for me to decide what to actually build on, and to hand the list to a grad seminar that can then argue about the list instead of about the views.

By a requirement I mean a thing the account has to be able to do, plus the case or consideration that shows why it's a requirement and not just a taste. Register I'm going for:

- it has to say what makes counterfactuals of creaturely freedom true, if there are any, without either making God the author of them or leaving them as bare facts. That's the grounding objection — but tell me what would actually count as answering it rather than restating it, because most of the literature seems to just relocate the problem.
- it has to handle the situation where no world God can actualize gets the goods he's after without some creature going wrong somewhere, and its nastier cousin: the possibility that there is no feasible world that's good enough at all.
- it has to say how much risk God is running, and whether that is compatible with the sort of thing a perfect being can do, given that there seem to be promises about the future in the mix.
- it has to deliver something to a parent whose kid was killed by a drunk driver — not an appeal to mystery, an actual content the view licenses. Say what that content is, and say whether a view that can't supply it is thereby false or merely cold.

and then two requirements nobody seems to state, where I want you to argue whether they're genuine or whether I'm smuggling in a prejudice of my own:

1. counterfactuals whose antecedents involve agents who never get created. Middle knowledge is usually glossed as knowing what any free creature would do in any circumstance, but the circumstances are ones God picks out and the creatures are ones he might never make. Does the account need truthmakers for claims about the never-created, and what exactly breaks if it says no?
2. counterfactuals whose antecedents contain an indeterministic physical event — a decay, a spin measurement — rather than a choice. Everyone argues as though the only two options are divine decree and free creaturely choice, but there's a third kind of truth about the world that is neither. Does an account of providence have to cover those too, and can Molinist machinery stretch to them without changing what the label means?

For each one, mark it non-negotiable or camp-relative — i.e. only wanted by people who already accept libertarian freedom, or meticulous providence, or exhaustive definite foreknowledge — and name which of those commitments is doing the work. Then the part I most want: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and which subset is jointly unsatisfiable. I have a hunch that strong libertarian freedom plus no brute counterfactuals plus meticulous control is not satisfiable together, and I want the argument for or against that, not a shrug.

Finish with a scorecard of open theism, simple foreknowledge, Thomist premotion, compatibilist Calvinism and Molinism, one or two lines each, that's all. No reading list and no citations — I'm after the criteria, I know where to look for the views.
