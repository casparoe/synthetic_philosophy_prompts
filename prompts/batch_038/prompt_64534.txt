I'm sitting in on a philosophy-of-interpretation seminar this term, sort of unofficially — I'm actually a linguistics grad student who just needed an extra slot for something humanities-flavored — and last week we did the whole fight over whether the meaning of a literary text is fixed by what the author intended or by what the words do to a reader on their own. The cases people kept trading around felt too tidy to me, like they were built to make one side win, so I've been sitting on a messier one and I want to see it argued properly. My roommate thinks the case is a trick question and that I'm wasting my time, which is mildly annoying and also possibly correct, so settle it for me.

Here's the case. In the mid-seventies a novel came out under a single pen name, but it was really written by two people, a married couple, who alternated chapters and told no one about the arrangement. Early in the book there's a recurring four-line passage about a lighthouse keeper who keeps the lamp lit for the ships that will not come. One of the two authors — call her A — wrote the first appearance of those lines intending them as an image of faithful devotion, hope sustained past all evidence. The other — call him B — wrote a later recurrence of the same lines and intended them as bitter irony, a picture of pointless routine. Neither knew the other's reading, they never once discussed the passage, and each was privately convinced it obviously meant what they took it to mean. Decades after both of them died, a box of letters surfaced in which each, separately and at some length, spells out their own intention about the lines. Readers had already split into a devotion camp and an irony camp, and each camp now says the letters vindicate it. Two details that matter, so don't route around them: the wording isn't archaic or dialectal, and the ordinary public language of the period supports both readings about equally; and the surrounding plot is genuinely compatible with either, so there's no contextual tiebreaker sitting in the rest of the book.

So write me an essay on what, if anything, determines the meaning of that passage. Weigh the arguments on both sides seriously — I want the best version of the view you end up rejecting, not a strawman. Work through the options you think are live: that there are two meanings, one for each half of the authorship; that there's one determinate meaning and it belongs to neither A nor B; that there's no fact of the matter at all; that meaning here tracks some kind of joint or collective authorial stance that A and B never actually had; that the dispute is partly verbal and the interesting question is which reading the text best rewards rather than which one is correct. Consider whether intentionalism can be patched to handle the case or whether the patch costs it the thing that made it attractive, and consider whether anti-intentionalism gets the right verdict here for the wrong reasons — because it seems to me it might, and that bugs me. Also take up a variant: suppose A and B had coordinated and jointly intended the lines to be ambiguous. Does your answer change, and what does the comparison between the two versions tell you about which theory is actually right? Then commit. End on a real verdict about the original case — a hedged "there's something to both sides" conclusion counts as a fail, though a conditional verdict is fine if the condition is specific. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, i.e. say what you think fixes the meaning of that passage (or that nothing does), and then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating the setup back at me, and no bibliography or citation hunting — argue it from the case itself.
