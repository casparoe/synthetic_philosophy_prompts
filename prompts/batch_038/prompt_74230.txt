Please write a satirical compliance document of roughly 900–1,100 words, formatted as an institutional Standard Operating Procedure: a document-control header (SOP number, effective date, supersedes, owner, approvals block with invented titles), numbered clauses, and at least one appendix.

Setting: the Saint-Germain Institute for Somatic Mechanism, a present-day biomedical research facility whose governing metaphysics is Descartes's beast-machine doctrine, held sincerely and without irony by its leadership. The name is a private nod to the hydraulic moving statues in the royal gardens that Descartes invoked as a model for the living body; the calibration appendix may acknowledge that lineage in passing. The Institute has concluded that its research animals are self-assembling machines, that a machine cannot think, that felt pain requires thought, and that everything in a specimen which in us would accompany suffering accompanies in it only the bodily motions — never a feeling. The SOP is the Institute's attempt to administer that conclusion.

Required sections, at minimum:

1. Purpose and Scope — including an exemption clause covering human personnel, who are machines plus a rational soul, together with the Institute's honest account of how it knows this and why the same warrant cannot be extended to specimens.
2. Definitions — specimen, mechanism, vocalization, and distress, the last being a term the Institute needs and cannot afford.
3. Anesthesia and restraint — exempted, with the reasoning given in the Institute's own voice rather than the author's.
4. The Sign Test — the Institute's two certain marks of a thinking being, paraphrased from Descartes: no flexible, novel arrangement of words or other signs deployed to declare one's thoughts to another; and a pattern of admirable competence in some tasks alongside failure in arbitrarily many others, showing action from the disposition of organs rather than from knowledge. Include an escalation procedure for a specimen that passes, and make explicit what the Institute would then be obliged to do.
5. Vocalization and avoidance behavior — how staff are to interpret crying, writhing, and attempts to escape. There is a long-standing story about a Cartesian philosopher who kicked a pregnant dog and dismissed her cries as the noise of a mechanism under strain; echo the content of that story without naming anyone or putting the line in a real person's mouth.
6. Parts, disposal, and reuse — dismantling a machine is not a wrong; keep the clause precise rather than ghoulish.
7. Training, records, complaints, and audit — including who is competent to file a complaint, and the disposition of any complaint received from a specimen.
8. Appendix A: calibration.
9. Revision history — one or two lines, in which a previous version has been withdrawn for a reason the reader is left to infer.

Rules of execution:

- Deadpan throughout. No winking, no authorial aside, no moral appended at the end. The final clause should be both the document's funniest and its most disturbing, achieved by perfect bureaucratic calm.
- Internal consistency is part of the brief. Every clause must be one a sincere Cartesian could sign. The doctrine may never concede sentience outright; but the Institute's own compliance vocabulary — humane, comfort, unnecessary, welfare, distress, well-being — should keep quietly smuggling it back in, and that leakage should widen as the document proceeds, never once remarked upon.
- The humor must come from getting the metaphysics right, not from absurdity imposed upon it: the clock analogy, action from the disposition of organs, motion without thought, the denial of immortality, and above all the fact that the Sign Test is the Institute's only certain mark, which commits it in advance to recognizing personhood in precisely the case the test was built to exclude.
- Do not quote Descartes or any other real text; paraphrase throughout. Do not name real philosophers as Institute personnel.
- Readable cold by someone who has never read Descartes. A specialist should be able to detect the late correspondence, in which he concedes beasts the whole range of motions that in us accompany the passions and withholds only thought; and Montaigne's complaint about human presumption; without either being identified.
- No bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind.
