So I had a three-hour argument with a friend last night about Zhuangzi and death and neither of us budged an inch, and I want a third opinion that isn't just one of us talking louder. We were both reading the same very old (1891) translation of the sixth chapter of the Zhuangzi — the one that gets called "The Great and Venerable Teacher" or "The Great Ancestral Teacher" depending on who you ask — and we kept going back to the same two chunks, right near the start:

> To complete one's natural term of years and not come to an untimely end in the middle of his course is the fullness of knowledge. Although it be so, there is an evil attending this condition. Such knowledge still awaits the confirmation of it as correct; it does so because it is not yet determined. How do we know that what we call the Heavenly in us is not the Human? And that what we call the Human is not the Heavenly? There must be the True man, and then there is the True knowledge.

and then, a bit later:

> The True men of old knew nothing of the love of life or of the hatred of death. Entrance into life occasioned them no joy; the exit from it awakened no resistance. Composedly they went and came. They did not forget what their beginning [had] been, and they did not inquire into what their end would be. They accepted their life and rejoiced in it; they forgot all fear of death, and returned to their state before life. Thus there was in them what is called the want of any mind to resist the Tao, and of all attempts by means of the Human to assist the Heavenly.

(I've fixed a couple of obvious scan-glitches in what I typed up, including one mangled character and one word that came out as "bad" where it clearly should be "had". Neither of us reads classical Chinese, so treat the translation as what it is.)

Her line: this is ancient fatalism wearing nicer clothes. Death is not an evil, so the correct posture is non-resistance, and the True Man is basically a Stoic sage with different vocabulary — she says "the exit from it awakened no resistance" is doing exactly the work that "some things are up to us and some aren't" does, and that anyone who thinks there's something special here is being fooled by the imagery.

My line: it's not about death being-or-not-being an evil at all. It's about symmetry and memory. The True Men "did not forget what their beginning had been" but "did not inquire into what their end would be," and they "returned to their state before life" — in the past tense, as though the return is already underway and death is just the completion of it. So the claim isn't "nothing bad can happen to me," it's more like "there was never a gap to fall into." Which makes it look like the mirror-image move — pre-birth nonexistence was nothing to me, so post-death nonexistence can't be either — except Zhuangzi isn't making an argument to a frightened person, he's describing a temperament.

Things I actually want from you, in the weeds, not a summary:

1. What is "resistance" in "the exit from it awakened no resistance"? Is it a refusal to die, a mental recoiling, or something like an attempt to steer? Note the other line in the passage about "want of any mind to resist the Tao, and of all attempts by means of the Human to assist the Heavenly" — resistance and assistance seem to be a matched pair of errors, and I can't tell whether the dying case is an instance of the first one or somehow of both.

2. Why is *not inquiring* into your end listed as a virtue right next to not forgetting your beginning? Is that epistemic humility, or is curiosity about your own end itself a form of the resistance in question — i.e. is the passage condemning a certain kind of question rather than a certain kind of feeling? My friend thinks the asymmetry is just sloppy Victorian translation and carries no weight; I think it's the whole point.

3. Is "returned to their state before life" doing real metaphysical work, or is it decorative? If it's the mirror-image move, does her objection land — does it collapse into a familiar Hellenistic argument and stop being distinctively anything? And is my "it's a description of a temperament, not an argument" reply a real difference or am I just building a little shelter for my side of the argument?

4. The circularity in the first chunk is the part I can't shake. Completing your natural term is called the fullness of knowledge, then immediately there's "an evil attending this condition," because such knowledge "awaits the confirmation of it as correct," and then: there must be the True man, and then there is the True knowledge. So true knowledge requires the True Man and the True Man seems to be identified by having true knowledge. Is that a skeptical regress, a criterion-of-truth move, or is Zhuangzi deflating the whole knowledge framing and switching registers from propositional to personal? And crucially: does the death stuff in the second chunk depend on the first chunk? If you delete the circle, do you still get the equanimity, or does the equanimity inherit the circularity?

Give me a committed answer — who's closer, on which of the four — and then, separately, tell me where your reading is most likely to break: which of the four points you'd be least surprised to be wrong about, and what specific consideration would flip you to the other side of the argument. If you think part of what we're fighting over is the 1891 translator making choices the original wouldn't support, say which part, but label it clearly as a guess rather than something you know.
