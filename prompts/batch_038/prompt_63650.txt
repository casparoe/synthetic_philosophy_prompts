You are an expert in formal social epistemology: partition models of knowledge, common knowledge and common belief, Aumann-style agreement results, the dynamics of belief exchange, merging of opinions, and the philosophical literature on disagreement among peers. Work slowly and carefully. Several of the claims below are mathematical rather than merely interpretive, and I want them checked from the ground up rather than assessed for plausibility. Do not split the difference by default: some moves here are flatly wrong, and some are right.

Background. I audit an interdisciplinary reading group at my university (philosophy and economics, mostly graduate students). Last week's session was on the epistemology of disagreement, and two members — Nadia, a philosophy student, and Owen, an economics student — got into a sustained argument about whether Aumann's 1976 agreement theorem has any bearing on real, actual disagreement between informed people. I was asked to referee at the end and could not. I recorded the exchange as six moves; I have no view about who won, and I suspect several turns were subtler in the moment than they look on the page.

Move 1 (Nadia). She stated the theorem: two ideally rational Bayesian agents share a common prior over a finite state space; each privately learns only which cell of their own partition contains the true state; each conditionalizes. If their respective posteriors on some event then become common knowledge between them, those posteriors are equal. Her gloss: fully transparent and stable disagreement therefore cannot be rational. If two colleagues have spent years exchanging views about whether some policy will work, know exactly what the other believes, and know that the other knows, then either their beliefs coincide or at least one of them is failing to update correctly. She added that this disposes of talk of reasonable disagreement among the well informed: such disagreement is not a permanent feature of the human condition, it is a symptom.

Move 2 (Owen). He replied that both hypotheses fail in practice, so the theorem cannot indict anybody. First, no two real people share a prior: a prior is what you would have believed before you got your evidence, and since our formative evidence, upbringing, and exposure differ, there is no single probability function from which both of our actual belief systems could have arisen by conditionalizing. Second, common knowledge is an infinite hierarchy and nobody gets past the first few levels; Aumann's own paper contains a small example in which each agent knows the other's posterior and the two posteriors differ, precisely because that knowledge is not common. A theorem whose antecedent is never satisfied tells us nothing about whether actual disagreement is rational.

Move 3 (Nadia). She said the first objection misreads the formalism. A prior is not a memory or a historical event; it is a representation of a whole disposition to believe — the function which, conditionalized on whatever evidence you end up with, yields your actual beliefs. On that reading, whether two people have a common prior is not a claim about their biographies but a consistency condition on their conditional beliefs, and there is even a behavioral test for it: a common prior exists exactly when there is no package of side-bets — transfers that sum to zero — that each agent's own conditional expectations would make favorable to that agent in every state. If such a package existed, she said, we would have found it. As to the second objection, she conceded nothing: common knowledge need not be a gift, it can be manufactured by talking. There is a dynamic result to the effect that if two agents alternately and publicly announce their posteriors on an event, updating each time, the process reaches agreement after finitely many rounds.

Move 4 (Owen). He said that dynamic result assumes the very common prior Nadia is trying to establish, so it cannot bridge the gap she needs bridged. Remove the common prior and iterated announcement need not converge at all. The most one gets with genuinely different priors is an asymptotic merging result: two agents' predictions about an ongoing stream of observations come to agree in the long run, and even that requires one agent's prior to be absolutely continuous with respect to the other's, so it fails outright when one of them assigns probability zero to a course of data the other considers possible. Merging, he said, is about agreement in prediction over an infinite stream, not about anyone's posterior on the single question under discussion, and it is a limit claim rather than a claim about beliefs at any finite time.

Move 5 (Nadia). Then take the approximate version. Suppose our priors are not identical but are close in total variation distance. The corresponding approximate theorem then yields posteriors that are close whenever those posteriors are commonly known: small divergence in priors can only buy small divergence in belief. That is exactly the situation real people are in — similar, not identical — and it restores the normative force. A large, stable, transparent disagreement still shows that our priors differ by a lot, which is a substantive and in principle checkable claim rather than a shrug.

Move 6 (Owen). He waved this aside and drew his conclusion. On Nadia's own reading, the theorem's real content is that disagreement is possible whenever priors differ, and nobody has any reason to think priors must be identical. So rational agents can know each other's minds, exchange beliefs indefinitely, and still disagree. It follows that when two well-informed people disagree about a policy, neither has any reason to lower confidence in their own view merely because the other disagrees: disagreement is only evidence against you if you already have independent reason to trust the other's starting point as much as your own, and that is precisely what is in dispute. Owen did not engage with the total variation claim; when I raised it afterwards he said he did not believe it but could not say why. Please settle it.
