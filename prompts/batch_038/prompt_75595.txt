I've spent more years than I'd like to admit in LessWrong-adjacent comment threads about measurement, Goodhart's law, and aggregation, and I've come to think the World Happiness Report is the most philosophically loaded artifact in that whole literature, while nearly all the discussion of it stays at the level of "self-reports are noisy" and "adaptation will fool you." I want the deeper excavation, and I want it argued rather than catalogued. Here is the practice as I understand it from the published methodology. A polling firm asks nationally representative samples in something like 150 countries a single evaluative question: picture a ladder whose top rung is the best possible life for you and whose bottom rung is the worst possible life for you; which rung are you standing on today? The headline ranking is nothing but the population-weighted mean of those answers, averaged across three years of fieldwork — no other input goes into a country's position, which is why the same handful of Nordic states has occupied the top of the table for the better part of a decade. Alongside it, the compilers run a pooled regression of national means on six national indicators (log GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceptions of corruption) and report how many points of each country's score each indicator accounts for relative to a hypothetical benchmark country assembled from the world's worst values on all six. Whatever is left is the residual, and it is not a rounding error: for at least one of the top-five countries in the latest edition it runs above two points on a ten-point ladder, which is to say it exceeds the contribution of any single indicator. Separately, and since 2021, the same enterprise has promoted a policy metric, the WELLBY — a wellbeing-adjusted life year, built like the QALY but substituting life satisfaction for health-related quality of life — and has recommended that policymakers aim to increase the WELLBYs of people alive today and of future generations. A friend of mine who works in a national statistics office defends the ladder question on the grounds that it's the most democratic instrument anyone has devised: nobody decides for you what a good life contains.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, with attention to what the apparatus strictly presupposes as against what merely sits comfortably with it:

1. What theory of well-being does a self-anchored life evaluation license? Note that it invites people to grade their lives against a standard that is theirs, not the survey's — which looks like it favours a desire- or judgement-satisfaction account over hedonism and over objective-list theories. Does the ranking actually require that, or would an objective-list theorist accept the instrument as a useful proxy while rejecting the stated rationale? Say where the practice is underdetermined and where it isn't.
2. Is the apparatus internally consistent? The ranking is an average over whoever happens to be sampled, while the promoted policy metric is a sum of satisfaction-years that explicitly counts future generations and therefore grows with population size. Are those the same theory of value wearing two hats, or two rival theories — one averaging, one totalizing — housed in a single institutional brand? And what does the residual's size presuppose about the epistemology of self-report: if the biggest single component of a country's score is whatever six indicators fail to explain, on what view of first-person authority is that leftover signal about wellbeing rather than scale-use culture, translation artefact, or mood on polling day?
3. Does the whole exercise presuppose that a nation is a proper object of welfare — that "Finland is happier than France" states something about a collective and not merely a distribution over individuals? If it doesn't, what is the ranking an index of, and what would the practice have to say about a country that climbed the table mainly by changing who is in the sample?
4. Suppose the presupposed view were replaced. Concretely, which parts of the apparatus survive and which collapse if a government instead adopted an objective-list or capability account, or an experience-sampling hedonic account built on momentary affect rather than global evaluation? I'm interested in what has to be abandoned, not in a general comparison of the theories.

Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey of positions: state which single account of well-being you judge the apparatus to be genuinely committed to, whether you think the averaging-versus-summing tension is a real philosophical inconsistency or a harmless division of labour between a measuring instrument and a policy recommendation, and whether a government should adopt climbing the ranking as an official target at all. Give your reasons, and where you think the practice is compatible with several theories, say which one you'd bet on and why. I'd much rather read a definite verdict I can push back against than a balanced list of options that leaves the choice to me.
