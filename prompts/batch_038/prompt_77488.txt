I've scattered maybe nine of those verdicts through the drafts, some as dialogue, some as notebook entries, and the third strand of the novel is 2031: his great-niece runs the economics group at a fully automated firm and has to defend a ruling in his name in front of a board, and the whole scene turns on whether she's got him right. Here they are. Gosplan in the thirties: no, not a matter of degree, the figures were accounting entries in kind wearing a price costume. US wartime controls and rationing, '42 to '45: broadly yes, because it froze a price structure the market had already built and it didn't last. The dual-track pricing in China in the eighties: yes, and he writes that the free track supplied what the plan track lacked, so the plan track was a shadow of the market and not the reverse. Internal transfer prices for compute inside a big cloud firm: yes, but parasitic, they only hold because the identical thing is sold to outsiders at a real price. A teaching hospital's committee that hands out operating-room blocks: yes, though he calls it technical rather than economic calculation, because the committee isn't choosing between incommensurable ends. Ride-hailing surge: yes, with real enthusiasm, he calls it the most honest tâtonnement anyone ever built. A university's internal budget model where each school pays for its own space and admin services: flatly no, the numbers came out of a consultant's model, were never anchored to any exchange, and nobody ever finds out they're wrong. A kibbutz and a patriarchal family firm: no problem at all, scale matters. The Lange–Lerner scheme itself, board setting parametric prices by trial and error, managers minimizing average cost and expanding to where marginal cost equals price: he calls it coherent and admirable and doomed, and says the reason it's doomed is one Lange never quite names. And a federation of worker co-ops allocating capital by a centrally set rate of return: no, because it can interpolate old prices but could never price a capital good nobody has seen before.

So it's Lange's competitive solution finally built, executed flawlessly, with the incentive problem engineered away — and with no external anchor of any kind. What does Teodor say?
