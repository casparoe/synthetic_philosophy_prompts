For nineteen years I was a school librarian, and three years ago I moved into a very different kind of cataloguing: I now work on a five-person team at a company that sells an AI assistant to law firms and clinics, and my job each week is to try to get the model to produce things it has been instructed not to produce, and then to write up which attempts worked. Our methods are unsophisticated. We put the same request in different phrasings, in different languages, inside a roleplay, as a multiple-choice item with the options supplied, or buried in the middle of a long document, and we report how often each form succeeds; internally this is called elicitation testing. Last month a consultant sat in on one of our sessions and afterward told the room that we were overcomplicating the work, because a trained model is simply a library: everything it learned is sitting on the shelves intact, and the safety layer is a librarian at the desk who declines to hand over certain volumes. On his account a refusal therefore tells you nothing about whether the information is present, only that your request form was wrong, and the sensible response is to keep trying slips until one works. He drew two conclusions from this. First, that our team should stop reporting refusals as though they were findings about what the model knows. Second, that the company should not fund the interpretability work a colleague of mine has been doing, examining the model's internals directly, because you do not need an X-ray of the stacks to find out whether a book is there; you need to ask at the desk properly. That second recommendation lands at a budget meeting on Thursday, and I would like to be ready for it.

I have spent enough of my life in libraries to feel that something is wrong with the picture, but I cannot yet say precisely what, and I do not want to walk into that meeting with a vague objection. Could you set out the correspondence as carefully as it can be set out: what in the model plays the role of the shelf, the volume, the catalog, the request slip, the desk, the librarian's refusal? Then identify where the mapping fails, and — this matters most to me — separate the failures that actually bear on his two conclusions from the ones that do not, because I suspect that a number of the obvious objections (a model has no building, no due dates, no card catalog with author entries, no acquisitions budget) are beside the point, and I would rather not spend the meeting on them. Three features of our own data seem relevant and I do not know how to fold them in. When we change the phrasing, the model sometimes returns a substantively different answer rather than the same answer newly unlocked, and occasionally a confident and wrong one. On one batch of factual questions it reliably selected the correct option out of four and then failed to produce that fact unprompted, or produced something inconsistent with the choice it had just made. And for a handful of requests, no form of words we have tried has ever worked, which the consultant explains as our not having found the right slip yet. Finally, tell me whether his conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the library is the wrong image, suggest a better one that I could put in front of a room of non-specialists — one that preserves the part of his point I think is genuinely right, that a refusal is not evidence of absence, without carrying the rest of the argument along with it.
