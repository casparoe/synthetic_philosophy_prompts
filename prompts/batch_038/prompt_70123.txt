So I spent the summer doing intake at a county records center — unloading pallets, typing folder titles into a spreadsheet, putting things in acid-free boxes — and tonight I got into a two hour argument with my friend Marcus about it on the walk back from a bar, and I'm still worked up, so I want a third party who doesn't know either of us.

Here's what I was actually doing, and I think the details are the whole point. Rule one: we never reorganize. Records come in from an office and they stay in the order that office kept them, so if the welfare department filed by case number, the boxes sit in case-number order forever even though not one researcher has ever wanted case-number order. Rule two: we transcribe folder titles exactly as written, including when they're awful — there's a box from the 1940s with the creator's own handwritten label that I'm not going to type out here, and the guidance was keep it, add your own title in brackets underneath, don't replace it. Rule three: most stuff never arrives at all, because every office has a retention schedule, and the vast majority of routine files — applications, case notes, payroll, inspection reports — get destroyed on a timer, while the office head's correspondence and the policy memos are marked permanent. Rule four: when the files do come and they contain living people's medical or financial information, they're sealed for a fixed number of years, the same number for everybody; the federal census ones are 72 years, ours were usually 50 or 75. And the finding aid, which is the thing researchers actually read, opens with a history of the office. Not of the people in the files. The unit of description is the agency, and the people the agency acted on show up only as contents of folders.

Marcus's take is that this is all just housekeeping plus shelf space plus legal compliance, that I'm projecting philosophy onto a pile of clerical habits, and that archivists are supposed to be neutral toward the record anyway, so there's nothing to dig for. My take is that keeping the creator's order and keeping the creator's words is not a neutral habit, it's a claim about where a document's meaning lives, and that destroying the case files while keeping the director's mail is a claim about whose lives are going to be reconstructable in two hundred years. He said I'm being dramatic and that retention is purely a volume problem. So: what view of evidence, or of what a document even is, does the arrangement practice presuppose? Does keeping original order only make sense if you think meaning comes from the creating context rather than from the content itself, and what rival accounts of a record's significance does that quietly rule out? And seperate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits well with — could a purely pragmatic story (don't destroy context you might want later, don't pay staff to re-sort, don't get sued) license all four rules, or is there a point where pragmatism runs out and something normative has to be doing the work?

Then I want you to actually sit with the tensions, because that's where Marcus got me. We treat the creator's order and titles as authoritative, but we also bracket them, and we rename collections when the old labels are offensive — which seems to presuppose the opposite thing about whether a creator's categories can be trusted. We describe the job as neutral custodianship, but appraisal is the most consequential judgment anyone in that building ever makes. And a fixed closure period treats the harm of disclosure as something that decays on a schedule and expires for everyone at once, which feels like a very specific moral view smuggled in as a number. Finally, what would have to change in practice if we swapped the contextual epistemology for a subject-based one — arrange and describe by the people and places the records are about rather than by the office that made them? Not "it'd be more inclusive," but concretely: what breaks, what do we lose that we can't get back, and which of the four rules survives the swap? I'd rather you tell me the practice is underdetermined in places and that several theories would license it than hand me a tidy single philosophy hiding in the box numbers.
