I write a fortnightly column for a science-and-ideas supplement — the sort of slot that sits uneasily between the research pages and the review section — and the piece I am currently drafting turns on a remark a philosopher friend made over lunch, half in jest: that moral beliefs are almost never defeated by moral evidence. What actually dislodges them, she suggested, is a change of view about the world — about causes, base rates, mechanisms — whereupon the moral belief is revised quietly in the slipstream, without the agent ever registering that she has crossed a normative line. I want to test that claim in the article by means of a case built to order rather than by anecdote, and the framework I have in mind is Quine's confirmation holism: the web of belief, the denial that any class of judgements is unrevisable in principle, and the view that when experience presses against the web we choose where to make the incision on pragmatic grounds — minimal mutilation, simplicity, fruitfulness, conservatism. For the purposes of the article, please treat the following as a stipulated refinement of that picture rather than as a claim about any empirical literature: every belief in a person's web has an *entrenchment profile*, meaning a rough measure of how many other commitments would have to be altered to accommodate its negation, and moral beliefs tend to sit neither at the periphery nor near the centre but in an intermediate ring, tightly bound to a local cluster of causal and psychological beliefs and only loosely bound to the agent's deepest evaluative commitments.

Against that background: construct a thought experiment meeting the specifications below, then demonstrate that it meets them.

- One paragraph, 150–220 words, realistic and contemporary. An institution one can imagine existing next year — a hospital, a local authority, a school, a regulator, a newsroom. No laboratories of the mind, no neurotechnology, no trolley-problem abstraction.
- The agent holds a settled moral judgement that underwrites a practice or policy, together with a settled causal belief that supports it.
- New evidence arrives bearing only on the causal belief: no evaluative content, no moral testimony, no appeal to sympathy, and nothing touching the agent's own interests or circumstances.
- She revises the causal belief and, without any fresh moral argument being articulated to her or by her, the moral judgement goes with it: she ends up endorsing what she formerly held impermissible, or refusing what she formerly held obligatory.
- The direction is the point of the exercise. Her general values and her conception of what matters are held fixed throughout; what is surrendered is the particular judgement, surrendered rather than defended by a reinterpretation of the evidence. That is the harder case to build, since the ordinary expectation is that a person under pressure will save the normative commitment and bend the descriptive one.
- On the holistic account the revision must come out as the rationally better incision and not as a failure of nerve. The judgement's comparative lack of entrenchment has to fall out of the stipulated profile and the structure of her other commitments, not from a stipulation that she is lukewarm about it.
- She must report that nothing moral has changed for her, only her view of the facts — and that self-report must be sincere rather than evasive.
- The judgement that gives way must not be one a present-day reader already considers abhorrent or obsolete. If the reader would have abandoned it anyway, for reasons independent of the new evidence, the case does no work.

After the scenario, please do the checking, in this order. First, the Quinean reading: state what is in conflict with what, enumerate at least two alternative incisions by which consistency might have been restored — including the option of retaining the moral judgement and leaving it without descriptive support — and say which pragmatic maxim does the real work in preferring the cut she actually makes. If the choice of where to cut is underdetermined, as I take Quine to insist, explain what nevertheless makes this one reasonable, and tell me plainly whether he has any principled way of ranking a moral commitment against a causal one, or whether entrenchment is the whole story. Second, the rival reading: take a moral epistemology on which at least some basic moral judgements are warranted non-inferentially — an intuitionist, a non-naturalist holding certain principles to be self-evident, or a reflective-equilibrium theorist treating considered judgements as data in their own right — and show what it must say about the same case. My expectation is that it will have to say either that no genuine defeater for the moral belief was ever present, or that the agent has mistaken a change in her descriptive commitments for a change in her values; tell me which, and why. Third, identify the single feature of the case that generates the divergence, and report what happens when it is removed — in particular, what happens if the agent is permitted to articulate the suppressed premise linking the new fact to the old judgement. Does the case then collapse into unremarkable moral learning, or does something of the original point survive the collapse? Fourth, give me the strongest attack a defender of the second theory would mount: that the moral belief never changed but only its scope, that the evidence was evaluative after all, or that the entrenchment profile is doing the work I asked it to do and is therefore a disguised stipulation of the verdict.

Two house rules. Please do not quote Quine from memory — paraphrase him; anything in quotation marks goes to the texts before print, and a mangled line would embarrass us both. Nor should you cite essays or dates you are not certain of. Write for an intelligent reader with no philosophical training: the finished piece has to survive trimming to about 900 words, so be economical rather than exhaustive.
