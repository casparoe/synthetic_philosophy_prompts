I'm running a 50 minute session for about 18 ward leads and infection-control nurses at our hospital. Smart, practical, one stats course each — they can do sensitivity and specificity and a simple expected value on the back of an envelope, but there's no Jensen's inequality and no measure theory in the room and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Whiteboard only, no slides. What I want them to leave holding is Good's result on the value of information: that evidence which costs you nothing, and which you're then going to act on rationally, can't leave you worse off in expectation — and, more usefully for them, exactly which assumptions are doing that work, and the fact that the sign can flip without the evidence getting any more expensive.

Don't explain the theorem to me. Plan how I explain it. Specifically:

- the order the pieces have to go in and what each one depends on, including what breaks if I do the natural thing and state the result up front. My hunch is that the contrast between deciding now and committing now to a plan you'll execute after you've seen the result has to be fully in place before a single probability gets written on the board — tell me if that's wrong and why.
- one example to carry the whole session. Not an urn, not a screening test, not anything they'll pattern-match to a lecture they've already sat through. It needs a single knob I can turn in front of them so the value of the free information goes positive → zero → negative with literally nothing else changed. Tell me what the example has to contain, what it has to pointedly not contain, and what the knob is.
- the misconception map, split into the ones they walk in with (I'd bet on 'information is only worth having if it changes your decision', and 'the value is what you'd pay for it', and some flavour of 'more data can't hurt') and the ones they'll construct during the session as a direct consequence of how I present it. For each: the exact moment in the sequence where it gets headed off. Some of these I think need defusing before I've said anything at all, not after.
- the whiteboard moment where the inequality stops looking like a sleight of hand. Two branches and no algebra if you can possibly manage it.
- one closing question that separates someone who actually got it from someone who's memorised 'free information is free, therefore good' — plus what the plausible-but-wrong answer sounds like, so I can hear it coming.
- what I should refuse to cover, and refuse out loud. I assume that means ambiguity versus risk, influence diagrams, information cascades, and the iterative value-of-clairvoyance calculation. Tell me what else is a trap in a room this size in this much time.

One complication you have to design around: about half the room has a live stake in whether the new sepsis dashboard's flags get written straight into the patient record or held in a seperate channel, and they know that I know. Whatever sequence you give me has to survive that argument instead of getting hijacked by it — and it mustn't leave them thinking the theorem settles that policy question, because it doesn't, and I need to know why not before they ask.
