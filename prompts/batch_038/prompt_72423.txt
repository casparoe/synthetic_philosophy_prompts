You are to occupy two roles simultaneously: a historian of eighteenth-century Scottish philosophy who has worked closely on Hume's short essay on suicide — the piece he withheld from publication during his lifetime and which appeared only after his death — and a philosopher who currently publishes on the ethics of assisted dying and on the metaphysics of personal identity. You are being asked to make a disciplined historical prediction, not a summary.

Background I am taking for granted. The essay is organized around the three duties suicide was traditionally said to violate: to providence, to one's neighbours, and to oneself. Its central moves are that providence acts through general laws of which human agency is as much a part as any other natural process, so that redirecting the course of nature in one's own case is no more presumptuous than draining a marsh or diverting a river; that withdrawing from society can, in specifiable circumstances, cease to harm it and may even benefit it; and that the horror a spectator feels at the act is not by itself evidence of a violated obligation. I do not want this restated at length. I want to know whether the machinery can be run on cases that did not exist in 1776.

Before drafting anything, work through a short diagnostic pass in five to eight bullets: identify the interpretive choices you have to make (for instance, whether the essay's argument is addressed primarily to the criminal law and public condemnation rather than to the individual deliberator; whether Hume's case rests on the burden of life or on the agent's settled judgment about that burden; how much weight his compatibilism and his account of the passions do in the essay), and flag which of those choices most affects the three predictions.

Now the three cases. In each, the person who authorizes the death and the person whose life is ended are related in ways the essay never contemplates.

Case A. A jurisdiction treats a written advance request as satisfying its voluntariness requirement for a patient who has since entered advanced dementia. The request is executed without any contemporaneous confirmation from the patient; at the time of administration the patient is not suffering visibly, sometimes reports enjoying simple pleasures, and may offer mild resistance or distress that is managed by sedation. Relatives report that the author of the directive would be appalled at what she has now become.

Case B. Eligibility for assisted death rests on unbearable psychiatric suffering alone, in a patient judged capable of the decision, where the suffering has been classified as treatment-refractory after years of failed interventions — a classification that depends on a contested forecast about a condition whose trajectory is poorly predictable.

Case C. Organs are procured immediately following a lawful assisted death, with the timing of the procedure arranged partly around organ viability, so that an act the essay treats as at best neutral with respect to one's neighbours becomes a scheduled source of benefit to them.

Deliverables:

1. For each case, state what Hume would conclude, and on which head of the argument the pressure falls. Where the essay's reasoning underdetermines the answer, say so and explain which of his wider commitments you are leaning on instead.
2. Separate two predictions explicitly: Hume in 1776, informed of the practice in plain terms and asked to judge it; and Hume transplanted into the present, retaining his philosophical commitments but having absorbed what we now know about the progression of dementia, the reliability of psychiatric prognosis, and transplant medicine. Where the two diverge, say which of his commitments forced the revision.
3. Confront the internal tension I am most interested in. The essay seems to require a forward-projecting author whose present judgment can settle the interests of a later stage of a life. Hume's own account of personal identity in the Treatise denies any impression of a continuing self, treats the self as a bundle of perceptions unified only by relations of resemblance and causation, and — in the Appendix he added — confesses himself unable to explain what binds those perceptions into a whole. Does his bundle theory reinforce Case A, undermine it, or simply not engage with it? Would Hume have seen this as a problem for his essay at all, and what would he say to a modern critic who thinks he owes an answer?
4. Attach a confidence level to each prediction and name the single consideration most likely to prove you wrong — ideally something a modern defender of the essay would not expect Hume to care about.
5. Close with one short section on a place where Hume's argument is currently claimed by both sides of the assisted-dying debate and is a bad argument for each.

Constraints. Do not put any sentence in quotation marks and do not attribute to Hume wording you cannot locate; paraphrase only. No citations, page numbers, bibliography, or reading list of any kind. No invented anecdotes about his life or death beyond what is uncontroversial. Where you are extrapolating from his moral psychology rather than from the essay, mark it as extrapolation. Do not modernize his vocabulary into the idiom of autonomy, dignity, or informed consent without noting that you are translating. Avoid anachronism in both directions: he is neither a contemporary liberal autonomist nor a pious conservative. Think carefully and reason through the cases before committing to a verdict. Total length: 1,400 to 1,800 words, with the diagnostic bullets at the top and headed sections for Cases A, B, and C.
