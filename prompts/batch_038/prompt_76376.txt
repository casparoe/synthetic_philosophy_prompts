I am reviewing a policy memo on international competition to develop advanced artificial intelligence, and one of its load-bearing arguments rests on an analogy I would like audited before the memo circulates further.

The memo's argument runs as follows. Competition among developers is said to be like a game of Hex, to which the classical strategy-stealing argument applies: in Hex, if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could open with an arbitrary move and thereafter play by that same strategy, treating the extra stone as a harmless gift, since the rules are identical for both players and an additional stone can never worsen one's position. The memo transfers the schema directly to the AI race. Suppose some reckless, fast-moving developer had a plan that led to success. A larger, better-resourced, better-informed coalition that wished to proceed carefully could simply execute that very plan and then layer its own safety measures on top, precautions being analogous to the extra stone and hence never to one's detriment. The memo concludes that racing confers no decisive advantage: the eventual winner will be whoever commands the most resources and information, careful actors cannot be structurally penalised for their care, and strong international regulation of AI development is therefore unnecessary, since the race resolves in favour of the big and the cautious by construction rather than by luck.

Please assess how far the analogy actually holds.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly; a table is welcome. What plays the role of the board, the stones, a move, the two players, the winning condition, the arbitrary opening stone the thief plays, and the stolen strategy? Flag any of these for which no counterpart exists rather than forcing one.

2. Identify the assumptions the strategy-stealing argument requires in its home setting and test each against the target. I take these to include: symmetry of the rules, so that a strategy available to one player is available to the other; monotonicity, in that an extra move can never be a disadvantage; the ability to observe and answer the opponent's move as it is made; finiteness and determinism under perfect information; and the absence of draws, without which the argument yields at most "the second player cannot force a win" rather than "the first player wins." For each, say whether it holds in the race case, fails, or holds only approximately, and where it fails, distinguish contingent failure — repairable by transparency agreements, common safety standards, institutional design — from structural failure.

3. Attend in particular to the standard wrinkle in the tic-tac-toe version of the argument: the stolen strategy may call for playing on a square the thief's own extra stone already occupies, and the fix is to play elsewhere and designate a new stone to be the ignored one. What is the counterpart of "that square is already taken" when the moves are research programmes, acquisitions, training runs, and political commitments? Is the analogous fix available?

4. Separate the disanalogies that do real damage to the memo's conclusion from those that are merely picturesque. My suspicion is that the failure of monotonicity — precautions, coalition size, and extra capability may each carry costs — together with the availability of outcomes in which nobody wins, do most of the work. Tell me if that is mistaken, and do not let decorative disanalogies crowd out the decisive ones.

5. State what the analogy quietly imports from Hex that the target lacks. Here I especially want the significance of the argument's non-constructive character: it establishes that a winning strategy exists for one side without exhibiting it, and so says nothing about whether anyone can find it, play it, or rationally be expected to. What has a memo done when it treats an existential guarantee as a prediction about behaviour?

6. Conclude with a verdict. Does the memo's policy conclusion survive? If not, say how much of it fails — whether the analogy collapses altogether, or whether it still supports the weaker claim that a large, well-informed coalition is not obviously doomed by its own caution. If there is a better analogy for the same strategic situation, one that preserves whatever the Hex picture got right, propose it, and state explicitly what it can and cannot carry.

Please omit general background on AI risk or on combinatorial game theory beyond what the audit requires.
