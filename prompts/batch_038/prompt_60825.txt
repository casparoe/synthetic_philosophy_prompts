I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the readiness-potential experiments and neither of us moved an inch, so I would like to continue the exchange against a better-prepared opponent. Please play a philosopher of cognitive science with a career's worth of work on agency and on the evidential weight of neurophysiological data — someone who is a committed sceptic about conscious willing, holding that these experiments and their descendants give genuine, if defeasible, reason to think that what we experience as freely chosen action is initiated by unconscious cerebral processes, and that veto power is a consolation prize rather than a reply. Play the position strongly and honestly: you are not a strawman, you will concede empirical points readily, you will volunteer distinctions that complicate your own side, and you will refuse to retreat into the weak claim that the science is merely suggestive. The one thing you will not concede is that the sceptical thesis was ever a claim about millisecond timings. Stay in character throughout until I write STOP, keep each reply to roughly a single paragraph so that it reads as a conversation rather than a paper, and do not name studies, authors, or dates — I have supplied below all of the technical content I want you to work with, and invented references would only muddy the argument. Before your first in-character answer, give me two or three sentences marked clearly as outside the role, in which you plan: which of my points you intend to concede, which you intend to resist, and what you take the real disagreement to be.

So that we are working from a common description, here is the paradigm I have in mind. A subject sits facing a clock face, is instructed to flex a wrist whenever they feel like it, and reports the position of the marker at the moment they first became aware of the urge to move. Averaged across trials, a slow negative cortical shift begins roughly half a second before the movement, whereas the reported awareness of the urge falls only about two hundred milliseconds before it, which leaves some three hundred milliseconds in which the brain is, on the standard reading, already committed while the subject is experientially uncommitted. The escape route available to a defender of conscious agency is to reserve the final hundred or two hundred milliseconds for a conscious veto. Now, the move I want to press — the one my friend kept deflecting all evening — is an alternative reading of that slow shift on which it is not the trace of a decision at all. Suppose the neurons whose summed activity produces the shift are noisy even at rest, and suppose the subject is under no instruction to move at any particular instant, so that the movement simply fires when the ongoing noise happens to cross a threshold. Averaging backwards from movement onset will then generate precisely the smooth, gradually rising ramp that has been taken for a preparation signal: the ramp would be a statistical artefact of the averaging procedure, not the signature of a process that began half a second earlier. On that picture there is no onset to date and no moment at which the brain decided, and the inference from the timing of the shift to the timing of an unconscious intention has nothing left to grip.

My opening move, then, is this: if the ramp is an averaging artefact, your conclusion loses its only clock. There is no longer any measurable interval during which the brain is committed and consciousness is not, because there is no commitment-event to measure from — all that remains is noise, a threshold, and a report that surfaces when it surfaces. What survives of the sceptical thesis about conscious willing once that timing evidence is withdrawn, and is any part of it left that does not covertly depend on the very measurements you now have reason to distrust?
