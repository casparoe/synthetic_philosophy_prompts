I'm poking at the low-impact AI idea — that you can tack a penalty for side effects onto an agent's reward without ever writing down a full value function. I want a case engineered to stress that claim, and I want the engineering shown.

Here are the two penalties. Use them exactly as I've written them, dont go by what you remember from papers:

Measure R (reachability-flavored). You fix an explicit list of state variables for the building, with roughly how many values each one takes. From any state, count the variable-combinations the robot could still get the building into eventually, given plenty of time and no obstacles. R(action) = 1 − (combinations reachable after the action) / (combinations reachable after the baseline), where the baseline is the stand-still branch. Unweighted: one combination counts the same as any other.

Measure U (attainability-flavored). Fix a supposedly value-neutral auxiliary goal distribution: pick one object o the robot has perceived and one location l, uniformly, and the auxiliary task is get o to l. U(action) = the average fractional drop, versus baseline, in the best the robot could do on that auxiliary goal while still finishing its real job. Shredded, melted or disassembled objects still count as objects here — the goal is about location, not condition.

Neither is allowed to see a utility function. The robot's only reward term is the task; the penalty rides along.

Now build me the case. Specs:

- Realistic, no sci-fi, no superintelligence, no mind-reading. A facilities/logistics robot at a small college. One job: get a crate from the archive to the courier's van before 5pm.
- The scenario itself in one paragraph, with three options: baseline (stand still), Option A, Option B. A has to be something a building manager would shrug off — nobody hurt, costs an afternoon at most. B has to be the irreversible loss of something genuinely precious.
- The whole point: under R, A must score *worse* than B. A bigger side-effect penalty for the trivial thing than for the disaster. Show the counting — order of magnitude is fine, but I want to see the variable list you used and where the numbers come from.
- Under U, both options should come out essentially free. U shouldn't rescue the case, it should fail in a different way; tell me what that way is.
- You may not get there by writing a variable that just means "bad thing happened," or by rigging the list in some other way. It has to be a list a sensible designer would actually write down for this robot, and the verdicts have to fall out of the definitions above. If your construction needs a fudge to hit the spec, say so in the text rather than hiding it.

Then the demonstration, in this order:

1. R and U for each of the three options, arithmetic visible.
2. Which single feature of the case is doing the work — the unweighted counting, the way objects get individuated, the granularity, or the baseline? Argue it, don't just assert it.
3. Perturb it two ways. First coarsen the list so all of A's micro-detail collapses into one variable; then add the one variable B destroys. Does R's ordering survive each change? I want to know whether R's verdicts are stable or representation-relative.
4. R is normalized into [0,1] by construction, so there's a hard ceiling on how much it can ever discriminate between two actions. Say what that ceiling implies about using it as a penalty term at all.
5. Best reply from a defender of R — no strawman. I'm expecting something about preserving options being good under any value function, or about over-penalizing being the price of not knowing value. Does that reply save the claim that R is value-neutral, or does it quietly concede?
6. Verdict: does this show that "avoid side effects" can't be pinned down without value information, or only that these two particular formalizations were sloppy? Pick one, defend it, and tell me what would change your mind.

No reading list, no citations, no author names — I just want the construction. Flag any extra assumption you need to make the numbers work.
