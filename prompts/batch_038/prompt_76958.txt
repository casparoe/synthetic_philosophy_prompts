I am working on the problem of responsibility gaps for autonomous systems — cases in which a system causes harm and it seems that no one is properly blameworthy for it — and I have run into a passage in Samuel Butler's Erewhon (1872), in the chapters headed The Book of the Machines, that I cannot make out. I want help reading it. I have no way to check the wording at the moment, so what follows is my own compression of the argument rather than the text's language: treat the steps as Butler's and the phrasing as mine.

Butler's first move is that a man's limbs are tools he has grown and his tools are limbs he has not grown; the difference is one of attachment, not of kind. His second is that what makes an organized being is not consciousness but the arrangement of parts serving one another, so asking whether a machine feels or thinks is not the right question to ask. His third is that machines already do what defines the living kingdoms: they reproduce, though only through us, and each generation is more complex and more independent than the last, though the selecting is ours. Hence they are already a further kingdom, continuous with the vegetable and animal ones, and our supremacy over them is a temporary fact about the present distribution of dependence, not a permanent fact about their nature.

He then turns to blame. When a machine does mischief we do not try it or reason with it; we destroy it — not because we take it to be wicked, but because we cannot have it among us. The man who made or directed it, however, we do punish, and the ground is that he could have made it otherwise. The difference between the cases, on my reading, is only where the chain of causes happens to pass through a human being — a fact about us, not about the machine — and if the difference is to be kept it needs a ground that does not appeal to freedom of the will, a question he treats as unanswerable on both sides.

Four things I would like from you. First, the structure: is the move from limbs-as-tools to a further kingdom an argument by analogy, or a redefinition of the criterion, with consciousness dropped out and functional organization substituted? If the latter, what does the substitution smuggle in? Second, responsibility: does the destroy-versus-punish passage make responsibility a matter of where causal chains intersect human beings, or a matter of which agents our practices of blame can actually get hold of? Does that difference bear on the contemporary distinction between a responsibility gap and a merely administrative or compensatory gap — that is, does Butler leave room for accountability without blameworthiness, or does he collapse the two? Third, seriousness: how much of this is intended argument and how much is reductio aimed at Darwinian extrapolation, what textual markers should I be looking for, and does your answer to the second question change under the ironic reading? Fourth, if a contemporary theorist of autonomous weapons wanted to break the argument at a single joint, which premise should they attack, and what becomes of the gap if they succeed?

Finally, point out any place where my compression, rather than the argument, is doing the work. You do not need to cite anything or name secondary literature; I want your reading and your reasons.
