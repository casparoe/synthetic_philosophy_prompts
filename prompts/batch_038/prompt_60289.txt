I have run the reading schedule for a small AI-safety meetup in my city for about four years — I'm a data analyst by trade, not an ML person, and I've been reading LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since long before that was a sentence anyone said out loud at parties. Last month we spent a session on low-impact AI: the family of proposals that try to penalize an agent for the side effects of its actions rather than for failing to achieve some value-laden target, including the relative-reach idea (measure impact as the loss of states the agent could still have reached) and the attainable-utility idea (measure impact as the loss of the agent's ability to achieve a grab-bag of auxiliary goals). The framing question I put to the group was whether "avoid side effects" can be made precise without a full specification of value. Two regulars — Dev, a controls engineer who works on grid automation, and Mara, a philosophy PhD student working on normative ethics — went at it for forty minutes afterward, and I have been asked to referee the thing at next week's session. I'm reporting it as neutrally as I can; please don't try to infer which of them I liked, because I genuinely don't know who was right.

Here is the exchange as I transcribed it. (1) Dev: no, and the reason is structural. Any such measure needs a weighting. Counting lost reachable states treats "the agent can no longer restore this particular arrangement of gravel in a parking lot" as a loss of the same kind as "the agent can no longer prevent an outbreak," because both are just reductions in reachability. To avoid that you have to weight states by importance, and importance is a value notion. The attainable-utility version is worse, since the list of auxiliary goals you protect is a list of values in all but name. (2) Mara: that demands something nobody proposed. The question was never whether the measure assumes nothing normative — obviously it does — but whether it needs a complete specification of what is good, and it doesn't. You can hold a confident partial ordering over a narrow band of changes without a theory of the good: destroying the only surviving instrument of a given kind, driving a species extinct, and contaminating an aquifer sit near the top; moving a mug six inches left sits at the bottom. The proposals need coarse comparisons, not a utility function over world-histories. (3) Dev: the coarse comparisons are precisely the contested part, and the gravel case isn't a strawman, it's what the formalism computes. If the permutation of gravel carries the same penalty as the outbreak, then either the penalty scale is doing normative work you refuse to own, or the measure is swamped by the astronomically many trivial losses and never registers the ones you care about. A formalism that is value-free on the page and value-laden in its interpretation just relocates the values to where nobody audits them. (4) Mara: that's a bug report, not a refutation. Baselines, state abstractions, and discounting are choices about representation, and the fact that a first draft mis-weights things doesn't show no representation could be right — a bad operational definition of toxicity doesn't make poison a value judgment. And you skipped the convergence point: the assumptions these measures need, that people's plans depend on the physical state of their surroundings, that options are worth keeping, that irreversible losses are worse than reversible ones of comparable size, are assumptions nearly everyone already grants. That's a shallow overlapping consensus, not a value theory. (5) Dev: the consensus is thicker than you're allowing, and the two standard failures prove it. First, clinginess — an agent penalized for changes in the world will treat other people's ordinary activity as damage to be prevented, so you must dial the penalty down, and the dial setting is exactly the trade between finishing the task and leaving the world alone, which is a normative question. Second, the baseline — relative to a do-nothing baseline, pulling a child out of a pond is itself a side effect, because the counterfactual default has her drowning. Neither is repaired by better representation; both are repaired only by deciding what we want. (6) Mara: every safety constraint has that knob, including a shutdown button or a speed limit; a contested setting isn't a value function, and choosing it conservatively is the entire point of using a constraint rather than a goal. The baseline point cuts the other way, too: that you must choose a baseline shows the concept is underdetermined by the formalism, not that it's value-laden. If a measure says the rescue counts as a side effect, that argues for a different baseline, not for abandoning the project. (7) Dev: then we agree on the substance and you've conceded the question as asked — the answer is no, and the reason to keep building these is pragmatic rather than conceptual. (8) Mara: no, the question was "without a full specification of value," and "without a full specification" is all I ever claimed, so this has gone verbal.
