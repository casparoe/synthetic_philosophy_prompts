I sit on a constitutional review commission in a country that has just emerged from a period of one-party rule and is drafting its religion clauses from scratch. Our subcommittee has been told that every proposed provision must be justified, not merely borrowed, so I have been collecting my own verdicts on the arrangements that exist elsewhere and trying to see what they add up to. They are as follows.

1. An established church: the head of state is its nominal governor, its senior bishops hold seats in the upper house by virtue of office, its ministers are civil servants, and it is funded partly by a tax on its own members and partly by a general grant from all taxpayers. My verdicts split — nominal governorship: tolerable; the bishops' seats: not permissible; ministers' civil-service status: tolerable; the members' tax: permissible; the general grant: not permissible. I cannot articulate what separates the two things I called tolerable from the two I called impermissible.

2. A state that collects the members' tax itself: the tax authority keeps the register of who belongs, employers withhold accordingly, and an individual can escape liability only by making a formal declaration of exit to a state office, a declaration the communities then treat as defection from the faith itself, refusing the defector religious marriage and burial. The collection is permissible. The apparatus is not: the state has made itself the registrar of apostasy.

3. One region of a country in which the state pays the salaries of the clergy of four recognized communities, provides confessional instruction in its public schools subject to a parental opt-out, and observes two extra religious public holidays; while in the rest of the same country the state funds no religion and prohibits conspicuous religious symbols for pupils in state schools. I cannot decide. The internal inconsistency troubles me more than either regime does on its own.

4. A prayer at the opening of a legislative sitting, worded by the body's own chaplain, which members may decline to attend: permissible, though I arrived at that verdict reluctantly.

5. A prayer composed or selected by the state and recited in public classrooms, from which pupils may be excused or may simply remain silent: not permissible. Cases 4 and 5 look to me like the same case — adults in a room, a state-arranged invocation, an available exit — and I cannot say why my verdicts differ.

6. Exemption of religious bodies from property tax, and of ministers from tax on their stipends: permissible. Direct state grants to build new places of worship: not permissible. State grants to restore an old church building listed as national heritage: permissible. I do not believe the age of the building is what is doing the work here, but I cannot say what is.

7. Public funding of schools that follow the national curriculum, hold daily worship, and select their staff by religious adherence: permissible. The same schools refusing to admit children of another faith: not permissible.

8. A crucifix on the wall of a state classroom, over a parent's objection: I said not permissible at first hearing and permissible on reflection.

9. A prohibition on visibly religious dress for primary-school teachers: I keep going back and forth. The same prohibition for university professors, and for parents attending a school assembly: not permissible.

10. A public holiday calendar keyed entirely to one religion's festivals, with no leave granted for anyone else's: not permissible. A common day of rest that happens to coincide with that religion's sabbath: permissible.

11. An individual doctor's refusal to participate in a procedure they object to, where a colleague is available: permissible. The same refusal by a religiously affiliated hospital that receives the bulk of its funding from the state and is the only provider within a day's travel: not permissible.

My working principle, which I have been defending in committee, has three clauses: the state may support religion generically but never confessionally; it may never coerce belief, practice, or profession, directly or indirectly; and it may never allow religious affiliation to determine access to public services or public office. I now suspect the first clause is doing no work at all — that it condemns 2, 3, and 9 and blesses 6 and 7 purely by stipulation.

What I want from you: (a) the principle, or the smallest set of them, that best accounts for these verdicts, stated in language I could put before the drafting committee as the skeleton of an article; (b) for each clause, which of the numbered verdicts it exists to capture and which it fails to capture; (c) a plain statement of which of my verdicts the principle obliges me to abandon, and in particular a resolution or dissolution of the 4/5 pair, the 8/9 pair, and the split inside case 1; and (d) your verdicts on four cases I have not listed — two of your own choosing, selected because rival principles would disagree about them, and two of mine: whether the state may deny legal personality to a small movement whose doctrines the majority regards as false but which has harmed no one, and whether a religiously affiliated university that takes state research money may require its faculty to subscribe to a creed.

Please keep the whole answer under 900 words. I would much rather have a principle I can argue with than a survey of the options.
