You are a professional philosopher who works on social choice theory and collective agency, and who has extensive experience explaining formal results to audiences whose mathematics stops at arithmetic. Please think carefully and treat this as a design problem rather than an essay.

I am a junior, and I run my school's Politics and Philosophy Society. In two weeks I have thirty-five minutes, one whiteboard, and a single printed handout in which to introduce the discursive dilemma — what legal scholars call the doctrinal paradox — to roughly sixteen students aged fourteen to eighteen. Several are on the debate team and will treat the session as something to win rather than something to follow. Three members of our seven-person Student Senate will also be in the room, because the case I intend to build on is not a thought experiment: it is our own vote from last month.

Here is what happened. Our bylaws state that the Senate endorses a proposal if and only if it affirms all three of the following:

- P1: moving to a later start to the school day would meaningfully improve student sleep and academic performance;
- P2: the change can be made without reducing instructional minutes or cutting any activity or bus route;
- P3: a majority of parents and faculty would accept the change.

Every senator accepts that endorsement requires all three, and every senator voted sincerely and consistently — one senator affirmed all three and endorsed; two affirmed P1 and P2 but rejected P3 and voted against; two affirmed P1 and P3 but rejected P2 and voted against; two affirmed P2 and P3 but rejected P1 and voted against. Counting premise by premise, the Senate affirms P1 by 5–2, P2 by 5–2, and P3 by 5–2, which under our bylaws requires endorsement. Counting the endorsement question directly, the Senate rejects it 6–1. Both tallies were published in the minutes, and the school paper printed both on the same page.

There is a second wrinkle I want the session to arrive at, and I would like you to check my arithmetic and my logic before you build anything on it. Our rules officer observed that the bylaws could just as well have bundled the reasoning into two questions rather than three: Q1 being the conjunction of P1 and P2, and Q2 being P3. The same seven senators, holding the same sincere views, then produce a majority against Q1 (4–3) and a majority for Q2 (5–2), so a premise-by-premise count on the bundled ballot rejects endorsement. Two logically equivalent ways of writing the same rule would thus deliver opposite group verdicts under the very same voting procedure, with nobody changing their mind about anything. If that is correct, it is the destination I want; if I have made an error, say so plainly and tell me what the corrected version shows instead.

Two senators have already circulated fixes, and both will be sitting in the audience. One proposes that the Senate adopt a standing rule that the conclusion vote always prevails and that premise votes be merely advisory. The other proposes that the Senate stop publishing premise tallies altogether.

Your deliverable is a teaching plan, not the explanation. I can already state the paradox; what I cannot yet do is sequence it for this particular room. Specifically, I need:

1. A short diagnosis before anything else: in one paragraph, what is the real conceptual difficulty here — a problem about logic, about procedure, or about what it even means to say that a group believes something? Everything that follows should be organized around your answer.
2. The dependency structure: the components in the order they must be introduced, with a line for each saying what has to be in place before it and why. My own guess is that nothing works until the room sees that the conditional linking premises to conclusion must itself be common ground rather than one more thing being voted on, but I may have that backwards.
3. A decision about the carrying example. Brainstorm three candidates — my Senate case and two you invent — then reject two and justify the rejections. Say what a case of this kind must have and must lack to work on fourteen-to-eighteen-year-olds who have never met the idea.
4. A misconception map: at least five errors the room will predictably arrive with or form along the way, each tagged to the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, together with the question or move that heads it off. Consider, if you think them relevant, "somebody must have voted dishonestly," "this is just Condorcet's paradox with extra steps," "so majority rule has been refuted," "just appoint one person to decide," and "the group simply has no opinion, so all talk about what the Senate believes is nonsense."
5. A way to handle the two senators' proposals in the room without letting the session dissolve into an argument about our bylaws.
6. A closing check that fits in five minutes and would show the idea landed rather than merely entertained: a small transfer item on material they have not seen, plus what a genuine answer sounds like and what a fluent-but-empty one sounds like.
7. What I should deliberately leave out, and say out loud that I am leaving out, with an honest one-line remark for each omission.
8. A minute-by-minute allocation of the thirty-five minutes.

Please do not send me away with readings, a bibliography, citations, or suggested further viewing; everything the room needs has to be inside the session. Work the vote counts through explicitly before you commit to any claim about them.
