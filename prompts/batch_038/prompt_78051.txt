I co-teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and for our week on imprecise credences I want to hand out a written dialogue that presents the disagreement as a live argument rather than as a taxonomy of positions. Could you write one, in American English, of roughly 1,200–1,500 words, between two members of a public-health advisory committee who disagree about how to represent their uncertainty in the case described below? One is a committed user of sets of probability measures; the other holds that a rational agent's degrees of belief are in the end sharp probabilities, and that interval-valued representations either collapse into that or smuggle in something incoherent. Give them names and a light touch of temperament, but keep all the drama in the argument itself.

The case — please have the characters work through it with the numbers on the page rather than waved at:

- A disease D has an unknown prevalence in the population under review. Because the surveillance data are fragmentary and come from two clinics with very different catchment populations, the imprecise-Bayes committee member represents her prior as the set of all measures assigning P(D) a value anywhere in [0.01, 0.05]. Her colleague insists on a single number and defends 0.03.
- A screening test with P(positive | D) = 1 and P(positive | not-D) = 0.5.
- Utilities, on a common scale, for the policy at issue (treat a patient who tests positive, or withhold): treat and D: +100; treat and not-D: −40; withhold and D: −500; withhold and not-D: 0.

Two features of the case should drive the exchange. First, a positive test result widens the imprecise member's interval for P(D) instead of narrowing it — to roughly [0.02, 0.095] — while a negative result drives P(D) to exactly zero for every measure in her set. Let the two of them dispute whether this genuinely counts as the phenomenon usually called dilation, given that the second outcome collapses the interval to a point, and what the correct diagnosis of the widening is. Second, the prior interval lies entirely below the prevalence at which treating and withholding break even, so before any testing all the standard rules point the same way; after a positive result the widened interval straddles that threshold, and maximin, Γ-maximin, E-admissibility, and interval dominance no longer agree about the recommendation. Have them compute the break-even prevalence explicitly and show what it does to the ranking before and after the test, then argue about which rule is the right one, about whether a rule adopted in order to make imprecise credences action-guiding is doing real normative work or merely relabeling a sharp judgment, and about the fact that the Γ-maximin verdict depends on how the set of averaging priors is chosen.

Requirements on the dialogue: neither character may be a strawman, and neither should win. Each must land at least one blow the other cannot fully parry — for instance, the imprecise member should press the charge that a sharp 0.03 is a bet the evidence does not license and that it exposes the committee to a sure loss against anyone who knows where the true prevalence sits within the interval, while her colleague should press the charge that the interval is inert, that it cannot by itself generate a recommendation, so every actual decision the committee makes reveals a hidden sharp preference, and that the width of an interval is itself a substantive empirical claim standing in need of exactly the justification the imprecise side says is unavailable. Both should be forced at least once to revise or narrow something they asserted earlier, and the dialogue should say something about whether their disagreement is decision-relevant in this case or merely representational, about whether imprecise credences can learn from experience at all, and about what would settle the matter — ideally ending on something concrete, such as a bet or an elicitation procedure that one of them declines. Please do not include citations, references to the literature, or appeals to authority: the exchange should run entirely on the case, the arithmetic, and the arguments.
