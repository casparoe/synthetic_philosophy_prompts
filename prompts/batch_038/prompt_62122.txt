I teach an introductory philosophy course on practical rationality. We are in week two; my 26 students have talked informally about expected value but have never been given the axioms of expected utility theory, the independence axiom, or the Allais paradox, and I intend to keep it that way until the end of the session. I have a single 75-minute meeting and I want a staged decision-making activity in which the students walk into the common consequence effect themselves and then feel what it costs, before anything gets a name.

Please design the activity in full, with the following components:

1. **A choice architecture.** Give me two decision problems, each a pair of lotteries written out as explicit probability–outcome tables, such that the second pair is derived from the first by mixing both of its options with a common third lottery, and such that the combination of choices most people will make is inconsistent with mixture independence. Do not use money as the outcome: use a token that converts into a capped amount of course credit, so the stakes are real, small, and require no cash. Explain how I can resolve the lotteries fairly using equipment an ordinary classroom already has and in a way students will trust, and how I can arrange things so that every student's own choice actually pays off rather than only a randomly drawn one.

2. **A reason-elicitation step.** Before each choice, students write one sentence explaining it; these are collected anonymously and a few are read aloud later. Tell me what wording on the ballot would contaminate the result — by hinting at the relationship between the two problems, or by inviting students to reason about consistency — and what wording would keep it clean.

3. **A control condition.** Include a third pair in which the certain option is replaced by a very high-probability but still uncertain one, so that I can tell whether the class's pattern tracks certainty as such or something else. Specify which distribution of answers across the three pairs would indicate that the activity failed to isolate what I was after, and what I should do with the rest of the hour in that case.

4. **A pump.** After the choices, each student holds the position they selected. Design a short menu of pairwise swaps, each priced in fractions of a token, such that a student who made the inconsistent pair of choices and who accepts each swap on its own merits ends up strictly worse off than a student who simply kept the original position. Show the arithmetic step by step, identify precisely which assumption about how the swaps are presented the pump depends on, and explain how to run the sequence so that the students who end up losing tokens do not feel ambushed.

5. **Logistics.** A minute-by-minute timeline, a list of materials, what is written on the board at each stage, and the skeleton of a facilitator script for the transitions that keeps the phenomenon unnamed. Include a one-page handout-ready summary of the lotteries and the swap menu.

6. **A debrief** that maps identifiable moments of the activity onto the formal content: the axiom stated in mixture form, what the common consequence transformation did to the two problems, and why the pump required sequential choice with no opportunity to precommit. Present both the charge of inconsistency and the strongest reply available to a student who wants to keep their original choices, without letting that reply dissolve the cost.

7. **Honest limits.** Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including the students who have encountered the paradox elsewhere and will choose so as to be interesting rather than truthful, and the risk that the swap menu gets solved before I reach it — and separately name the feature of the phenomenon this activity cannot reproduce, which I will have to supply by talking about it.
