I maintain the strategy-pruning pass on a solver we run at work — a few hundred small normal-form games a day, and the pruning is the only reason it finishes inside a minute. I wrote up the justification for the team, a colleague read it, and came back with a 2x2 I still can't reconcile with it. The write-up went:

1. In any Nash equilibrium, pure or mixed, every pure strategy in a player's support is a best response to what the others are actually playing.
2. If s is strictly dominated for me — by another pure strategy, or only by some mixture of my own strategies — then s is never a best response to any belief I could hold about the others. So s is in no equilibrium's support, and deleting it costs nothing.
3. That deletion stays justified at every later round: removing strategies of the opponents only shrinks the set of beliefs s could be a best response to, so a strict domination established at any round is still true afterwards. Order can't matter and the surviving set is unique.
4. Weak dominance is the same argument with "strictly better" swapped for "at least as good." If t weakly dominates s, then against any belief whatsoever t does at least as well as s in expectation, so any equilibrium leaning on s could lean on t instead and nobody's worse off. Deleting weakly dominated strategies doesn't destroy equilibria either, it just relabels them.
5. And step 3 carries over verbatim, so weak deletion is order-independent too.
6. Therefore: delete everything dominated, strictly or weakly, by pure or mixed strategies, iterate to a fixed point, and the survivors contain every equilibrium of the original game.

His game, payoffs are (row, column):

```
        L        R
U    (2,1)    (0,0)
D    (1,1)    (0,2)
```

U weakly dominates D for row — strictly better at L, a tie at R — so 4 says delete D; then against U alone, L strictly beats R for column, so 3 says delete R; we're left with (U,L). Except brute force says (D,R) is a Nash equilibrium as well, since row is indifferent between U and D against R and column strictly prefers R against D, and there's a whole stretch of mixed ones with column on R that get wiped out alongside it.

So one of my steps is bad and I can't see which. Honestly I'd rather give up 5 than 4, since 4 is the one doing the work in the runtime numbers. Tell me which step actually fails, why it looked airtight when I wrote it, and what I'm on the hook for once I drop it — including whether this is supposed to make me nervous about the usual dominance argument for bidding your value in a second-price auction, which is weak dominance and not strict.
