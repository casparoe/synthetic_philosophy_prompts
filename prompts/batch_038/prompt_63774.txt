I teach a senior seminar next term that's half historiography of the July crisis and half philosophy of collective responsibility, and I need one final-exam essay question for it. It came out of an arguement I had with a friend last night that ran about three hours and ended with neither of us convinced, which I figure makes it good exam material.

The fight was over how you blame a war. His line: the Fischer thesis only bites if blameworthiness runs through authorship — Germany matters because German decision-makers actually wanted and planned for the general war, and the war aims program is the evidence for that. Strip out intention and all you have left is causation, and causation in 1914 was spread all over Europe. My line: that's too narrow. There's a second way to be blameworthy, call it exposure — you raise the objective odds that everybody else ends up in a general war, whether or not you want one. An unconditional guarantee to an ally you can't actually control is culpable on that view even if you sincerely wanted the crisis to stay local. His comeback, which I couldn't answer at the time: if exposure is what matters then every great power in July 1914 is blameworthy, the view collapses into "everyone shares responsibility," and it can no longer do the discriminating work that either the Versailles clause or Fischer were trying to do. It just licenses a shrug. I think he's wrong — exposure comes in degrees, and it interacts with how many alternatives you actually had — but I couldn't say why cleanly, and that's exactly the spot I want the exam question parked on.

So: write one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes, that makes students commit to a position on whether responsibility for a war has to run through intentions. Build a stylised scenario into the question itself — fictional states, a client provocation, an unconditional guarantee, a mobilisation plan nobody can halt once it's started, and no government that actually wants the general war — so nobody has to reproduce the July crisis from memory. The scenario should be constructed so the exposure view has to answer the collapse objection rather than just get described.

Then give me these sections, in this order:

1. "What the question is testing" — three sentences, so I can check it does the job before I read on.
2. "The question" — about 200 words including the scenario, worth 25 marks, with no primary sources quoted in it (I hand out documents separately).
3. "Model answer" — 800 to 900 words. It should pick a side and defend it. Hedging loses marks.
4. "Marking scheme" — six must-hit moves, marks adding to 25, plus one line each on how students typically botch that move.
5. "The two failures I expect" — short. Name the two most likely bad answers (my guess: reciting the Fischer controversy as a story about who said what, and the everyone's-a-bit-responsible shrug) and say specifically what's missing from each.

No reading list, no bibliography, and don't put any invented quotations from historians into it — I've got the sourcing covered. One thing I do want weighted in the marking scheme: students should notice that the Versailles clause was drafted to establish liability for damages rather than to make a moral finding, and that a lot of the heat came from reading it as though it did the latter. If they miss that distinction entirely, cap them at a B.

Small last thing — my friend reckons "responsibility" and "blame" get run together constantly in this debate and I think he's right about that, so have the model answer keep them apart and say why the difference changes the verdict.
