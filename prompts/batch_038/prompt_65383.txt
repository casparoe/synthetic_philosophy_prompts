I do the annual in-service training for a volunteer rescue squad, and the legal block is where I keep getting stuck — not on what the rules are, I know those, but on why they're shaped like that and I can't explain it to the rookies in a way that doesn't sound arbitrary.

Here's the pile of stuff I have to teach. In our state, like most of the US, there's no general duty to help a stranger: someone can stand on the bank and watch a swimmer go under and walk away with zero liability. But the exceptions get drilled in hard — parent, on-duty lifeguard, contractual undertaking, or you caused the peril, then you do have to act. And then the one that always freaks them out: if you start helping, you can be liable for doing it carelessly or for quitting halfway, even though ninety seconds earlier, hands in pockets, you owed this person nothing. So beginning a rescue can put you in a worse legal position than never beginning. Meanwhile the same broad tradition flips completely at the shoreline — out on the water there's an affirmative duty on the master of a vessel to go to the aid of anyone in distress, no relationship, no contract, no fault required. And layered on top: the state hands out civilian bravery medals to precisely the people the doctrine was so careful never to obligate, and most states have good samaritan statutes shielding rescuers from the liability the doctrine just created.

So what theory of obligation is all this standing on? Does the no-duty rule actually presuppose a real moral category of the good-but-not-required, or is it just liability plumbing — who can sue whom, proof problems, floodgates — with no moral content at all, and how would I tell the difference from inside the practice? Say which parts the practice strictly needs and which it merely happens to sit nicely with; I suspect more than one theory licenses the whole thing and I'd like to know which bits are genuinely underdetermined. Then the start-and-stop asymmetry: is that consistent with whatever view you land on, or does it only make sense if you already accept some doing/allowing distinction? And what would have to be true of the sea that isn't true of the sidewalk, on that view — or is the admiralty rule the tell that the land rule was never moral in the first place? Last: if you swapped the entire structure for a straightforward expected-harm calculus, what concretely changes in doctrine and in the medal-giving, and does the bravery award survive the swap or turn into something incoherent?

Don't bother with case names or citations, I couldn't look them up anyway — I want the underlying view and the shape of the tensions.
