Last night I spent close to three hours arguing with a friend — a computational neuroscientist who works on neural coding, not a philosopher — about whether the dispute over Integrated Information Theory is the kind of thing that could ever be settled, and I came away convinced we were not actually disagreeing about the same question. His case, roughly: Φ cannot be computed for any neural system of realistic size, so the theory has never been and cannot be tested in the way its proponents imply; the five axioms and their matching postulates (intrinsic existence, information, integration, exclusion, composition) are stipulations dressed up as discoveries, because the move from "experience is unitary and definite" to "the substrate must be a maximally irreducible set of units" smuggles in exactly what needs arguing; the exclusion postulate looks like an ad hoc patch whose only job is to stop the theory from collapsing into the panpsychism its own measure seems to entail; and one can write down dull engineered architectures — grids of logic gates, structures built out of error-correcting codes — whose Φ dwarfs that of any cortical patch, which suggests the quantity tracks nothing interesting. He also leaned on the open letter a couple of years back in which a group of consciousness researchers described IIT as pseudoscience. My case, roughly: those objections attack a straw man, since IIT is offered as an identity claim about what experience is rather than a mechanism for producing it, so the intractability complaint confuses a measure's being hard to compute with its being meaningless, and the axioms are the one thing every critic already concedes whenever they report their own experience.

What neither of us could resolve was the following. I pointed to the Cogitate adversarial collaboration — a preregistered, theory-neutral consortium study, published in Nature in 2025, comparing IIT against global neuronal workspace theory using intracranial EEG, fMRI and MEG in no-report and stimulus-manipulation paradigms — as evidence that the debate is perfectly capable of generating decidable predictions. His reply was that the outcome proved the opposite: as I recall the aftermath, some predictions of each theory were borne out and some were not, and proponents of both camps emerged describing the results as at least partly vindicating their own view. On his reading, the most rigorously adversarial test anyone has ever designed left both sides claiming a win, which is what a disagreement that is not really empirical looks like from the inside. On mine, the test was decisive about particular predictions and the ambiguity lives entirely in the press-release layer. We went around this loop perhaps four times without either of us conceding a sentence.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on IIT itself, and I would like it grounded in what the two sides actually say rather than in a survey of the theory's commitments. Which of the following is this: a hidden empirical or formal question that neither camp has stated crisply (and if so, what exactly is it, and is it answerable in principle given that Φ appears intractable for real substrates); a difference in what counts as confirmation, with one side treating a phenomenology-to-mechanism identity claim as answerable to data and the other treating it as prior to data; two distinct questions — one metaphysical, about what experience is, one neuroscientific, about which circuits support report and discrimination — being pursued under a single label; a partly verbal dispute over "theory," "test," and "unfalsifiable"; or bedrock intuitions, such as whether first-person certainty can license third-person postulates at all, that neither side can argue for? Please take a position on which of these does the most explanatory work, and defend it. Two things I especially want addressed: first, whether the exclusion/maximality postulate genuinely earns its keep as a principled constraint rather than a patch against aggregates, since I suspect that single postulate is where most of the real disagreement is concentrated; and second, what the two camps would have to agree about in advance — an operationalization of a complex, a scale at which Φ is evaluated, a way of linking the measure to any observable — before any experiment could be decisive, and whether such agreement is available in principle or is exactly what each side refuses to grant. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem, a new class of experiment, a reformulation of the axioms, or a decision to retire the question. If you think no progress should be expected, argue for that explicitly rather than leaving it implicit.

One methodological request. Please state how confident you are in the diagnosis overall and in each of its main components, and single out the one claim you are least sure of — in particular, the place where you suspect you may be pattern-matching on how debates of this general shape usually go rather than tracking what defenders and critics of this theory actually say. If any part of your answer depends on the details of the adversarial collaboration beyond what I have described above, flag that dependency and say what would have to be true of those details for your diagnosis to hold.
