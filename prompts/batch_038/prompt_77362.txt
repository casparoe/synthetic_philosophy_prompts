A colleague in my metaphysics reading group has an argument he offers as showing that ordinary disposition ascriptions — fragility, conductivity, solubility, irritability — are empty, and I have been unable to locate the flaw in it. He begins from the simplest available analysis and then generalizes from the standard trouble cases. His steps are these:

(1) To say that x is disposed at t to manifest M when it undergoes stimulus S is to say no more than that, were x to undergo S at t, x would M. Nothing over and above the truth of that counterfactual is meant by the disposition.

(2) Consider the familiar electro-fink: a live wire attached to a device that, at the instant a conductor touches the wire, stops supplying current and simultaneously alters the wire's structure so that it is no longer conductive. At t the wire is disposed to conduct when touched by a conductor; yet were it touched at t, it would not conduct. So the left-to-right direction of (1) fails.

(3) The device in (2) does no special work. Whatever intrinsic basis B underwrites a disposition, B can be described as removable, and as removable in the interval between stimulus and manifestation. Equally, B can be described as blocked in that interval while remaining wholly intact: the styrofoam cup that keeps its molecular structure but is packed against breaking, the glass that would be caught by a guardian. So for every x, S, and M there is a possible case in which x has the disposition and the counterfactual of (1) is false.

(4) Reverse the device. Suppose a malign spirit despises fragility and would arrange that any styrofoam cup, however spongy its intrinsic structure, is struck and shattered. Then the counterfactual in (1) is true of the cup, although the cup is not fragile. So for every x, S, and M there is also a possible case in which x lacks the disposition and the counterfactual is true.

(5) Hence the truth or falsity of the counterfactual is not even weakly diagnostic of the disposition: for any ascription there is a nearby possibility in which the two come apart.

(6) But (1) was supposed to state what the disposition consists in. If the counterfactual is not diagnostic of it, and there is nothing further to it, then there is no fact of the matter which objects have which dispositions.

(7) Therefore nothing is fragile, conductive, soluble, or irritable. Such ascriptions are false, or at best vacuous.

He maintains that the argument is valid and that he cannot see which premise to surrender. For my part, I am most attached to the reality of the dispositions named in (7) and least attached to the bare formulation in (1): I would rather abandon (1) than abandon fragility. I am also willing to grant him (2) as a fair description of a case, though I would like to be told if that concession is itself doing hidden work later on.

Which step is the first one I should refuse, and what exactly licenses the transition it appears to make — in particular, what permits the move from the existence of merely possible finks and blockers to a verdict about what actual counterfactuals say about actual objects, and from there to a verdict about what there is? I would also like the alternative exits priced. If the analysis is repaired by holding the intrinsic basis fixed, so that x must undergo S and retain whatever underwrites the disposition through to the manifestation, does that dispose of (3), of (4), or of neither — and what new problem does the repaired version face? If the repair fails, must one give up on analyzing dispositions counterfactually altogether, or only on analyzing them in terms of this particular counterfactual? And what do the leading fallbacks cost: treating dispositions as primitive causal powers rather than as anything analyzable, identifying each disposition with its categorical basis, or reading dispositions off the laws instead of off counterfactuals? For each, say what it saves, what it forfeits, and whether it leaves (7) any threat at all.
