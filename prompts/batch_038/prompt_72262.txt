I produce a monthly podcast on the technical side of AI alignment; the audience is mostly ML researchers and graduate students who have read the 2018 debate proposal but have never thought hard about its assumptions. Next week's guest is a researcher who spent several years building debate-style oversight systems and has come away arguing that the approach fails for a structural reason rather than an empirical one. Because the guest is not a public figure with a written record I can point to, here is the position as they have summarized it to me in advance, and the questions should engage that position directly:

- Their claim is that debate's optimism depends on an unstated premise they call the locality of error: that whenever an agent argues for a false conclusion, there is at least one statement in the transcript that a competent judge, considering that statement on its own, can reject. The familiar rejoinder to hard-to-check arguments — that the honest opponent will simply point at the flaw — quietly assumes the flaw is pointable, meaning localized in a single move short enough for a judge to evaluate in isolation.
- They argue that a capable debater can reliably produce errors that are not local: the falsity sits in the choice of which quantity to argue about, or emerges only from the aggregation of many individually unobjectionable steps, or rests on a background assumption both sides share, so that no single statement in the exchange is actually false.
- Their central asymmetry claim is that manufacturing such an error is cheap while detecting it is expensive, and that the honest side cannot answer with a localized rebuttal: exposing a distributed flaw requires walking the judge through a reconstruction at least as long as the obfuscation it targets. Within a fixed transcript length and a fixed judge attention budget, honesty therefore becomes competitively disadvantageous, and selection favors arguments that are compressed enough to survive scrutiny rather than arguments that are true.
- Their proposed fix is atomized adjudication. The judge renders no verdict on the exchange as a whole; instead the debate produces an explicit list of atomic claims, each paired with a warrant that is either mechanically checkable or cheap for a human to check, and payoffs are assigned claim by claim. The debaters choose the decomposition themselves, which the guest concedes is itself a move within the game.
- Their proposed empirical handle is a quantity they call audit cost: the human or compute time a careful reviewer needs to reach a confident verdict on the winning argument. They predict that as the debaters get stronger, audit cost rises while judge accuracy stays flat or declines, and they think that pattern would distinguish their view from the optimistic one.

Please draft the question set for a forty-five-minute conversation, arranged so that the pressure increases as it goes. I need ten to twelve questions, each written the way I would actually say it on air — one to three sentences, no stacked subordinate clauses, no paragraph-length setups that answer themselves. For every question give the follow-up I should use, keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give on first pass, plus a one-line note telling me what a good answer concedes and what an evasive answer sounds like.

At least one question each must land on the following, though you should fold several of them together where that makes for better radio:

1. Whether locality of error is a premise they discovered in the proposal or one they have projected onto it, and whether they can produce a single concrete example of a genuinely non-local error — a transcript in which no individual statement is false and yet the conclusion does not follow. Push on whether the example survives once the honest side is allowed to name the missing assumption explicitly.
2. The self-referential problem: their own critique has the shape of a persuasive argument with no single checkable false step, so ask what distinguishes it from the obfuscated arguments they warn about, and what a listener should do to audit it.
3. The decomposition objection. If the debaters choose the atoms, has the obfuscation simply moved up a level into the choice of what to atomize — into which quantities get named, which confounds get listed, which background assumptions stay implicit because no atom captures them? Ask what constrains the decomposition and whether any constraint they offer is itself checkable.
4. The claim that true conclusions admit of shorter and more auditable derivations than false ones. Their asymmetry is asserted more than argued, so make them defend it, and make them say what happens to the whole picture if, in domains like forecasting or policy evaluation, false narratives are simply more compressible than true ones.
5. The comparison with formal mathematics, where verification really is cheaper than search and where the prover-verifier gap is a theorem rather than a hope. Ask why the empirical and political domains should be expected to behave differently, and whether their worry is anything more than the familiar observation that checking a complex empirical claim can cost as much as producing it.
6. Testability. Their audit-cost prediction needs a judge that is not a human and a task with a ground truth, so ask what the experiment would look like concretely, what result would falsify their view rather than merely fail to confirm it, and whether audit cost can be measured independently of the reviewer's prior sympathy for the conclusion.
7. What replaces debate. If the locality premise fails, does recursive amplification or iterated distillation fail for the same reason, since both also rely on a human inspecting a decomposition of a harder task into easier pieces? Ask them to say which oversight scheme, if any, survives their argument.
8. The operational question: given their view, what should a lab actually stop doing on Monday, and what does the guest say to the objection that atomized adjudication is too expensive to run at the scale real training requires.

Also mark explicitly which one of the twelve is the question the guest will be relieved to get, and which is the one they will dread, and say in a sentence why for each. Finally, tell me which two questions I should cut if we are running out of time, and why the remaining set still makes the argument legible to a listener who has only skimmed the original proposal.

Do not include any question asking the guest to recommend papers, name sources, or summarize the debate proposal — the audience has been briefed, and I want the whole set to turn on the content of the position as I have laid it out above.
