I am a junior at a public high school in a mid-sized district, and I am one of four members of a student cybersecurity club that meets on Thursdays with a teacher who mostly lets us work on our own. In October, while experimenting on a practice server our advisor had set up, one of us noticed that the district's online gradebook — the same system our parents use to check assignments — would return a student's complete record, including attendance, counselor notes, and discipline history, if you changed a single number in the address of the page. It requires no password beyond the ordinary login of any one account. We confirmed this on our own four accounts and on two volunteers from another class, and then we stopped. In November we reported the problem privately and in writing to the district's technology office, describing how to reproduce it without including anything that a competent person could not reconstruct alone. Four months later nothing has changed: no acknowledgement, no patch, and the system behaves exactly as it did.

The four of us now cannot agree on what to do, and I have come to think the disagreement is not really about the facts, since we accept the same ones. One member has drafted a public write-up — a blog post with screenshots, plus a message to a reporter at the local paper — on the grounds that the district will only move once it is embarrassed. Another insists that anything public would be reckless: the post would hand the method to every curious student in three high schools, the district could respond by investigating our access rather than fixing the exposure, and our parents and college admissions officers would hear about it long before the flaw harmed anyone. The third wants to say nothing until we graduate and it becomes someone else's problem. I am somewhere in between, and I have noticed that I argue for publication by pointing at how many students' records are exposed, while the cautious one argues by listing the different ways an announcement could go wrong — of which there seem to be very many, and only one way in which it goes right. That imbalance troubles me, because I cannot tell whether it is a genuine feature of our situation or merely a way of making hesitation sound more reasonable than it is.

What I most want help with is the decision procedure, because whatever the four of us settle on, any one of us could simply act. The member with the draft has said, half in jest, that he could publish it tonight from his own laptop and that the rest of us have no way to prevent this except by persuading him, whereas the three of us could only stop publication by all continuing to persuade him and by nobody else deciding to publish in the meantime. It strikes me as strange that a rule requiring agreement before acting would hand the deciding vote to the single most confident person, while the three of us who are less sure must be unanimous in the opposite direction merely to leave things as they are. Is there any principle that tells a group like ours what it ought to do when its members disagree about an action that each of them is individually able to take? A related point came up at our last meeting: one member observed that if twenty people knew about this instead of four, somebody would almost certainly have published it by now, and asked whether the likelihood that disclosure happens anyway is a reason for us to make it happen on our terms. I cannot judge whether that reasoning is sound or a rationalization dressed up as realism. I would rather not be told to bring in an adult, since our advisor was told in November and asked us to do nothing at all until he had spoken with the superintendent, which was in January and has apparently not occurred.
