I teach intro philosophy at a community college and every spring I run the same exercise on Berkeley's master argument. Setup is dead simple: I lay out the challenge — go ahead, try to conceive of a tree, or a book, existing with nobody perceiving it — and then I hand out index cards and tell the students to either actually do it and describe what they did, or explain why it can't be done. Cards are anonymous. I get about sixty of them per section and my grading method is currently "read them and sort them into piles by vibes," which is no good and totally unrepeatable between sections.

What I want is a triage procedure I can run on a stack of cards in an evening, roughly a minute a card. Not an essay on Berkeley, not a lesson plan — a thing I execute, that spits out one of maybe five or six labeled outcomes per card, plus a one-line note to write on it. You'll have to settle the outcome categories yourself and say why those and not others. Ground them in what the argument is actually doing and where the pressure points are, and make sure the distinctions that carry the real weight survive translation into something I can apply to a nineteen-year-old's handwriting without invoking any jargon in front of the class. I'm thinking at minimum the procedure has to keep these separate: whether the card reports forming a mental image vs. whether it reports entertaining a proposition; whether whatever is doing the framing of the scene got smuggled into the scene as one of the characters; and whether the objection, if it works at all, works against the strong version of the challenge or only against a weaker one. (I'll tell you up front that the label "master argument" isn't Berkeley's own, it's a later coinage, so if your procedure treats the thing as if Berkeley presented it as a single formal argument with numbered premises, I'm going to assume you didn't think about it hard.)

Also — and this is the part I most want — build in a domain switch. The same style of challenge gets aimed at three different targets: a physical object nobody's perceiving, a stretch of the past before anyone was around to experience it, and other people's minds. I don't think the challenge has the same force in all three, and if your procedure grades cards against a single standard it's going to misfire. So either sort by target first or handle the difference inside the tests, but tell me which you did and why. And here's a check on whether you've got the structure right: Berkeley's own way of dealing with the tree-when-nobody's-looking case is to say it stays in God's mind, which concedes something to one reading of the challenge and not to the other. If your procedure can't register that concession, or sorts it into the same bucket as a card that just fails, fix the procedure.

Then run it on these cards, which are roughly representative (lightly cleaned up, and I've dropped the swearwords):

- "I just pictured a tree in an empty field. So there, done, Berkeley loses."
- "Whatever I picture, I'm the one picturing it, so it's in my mind. But that's a fact about my picturing, not about the tree. He's moving the goalposts."
- "You can't think about something without thinking about it. That's not a discovery, it's grammar."
- "It just seems to me like there are trees in forests with nobody there and I trust that more than a philosopher's word puzzle."
- "Fine, I can't imagine it unconceived. But that's a limit on my imagination. If my limits were facts about the world, nothing would exist past the edges of my head."
- "I imagined the tree and then I imagined *myself* standing there looking at it, and if I'm honest I can't get rid of the second part."
- "I can definitely conceive of a tree that existed before there were any minds. I just can't conceive of anyone conceiving it."
- "When I try, all I get is a copy in my head, and I don't think he's actually asking about copies."

Two of those I expect your procedure to land somewhere I'd disagree with. Flag them as they come up and tell me where you think I'm wrong, because I want to see the procedure bite rather than just flatter me. And if there's a card type the procedure genuinely can't settle — where I have to just decide — say so explicitly and mark that step as judgment instead of dressing it up as another test. Last thing: the students never see the labels, they see my note, so make the notes something a person can read without feeling like they got fed through a machine.
