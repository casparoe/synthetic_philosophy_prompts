So I'm TA-ing a grad seminar on religion and public justification, and by week nine the two camps in the room have basically stopped engaging. One side is the Rawls-style consensus line: for a coercive law to be legitimate its justification has to run through reasons drawn from a political conception that all reasonable citizens could share, and religious reasons come in only downstream, in that proviso-shaped way, as a way of showing how the shared reasons are rooted in people's own comprehensive views. The other side is the convergence line you get from Gaus and Vallier: there's no shared pool of reasons and no need for one — what's required is that each citizen, considered individually, has sufficient reason of her own to accept the law, and those reasons can be religious, prudential, aesthetic, whatever, so long as they're genuinely reason-giving for her and she'd endorse them under the right idealization. They keep sniping without ever pinning down whether they're even answering the same question. The consensus people say convergence has given up on a common civic language and made legitimacy a matter of private bookkeeping; the convergence people say consensus demands an impossible unanimity of rationales and smuggles in a contested comprehensive view of what reasoning counts as public.

I've been volunteered to write up the document the two sides should have produced months ago — a joint statement from a proper adversarial collaboration, the kind both could put their names to. Draft it. I want a numbered section of claims both camps can actually sign (things about what the fact of reasonable pluralism does and doesn't imply, about the difference between legitimacy and truth, about whether religious citizens may voice religious reasons in the background culture versus in a courtroom or legislative floor debate, about whether the proviso is doing any work at all, about who the relevant justificatory constituency is); then a disagreements table giving, for each contested point, a neutral statement of the question plus each side's formulation written the way that side would want it written — no strawmanning, and no wording that only a critic of that view would use; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where settling the issue would actually move at least one camp; and for each crux, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance rather than improvised after the fact. Each side should also state what would count as evidence or argument against its own view, and you should say explicitly which chunks of the dispute are verbal — my own suspicion is that "accessible," "shareable," and "intelligible" are doing a lot of covert sliding, and that some of the fight is about how idealized the citizens in the theory are supposed to be (fully rational and fully informed versus modestly idealized and keeping their actual doctrines versus actual people as they are), so for every crux I'd like it flagged which idealization level each camp is tacitly assuming and whether moving the level flips the verdict.

To keep it from turning into pure abstraction, run it through a case I've made up for the seminar: a city ordinance banning all animal slaughter within city limits, passed after a campaign led mostly by congregations arguing that ritual slaughter defiles the community and that the city shouldn't tolerate it — though the drafting committee's own stated rationale is public health and nuisance, there's an available fully secular defense on those grounds, a couple of secular animal-welfare groups supported the ban independently, and the ordinance as written also shuts down a nonreligious urban homestead that was doing open-air slaughter for its own members. Ask whether that law is publicly justified, whether the two criteria actually yield different answers, and — this matters — whether each camp's verdict survives being told that the two sides are disagreeing about how to individuate the proposal under test (the ban-as-enacted versus the ban-as-campaigned-for versus the ban-plus-exemptions). If you conclude the case doesn't discriminate between the views, say so and design one that does, and record what each side predicted about the new case. Please don't hand me a bibliography or a list of readings — half the seminar hasn't got library access and I'm not chasing down citations, and honestly a made-up reference is worse than none. Just write the document, table and all, in a register that two people who still dislike each other could both sign.
