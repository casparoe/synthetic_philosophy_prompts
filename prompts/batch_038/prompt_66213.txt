Novelist here, third book, and I've got a scene I cant get to work. My protagonist Ines was blind from birth — dense congenital cataracts in both eyes — and had the surgery at 43. Fourteen months later she can do some things and not others. She can pick a cube out of a pair of objects by sight alone, but only after weeks of practice, and when she does she calls it the shadow one, which is a detail I made up and im quite proud of. She can't pick her wife out of a crowd visually, she gets around a familiar room by counting, and she says looking at things is loud.

The scene I need: she's in a hospital cafeteria with the vision scientist who tested her for a study, and with her brother-in-law, who teaches philosophy at a state school and has no professional stake but a great many opinions. The two of them get into a fight about what her case proves about Molyneux's problem, and the fight is the scene. Not a lecture with two people standing in it.

What I want you to write: the dialogue, 900 to 1200 words. The scientist's position is that the thought experiment is asking about a moment that doesn't exist — sight comes on gradually, acuity ramps up, there's no first look to speak of — so the only well-posed question is what actually transfers between the senses and how long that takes, and Ines's data is an answer to that. The brother-in-law's position is that the immediacy clause is doing real work and she's dodging it: the question was never about pointing accuracy, it's about whether what Ines sees when she looks at a cube is the same spatial content she had when she handled one, and her managing to point at it three weeks later leaves that untouched.

Both of them have to be good at this. No flat empiricist, no pedant. Each one lands something the other can't fully parry, and they respond to what was actually said rather than to the speech before it — including the specifics of her case, the shadow one, the faces, the loudness. Ines is in the room and has her own view about being turned into evidence; she gets to interrupt and she gets to be sharp, but don't hand her the last word just because she's the protagonist.

Don't have anyone recite the history of the problem or explain it to each other like the reader just walked in. A name-drop of Locke or Berkeley is fine as an aside but nothing can rest on it, and nobody should be quoting eighteenth-century letters at each other in a cafeteria.

Then after the scene, give me a paragraph or two, plainly, on where you cheated: which of the two positions you actually think is better supported, what argument or what kind of finding would move you to the other side, and any spot where you gave a character a weaker line than the best one available to them because it played better dramatically. I want to know where the scene is soft so I can decide whether to keep the softness.
