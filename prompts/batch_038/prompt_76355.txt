I do policy-adjacent work on farmed aquatic animals — mostly writing things up for people who fund welfare interventions in shrimp aquaculture — and in meetings I keep getting asked the same question: how sure are we that shrimp are actually conscious? My answer has been mush ("the evidence is stronger than the public assumes, weaker than activists claim") and I want to stop giving it. So here's what I want from you: your actual credence, as a single number between 0 and 1, that at least some decapod crustaceans have phenomenally conscious states at some point in their lives — let's pin it to farmed whiteleg shrimp rather than lobsters, since that's where the ~eight million tonnes of crustaceans pulled out of the water each year mostly comes from, and since the legal picture is such a mess that it matters (the UK's 2022 sentience act names decapods, while the EU's directive on animals used in science covers cephalopods and just leaves decapods out entirely). Then break that number into the terms you actually combined to get it, each with its own number and one or two sentences of justification.

The terms I care about, though add or cut as you see fit: (a) the probability that some account of consciousness that doesn't require cortex-like laminar structure is correct — and tell me which family you have in mind, global-workspace-ish, recurrent processing, predictive/active-inference, higher-order thought, or "there's no further fact beyond functional organization"; (b) given that account, the probability that a shrimp's nervous system instantiates it, bearing in mind that it's mostly a ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia plus a pretty small supraesophogeal ganglion, and that a lot of decapod behavior looks locally runnable — a detached leg keeps twitching, a severed claw keeps pinching; (c) the probability that the indicators people lean on (motivational trade-offs where the animal pays a cost to avoid a noxious stimulus, prolonged tending of an injured appendage, behavioral changes that outlast the event, responsiveness to analgesics) genuinely discriminate experience from nociception-plus-associative-learning-with-nobody-home; (d) whether "sentient" is binary or graded here, and if graded, where shrimp sits relative to the level at which suffering starts to be welfare-relevant. And then check yourself for phylogenetic consistancy: hexapods sit inside Pancrustacea, so insects are cladistically crustaceans — if you tell me 0.8 for teleost fish and 0.3 for insects, I want to know where shrimp falls given the bracket those two set, and whether the headline number above survives that check. Finally, rank these by how much they'd move you: a clean result showing an opioid antagonist reverses the protective behavior in shrimp; more tail-flick latency studies; a much better decapod connectome; a decisive philosophical argument that higher-order theories can't apply to any nonhuman animal. Say explicitly which of those four you think would move you not at all, and why.

For context on where a disagreement lives: my number is about 0.55. A colleague at the same org says 0.9 and thinks I'm a closet speciesist; the marine biologist we pay for occasional reviews says 0.15 and thinks the entire debate is anthropomorphic noise. Point at the single term in your decomposition that you think explains most of that spread, and tell me whether the 0.9 person and the 0.15 person actually disagree about shrimp, or about something else that they've each smuggled into the word "sentient." One more thing before you write: commit. I don't want a range wider than fifteen points on the headline number and I don't want "reasonable people could differ" as your closing line — that's the answer I get in meetings already. Then tell me how confident you are in the number you landed on, in the sense of whether you'd bet on it, and single out the one sub-estimate you're least sure of and what would make you revise it.
