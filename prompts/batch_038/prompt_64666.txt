Last session of my grad seminar on choice under imperfect recall and I want to hand them fiction instead of a fifth formal treatment. They've all ground through the Piccione–Rubinstein absent-minded driver tree, they can recite why the plan you'd pick before setting out isn't the choice you'd make once you're there, and not one of them feels it. So: write me a short story, call it 1200–1800 words, that dramatizes that gap from the inside. Its not a mood piece I'm after, it's a teaching object.

Here's the spine — keep it. A driver has to pass two junctions on a stretch of road that are indistinguishable from the seat: same lights, same gravel shoulder, same blue signage. Getting off at the first one ruins the trip outright. Getting off at the second one is exactly what she was dispatched to do. Blowing past both is survivable and nothing more. Before she leaves she knows all of this, and she also knows that once she's rolling she'll stop laying down memory of the stretch, so when a junction looms up she genuinely cannot tell which one it is.

The constraints are where the value is, so please honor them:

- First person, present tense throughout. The reader has to be in her epistemic shoes rather than watching her from above. No scene breaks or datelines that quietly tell us where we are.
- Include a scene before departure in which she settles on a plan and writes it on the inside of her forearm, and a later scene in which she reads that writing at a junction and has to decide whether it's an instruction from herself or a suggestion from a stranger who happened to be her an hour ago. That second scene is the hinge of the whole thing, and I want it to be a decision she makes, not a flashback we get to watch.
- She feels the pull between what she wrote and what looks sensible right now. She gets no vocabulary for it. Nobody in the story says "inconsistent," "probability," "rational," or "belief." There's a dispatcher on the radio who is convinced she's just flaking out and keeps saying so; that's as close as the story comes to an argument.
- End at a junction, with her having chosen, and don't tell us which junction it was. No reveal, no pull-back shot, no detail in the last paragraph that lets the reader settle the question afterwards. The reveal is the cheap version and my students will go hunting for it.
- Register: plain and concrete, shift-log spare. No weather-as-metaphor, no lyrical fog, no one reflecting on what memory means.

And it can't be an amnesia story with the decision problem wallpapered over. Someone who has never seen the formal setup should finish it and be able to say why her sensible plan and her sensible action are pulling in different directions. So after the fiction, in three or four sentences out of character, tell me which feature of your story is doing the work the payoff numbers do in the tree, which detail is standing in for her inability to tell the two junctions apart, and what you had to leave out to stop it turning into a puzzle with an answer.
