H. G. Wells's *The Country of the Blind* (1904) is habitually glossed as an inversion of the cave allegory in *Republic* VII, and I want to know whether that gloss survives the detail of the two texts. Nuñez, a mountaineer, falls into an Andean valley whose inhabitants have all been blind for generations; he repeats to himself the old proverb about the one-eyed man being king, fails to demonstrate any such advantage, and is diagnosed by the village doctor as having diseased, distended eyes that keep his brain in constant irritation — for which an operation is offered as a cure.

Write me a critical reading that takes both works seriously, using neither merely to illustrate the other. What becomes of the prisoners' verdict that the ascent has ruined the returning man's eyesight when that verdict is delivered by a physician as an act of kindness? Is anyone in the story actually in a cave, given that the villagers are misled by no images and cope with their world rather better than Nuñez does? And how far does the allegory lean on visual imagery that its own content — an ascent to the Forms, which are not objects of perception — cannot supply? Then compare the endings: in 1904 Nuñez flees alone into the snow and lies smiling under the stars, while in Wells's expanded 1939 version he foresees a rockslide, is disbelieved, and escapes with Medina-Saroté. Does the revision make the story more Platonic by letting sight finally cash out in a true prediction, or less so?

Say frankly where the Platonic frame distorts the story — its treatment of love, of assimilation, of the condescension latent in Nuñez's ambition to rule — and close with what Wells adds to our reading of the allegory that argument alone would not. This is for a second-year seminar whose students have read the *Republic* and should be able to argue back.
