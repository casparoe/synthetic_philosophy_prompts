**Dosage: Nietzsche's Three Histories and the Problem of Termination**

The second of the Untimely Meditations distinguishes three ways of relating to the past — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — and ties each to a distinct need of the living. The one who acts and strives needs models, and gets monumental history; the one who preserves and reveres needs origins, and gets antiquarian history; the one who suffers and seeks liberation needs a tribunal, and gets critical history. The whole essay is governed by the formula Nietzsche offers in the preface: We would serve history only so far as it serves life. (I quote an older English translation here; the standard modern rendering says very nearly the same.)

The received reading treats the triad as a set of options, or as an ascending scale of maturity on which critical history is the adult mode and monumental history the naïve one. I want to argue something else. The three modes form a closed circuit, in which each is the remedy for the pathology its predecessor generates, and the circuit has no exit. Monumental history idealizes until it becomes a romance that effaces the difference between the great moment and its conditions, and then supplies the mediocre with a weapon against the next great man. Antiquarian history reveres until it mummifies: it loses the capacity to discriminate, so that everything inherited is conserved at the same value and the tree dies at the roots. Critical history judges until it amputates the tissue from which the judge himself grew, since on Nietzsche's account we are the outcome of the very past we arraign, and the operation is performed on ourselves. What keeps this circuit from being vicious is not a fourth standpoint but what Nietzsche calls plastic power: the capacity to heal wounds, to transform and incorporate what is foreign and past, to recreate broken forms out of oneself. Plastic power is not a view from outside history. It is a sense of timing and dose.

The one apparent exception is the suprahistorical figure of the first section — the man who no longer sees becoming but a completed whole, and who accordingly turns away from history in nausea. I take him to be the critical historian pushed to its limit: the judge who has escaped the circuit by pronouncing sentence on all of it at once. Nietzsche introduces this figure, on my reading, in order to discredit him, and the consolation he offers the suprahistorical man through art and religion is a concession rather than a solution.

The circuit explains a dispute in my own institution. A medical school is arguing about the founder whose name is on the principal building and whose published work was done on patients unable to consent. The monumental option keeps the name and teaches the discovery, holding the man up as a model of scientific nerve; its danger is Nietzsche's exactly, that the story hardens into a romance in which what he did and what we do become indistinguishable, and the latecomers learn that greatness excuses its conditions. The antiquarian option keeps the name and adds a plaque, insisting that gratitude to one's origins is not endorsement of them; its danger is the mummification Nietzsche names, the refusal to rank what has been inherited. The critical option strikes the name, reopens the archive, and puts the founder on trial; its danger is that the trial cannot stop with him, because the endowment, the methods, and the standing of the school are continuous with what he built, and the prosecutors discover that they are also defendants. These are not three opinions about one fact. They are three needs — of the ambitious, the grateful, and the harmed — and Nietzsche's claim, if I am right, is that a healthy institution does not settle permanently among them. It administers them in doses.

My conclusion is that the triad is not a taxonomy of historiography but a theory of institutional metabolism, and that the famous opening image of the grazing herd is the negative picture of what such metabolism looks like once it stops.
