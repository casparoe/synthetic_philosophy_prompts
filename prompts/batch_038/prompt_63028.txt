I spent 38 years doing analytical chemistry — mostly trace metals in estuaries — and retired three years ago, and to keep my head from going soft I started reading philosophy properly for the first time, beginning with language, because that seemed like it would be concrete. It has not been concrete. I've got forty pages of notes now on words used to demean a group — the technical term seems to be slurs — and I can't tell which of the things people insist an account of them has to do are real requirements and which are just somebody's hobbyhorse. So what I want from you is the specification, not the candidates: write out the desiderata, the list of things any theory of what such a word means has to deliver before you'd call it adequate. For each one, give me the case or observation behind it in a sentence or two so I can see where it comes from. Embedding is the one everybody leads with, but there's also what happens when the word turns up inside a quotation or read aloud in a courtroom, the fact that a target of the word can use it about themselves and it doesn't come out the same, the fact that two speakers can use the same word with wildly different force, the fact that the slur and its neutral counterpart pick out the same people and are still not interchangeable, and the fact that "I didn't mean it that way" doesn't rescue you here the way it rescues sarcasm. Tell me which distinctions a theory has to keep apart rather than collapse — I'm thinking of things like what a word says versus what using it does, derogation versus reference, being offended versus being harmed — and which plain facts about how these words actually behave it must not contradict.

Then the part I care about most: sort them. Which of these are non-negotiable, and which are the preferences of one camp dressed up as requirements? I have a suspicion that a couple of them are just the whole problem restated in different words, and if so I want that said out loud. Tell me plainly if some subset can't all be satisfied at once, because if it can't then the list is itself the result and I'd rather know that than read forty more papers. And tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking candidates out. After that, briefly, how the main functional families of answer do against your list — derogation as part of what the word literally says; derogation as a non-truth-conditional expression of attitude; the word as an echo or report of somebody else's contempt; the word as simply forbidden, so that any theory of its meaning is beside the point. Two or three sentences each. No authors, no reading list, no citations — I definately can't check them and I'm not interested, I want the structure.

Two more things. First, the purpose, since adequacy is relative to one: I volunteer at a small local history archive transcribing taped interviews with men who worked the shrimp boats in the 60s and 70s, and the tapes are full of words that are slurs now and were just talk then, so I want a theory good enough to help me decide case by case what goes into a printed transcript and what doesn't. The list has to be one a theory could actually meet and still hand me verdicts, not just a classification. Second, a requirement I invented myself and want you to judge, which I call the thermostat problem: the account has to let the force of a word vary with who says it, to whom, and in what setting, but it can't let it vary so freely that force becomes a dial you turn to whatever you'd already decided to say — its a calibratable instrument or it isn't a theory. Does that belong on the list as a genuine desideratum, or is it a prejudice imported from a career spent calibrating instruments? And while you're at it, one thing I can't find anybody addressing: is slur-hood even a projectable kind, or is it a grab bag of different words doing different jobs that happen to offend in the same register? If a theory is allowed to be disjunctive — no single mechanism, just a list — say whether that's a cop-out or whether my search for an essence was a mistake I brought over from chemistry. For a concrete case wherever you need one, use the invented example from my notes: in a made-up port town, families resettled from a flooded inland valley get called murlacks, a word that began as straight occupational description — picking shellfish off the mudflats at low water — and hardened into contempt over roughly two generations, so that now it lands as an insult even from people who mean nothing by it. Murlack isn't a real word, which is the point. Please write the whole thing in American English, and a table is fine for the list if that's cleaner.
