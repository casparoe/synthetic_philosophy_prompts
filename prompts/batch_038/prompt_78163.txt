I am preparing a structured exercise for a graduate seminar in social epistemology, and I would like your candid judgement rather than a survey. The exercise concerns Aumann's 1976 result: if two ideal Bayesian agents share a prior, and their posteriors about some event become common knowledge to them, those posteriors must coincide. Since persistent disagreement is a plain fact, at least one of the theorem's idealizations must be doing the explanatory work, and the literature offers a great many candidates. My students are asked to rank those candidates; I want your ranking first, so that I have something to argue against.

Please fix the explanandum as narrowly as I do. I am not interested in disagreement about values, in disagreement sustained by identity or incentive, or in disagreement between parties who have not seen one another's evidence. The target case is this: two research groups that read each other's papers, share the underlying data, attend the same workshops, understand each other's arguments, and after a decade still assign materially different probabilities to the same empirical hypothesis. Further, I impose a competence constraint: an acceptable diagnosis may not smuggle in the claim that one party is biased, careless, dishonest, or akratic. That literature exists and it is not what I am asking about. The route you endorse must be one on which both parties can be entirely rational, entirely sincere, and entirely well-informed, and still fail to converge.

Rate each of the following eight routes on what I shall call its diagnostic yield: of the disagreement cases that survive the competence constraint, what share does this route genuinely account for? Score each on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 means it is a real logical possibility that buys you nothing in practice, and 10 means it dissolves the puzzle outright. Ties are not permitted; you must break them and defend the break.

A. Heterogeneous priors. The parties never shared a prior to begin with, and no argument establishes that priors must coincide merely because people share a training, a language, and a body of public evidence.

B. The inoperability of the common prior. Even where a common prior exists, it is not the sort of thing the parties could ever be jointly aware of holding, so the theorem's premise cannot be invoked by anyone inside the disagreement. On this route the result is not false but unusable.

C. Unawareness and off-model hypotheses. The state space is not fixed and commonly known; one party may lack the conceptual resources to represent a hypothesis the other is conditionalizing on, so there is no single shared algebra over which the theorem is even stated.

D. Common knowledge versus very high-order mutual knowledge. The parties reach arbitrarily deep mutual knowledge without ever reaching common knowledge, and the discontinuity between the two means that the near-case inherits none of the conclusion.

E. Computational intractability. The correct posterior may be well defined yet infeasible for any actual reasoner to compute, so agreement is a claim about a state no one ever occupies.

F. Indexical and self-locating evidence. Part of each party's evidence consists of essentially first-personal information about who and when she is, and information of that kind does not pool into a common prior over a shared space.

G. Non-Bayesian updating under ambiguity. Where the evidence does not determine unique likelihoods, updating is not conditionalization, and parties can remain in reflective equilibrium while diverging.

H. Composition. The theorem governs individuals, but the disagreeing units are groups, and a group's stated position need not be any member's posterior; group-level disagreement is therefore compatible with universal individual agreement.

Please structure your response as follows. First, the eight scores in alphabetical order by label, each followed by one or two sentences justifying the number and saying explicitly what the route gains and what it fails to cover under my competence constraint. Second, a three-tier grouping of the eight labels — routes I should teach at length, routes worth a paragraph, and routes worth a footnote. Third, a short paragraph identifying the route you think is most underrated relative to the attention it receives, and stating in one sentence what an institution designed around that diagnosis would look like. Fourth, a single ordering of all eight labels from highest diagnostic yield to lowest, which must be consistent with your scores.

Write from reasoning alone; I would much rather you cite nothing than cite from recollection, and I do not want a bibliography. Where you are genuinely uncertain, commit anyway — the exercise is worthless if every score clusters near the middle.

End your response with the ordering on its own line in exactly this form, with the labels in descending order of diagnostic yield and separated by ">":

\answer{A > D > C > ...}
