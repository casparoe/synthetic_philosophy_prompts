I am a doctoral student in the history of economic thought, and I have come across the following passage in a general-audience history of twentieth-century economics, which I am considering using as a framing device for a chapter of my own:

'Mises's argument in 1920 was that socialism is impossible, full stop: without private property there can be no prices, and without prices no calculation of any kind, so a socialist ministry could not even decide whether to develop a watercourse for the generation of electricity or to extend a coal mine. Hayek generalised this into the knowledge problem — the relevant facts about local conditions and shifting scarcities are dispersed among millions of people, so no planning board could ever collect them, however large its computers. Lange replied by dissolving the problem altogether: a central planning board need only mimic a market, announcing provisional prices, observing the surpluses and shortages that result, and revising by trial and error until supply equalled demand in every market, which is precisely what a competitive market does automatically. This refuted Mises and Hayek on their own terms, and market socialism was the settled answer for the next two decades; the collapse of the Soviet bloc in 1989 was therefore a humiliation for the profession, since it showed that Lange had been wrong all along.'

I am myself inclined to side with the Austrians, so I would rather not have that inclination confirmed uncritically. Please work through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a historian of the debate would accept, which are simplifications that survive correction, and which are substantive misstatements. In particular: is 'impossible, full stop' what Mises argued, or does it collapse a narrower thesis about the means of production into a much broader one about socialism as such? Is the gloss on Hayek fair, or does it convert an argument about the kind of knowledge prices carry into a mere complaint about information-processing capacity? Is the description of Lange's proposal accurate — and if it is, does 'refuted on their own terms' follow from it? Here I should like both sides: what Lange's critics said back, and what a defender of Lange would reply to that. Finally, does the 1989 inference survive, given what actually-existing Soviet planning was rather than what Lange's blueprint described?

Please end by telling me how much of the passage's conclusion still stands once the misstatements are removed. I am after the argument, not a bibliography or a reading list.
