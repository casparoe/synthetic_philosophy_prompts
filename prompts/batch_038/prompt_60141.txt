I have spent four years on a novel whose engine is almost entirely negative: the harm arrives by way of a letter not sent, a warning not given, a hand not reached out. Before I revise the final third, I want to know what I am entitled to assume. Please write the specification — the desiderata — that any account of causation by omission must satisfy in order to count as adequate, where adequacy is fixed as follows: the theory must be good enough to tell me, case by case, whether the causal claim embedded in a scene is true or merely rhetorically effective. I do not want a survey of the literature, a bibliography, or named citations; I want the criteria themselves, together with the consideration that motivates each.

For every requirement, please give me: the requirement in a sentence or two; the case or consideration that forces it upon a theory; whether it is non-negotiable for any account in this area or is instead a demand peculiar to a camp — and if the latter, which camp presses it and what it buys them; and, where the requirement constrains the formal shape of the causal relation rather than its content (asymmetry, irreflexivity, transitivity, whether causation is a two-place relation between events at all or requires contrastive or otherwise enriched relata), say so explicitly, since those are the ones a writer is most likely to violate unknowingly.

Make sure the list covers at least the following kinds of demand, though I expect you will think of others: the distinctions a theory may not collapse — between an omission and a mere absence, between prevention and causation, between causing and allowing, between being a cause and being causally relevant or explanatory; the outside results it must not contradict — that physics finds no negative events among its furniture, so a theory must say what it is committing itself to ontologically when it makes an absence a relatum; the legal data — that most common-law systems will not treat a stranger's failure to intervene as a cause of the harm absent a special duty, while several civil-law systems criminalize the failure to render assistance at all, and a theory should either explain that asymmetry and its variation or explain why it is irrelevant; and the asymmetric pattern of our reactive attitudes — that we blame for failures to act, but less than we blame for acts, and that we do not blame everyone for every bad thing they did not prevent.

Then tell me what verdict a theory meeting your list would be obliged to give, and where it would remain free, on these situations from my manuscript:

1. A woman writes a letter telling her brother that their father's affairs are being mismanaged, then decides not to send it. The brother, believing all is well, does not come home, and the father, alone in the house through a freeze, dies of exposure.
2. The same plant dies whether I describe it as killed by the gardener's failure to water it or by the failure of a distant head of state to water it. Both descriptions are true of the world; only one seems to be a cause.
3. A child drowns within sight of twenty strong swimmers, each of whom would have saved her, each of whom assumed another would, none of whom stands in any special relation to her. Had any one acted, the child would have lived.
4. A father fails to pull his child back from the curb, the child is struck — but the failure is the product of an absence seizure that seized him at that moment, so that there was, arguably, no omission at all, only an event and a non-occurrence.
5. A nurse fails to sedate an agitated patient; the patient tears out a line; a drug is therefore never delivered; and because it is never delivered, a fatal interaction with something else administered that night does not occur. The failure to sedate is thus a cause of the patient's survival, by way of two preventions.
6. A man fails to warn a colleague of a danger, but the colleague, warned or not, would have walked straight into it.

If any of these is a defective test — if one of them begs a question against a whole class of theories, or turns on the way I have described the situation rather than on the situation itself — say so, and say why. I would rather cut the scene than build a plot on a distinction that cannot be drawn.

After the list, three shorter things. First: which of the demands a novelist instinctively makes of causation — salience, focalization, the conviction that the absence which mattered is the one the reader was made to feel — are genuine desiderata, and which are precisely the interest-relativity a respectable theory must refuse? I want to be told when my own trade's intuitions are camp prejudice rather than data. Second: if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, identify it and give the argument in words rather than symbols; that would be the most valuable part of your answer, since it would tell me the tension is real and not a defect in my plotting. Third: which single requirement eliminates the most candidates, and a brief scorecard — a few sentences each, no more — of a counterfactual-dependence account that accepts negative causation, a production or process account that denies causation by absence and reconstructs omission talk in terms of what actually happened, and a contrastive account that treats causation as a relation among difference-making contrasts.
