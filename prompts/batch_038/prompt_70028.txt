In three weeks I present a workshop paper to an audience of private-law scholars who work on restitution and unjust enrichment, two economic historians, and one philosopher of history. My thesis is that a present-day state owes reparations for land seized by its colonial predecessor in 1894 not because it inherits the wrongdoer's liability, but because it still holds the proceeds: the purchase money was paid into a development fund that has grown continuously ever since, so the correct measure is disgorgement of the gain rather than compensation for the loss. The 1894 case is a constructed illustration, but treat its facts as stipulated.

Prepare my question-and-answer sheet. I want the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — the ones that come out of tracing, defences such as change of position, limitation and the standing of a successor state, and out of the historians' distrust of counterfactual growth rates over a century — rather than the objections a general audience would put. Rank them by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it. For each: phrase it as that objector would phrase it; give me a reply that fits in roughly two minutes; state what I should concede at the outset; and note where the reply is thinnest, so that I know which point to shore up rather than hope goes unasked. End with the single question I am dreading most.
