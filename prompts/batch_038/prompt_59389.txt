I was a bystander to a dispute this autumn that has since landed on my desk, and I would like help working through it rigorously rather than diplomatically.

Some context. I belong to a small methods working group at a research institute — about eight people, mixed disciplines, no formal hierarchy. In the spring, one member, whom I will call Nadia, presented an unpublished technique she had developed for correcting a systematic artifact in a class of instrument readings, and circulated a draft write-up to the group. In the autumn, another member, Ilan, published a paper in an adjacent field using what most of us recognized as essentially that technique. He had not cited her, had not told her he intended to use it, and the published description was in his own words, with different notation and a few substantive additions of his own.

They confronted each other at a meeting I attended. The exchange went roughly as follows; I have numbered the moves because I want each one assessed separately.

1. Nadia said he had stolen her idea. Ilan replied that ideas are not the sort of thing that can be stolen: taking requires that the owner be left without the thing, and he had left her with everything — she still understands the technique and can still use it. Nothing was transferred and nothing was diminished.
2. Nadia said that was the wrong test. What she lost was not the idea but the position of having had it first, and everything that follows from that position: the recognition, the priority, the claim on the next several years of work in that direction. Those are gone, and he has them.
3. Ilan said that on her view every case of learning from a colleague would be a case of theft. He read her draft, understood the approach, and applied it, which is precisely what a working group exists for; nobody in that room regards reading one another's drafts as predatory.
4. Nadia said learning from a thing and arriving first with it are different acts: he had not built on her work, he had displaced it. She added that the law largely agrees the situation is actionable, since duties of confidence bind people who receive something in trust even when what they received is not property in the ordinary sense.
5. Ilan said this conceded his point. If the duty arises from the relationship rather than from the idea, then what he broke was something like a promise, not a property right — a real wrong, but a narrower one, and not the wrong she had accused him of.
6. He went on to say the technique was not wholly hers in any case: two of its components were things he had been circling independently, and the combination was the sort of thing several people in the area would have reached within a few years. One cannot own what was going to happen anyway.
7. Nadia said that if so, he should have produced it before reading her draft, and he had not. She said "independent" was doing a great deal of quiet work, because once you have seen a solution your memory of what you were on the verge of thinking reorganizes itself around it.
8. Ilan said that was an argument that she cannot tell the difference, not an argument that he is on the wrong side of it.
9. Nadia said his principle proves too much: if a wrong requires that the victim be deprived of a thing, plagiarism is not a wrong either, since a plagiarist leaves the original perfectly intact — and she assumed he did not want that consequence.
10. Ilan said he did not want it and did not get it. Plagiarism is a wrong about misrepresentation: it deceives an audience about where what they are reading came from, which is why it remains a wrong on his account, with the reader rather than the author as the injured party. He allowed that he might be guilty of something in that family. But misdescribing provenance is not taking, and the two call for different responses: if he took property from her, he owes restitution and the paper is tainted; if he misstated the provenance of a contribution that was substantially his own, he owes a correction — to the readers and to her — and the paper stands.
11. Nadia said that he had by then conceded the wrong and was only haggling over its name. Ilan said the name was the entire point, since it determines what is owed and to whom.

Both of them have now asked me, as the person who took notes at that meeting and who was in the room when the draft was circulated, to write an account of the disagreement for a departmental process, and I have found that I do not know what I think. Separately, I am about to begin sharing methods of my own with outside collaborators on a small venture, and how this gets settled determines what protections I would want to insist on, so the question has stopped being abstract for me.

What I would like is a move-by-move adjudication. Rate each of the eleven moves — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, and say explicitly where a move is rhetorically effective but logically empty. Then answer the question the two of them kept circling without reaching: is what Ilan did a taking, a misrepresentation, a breach of a duty that is neither, or something the pair of them lack a category for? Finally, identify what both of them are missing; I suspect they share at least one assumption that neither would accept if it were stated plainly.

Please commit. I am not after a survey of the considerations on each side, and I do not want to be handed a framework and left to apply it myself. Where you think one of them has simply made a bad argument, say so and say what is wrong with it; where you think a distinction they are relying on cannot be made to hold, say that too. If the case is genuinely balanced at some particular point, tell me which point and why, rather than hedging across the board.
