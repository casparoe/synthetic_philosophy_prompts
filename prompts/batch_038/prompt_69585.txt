I'm a staff engineer at a company that builds small autonomous inspection vehicles for long pipe runs, service tunnels, and conveyor corridors, and I also own part of the fleet-coordination stack on the ground side. Roughly twice a month I have to write or review a section of a control specification with the same uncomfortable shape: a component arrives at a decision point that is, so far as it can observe, indistinguishable from other decision points it has already passed or will pass, and yet the correct behaviour differs depending on which one it actually is. Sometimes the component cannot tell because the environment is genuinely repetitive — a row of identical side chambers, a stretch of pipe with no landmarks — sometimes because it restarted and lost state, and sometimes because a human handover was incomplete. We have been patching these cases ad hoc: a hop counter here, an idempotency token there, a randomized delay somewhere else, and in one embarrassing instance a sticky note in the operating manual. Each fix is defended by intuition rather than by any account of why it is the right *kind* of fix, and the specs then go to a safety review board of competent engineers who are not decision theorists and who reliably reject any step that cannot be justified by the information available to the component at the moment it acts. The stakes are bounded but not trivial: a wrong call usually costs a stuck vehicle, a duplicated actuation, a wasted shift, or an audit finding, though a duplicated actuation on a live pipeline is the scenario that keeps me up at night.

The principle I want operationalized is the one behind the absent-minded driver problem. The version I have been working with, in my own paraphrase and with my own numbers: a driver is heading home along a highway with two junctions that look exactly alike and he cannot tell them apart from the driver's seat; his home is reached by exiting at the second junction, which is worth 4 to him; exiting at the first is worth 0; and driving past the second is worth 1. Ex ante, the best plan is obviously to continue at the first junction and exit at the second — but no rule he can follow *at a junction* implements that plan, because a rule has to give the same output at both. His only faithful option is to randomize, continuing at each junction with some probability p, and my own arithmetic says p = 2/3 for an expected value of 4/3, which I would like you to check rather than trust. What interests me is not the puzzle but the four things it seems to teach: that "what should I do now?" is the wrong question when "now" is ambiguous and one should be evaluating whole plans instead of single actions; that the driver's evidence about which junction he is at depends on the policy he adopted, so local updating is circular rather than merely incomplete; that randomization is not a hack but sometimes the only way a plan can be executed by an agent who cannot locate itself; and that the alternative is to break the symmetry deliberately by carrying state — a counter, a token, a log — which converts the problem into a different and usually more expensive one. I want a design procedure my team can actually run on a spec section in about half an hour: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and an explicit choice rule among the four responses the theory licenses — reason locally, commit ex ante to a plan that binds every instance, buy symmetry-breaking state, or randomize with a probability derived from the payoff structure. It must say how to derive that probability when payoffs are quantified and what to do when they are not (which is most of the time), it must say where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is about risk appetite or audit exposure rather than about the structure of the decision, and it must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Please don't pad it with references or a reading list; the reviewers will not read them and neither will I.

Then run it on three cases. First, a retry policy: after a crash our workers restart with wiped state, so a restarted worker cannot tell whether it is on its first attempt or its fourth, each attempt costs, and a repeated attempt can duplicate an irreversible side-effect; there are nine such workers and no coordinator that survives the crash. Second, the same shape with people instead of machines: a rotating on-call operator inherits an incident mid-stream with a partial handover and cannot tell whether a disruptive mitigation has already been tried once. Third, the case I expect to lose: a tunnel-inspection vehicle that must behave differently at the first, third, and fifth of a series of identical side chambers, under a certification regime that demands deterministic, reproducible behaviour and requires every action to be justified by locally available evidence. For that one I want the procedure to be blunt — tell me whether it forces us to randomize and, if so, how one argues for nondeterminism inside a safety case, or whether it forces us to pay for symmetry-breaking state and roughly what we are paying for. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and where the analogy to my systems is strained: the driver is one agent at different moments, whereas my components are distinct entities that never share a mind, and I want to know whether that difference breaks the reasoning or only changes its conclusions.
