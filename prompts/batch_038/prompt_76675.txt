Last night I sat through four hours of argument with a friend from my department that ended, as these things always end, with both of us conceding we would never convince each other and neither of us having said anything new. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a survey of it, so let me describe the setup carefully; please engage the positions as I describe them here and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or a tour of who has written what.

I work on the experimental evolution of a soil bacterium, in a lab that is split down the middle between biologists and philosophers of biology. Under nitrogen limitation the organism secretes a sticky exopolymer; secretion binds cells into millimetre-scale aggregates, which I will call pellets. Pellets retain water and extracellular enzymes, and once one reaches a critical size it fragments into two or three daughter pellets that disperse. Non-secreting cheater variants pay none of the secretory cost but ride inside pellets built by others. Our numbers over twenty serial transfers: the global frequency of the secreter allele fell from about 0.42 to 0.31; meanwhile pellets founded predominantly by secreters fragmented roughly 2.6 times as often as pellets founded predominantly by cheaters, and in reconstitution assays where we assemble pellets by hand at a specified secreter fraction and let them grow, that fragmentation advantage reproduces reliably. So both of the intuitive claims are true at once: cheaters spread, and high-secreter pellets win.

Four positions were on the table, and the argument I care about was with R., who holds the second. A. is a straightforward gene's-eye partisan in the Dawkins–Williams tradition: only the allele at the exopolymer locus has the properties — heritability, variation, differential replication — that make selection a fact about it; pellets are vehicles, or at most part of the allele's environment, and pellet-level talk is a façon de parler. R. is a multilevel-selection partisan in the Wilson–Sober tradition: there is heritable variation among pellets in secreter fraction and there is differential pellet productivity, so pellets are a genuine level at which selection acts, and the trait loses overall only because the within-pellet component of the variance swamps the between-pellet component. Our supervisor is a pluralist: the same causal process admits a gene-level and a group-level description that always yield the same predictions, and the quarrel is about which description explains better, not about fact. The quiet statistician in our group says all of this turns on whether fitness means expected or actual offspring number, and that a group's fitness is not even well defined until that is settled — the difference being, roughly, whether group fitness is an aggregate of the fitnesses of the organisms inside the group or a property of the group as a reproducing whole. What turned the meta-debate into a shouting match was technical. We ran two partitions of the very same twenty-transfer dataset. Partition A is a Price-style two-level partition of cell fitness with cells nested in pellets; it returns a sizeable positive between-pellet term. Partition B takes as the relevant context not pellet membership but the local neighbourhood composition around each focal cell — the cells actually sharing exopolymer and enzymes with it — and regresses cell fitness on that; the between-pellet term then comes out zero to within our error bars, with the whole change loaded onto within-neighbourhood selection plus a covariance with pellet size. A. and our supervisor read this as vindication: two slicings of the same numbers, no factual difference. R. read it as a refutation of pluralism: if two legitimate methods disagree about whether the between-group component exists, then one of them is wrong about something, and the disagreement was empirical all along.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I would like it attacked if it is wrong — is that this is not one disagreement but three tangled ones: a question about what varies and is inherited, a question about whose expected reproductive output we are computing, and a question about what level the best causal explanation must cite. Each camp answers one of these and mistakes it for the other two, which is why nothing anybody says lands. Test that. Using what each side actually says, tell me what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, in that "selection acts on X" is doing different work in each mouth; methodological, in that the live issue is what makes a partition of a selection differential correct; metaphysical, in that it concerns whether causal explanation requires a privileged grain; or empirical, in that there is a hidden fact about heritability at the pellet level that one side is silently assuming. Say which of these is carrying most of the weight and where the others are parasitic on it, and in particular say whether the partition mismatch is itself decisive evidence for one camp or just a restatement of the old disagreement in regression notation. Then tell me what would count as progress: a decisive experiment and what it would have to measure; a theorem fixing the conditions under which the two partitions must agree and those under which they may diverge, together with an assessment of whether our data satisfy them; a change of question; or nothing. If your answer is that no progress should be expected, I want the argument for that, and I want to know whether the residue — whether the pellet counts as a Darwinian individual, say — is a live question or one that only looks live because the term is underdetermined.
