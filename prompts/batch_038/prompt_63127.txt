I am a novelist, and the book I am composing is built out of two irreconcilable reconstructions of the same events: a death in a small town, the correspondence that preceded it, and three witnesses who cannot all be right. I have taken both of my reconstructions to be partly false, and in my notes I keep writing that one of them is nearer the truth than the other. I have begun to suspect that I am borrowing a phrase whose logic I cannot support, and I would like an essay that settles the matter rather than comforts me.

Let me fix what I mean, because the expression is used in my trade in an unrelated sense. I do not mean lifelikeness: not credibility of texture, not whether a scene reads as though it could have happened. I mean the relation that is supposed to hold between a false account of the world and the way the world actually was — a relation of degree, which is meant to survive the account's being false. That is the one I need, since both of my reconstructions are false and I still want to say that one of them is nearer the mark.

Here is the difficulty as I have been able to reconstruct it, and I would rather you correct my version than flatter it. The classical proposal measured an account's truthlikeness by setting what it gets right against what it gets wrong: its true consequences minus its false ones, where consequences are counted generously enough that any account turns out to say an enormous amount. The objection that undid it, as I understand it, was that once consequences are counted this way no false account can ever rank above a true one, and among true accounts nothing separates them except how much they risk. On that picture a single safe truism is nearer the whole truth than a rich, largely accurate, partly false reconstruction — which is exactly the comparison my book depends upon, and exactly the comparison the definition forbids. If I have misstated the proposal or the objection, say so plainly and put it right before you go on.

The cases that matter to me are these. Please treat them as data and test whatever account you favour against each one.

1. Account A makes one hundred substantive claims about the events, ninety-five of them true. Account B makes ten claims, all of them true, and all ten are among A's ninety-five. A therefore contains every one of B's successes, eighty-five further successes, and five errors.

2. Accounts A and C each make one hundred claims and each is wrong on exactly five. A's five errors are idle details — the colour of a door, the season of a journey. C's five errors concern the motive on which everything else in C's account turns, and four of the five are consequences of the fifth.

3. Accounts D and E each make one hundred claims, each is right on ninety and wrong on ten, and the two have the same distribution of errors across the same details. But D's falsehoods contradict one another as well as D's truths, so that D does not describe any possible course of events at all; E's falsehoods hang together, and E describes a coherent way things might have gone.

4. The same reconstruction is stated twice: once in a coarse vocabulary, in which it comes out wholly true, and once in a finer vocabulary, in which it acquires further commitments, some of them false. Nothing has been added to what it says about the world except precision.

5. An account of my events, mostly false, and an account of the distribution of a certain mineral on a body nobody has visited, also mostly false, matched claim for claim in length and in accuracy.

Against these, consider at least the following four repairs, and say for each whether it preserves the verdicts I want, at what cost, and on which case it fails if it fails: restricting the count to a fixed and finite set of questions an account answers and measuring distance within the space of complete answers to them; weighting true and false consequences by relevance rather than counting them; measuring nearness by how similar the situations an account leaves open are to the way things actually are; and abandoning the enterprise altogether in favour of ranking accounts by the expected accuracy of a believer who must act on them. If you think the last of these is a different target rather than a repair, argue that rather than assert it.

The essay I want should be roughly two thousand words and should reach a verdict, not a survey. Specifically: is truthlikeness a genuine comparative relation with determinate answers among false accounts, or is it a family of unrelated measures wearing one name, or is it a notion that should be retired in favour of something else? Identify which of the five cases does the decisive work and explain why the others cannot. Where you think my intuitions about the cases are unreliable — and I expect at least one of them is — say which and why, and show what follows for the verdict once that intuition is discarded. End without hedging: I do not want a closing paragraph that restates the difficulty and leaves the question open.

Finally, close with a short separate passage, no more than a paragraph, addressed to me as a working novelist rather than as a student of the subject: given the verdict you have reached, what am I entitled to mean when my narrator says that one false reconstruction of these events is closer to the truth than another — is that a claim with content, a claim with content only relative to some specification I owe the reader, or a rhetorical gesture I should stop making?

Argue from the cases and from the logic of the proposals. Do not append a reading list, do not cite or attribute anything, and do not lean on the authority of any name: if a position is worth holding, it should be reconstructible from what you have said.
