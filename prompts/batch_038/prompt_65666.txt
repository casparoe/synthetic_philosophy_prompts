I sit on the transportation review committee at a regional grocery distributor. Our standing charge this quarter was to compare our two contract carriers — I'll call them A and B — on on-time delivery, and to recommend where to place roughly 2,000 additional shipments a quarter that we're adding in the fall. Two of our analysts, Dana and Elias, presented the underlying numbers on Thursday and then deadlocked over what the numbers say; the meeting ran an hour over, our VP of supply chain left before it was resolved, and I have been asked to circulate a memo by Friday. I took the notes and I have reproduced their figures exactly, but I could not follow the second half of the argument, and I am not able to tell which of the two is confused. Everyone in the room agrees the data below are correct.

For the quarter overall, A made 1,000 shipments and hit its delivery window on 723 of them (72.3%), while B made 1,000 and hit on 870 (87.0%). Split by lane type, which is recorded on the customer's order before any carrier is assigned: on short regional lanes, A ran 100 shipments with 93 on time (93%) and B ran 900 with 810 (90%); on long cross-country lanes, A ran 900 with 630 on time (70%) and B ran 100 with 60 (60%). Split instead by the service flag in each carrier's own transportation-management system — a load is coded as relay-scheduled when that carrier's planners assign it to a team-drive or relay schedule to protect the window — A had 600 relay-scheduled shipments with 423 on time (70.5%) and 400 ordinary shipments with 300 on time (75%), while B had 400 relay-scheduled with 330 on time (82.5%) and 600 ordinary with 540 on time (90%).

Their exchange ran roughly as follows. (1) Dana opened with the overall figures and said the recommendation writes itself: B is ahead by nearly fifteen points. (2) Elias said the overall figures are precisely the wrong thing to look at — this is a Simpson's reversal, A is ahead on short lanes and ahead on long lanes, and the aggregate favors B only because we happened to hand A the harder mix; the honest comparison is within lane type. (3) Dana replied in two parts: first, the committee's charge asks which carrier has the better on-time performance, and 87% is not an artifact or an illusion but a true fact about the book of business we actually gave B, whereas Elias's preferred comparison describes a hypothetical book that nobody operated; second, the within-lane superiority he is leaning on rests on a 100-shipment cell for A on short lanes and a 100-shipment cell for B on long lanes, and neither is sturdy enough to reallocate 2,000 shipments a quarter on. (4) Dana then produced the relay/ordinary split, in which B leads in both cells, and said this shows that "condition on the confounder" is not a procedure that delivers an answer at all — you can find a partition that flatters either carrier, so the only partition-independent summary is the aggregate, and Elias has simply picked the split that happens to favor A. (5) Elias said the two splits are not on a par: lane type is a property of the order, fixed before a carrier is assigned, whereas the relay flag is applied by the carrier's own planners during handling, so conditioning on it holds fixed a decision that is itself part of what distinguishes the carriers. Dana countered that A also chose which lanes to bid on, so lane mix is downstream of A's decisions too, which makes Elias's asymmetry look improvised. (6) Elias conceded the thin-cell point but said it argues for running a controlled pilot — deliberately putting B on a sample of long lanes and A on short ones — rather than for reporting the aggregate; he proposed standardizing both carriers to a common half-and-half lane mix, which yields 81.5% for A and 75% for B. Dana's final move was to ask why half-and-half is the right standard rather than the mix we actually ran, or the mix we are planning for the fall, and to say that if the ranking changes with the choice of standard, Elias has not escaped the arbitrariness he accused her of but has only relocated it.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate: rate each of the six numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, and do the arithmetic yourself where it matters — including checking whether Elias's claimed within-lane superiority is actually supported at the sample sizes involved, and whether Dana's alternative split really is comparable in evidential weight — instead of taking either analyst's word for it. Then tell me who is more right overall about what the committee should report and recommend, whether either of them is entitled to the conclusion they end up at, and what important consideration neither of them raised.
