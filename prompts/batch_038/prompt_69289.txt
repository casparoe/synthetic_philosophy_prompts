You are a professor of moral philosophy who works on social norms, on the boundary between law and convention, and on the sources of obligation. Please treat what follows as a conceptual reconstruction problem rather than a policy debate, and think it through carefully before answering.

Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of two hours and got nowhere, and I would like a better analysis than either of us managed. The trigger was trivial. I bought a coffee at a counter where nothing was done for me beyond the handing over of a cup I had ordered and paid for at a touchscreen. That same screen offered three preset gratuity amounts — 20, 25 and 30 per cent — with the option to decline placed last, requiring its own separate and deliberate tap, and angled so that the person who had made the drink could see it. I declined. Ten minutes later I crossed a street against a red signal at an empty intersection, in my friend's full view, and neither of us registered anything at all.

A third case arrived midway through the argument, when my friend pointed out that in the laundromat downstairs a stranger's jacket on a chair holds a machine absolutely: people wait, they do not move it, and disputes are settled by appeal to it. What struck me afterwards is that the jacket case is usually anonymous and one-shot — a food court table, a beach towel line, a lawn at a free concert, among people who will never meet the owner again — so neither reputation nor reciprocation can obviously be doing the work. The coffee case is also one-shot, but it has an immediate audience. The crossing case typically has neither audience nor repetition, and no one is harmed. Meanwhile the three are sorted in opposite directions in ordinary judgment: the declined gratuity feels like a small moral failure even to people who think the underlying wage structure is unjust, the jacket feels genuinely binding, and the empty-crossing violation feels like nothing — even though it is the only one of the three that is actually illegal in most places. And the whole configuration flips sign at a border: in some countries the very gesture I refused to make is declined back, and can give offence.

My friend's position, roughly, is that all three are coordination devices wearing a moralized shell — that nothing is owed in any of them, and that the guilt is manufactured by interface design and by public visibility. My counter-intuition, which I could not defend, is that the jacket case feels binding even when nobody is watching and even to a person who has no stake in the practice whatsoever. I also noticed something I could not explain: third parties get indignant about a stranger's stinginess toward a server who is visibly unbothered by it, which suggests the complaint is not being made on the worker's behalf at all.

Your deliverable is the implicit theory, not a verdict on tipping. Please address, in this order:

1. Reconstruct the theory of obligation that these practices jointly presuppose — or, if they do not share one, reconstruct each separately. In particular: what has to be true about normativity for a unilateral act performed among non-consenting strangers to bind them; for a preset default that must be actively refused to count as an entitlement rather than a mere psychological nudge; and for an obligation to survive the indifference of the person it supposedly runs to? Can a design choice create an obligation, or only the appearance of one?

2. Separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Test the pattern against a harm-based account, a consent-or-expectation account, a reciprocity or fair-play account, a rule-consequentialist account, and an account on which norms are not obligations at all but signals of group membership and deference. Say which survive and which are killed, and say why.

3. Audit the consistency. Is the sorting of these cases predictable from a single theory, or is it better predicted by non-moral variables — presence of an audience, prospect of repeat interaction, receipt of a benefit, legal backing, visibility of the refusal? Tell me which variables actually track the pattern, and whether my description of the cases is loading the dice.

4. Take up the outsider directly. What does the expectation that visitors fall in line presuppose about the scope of obligation: territorial, participatory, relational, or expectation-based? Handle the hard version — the one-time stranger who will never return, has received no benefit from any cooperative scheme, and whose refusal nobody will ever learn of. What is left for a fairness-based account to bite on?

5. Describe concretely what these practices would look like under the rival theory you judge strongest, and then say what we should learn from the fact that some of those replacements already exist — gratuity folded into posted prices as a mandatory service charge, numbered ticket dispensers instead of jackets on chairs, decriminalization of crossing against a signal — and from the observation that where the mechanism arrives, the moral pressure simply evaporates rather than relocating.

Begin with a short planning stage, kept terse, in which you list the candidate accounts and mark for each which of the three cases it can and cannot accommodate; then write the main analysis. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words of analysis after the planning stage, with clear headings. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific works, and do not invent any; use a theorist's name only where the label does real argumentative work. Where you are unsure whether something is a strict presupposition or merely a comfortable fit, say so explicitly and mark the practice as underdetermined rather than forcing a single answer.
