So my committee wants a prospectus for the personal-identity chapter of my dissertation by Friday and all I really have is a nagging feeling about an analogy. Roughly 2,500 words, one chapter's worth, no more.

The feeling: Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity gets used all the time as a way of saying the self is a harmless abstraction — no substance, but perfectly respectable, same as a center of gravity in physics, and the analogy is supposed to make eliminativism feel unscary. But I can't find anyone asking what actually makes the center-of-gravity posit harmless, and I think the answer is a short list of conditions that a narrative self might not meet. Center of gravity is (i) a function of the mass distribution, so once you fix the distribution there's no further fact to settle; (ii) worth keeping anyway because it compresses a huge amount of prediction; and (iii) dispensable in principle with no loss of empirical content. My worry is that the narrative self fails (i), and fails it interestingly: the same psychological and behavioral facts admit lots of different emplottings, and which one counts as the self seems to depend on who's narrating and what they're narrating for. Underdetermined in a way a center of gravity just isn't.

So here's the question I want the prospectus to sharpen into something with a determinate answer. If the narrative self is underdetermined by the base facts, which of these three is it?

(a) Still a benign abstraction, but the analogy is to the wrong thing — the right analogue is something like a coordinate choice or a free parameter in a model, where the underdetermination is a feature rather than a bug, and the theorist's job is to say what fixes the choice.
(b) Not a mere abstraction at all but a causally potent social object, closer to money or a border, because people act on their self-narratives and those actions change the facts the narrative was supposed to summarize — a feedback loop a center of gravity could never have.
(c) An abstraction that fails the harmlessness test outright, in which case the whole unscary-eliminativism move is doing unearned work and the analogy should be retired rather than refined.

What I need from you: the prospectus itself. State the question so it can be answered and not just gestured at. Then explain why the standing positions — the narrativists, the anti-narrativists who think selves need no stories, and the abstractionists who take the analogy at face value — each leave it open; say specifically what each of them has to say about the underdetermination and why that response isn't enough. Then the method: what I'd actually do, section by section, and why that method instead of the obvious alternatives, which as far as I can see are either a straight conceptual analysis of narrative or a tour of the empirical work on autobiographical memory and confabulation. Tell me if I'm wrong to want to rule those out — I'm not attached to it. Then say what a result would look like for each of (a), (b), (c), whether the three are really exhaustive or two of them collapse into each other, and what would count as the project failing or the question turning out to be malformed. Finally, be honest about scope: where this could balloon past one chapter and what I should cut first if it does.

Don't bother with a bibliography or a reference list, I'll handle citations myself — just name positions and arguments descriptively. And please write the whole thing in American English.
