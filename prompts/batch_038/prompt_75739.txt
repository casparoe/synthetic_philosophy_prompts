I'm in the second year of an MA in philosophy and this term I'm a teaching assistant on a second-year module in distributive justice. I run one seminar group of sixteen undergraduates, fifty minutes a week, mixed ability, two of them rather fond of argument for its own sake. Next week's topic is the distinction, familiar from Parfit, between valuing equality and giving priority to the worse off, together with the objection that the first of these commits you to saying something rather startling: that a change in which nobody gains and somebody loses can nonetheless be, in one respect, an improvement, simply because it narrows a gap. I want to hand essentially the whole fifty minutes over to an activity and keep my own contribution to a framing of two minutes at most, and I want the labels for the competing positions to arrive only at the very end, after the students have been caught out by their own recorded answers. I'm not asking for a lecture, a summary of the literature, or any references or suggested readings — I have all of that and I don't want you guessing at it. What I want is a design I can actually run on a Tuesday afternoon with a whiteboard, some index cards and a bag of sweets.

The activity has to be built around choice pairs, and I've sketched three that I'm not confident about, so a good part of what I need from you is a check on the arithmetic of the intuitions. The first is meant to drive a wedge between the two views: ten and ten against eight and thirty. A comparison of lives, so the equal option is the one the egalitarian must prefer, yet my back-of-envelope suggests that a prioritarian with a moderately concave weighting prefers the unequal option outright, since its total is so much larger and the fall in the minimum is so small, and only a weighting close to maximin sides with the equal option. Please work out where that crossover actually falls, tell me whether the case is robust or a hostage to my choice of numbers, and renumber it if you can make the divergence hold across a wide range of weightings — I'd rather the students hit it than have it dissolve under a clever undergrad with a calculator. The second is the pure case: two options that are identical for some people and differ only in that someone has less in the second, so that the second is more equal and better for nobody at all. The third is meant to isolate a threshold view from both: something like nine and nine against ten and two, where a sufficiency principle with the bar set at ten prefers the option with a worse minimum and a smaller total. If you can think of a fourth pair that isolates something these three miss — the difference between minding the gap and minding the absolute position of the person at the bottom, say — add it, but don't pad the session.

What I most want you to design, though, is the machinery, because a straight show of hands on these pairs would just be a poll in costume. Three features seem to me necessary and I'd like you to develop, repair or replace them. First, a two-column private ballot: before any discussion, each student marks which of the two options is better overall, and separately answers something that gets at whether the option has any good-making feature at all. The contrast between the columns is where the whole session lives — a student who prefers the leveled-down option overall has one view, a student who prefers it only on the second question has quite another, and the aggregate pattern across sixteen ballots is what we discuss afterwards. The difficulty, which I want you to solve rather than merely flag, is that any honest phrasing of the second question already contains the answer; asking whether an option is better "in one respect" or "in itself" is practically to state the objection. Find me a formulation that elicits the comparative verdict without tipping them off. Second, three conditions across which the same pairs can be compared: one where the students judge as spectators over anonymous lives, one where each has been dealt a sealed card identifying them with one of the people in the distribution and the sweets actually get divided accordingly, and one where they know the card will be dealt at random but it hasn't been yet. My own suspicion, which I'd like them to test on themselves rather than hear from me, is that a good deal of what presents as egalitarian conviction is nothing but dread of landing at the bottom; the design should make that suspicion checkable and should also let it fail, if it fails. Third, small deliberation under a constraint — committees of four required to reach unanimity within four minutes, with a coin flip deciding otherwise — because the interesting thing is hearing someone argue for a distribution that cannot help anybody, and hearing what they say when a committee-mate asks them exactly that. Give me the materials list, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the fifty minutes with an explicit note on what to cut if the second condition runs long, and an account of whether the sweets really get eaten. If you recommend promising real stakes and then not paying them out, say so plainly and tell me what that costs; I'd rather not end the term having taught sixteen people that their instructor bluffs.

Then the debrief, which should map specific moments in the session onto the parts of the distinction — which ballot column corresponds to which claim, what the shift between the spectator and the sealed-card conditions does and does not show, and how to introduce the names only once the students have already produced the phenomena. I want you to script the two responses I know are coming: the student who insists that saying an outcome is better in one respect while worse overall is entirely innocuous and that no objection has been made out, and the student who cheerfully accepts that leveling down is better all things considered and asks what's wrong with that. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — someone working out the point halfway through and playing to the reveal, the committees deadlocking two against two, the arithmetic of the sweets overwhelming the question, the two combative ones turning it into a contest — and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about what the exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in my own words: I suspect at least that anonymous one-shot token distributions cannot carry the weight of the complaint, made by those egalitarians who reject the whole distributive framing, that what is objectionable about inequality is the relations of domination and standing it produces rather than any pattern in the numbers, and I suspect also that a handful of sweets has none of the diminishing marginal utility that real welfare has, which quietly removes part of the empirical support for weighting the worse off more heavily. Say whether those are the right limits or whether there is a more serious one I've missed. One request about form: please write the whole thing to me as continuous prose, in the order I'd use it, the way you would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists of materials or timings folded into columns. I know that is an awkward way to receive an activity plan, but I'll be reading it on a train and marking it up, and I would far rather have four well-ordered paragraphs I can annotate than a structured handout.
