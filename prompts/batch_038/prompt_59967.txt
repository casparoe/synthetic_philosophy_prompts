I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and maybe twice a month somebody pitches me a variation on "isn't it spooky that math works so well?" — usually with a physicist's name attached and no actual argument in it. I want to be able to write back with a position, so I need an essay that takes a stand on whether the effectiveness of mathematics is evidence of anything at all.

Here's the wrinkle I'd like you to build the thing around. People seem to be lumping together at least three different phenomena: (1) a mathematical framework can be bent to describe data we already have; (2) a framework entails something nobody went looking for, and it turns up anyway; (3) structures invented with zero interest in application get drafted into physics decades later. My suspicion is that (1) is its own kind of survivorship bias — we only ever hear about the math that worked — and that (2) and (3) are the only ones that could possibly carry an inference. Related: the whole puzzle's force has to depend on a hit rate, i.e. what fraction of the mathematical inventory ever gets used, and I've never seen anybody try and count it.

So weigh the candidates — Platonism, the universe-is-literally-mathematical view, "we built math to fit the world so of course it fits," some kind of category confusion — and tell me which one survives, and whether your verdict flips depending on whether that ratio comes out high or low. Dont end on both-sides mush.

Two housekeeping things: no reading lists or citations, just the reasoning. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the verdict and which single step in your argument you're least sure about.
