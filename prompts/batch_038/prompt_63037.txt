I'm writing an essay on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day and I keep reaching for Sartre's bad faith, but I want to test whether the label actually earns its keep rather than doing automatic work. Stevens is the obvious case: a butler who devotes himself to the "dignity" of his role, insists that matters of consequence belong to Lord Darlington's judgment and not his own, and reaches what looks like an awakening only on the pier at Weymouth, where he resolves to get better at bantering. Three questions, and I'd like the essay to turn on the discriminations between them rather than on a general diagnosis.

First, how is Stevens unlike Sartre's café waiter, whose gestures are a shade too precise? The waiter knows he is playing at being a waiter; Stevens may genuinely believe he is one. Does that make him a purer case, or does it locate him under a different Sartrean heading — perhaps the spirit of seriousness, in which values are encountered as already installed in the world? Would that be a distinct failure or only a species of bad faith?

Second, is bad faith even the right structure here, or is Stevens better described through the Look: his whole project is another man's, so his fault is that he has let his freedom be alienated through Darlington's rather than that he deceives himself. The two readings imply different things about what he could have done, and about blame.

Third, where does the novel resist the Sartrean frame? Its attention to class, employment, and an era that made the great house look like the natural seat of judgment may mean that what Sartre calls facticity has swallowed nearly all transcendence. If so, is the charge of bad faith too cheap — or does the Weymouth scene, coming decades too late, refute the idea that we are always free to choose otherwise? Please end by saying what Ishiguro contributes to the debate that argument alone would not: something about the retrospective memoir form, the reader's knowledge outrunning the narrator's, or the fact that Stevens' final resolution is itself a return to the role.
