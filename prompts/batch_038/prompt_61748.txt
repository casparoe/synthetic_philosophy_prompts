A friend and I argued for most of a Sunday about Kripke's Wittgenstein and ended up deadlocked. Her position: the skeptical paradox gets its grip only from finitude and error — I have applied 'green' only finitely many times and could always misapply it, so my dispositions underdetermine whether I mean green or something grue-like; give me complete and exceptionless dispositions and the paradox loses its bite. Mine: the real problem is normativity, and a speaker with perfectly complete, perfectly regular dispositions can still lack a fact of the matter about what she means, because those dispositions can be masked by the very self-correcting stance that makes her a rule-follower at all.

I want the case I claim exists, built to order. Specifications: one paragraph, an ordinary realistic setting and an ordinary predicate — no quus-like coinages, no arithmetic gimmicks, no neurosurgeons or demons. The speaker applies the predicate on every occasion she meets, never abstaining and never erring, so that finitude and mistake are simply absent from the case. But her applications are held steady by a standing intention to retract any application she comes to judge a misapplication, so that whatever settles her uses settles them through her own view of what she means.

Then show me the design works: what the dispositionalist says about the case and exactly where the mask stops it; why the skeptic's challenge survives the removal of finitude; which single feature is doing the work, and what the argument looks like with that feature taken out; and finally her best reply — that the retracting intention is itself just another disposition, so the total dispositional profile still fixes the answer — together with a verdict on whether that reply rescues the account or merely restates the difficulty.
