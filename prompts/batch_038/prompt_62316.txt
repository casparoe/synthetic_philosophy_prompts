I teach a two-day workshop on deception and disclosure at a school of public policy; the participants are mostly mid-career government advisers, with a few investigative reporters and one or two former intelligence analysts in each cohort. The second session assigns Arendt's essay on the Pentagon Papers, and the seminar that follows it collapses into the same shared mood every year: everyone agrees that officials lie, that lying from inside an office is worse than lying in ordinary life, and that the public no longer believes anything anybody says. That agreement turns out, on inspection, to conceal at least five mutually incompatible positions, and the participants leave convinced that they have all been converted to something when in fact nothing has been settled. I want a diagnostic instrument to run at the top of that session: a decision tree of roughly nine to eleven questions that sorts each participant onto a named position about official falsehood. The response should begin directly with the first question of the tree, with no preface, no restatement of the task, and no commentary of any kind until the tree itself is finished.

Two constraints on the questions. First, every node must be a concrete situation followed by two or three verdicts a person might actually say out loud about it; no node may state a principle in the abstract, ask which view seems more plausible, or use the vocabulary of the positions in its stem — in particular, no question may contain the words for bullshit as a term of art, dirty hands, defactualization, noble lie, or anything else that gives away a leaf. Second, the branching structure should be built out of the logical space rather than out of a list of thinkers. The axes I care about are these: where the wrong of an official lie is located (in the false belief it plants in someone, in a duty breached toward the audience, in damage to a common stock of recorded facts, or nowhere that matters); whether the contingency of facts cuts in the liar's favour or against it — that is, whether the fact that things could have happened otherwise makes the liar more powerful than the truth-teller, because what is merely true can be replaced, or less powerful, because the liar is the one who has to keep track; whether deceiving oneself ranks above, below, or merely alongside deceiving others, and whether an official who has come to believe his own briefing has committed a worse wrong than a cynic who never did; whether one lie and a coordinated, staffed, budgeted campaign of lies differ in degree or in kind; whether a speaker who never once asserts anything false but simply drowns the record in competing accounts has done something different from lying; and whether machine-generated fabrication at negligible cost changes the analysis in kind or only raises the volume. At least two of the nodes should be built directly out of those middle claims, since the participants have just read them and will not recognize them in that form.

For the situations, draw at least three nodes from the following, and invent the rest: a national security estimate drafted backward from a conclusion its authors already held; a central bank official who answers a reporter's question about the timing of a rate decision with a strictly true and deliberately unhelpful non-denial; a health ministry that publishes real figures selected so that the trend they imply is false; a negotiated settlement whose central clause is described one way to each side's own public; a fabricated video of a candidate that the candidate's opponents decline to deny and the candidate cannot quite disprove; an intelligence service that leaks an accurate story in order to produce a belief it knows to be false.

The tree must terminate in named leaves, and the following must appear among them: Arendt's own position, on which organized lying aims less at getting lies believed than at destroying the audience's footing in what happened; a near-absolutist position on which the office-holder owes the public justification and cannot lie to those to whom he is accountable without destroying the relationship; a dirty-hands position on which a particular official lie can be the right thing to do and still leaves a real moral remainder on the liar rather than on the ledger; a paternalist or noble-lie position on which the governed are better off deceived about some things and the deceiver bears the cost knowingly; a position on which the central modern vice is indifference to whether anything is true, which is not the same state as intending to deceive; a strategic-ambiguity position on which deliberate imprecision in deterrence and diplomacy is not lying at all and so needs no special excuse; and a marketplace position on which the remedy for official falsehood is disclosure and counterspeech rather than any rule against the falsehood. You may add leaves if the space demands them, and you should mark any leaf that you expect nobody in the room to actually occupy. For each leaf give the name of the position, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, one sentence on what it cannot explain, and the exact path of answers that reaches it, so that I can run the instrument live off a single page.

After the tree, and only after it: identify at least three combinations of answers the space permits but no path reaches or no participant will hold, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the resulting view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored, and say what contemporary pressure might drive someone into it. Then flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate — I suspect at least the indifference-to-truth leaf and the Arendtian one will look identical on a questionnaire, and I suspect the strategic-ambiguity leaf may be reachable from several contradictory places — and for each such pair say whether some further question would distinguish them, or concede that nothing asked in forty minutes will. Do not quote Arendt or anyone else, do not cite, and do not attach dates, titles, or page numbers to anything; paraphrase the ideas instead, since I am checking the substance of the instrument rather than its sourcing.
