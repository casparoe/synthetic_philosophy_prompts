Simone Weil's little essay against political parties was written in London in 1943, months before she died — so before the French Fourth Republic even existed. Her claim, roughly: a party is a machine whose first and really only goal is its own growth; joining one means signing over your judgement; and collective passion then crowds out the slow, individual search for truth and justice. Her fix isn't a better-designed party system, it's no parties — just currents of opinion, with candidates allowed to say "I lean this way, but on this question I think otherwise."

Run the Fourth Republic through that. Salient facts: proportional party lists, coalition cabinets that fell every few months, an assembly that was close to impossible to dissolve, and the whole thing ending in 1958 when Algeria blew up and de Gaulle came back with a constitution that put a strong president above the parties instead of abolishing them.

I want it in both directions. Weil on the episode: which features does her account actually explain — the discipline, the coalition arithmatic, factions coming to matter more than the country's problems — and where does it misdescribe what happened? And where is she just silent: she never says who gets people onto a ballot, or how you assemble a government on Monday morning without a party to do it. Then the episode on Weil: does the collapse confirm her, or is it all explicable as bad constitutional engineering (proportional lists, no dissolution, loose confidence rules) with little to do with what parties are? The regime that replaced it kept parties and neutered them institutionally — is that a refutation, a partial vindication, or a change of subject?

Then tell me what would flip your verdict: which part of the episode or which counterargument would make you come down the other way, and where you think your own mapping of theory onto case is shakiest.
