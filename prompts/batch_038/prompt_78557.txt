Consider the following deliberately artificial case. A beetle species breeds in discrete, short-lived patches of fungus. Each patch is colonized at the start of a season by a random sample of adult females drawn from the previous season's globally mixed offspring pool, so patch-mates are on average no more closely related to one another than two individuals drawn at random from the population as a whole. Females vary in a heritable life-history trait: some convert fungal resource into offspring rapidly and in large clutches, others convert it slowly and in small clutches. Within any patch, the fast type increases in relative frequency, because its per-capita output is higher throughout the season. Patch composition nonetheless matters at the level of the patch: patches whose founders are predominantly fast types exhaust the fungus before the season ends and collapse, destroying their own brood, whereas slow-dominated and mixed patches run to term and contribute normally to the dispersing offspring pool. At season's end all adults die; the surviving offspring enter a common pool from which next season's patches are again drawn at random. There is consequently no patch genealogy: patches are not reproduced, and the composition of an existing patch does not, except by chance, predict the composition of any later patch.

It is standard to conclude that this case involves no genuine selection at the group level, on the grounds that although groups vary and groups differ in productivity, group-level variation is not heritable. I want to resist that verdict, and I would like you to make the case against it as strongly as you can.

The position to defend is this: group-level selection is occurring in the case as described, and the standard verdict depends on running together two distinct roles that heritability can play in an account of selection — a role as a condition on there being selection at a level at all, and a role as a condition on selection at that level having cumulative, adaptive consequences across generations. On the view I want defended, only the second role belongs to heritability. The requirement that group-level variation be transmitted is therefore a requirement on group-level adaptation, not on group-level selection, and its importation into the conditions for selection — as happens when the familiar tripartite schema of variation, heritability, and differential fitness is transposed from organisms to groups — is a category mistake rather than a discovery.

Your argument should accomplish at least the following. First, state precisely what it takes for there to be selection at a level, in a way that is not simply stipulated to favor the conclusion, and explain why patch-level differences in productivity in this case satisfy that characterization instead of merely supervening on, or serving as bookkeeping shorthand for, differences in individual fitness. Second, confront the objection that patch effects of this kind are better described as sorting, or as ecological background conditions on individual-level selection, rather than as selection at a higher level, and say what — if anything — is actually lost by that redescription. Third, address the worry that the position makes group-level selection implausibly cheap: state what the view is committed to saying about a variant of the case in which patch composition does persist across generations, and explain why conceding that such cases differ in their evolutionary consequences does not oblige you to concede that they differ in whether group-level selection occurs. Fourth, say whether your argument depends on any particular way of partitioning total evolutionary change into within-group and between-group components — for instance a covariance-style decomposition — and if it does, identify which partition it needs and why that partition is not question-begging here, given that the between-group term can be nonzero while the group-level response to selection is zero. Finally, construct the strongest objection you can to the position and either answer it or concede what it costs you.

Write this as a continuous argument rather than a list of considerations. I am not asking for citations or for a survey of who has held what; I am asking you to make the case.
