I serve as secretary — non-voting — to a nine-member board that certifies hospital pharmacy residency programs. The board clears six programs per afternoon, on paper ballots, and its members are part-time practitioners with no training in social choice; I have a ballot template, a laptop, and no software. Our bylaws have us vote separately on each of four published criteria (supervision ratio, didactic curriculum, preceptor qualifications, assessment outcomes) and then vote on certification, which requires all four findings favorable. Findings are published with the decision, and the appeal panel that reviews our work reads nothing but the minutes, so the chain of findings has to be consistent on its face. The chair votes only to break a tie. I want a procedure I can execute in the room, not a lecture on the theory underneath it.

Three situations from the last two cycles:

Alpha. Five members find the supervision ratio adequate, five find the curriculum adequate, five find the preceptor qualifications adequate — but they are three different fives, and no finding commands more than five. On certification itself, three vote yes. Aggregating premise by premise, the board certifies; taking the certification vote, it does not. I think it should certify: the published findings would say every criterion is met, and refusing a program that meets every criterion as we found it would not survive appeal. But I also think it matters that six of the nine, asked directly, want it refused, and I do not want the procedure to render that majority invisible.

Beta. A revocation, with three grounds available: falsified rotation records, inadequate preceptor supervision, curriculum below standard. Ground one beats ground two five to four; ground two beats ground three five to four; ground three beats ground one five to four. The bylaws list them in a fixed order and we vote in that order, taking amendments in turn, so a member who wants ground three can secure it by moving the order. I think a sequence should not be able to manufacture an outcome — and yet I think the board is entitled to rest a revocation on the ground it can defend most forcefully, and reordering is how a member presses for that ground.

Gamma. A numeric criterion: at least eighty percent of residents passing our assessment at the first attempt. Asked for individual estimates, members give 82, 82, 81, 80.5, 80.5, 79.5, 77, 77, 71. The median is 79.5, so the program fails; but on the finding as worded — does the program meet the threshold — five vote yes, so it passes, and on the certification vote it is certified. I think the board should have been permitted to hear the estimates and then re-take the finding, on which it would fail. I cannot say why I want a second chance here and not in Alpha.

Deliverable: exactly five numbered items, in the order I would execute them, with nothing before or after — no introduction, no summary, no closing caveat. Each item is one stage, two or three sentences at most: the question I put to the vote at that stage, the rule for aggregating it, and what I enter in the minutes. Where Alpha, Beta, or Gamma turns on that stage, end the item with what the stage does to that program. Abstentions and recusals must be disposed of somewhere within the five. One item must identify the point at which the procedure stops computing and hands the question back to the board as a matter of values rather than arithmetic, and one must name a kind of case it cannot settle at all. Between them, the five must also tell me which of my three verdicts they overrule.
