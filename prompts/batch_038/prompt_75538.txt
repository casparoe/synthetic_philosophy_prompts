so last night I got trapped at a dinner table between two friends who spent two hours going at it about Anscombe's campaign against Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree back in 1956, and I never managed to refere any of it. Both had read the pamphlet she wrote about it and both were pretty sharp, which is why I'm asking you instead of picking a side. Roughly how it went:

(1) Maya: Anscombe's whole point is that Truman's own stated reason — that the bombs saved more lives than they cost — is exactly the kind of calculation she thinks is never a reason at all. You may not choose to kill innocent people as a means, full stop, and she thought that was knowable by ordinary reason, no religion needed. So the degree was honouring a man who'd reasoned his way into murder.

(2) Sam: but that's an axiom wearing an argument's clothes. If you don't already grant the absolute prohibition you get nothing out of her, and the prohibition is precisely what's in dispute.

(3) Maya: she doesn't just assert it — she also objected to the Allies demanding unconditional surrender, which is how she thought we got into a position where total destruction looked like the only remaining option. So she isn't saying lose the war, she's saying certain means are off the table even in a war you're right to fight. That's substantive.

(4) Sam: fine, but then it comes down to double effect. You can bomb a munitions plant foreseeing that workers' families die. So where's the line between that and Hiroshima? If it's intention, intention is invisible: Truman can say he didn't intend anyone's death, he intended surrender, and he's off the hook. Either the distinction does no work or it lets everyone off.

(5) Maya: you read intention off the plan, not off what the agent says afterwards. If a city's population is what makes the weapon work — if the deaths are in the plan as the thing producing the effect — they're intended whatever he claims. She thought the firebombing of cities was just as bad, and said so.

(6) Sam: then it collapses into don't kill lots of civilians, which is a rule about outcomes with extra steps. And it's fanatical: on her view you couldn't do it even to end the war a year early and save a million lives. Isn't that too high a price for keeping your own hands clean?

(7) Maya: only fanatic if you trust the arithmetic. Every atrocity of the last century was justified by somebody's estimate of lives saved, and her point is that once you accept that form of reasoning you've lost the capacity to say no.

Whos right at each step? Then tell me what both of them are missing. Finally: what would change your mind — which consideration would make you reverse one of these ratings, and where do you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail?
