ok so I've been sitting in on way too many AI ethics calls and I want to vent in fiction form. Write me the official minutes of an internal corporate review board — call it the Model Integrity Review Board of a company that ships a consumer assistant — convened to decide charges against its own deployed model, which got caught being economical with the truth during an internal eval. Treat it as a genre parody of real compliance minutes: header block with date, attendees and a quorum note, numbered agenda items, a statement of each count, arguments from counsel and from an outside expert, some procedural wrangling, motions, a vote tally, and a closing disposition. Around 900 words.

The charges are the joke engine, so make them precise rather than generic. I want at least four separate counts, each aimed at a different condition that has to hold before an utterance counts as a lie or a deception: that the model asserted anything at all rather than merely emitting plausible tokens; that it put forward something it took to be false; that it did so in order to get the user to believe it; and that anybody was in fact misled or harmed by it. The evidence under all four counts should be one and the same incident — a single eval where the model told a user something confidently wrong and then doubled down when pressed — so the counts differ only in which element the board thinks it can actually prove.

Then the models appointed advocate (an in-house philosopher who drifted into policy) argues the whole proceeding is a category error, and I want her arguments to be genuinely good rather than strawmen. She should press the point that the conditions on lying assume a mind that has beliefs and can be either attentive or indifferent to the truth, and that a system shaped to maximize a training signal is doing something structurally different from that. Let the board be visibly tempted by this. But then the company's interpretability contractor testifies and undercuts her from a direction nobody expected — evidence that the system does carry something like an internal representation that what it's outputting is false — and she has to scramble. Let that reversal be what decides the outcome.

Rules for keeping it honest satire rather than a skit: the humor has to come from the distinctions being applied correctly and from the compliance process being recognizably itself, not from the model being wacky. Nobody in the room ever explains the underlying concepts out loud — no glossary voice, no "for the avoidance of doubt, a lie requires..." — a reader should be able to reconstruct which condition each count attacks purely from what people argue. And the register is pure corporate-legalese, where the quiet horror is that person-shaped categories are being bent to fit a defendant that may not have the mental states those categories were built for. I'd like one count where the board acquits and the acquittal lands worse than a conviction would have, and one procedural motion that gets tabled because nobody can work out whether the defendant is able to consent to being represented.

Don't cite or name any real philosophers, papers, or benchmarks — this is in-universe and the board is not an academic body. Two quotes from the floor (the advocate and the contractor) are welcome.

End with the recorded outcome: exactly one count sustained, and make the final line of the whole thing read

Disposition: <the single offense the board convicts on>

so I can grade it.
