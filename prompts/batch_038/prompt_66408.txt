I run a monthly reading group and we've been going back and forth for two sessions on when someone loses the right to blame another person — you know the territory, the cases where the accuser is guilty of the exact same thing and people argue about whether that changes anything, or only changes who gets to say it. The argument has gotten abstract and stuck, and I think a story would loosen it up better than another round of positions. So: could you write me a short story, maybe 1,400 to 1,800 words, that stages the problem instead of explaining it?

Here's the situation I'd like you to build, feel free to change any of the furniture: a small housing co-op, forty-ish units, self-managed, and the reserve fund is short by an amount that matters. A longtime board member — call him whoever you want — has gone through the landscaping invoices and found them padded, and the padding runs to another board member he's known for fifteen years. He's right. He's also been doing the same thing himself with the snow-removal line for years, not quite as much, and no one else knows yet.

The thing I want the story to actually wrestle with is the two ways his own conduct could matter, which our group kept sliding between without noticing:

- On one reading, his guilt is purely about the messenger. The charge stands, it's true, it's provable from the invoices, and the only problem is that he's the wrong person to bring it — the wrongdoer still owes an answer, it's just that this mouth can't say it. On that reading what's needed is a clean accuser, and the same sentence spoken by someone else would do all the work.
- On the other reading, the blame itself is the injury. For him to sit in that meeting and pronounce judgment is to treat the other one as beneath a standard he doesn't hold himself to, to take a benefit from a rule he's exempted himself from, and the accused is wronged by the pronouncement even if every word of it is accurate. On that reading, no clean accuser fixes anything, because what got damaged isn't the record.

Please make both readings available in the scenes and the dialogue rather than announced, and don't let the story pick a winner by having a wise character give a speech — I want the group to argue afterwards. But do give it an ending that forces the question: have someone else at the meeting, someone with clean hands, say the identical accusation out loud, so the reader has to notice what did or didn't change for the accused man in that moment. The narrator can be the secretary, who's trying to run the thing by some simple rule about consistency and keeps finding the rule doesn't cover the case in front of her.

A few practical notes. American English, please. Keep it a story, not a parable with the moral visible — no character should sound like they've read about this, they should sound like tired people in a church basement with a spreadsheet. If you think my framing is off, say so plainly instead of just complying: for instance if you think the clean-accuser ending can't carry the weight I'm putting on it, or if you think the two readings I've sketched aren't really two readings and I've muddled them, tell me and then do whatever you think works better. And after the story, add a short note — a paragraph, no more — saying where you took liberties with the ideas or leaned on a contested way of drawing the distinction, so I can flag it to the group beforehand.
