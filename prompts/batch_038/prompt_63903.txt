I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size B2B software company (roughly nine hundred people, about three hundred of them in engineering), and for the last three years I've owned our internal people-analytics dashboards: headcount, attrition, on-call load, engagement-survey results, that kind of thing. After a brutal year in which we lost nineteen percent of engineering, leadership funded a company-wide program they called Sustainable Pace — no-meeting Wednesdays, a hard cap on consecutive on-call weeks, subsidized external coaching, and mandatory manager training on workload allocation — at about $2.1M a year. Attrition fell to eleven percent the following year. Now the program's two-year pilot term is up, and leadership has to decide whether to renew it, scale it back, or redirect the money elsewhere. I sit on the People Committee that will make the recommendation, mostly as the person who can actually query the data; the other members are our VP of People, the CFO, two directors, and an outside advisor. I don't have a statistics or philosophy background — I can read a scatter plot and I wrote the SQL, and that's the extent of it.

We brought in an analytics firm to evaluate the program, and their deck has become the center of the argument. Their headline claim is that disposition toward burnout is a stable trait with a heritability estimate around 0.7, drawn from large twin-registry studies, and that this means roughly seventy percent of the variation we observe between our own engineers is fixed and simply will not respond to program spending; the remainder is environmental but diffuse and hard to target. On that basis they recommend we stop funding a universal program and instead spend the money on a short dispositional screen at the hiring stage, selecting people who are unlikely to burn out in the first place. Two of their supporting points in particular I could not answer in the room. First, they argued that because our engineering organization is unusually uniform — same hardware, same tooling, same salary bands, same review calendar, same on-call system — the environmental variance in our population is unusually small, and therefore the 0.7 figure is, if anything, conservative when applied to us. Second, one of their slides showed a single horizontal bar split into two colors with the label "share of your resilience determined by genes: 70%." I raised both with my wife afterward, who teaches high-school biology; she said flatly that heritability doesn't work the way the deck implies and offered an analogy about genetically identical seeds planted in two different fields, but neither of us could articulate what the deck is actually getting wrong, or why their uniformity argument cuts the way they say it does. The vendor also dismissed our attrition improvement as an artifact of a cooling labor market rather than the program, which I'll admit is not a crazy thing to say.

The committee is currently split three-three, and my VP has asked me to circulate a written opinion before we meet on the fourteenth. Please draft that document for me: a decision memo the committee can act on, with a clear recommendation — renew as is, renew with specified modifications, or redirect the funds to selection — and a time frame and set of conditions attached to it. I need the reasoning in plain language that a CFO and an HR director will follow without footnotes or jargon, but I also need the technical part handled precisely: where the vendor's numbers and slides don't mean what they claim, say exactly what those quantities do mean, what would have to be true for their inference to go through, and what evidence we would actually need in order to choose between renewing the program and screening candidates instead. And please give their side a fair hearing rather than a takedown — the strongest version of the case that selection beats universal intervention, and an honest statement of what our own attrition data can and cannot show given the labor-market confound. Two pages is about right; I'll send it under my name to people who will assume I know more statistics than I do.
