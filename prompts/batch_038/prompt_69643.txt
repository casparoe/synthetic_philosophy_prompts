I chair a small working group that has spent the past year trying to agree on criteria for when outside military force may be used to stop mass killing, and I have come to distrust the criteria we keep producing, because my own verdicts on the historical cases will not line up with them. So I want to start from the verdicts and state them as flatly as I can. NATO's 1999 bombing campaign over Kosovo, undertaken without Security Council authorization and against an expected veto: permissible, and the review commission Sweden convened afterwards was right to judge it contrary to the Charter yet justified. The 2011 Libya operation: permissible as authorized up to the point where protecting civilians shaded into hunting Gaddafi, after which it became impermissible — though I also think that slide was foreseeable in March 2011, which may make the whole thing impermissible from the start, and I cannot decide between the two readings. The 2003 invasion of Iraq: impermissible, and the fact that Saddam's regime had gassed the Kurds at Halabja fifteen years earlier does not retroactively supply a cause. Non-intervention in Rwanda in 1994: a grave wrong, the worst entry in the set. France's Operation Turquoise the same summer, authorized under Resolution 929 and run as a safe zone in the southwest: wrong, even though it saved lives inside the zone, because it handed the génocidaires a corridor into Zaire. Vietnam's invasion of Cambodia at the end of 1978 and its overthrow of the Khmer Rouge: right, and I will defend it against the General Assembly vote of November 1979 that left the Khmer Rouge holding Cambodia's UN seat. India's intervention in East Pakistan in December 1971: right. The Australian-led INTERFET in East Timor in 1999, authorized and conducted with Jakarta's grudging consent: right, and the consent matters a great deal to me, though I cannot articulate why it should matter more than the killing it stopped. UN and French action in Côte d'Ivoire in April 2011, which settled a disputed presidency by force: right, though I concede this is regime change wearing a mandate. The 2018 missile strikes on Syria after the attack on Douma: permissible and pointless, and I am uneasy that my framework has no category for an act that is both. The decision not to strike in 2013: also right. Somalia in 1992: right when it went in, and I refuse to score the operation against what happened in Mogadishu a year later.

That refusal is where I suspect the trouble lies, because I do score Libya against its aftermath and I do score Turquoise against the wars that followed in Congo. My tentative principle — I have been calling it the standing-successor test, and I want it attacked rather than flattered — has three clauses. Cause: force requires killing that is ongoing or imminent and traceable to a government, or to armed groups the government cannot or will not restrain; the general vileness of a regime is not itself a cause. Ceiling: the force must be capped at stopping the killing, and anything aimed at removing the government exceeds the cap unless a successor is identifiable in advance, has genuine domestic standing, and the intervener commits publicly to handing power to it on a stated timetable. Custody: where no such successor exists, the intervener must either stop short of toppling the regime or take responsibility for governing, and if it governs, the operation is judged by how the governance turns out. The test is meant to explain why Cambodia passes (the killing stopped, and the government Hanoi installed in Phnom Penh in January 1979 did in fact govern) and why Iraq fails (no successor with real standing, and the intervener governed disastrously). But on my own clauses Cambodia should fail — that government was a foreign creation that most of the world refused to recognize for a decade — and Iraq should at least partly pass, since elections followed within two years. Either I have the verdicts right and the reasoning backwards, or the clauses are wrong.

What I want back is the principle, not a survey. Give me one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough that a colleague could apply them without consulting me, clause by clause, and for each clause tell me which of my cases forced it in. Then say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, and for each one whether the honest move is to abandon the verdict or to purchase it with an additional clause, together with the price of that clause: I would rather have a spare principle that overturns two of my verdicts than a baroque one that rescues all ten, but I want the cost of the sparseness stated rather than assumed. Address the custody clause specifically, since it makes permissibility turn on how things go, which seems to render the same decision right or wrong on facts unavailable to the people making it; I cannot tell whether my Turquoise and Somalia verdicts are compatible with that, or with each other, and I would like to know which of them a consistent account would make me give up. Finally, apply the repaired principle to three cases I have not listed and give your own verdicts with reasons: (i) a democratically elected government begins bombing the cities of a region that has voted for secession, deaths run to a few thousand a month and are rising, the regional authority formally invites outside force, and a permanent member of the Security Council blocks any resolution because of an arms contract with the state in question — is force permissible, and does the invitation change the answer; (ii) the same operation, except that the intervener announces it will annex the border province it occupies as a security measure, does so, and the killing stops; (iii) the only state with the airlift and command capacity to halt an unfolding genocide is at that moment persecuting a minority of its own, yet offers to lead the coalition and would in fact stop the killing. If your principle returns a verdict on any of these that strikes you as wrong, treat that as a reason to revise it once more and show me the revision. I am not looking for references or a reading list; I know the literature and want the reasoning.
