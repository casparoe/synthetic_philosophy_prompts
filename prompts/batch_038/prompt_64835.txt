I am revising a paper on the design of collective decision procedures, and a reader has raised the following argument against my claim that manipulability results leave electoral practice largely intact. It is offered in support of replacing elections with sortition. I cannot accept its conclusion, yet I cannot confidently locate the defect, since each step reads as unobjectionable in isolation.

(1) A voting rule is strategy-proof exactly when reporting one's true preferences is a dominant strategy for every voter, whatever the others do.
(2) By the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, every deterministic voting rule that is onto and strategy-proof over the domain of all strict preference orderings of three or more alternatives is dictatorial.
(3) Hence every deterministic, onto, non-dictatorial rule of that kind admits some voter and some preference profile at which that voter gains by misreporting.
(4) Where a rule admits such a voter and profile, whether the rule's outcome tracks the voters' preferences depends in part on the voters' strategic choices rather than on their preferences alone.
(5) An outcome that depends in part on strategic choices rather than on preferences alone is not an outcome that reflects those preferences.
(6) So no deterministic, onto, non-dictatorial voting rule ever yields an outcome reflecting the voters' preferences.
(7) Therefore elections cannot express a popular will at all, and a procedure soliciting no strategic input — sortition, say — is strictly preferable where the aim is to track what people actually want.

I would rather abandon (7) than (3), which seems to be nothing more than the theorem's contrapositive. My unease is that (4) through (6) appear to condemn the replacements proposed in (7) just as thoroughly as they condemn elections.

Identify the step or steps at which the argument fails, say why each of them seemed acceptable, and state what it would cost to repair the argument rather than reject it — including what the standard escapes from the theorem (restricted domains, set-valued or randomized rules, weakened incentive notions) do and do not buy, and which repairs leave some version of (7) defensible. Throughout, distinguish what voting theorists treat as settled from what remains genuinely contested among them, particularly over the practical significance of manipulability; do not present a contested position as the consensus.
