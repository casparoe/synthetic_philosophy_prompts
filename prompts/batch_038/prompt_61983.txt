I build a test-triage tool for a large monorepo: when a build fails, it ranks candidate causes — a dependency bump, a chronically flaky integration test, an unrelated merge from another team — and prints a probability for each. Partly as a sanity check on whether the thing I'm building is even coherent, I wrote out an argument against it, and I have not been able to find the flaw, which bothers me more than the conclusion does. I have no formal philosophy training; I picked up "coherence" and "Dutch book" from an online course on probability and decision theory, so please say plainly if I have misused either term.

The argument. (1) A credence function is rational only if it is coherent, in the sense that there is no package of bets offered at odds matching those credences that guarantees the agent a net loss. (2) Coherence requires credence 1 in every logical truth and equal credences in logically equivalent claims; this strikes me as a consequence of (1) rather than an extra assumption I could simply opt out of, since a package that bets for and against the same claim at different prices is one the agent is guaranteed to lose on. (3) Now take a program P whose source I have read in full, a specification of its interpreter that I accept with credence 1, and an input n; the claim that P halts and prints "yes" on n is entailed by things I already fully believe. (4) So coherence requires my credence in that claim to be either 1 or 0, whichever it in fact is. (5) I cannot tell you which, and there is no empirical fact I am missing: I am not uncertain about the physical world, about which branch of some measurement I occupy, or about the reference of any term in the claim. Two escapes I already tried and lost. First, the deflation that logical uncertainty is really uncertainty about my own cognitive hardware, and hence ordinary empirical uncertainty — that cannot be right, because the same argument runs unchanged on whether the ten-to-the-thirtieth decimal digit of π is odd, a claim I do not know the answer to and which mentions neither me nor any machine. Second, conditioning explicitly on my own computational state — that fails too, because a bettor can offer me bets conditional on exactly the same information, and step (4) comes back intact. (6) Therefore my credence of 0.5 that P prints "yes" makes me exploitable purely for failing to carry out a long calculation, and, more generally, no bounded agent has coherent credences at all. (7) And the reasoning generalizes well past my program: my 0.9 that the build will pass after a one-line fix, a chess engine's 0.7 that White is better from a given position, and my 0.6 that a long arithmetic identity holds are equally incoherent, since each is entailed by facts fully believed by whoever holds them — so the only credences a finite agent can rationally have are ones about brute empirical contingency.

What I cannot accept is (6) and (7) together: that my triage tool is not merely difficult to build but conceptually impossible, and that everything probabilistic I do at work is incoherent except where I am literally ignorant about the world. If it comes to a choice, I would most like to keep (1) and (2), because I want to preserve the idea that credences can be right or wrong in the first place, and I would most like to lose (6). So: which step do you reject? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing remarks — the step you reject; why that step looked unobjectionable from inside the argument; what giving it up costs the rest of the Bayesian machinery I depend on; the second-best repair available to me and the price it carries; and a final verdict on whether the tool is conceptually incoherent or merely expensive.
