So, context: I'm a software engineer, I build clinical monitoring stuff, zero philosophy training. I hold the "lay member with technical expertise" seat on our hospital's organ donation committee, and next month they've given me 20 minutes plus Q&A to pitch something that I suspect is going to go badly.

The pitch: our controlled-DCD circulatory death determinations are, from where I sit, a stopwatch, a nurse eyeballing a monitor, and no audit trail. I want to instrument it. Concretely what I'm proposing:

1. Fuse three independent signals — arterial line waveform, pulse-ox pleth waveform, and a continuous EEG — all three flat, with artifact rejection, before the clock even starts.
2. Run the hands-off window as a hard interlock timer that can't be paused or overridden from the console. (Five minutes is what I've been told we do, though I've seen two mentioned somewhere — correct me if I've got the practice wrong.)
3. Log everything, including the withdrawal-of-support order and the team's do-not-resuscitate commitment, as a timestamped field in the record.
4. The part that's going to start a fight: I want to say out loud in the talk that the criterion we're actually applying is permanent cessation, not irreversible cessation. The patient is dead because circulation stopped and nobody is going to restart it — and "irreversible" read literally is false every time a code succeeds on somebody down the hall. My argument is that naming this makes the determination more defensible, not less, because right now we're leaning on a decision the team made and presenting it as a fact about the body. Better to own the decision and record it than to hide it in a word.

The room is about ten people and they are not one audience. Two transplant surgeons who basically only care about warm ischemia time and will hate anything that adds minutes. An ICU intensivist who does most of our withdrawals and will hear "a machine declares people dead." A neurologist who does our brain death exams and has very strong feelings about ancillary testing. An OPO nurse coordinator whose whole job is the family conversation. Hospital risk counsel, who is thinking about the statute. And one bioethicist — the only person in the room who's actually read the literature on this — who I strongly suspect thinks the dead donor rule is a convenient fiction. He'll be polite about it in front of the surgeons but he will absolutely take a shot at it.

I want the prep sheet, not a defense of my idea. So: the six to eight objections this specific room will actually raise, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them — the surgeons and the intensivist are not going to use the bioethicist's vocabulary, so don't write all eight in his voice. For each: how much damage it does, a reply I can deliver in under a minute of airtime, and a note on where my reply is soft, because I'd much rather find that out now than in the room. Then tell me what to concede in the first two minutes so I'm not spending the whole Q&A defending it, and which single reply has to land for this proposal to survive the meeting at all. And give me the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one that makes me wish I'd brought a different slide.
