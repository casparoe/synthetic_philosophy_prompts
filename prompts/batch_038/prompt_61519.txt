I am a sixth-form student entering my school's history essay prize, and I have arrived at a position I would like to be able to defend under questioning. I have not studied philosophy formally, so I will state the view as precisely as I can and ask you to be explicit about anything technical you introduce. I am not looking for reading recommendations or for names of authors who have said similar things; I want the view's exposure mapped out.

Here is the view. I hold a restricted form of the defence that is usually phrased as the claim that a person should be judged by the standards of their own time. My version is narrower and, I think, less often attacked directly. On my account, the question to ask about a historical agent is not whether their beliefs matched the moral truths that are now obvious to us, but whether they were appropriately responsive to the moral reasons that were actually available to them. Concretely, an agent is not blameworthy for holding or acting on some view V when three conditions are jointly satisfied: (a) V was the default in their epistemic environment, so that holding it required no special effort of justification; (b) there was no substantial body of testimony or argument against V that they could reasonably have been expected to attend to; and (c) they possessed a standing disposition to revise their moral views when such counter-evidence was put in front of them. The historical record should therefore be read primarily for evidence about (c) — how the person treated the dissenters who did in fact exist, whether they engaged or merely silenced them — rather than for evidence of agreement with us. I take this to preserve what is right about the familiar defence while blocking the obvious objection that it lets everyone off, since agents who failed condition (c) remain fully blameworthy.

What I want from you is an account of what would count against this view. Specifically:

First, give me the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether it is a counterexample, a consequence I am committed to and should find unacceptable, or a collapse of my view into one of its rivals. I am especially worried about the possibility that my view is not really a third position at all but a restatement of either the ordinary excuse of invincible ignorance or the claim that culpability attaches to character rather than to belief, and I would like to be told plainly if so.

Second, distinguish the objections a committed defender of my view would themselves concede as counting against it from the objections they would dismiss as resting on a misreading of the three conditions. I would rather know where my own side gives ground than be reassured.

Third, assess condition (c) honestly. Is it something the historical record can actually confirm or disconfirm, or can it always be rescued by reinterpreting silence, hostility, or inaction as consistent with the disposition after all? If the condition is unfalsifiable in practice, say so, and then tell me whether that is a genuine defect for a view of this kind — a view about moral appraisal rather than an empirical theory — or whether I am entitled to it anyway.

Fourth, tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not. My understanding is that it should sometimes praise people who held views we find abhorrent and sometimes blame people who held views we find relatively enlightened, and that it should make the possession of unusual moral insight look less praiseworthy than ordinary accounts allow. If those predictions are not what follows, correct me.

Fifth, identify the cases my framework cannot describe. I suspect these include the person who saw the objection clearly and said nothing, the person whose bad belief was a personal innovation rather than an inherited default, the person who shaped their own epistemic environment by controlling what others could read, and collective agents such as a trading company or a state. Test the view against a small number of familiar examples — a slaveholding founder who also wrote in favour of human equality, a wartime leader associated with a colonial famine, an early modern navigator whose voyages inaugurated conquest and dispossession — but argue from the structure of each case rather than from particular documents, speeches, or dates, since I cannot check them and would rather not build an essay on a detail you might be misremembering.

Finally, consider the strongest rival: the claim that certain wrongs, such as treating people as property, are manifest enough that no epistemic environment excuses failing to see them, so that the failure is one of character and therefore culpable rather than one of information. Say whether my condition (b) can absorb that claim or must deny it outright.

The thing I most fear being asked, and which I would like you to address directly rather than leave for last, is this: a person's private writings sometimes appear to show that they articulated the objection to themselves and then rejected it. If that is so, does my framework simply collapse into blaming them, and if it does, have I bought the plausibility of my view by surrendering the cases that mattered?
