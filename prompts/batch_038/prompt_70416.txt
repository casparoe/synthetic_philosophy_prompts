The question was worth 20 marks:

*In Part I of* The Theory of Moral Sentiments, *Smith insists that sympathy is not simply pity or compassion. Explain what he means by sympathy and how he thinks it works. Then explain the role of the impartial spectator in Smith's account of moral judgement, and say whether Smith's impartial spectator is just Hume's general point of view under a different name.*

Here's the answer, more or less verbatim (it was in-class, closed book, they'd read the whole of Parts I–III):

> Smith's starting point is that we can never have any direct experience of what another person feels, because our senses only ever carry us beyond our own person. So sympathy for him is an act of the imagination: we place ourselves in the other's situation, work out what we ourselves would feel there, and in doing so become "in some measure the same person with him." That's why he insists sympathy isn't just pity — pity and compassion are restricted to fellow-feeling with sorrow, while sympathy on his use covers any passion whatever, including joy, gratitude and resentment. He even holds that we sympathise with the dead, who obviously feel nothing at all, which shows the feeling is really our own imagined version of their situation rather than anything we pick up from them.
>
> Where Smith is closest to Hume is the mechanism. He admits that passions are sometimes transfused from one person to another instantly, just from the look on their face, before we know anything about what excited them — a smiling face is cheerful to everybody who sees it. That's the same contagion model Hume has in the *Treatise*, where sympathy works by the imagination turning our idea of another's feeling into the feeling itself, and Smith is basically applying it to morals. Because it works by contagion, sympathy tends to converge: people living together end up feeling alike, and that's how shared moral standards arise.
>
> Approval then comes from a match between two sets of feelings. When I imagine myself in the agent's place and the passion I come up with coincides with what he actually feels, I approve of it as proper; when it doesn't, I disapprove. Propriety is about whether the feeling fits its cause, and Smith keeps that seperate from merit, which is about whether the action does good — we approve of benevolent actions mainly because we sympathise with the happiness they bring about.
>
> The impartial spectator is this procedure turned back on the agent. We naturally want praise, but the actual people around us are bad judges: they're partial, misled by appearances, and we deceive ourselves. So we learn to see our own conduct through the eyes of a supposed spectator who is well-informed and detached, what Smith calls the man within the breast, and that's what conscience amounts to. It also lets Smith answer Mandeville: Mandeville said virtue is just vanity flattered, and Smith can reply that wanting to be praised and wanting to be praiseworthy are different desires, and only the second is moral.
>
> This spectator is meant to be universal, since it's basically human nature plus full information, so any rational person anywhere would reach the same verdicts. Smith does have a section on custom and fashion where he says habit distorts our judgements of propriety, but I take that to be about exceptions and corruptions of the faculty, not about its content changing.
