Please take on the role of a professional philosopher working in metaethics who has engaged seriously with the literature on evolutionary debunking arguments, rather than one who treats them as already refuted. Begin by brainstorming candidate defeaters as broadly as you can, and only then sort and assess them.

I am a retired metrologist — some forty years spent calibrating instruments — and have turned lately to philosophy. I have come to accept a particular version of the evolutionary debunking argument against non-naturalist moral realism, which I would call the bias-not-noise version. It runs as follows. In measurement, random error is tolerable: it averages out under replication and can be bounded from within the practice itself. Systematic bias is not. A directional offset cannot be detected by further measurements taken with the same instrument, because detecting it requires an independent reference standard. Natural selection, on this view, installs precisely such a bias in our normative judgment — a persistent push toward whatever enhanced reproductive success — while the realist is committed to a domain of normative truths that supplies no reference standard, since the truth of a moral proposition plays no causal role in the production of our belief in it. The realist therefore cannot bound the offset and cannot claim to know any normative proposition. Note that the argument requires only that the offset be non-negligible and non-canceling; it does not require the distortion to be large.

What would count against this position? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, and empirical developments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to flag which have arguably already occurred — in comparative psychology, anthropology, or the study of moral judgment. Specifically: does the self-undermining worry, that the argument debunks the very epistemic norms it invokes, actually bite here, or does the bias asymmetry answer it? Do the third-factor replies, which hold that fitness and moral truth were bound to align because survival and flourishing are themselves good, defeat the bias claim or merely relocate it? What does this position predict that a moral realist does not? And does it risk anything at all, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that a defect for an argument of this kind? Finally, distinguish which of these defeaters a committed proponent would themselves concede as counting against the view, and which they would dismiss, and say why.
