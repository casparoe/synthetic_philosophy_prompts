Draft an internal corporate policy document of about 600 words, in numbered sections with a definitions box and a short FAQ: the employee-handbook page for a new Above & Beyond Recognition Program that undertakes to make supererogatory conduct administratively measurable. The voice is a competent, faintly beleaguered HR policy writer who has never read moral philosophy and does not realize he is drafting his way into a paradox; the piece stays deadpan throughout, and every philosophical difficulty must surface only in the machinery of the policy, never in commentary.

Include as genuine policy content: an eligibility test requiring the act to go beyond duty, which forces the program to say how the duty baseline is set each quarter and what becomes of past awards when that baseline moves; a documentation rule for the cost the act imposed on the employee, plus a provision covering acts that cost nothing; an award tier whose own existence converts last year's heroics into this year's expectation, together with a containment clause; a grievance procedure for staff who allege that a colleague's award raised the standard for everyone; a section declining to recognize suberogatory conduct — acts that are bad but not prohibited — with the stated reason; an FAQ entry on whether accepting an award creates a contractual obligation to repeat the act; and a closing note recording that the quarterly budget has lapsed because, on the program's own definition, no eligible act occurred.

The target is the institutionalization of moral praise and the demand that the best always become the required — a practice and a style of discourse, not a named person or real company.
