Our dining hall's caterer places its weekly chicken order on Monday for the whole following week, in crates of 20 birds, and a meal uses about half a bird — so one crate covers roughly 40 meals. Two people in my environmental ethics seminar spent like forty minutes after class arguing about what that scheduling fact does to the inefficacy objection, and they've roped me in as the neutral party to sort it out. I'll try to lay their moves out in order as acurately as I can. Please play the role of a professional philosopher who works on collective harm and causal impotence: think it through carefully, and before you commit to any ratings, give me a couple of sentences of brainstorming about which theoretical fault lines this fight is actually tracking. Then no fence-sitting.

Nadia had decided to stop eating the chicken, and Colin told her it was pointless in a very specific sense: the crate count for that week was already locked on Monday, so the chance her tray changes how many birds get processed isn't 1-in-40, it's exactly zero.

(1) Nadia: so here's a case where the objection goes through clean — no expected-value escape hatch, because you can prove the marginal probability is zero instead of just small.

(2) Colin: fine, but that's an artifact of this case, not an answer to the general problem. Normally your meal has a 1-in-40 chance of triggering an extra crate; 1/40 × 20 = half a bird, which is exactly the half bird you ate. Expected harm equals actual harm, so there's nothing to escape.

(3) Nadia: real supply chains don't work like that. The caterer forecasts off months of sales data, so there's no sharp trigger — the response to one fewer tray gets smeared across thousands of purchases. The 1-in-40 is made up.

(4) Colin: smearing doesn't help you. If the response is gradual, your tray shifts the forecast by a tiny nonzero amount, which is the same conclusion as the lumpy version with a smaller number stapled on. You wanted zero and you argued yourself out of it.

(5) Nadia: okay, zero versus negligible, the practical upshot is the same, and I'd rather eat a rounding error than give up the principle. The principle is that causing harm requires making a difference, and if you can't point at a difference you made you haven't harmed anybody. Which is why the torturer-style cases don't scare me: a thousand people each adding an undetectable extra increment, the group makes a difference and no member does, and I'm perfectly happy saying the group harms the victim while no individual harms him.

(6) Colin: you can't have it both ways. The group only makes a difference in virtue of what the members do. The moment you let the group be a cause you've dropped "only individual difference-making counts," and you're on the collective-harm view — participating in a set that harms, whether or not your tray triggers a crate.

(7) Nadia: and I'll actually grant you something I think you're skipping. Even where the causal probability is provably zero, my choice isn't inert — it's evidence about what people like me do, and norms move by people watching each other. So there's a channel. It just isn't a causal one.

(8) Colin: which was my whole point. You just conceded the only thing that mattered.

Rate each of (1) through (8) on something like clearly mistaken / partly right / clearly right, with a line of justification for each, then tell me who's ahead overall and which single sub-question the whole dispute really turns on. Also — and this matters to me — say what would change your mind: which of these moves, if someone pushed you on it with a better argument, you'd end up rating the other way, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to break down.
