Last night a friend and I argued for four hours about Anatoly Fomenko's New Chronology and arrived, to our mutual irritation, at complete agreement about the verdict and complete disagreement about the reasoning that yields it. We both think the theory is pseudohistorical and its chronology false. What we could not settle is what makes it pseudohistorical rather than merely and ambitiously wrong, and we came to suspect the question is not academic, because our two accounts diverge on cases we had not yet reached.

His account is content-based: pseudohistory is marked by features of the claims themselves and of the inferential structure binding them to evidence. On his view the decisive feature of the New Chronology is that it can absorb any disconfirming result, since it treats the disconfirming instruments — radiocarbon sequences, dendrochronologies, conventional datings of astronomical records — as calibrated by the very chronology under dispute, and then adds a conspiracy among chronologists to explain the resulting pattern. On this account nothing reachable from outside the theory's frame could count against it.

Mine is practice-based: pseudohistory is marked by how an enterprise is socially organized and by how its proponents stand toward a community both competent and willing to correct them. On my view the decisive feature is not the argument's shape but a withdrawal from scrutiny — the fact that, after a period in which Fomenko and his collaborators published in refereed venues and were answered there, the enterprise migrated to mass-market books addressed to readers unable to check the mathematics, and now draws its credibility, revenue, and audience from a constituency that no refutation reaches.

Take the case as I have stipulated it. I do not want a survey of the theory, a reconstruction of its details, or references of any kind. I want the document he and I should have produced instead of the argument:

1. A numbered list of claims we can both sign. This should include claims about the New Chronology itself; about Newton's amended chronology of the ancient kingdoms, which revised the traditional dates of the Argonautic expedition, the Trojan War, and the founding of Rome and is substantially wrong without being pseudohistorical; and about Hardouin's seventeenth-century claim that most Greek and Roman texts were forged by monks, whose author, pressed for his reasons, said they would appear in a letter to be opened after his death, and no such letter was ever found among his papers.

2. A table of our residual disagreements, each formulated in wording its own proponent would accept as a statement of his position, and each also rendered in a neutral description under which the other could recognize it.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I suspect one is whether immunity to refutation is a property of a set of claims or a disposition of a community, and another is whether the conspiratorial element does independent work or is a downstream symptom of the audience structure.

4. For each crux, a case both of us would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please include at least these three: a lone researcher who advances a claim structurally indistinguishable from Fomenko's, is competently refuted, and abandons it; a credentialed and well-funded program inside an established discipline whose core assumptions are never permitted to be at risk, whose practitioners treat anomalies as contamination by default, and whose members are rewarded independently of accuracy; and the ancient-astronaut literature, where the case for logical immunization looks weaker and the case for audience capture looks stronger. For each, state which account classifies it as pseudohistorical, whether the two accounts part company, and whether the resulting classification is a cost or a feature of the account that delivers it.

5. An explicit statement of which portion of our dispute is verbal — whether we are in fact answering different questions, one about what is wrong with the reasoning and one about whom to trust — and what remains genuinely contested once that portion is subtracted.

The standard for the whole document is signability. No strawmen, no formulation that smuggles one account's assumptions into a claim the other is asked to endorse, and wherever a claim is shared only under a description, say which description each of us is signing.
