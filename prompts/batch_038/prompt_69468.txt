Last month I was elected to the board of a limited-equity housing cooperative of about 180 households, and within three weeks I was handed a memo that I have not been able to answer. Our bylaws contain an entrenched Article XI: it fixes one-member-one-vote and caps the resale price of a membership share so that the units remain permanently affordable. Article XII sets out the amendment procedure, under which any provision may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the membership. A faction that wishes to convert the building to market-rate ownership has circulated an argument, drafted by a retired attorney who lives here, to the effect that Article XI cannot possibly bind them. It has persuaded enough members that I need a diagnosis before the general meeting in six weeks.

The argument runs:

1. Every provision of the bylaws owes whatever force it has to Article XII, since that is the only clause authorizing anyone to change anything.
2. Article XII is itself a provision of the bylaws, and by its own terms applies to any provision; therefore it authorizes its own amendment or repeal.
3. Consider the first branch. If Article XII is repealed, the authority it confers goes with it; there is then no clause empowering anyone to legislate, and every later enactment is void.
4. If Article XII is instead exercised, then nothing in the bylaws stands above it, so the power it confers is unlimited as to content: it authorizes the repeal of Article XI, and equally the repeal of any clause purporting to forbid that repeal.
5. A rule that can be set aside by the very procedure it governs does not bind; it merely records what the current membership is willing to go along with.
6. Therefore there is no such thing as an entrenched provision, here or in any legal system: entrenchment names a fact about expectations rather than a constraint on authority, and Article XI is at most advice to future members.
7. So the conversion amendment is validly adoptable by a two-thirds vote, and objections to it are political rather than legal.

I cannot accept (6) or (7). I also notice that if (5) is granted, the same reasoning appears to take down the two-thirds threshold, the notice requirement, and indeed every rule the memo itself relies on, including the claim in (1) that Article XII is the source of authority; but I cannot tell whether that observation is an objection to the argument or merely my own discomfort. What I want is a diagnosis, not reassurance.

Which step or steps do you reject? For each candidate, explain why it looked unobjectionable to someone reading the memo quickly, what exactly does or does not license the move to the next step, and what it costs to give it up. In particular: if I insist that Article XI really does bind future members, which conception of the amending power am I thereby committed to — the continuing one, on which the power cannot be used to destroy itself, or the self-embracing one, on which it can repeal itself along with anything else — and what does each have to say about step 3, where the memo thinks it has me trapped? I would also like to know whether the answer changes if one holds, with Hart, that the ultimate criteria of validity in a system are not themselves validated by any further rule but simply exist as a matter of what officials and members do and accept. Does that view rescue Article XI, or bury it more thoroughly than the memo does?

Finally, suppose your diagnosis is right and the memo fails somewhere. Should I be content with that, or does the failure point to a drafting problem — something Article XI and Article XII ought to say explicitly, so that the argument cannot simply be reconstructed next year by someone more careful? For what it is worth, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (6); tell me plainly what keeping them costs.
