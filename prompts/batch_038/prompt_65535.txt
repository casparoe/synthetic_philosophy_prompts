Our family foundation's deed of gift contains a clause my grandfather's lawyers drafted: before the board may approve any grant justified by long-run impact rather than present need, it must pass a consequentiality finding — a recorded vote that the coming decade is more consequential for humanity's long run than any later decade in which the fund's principal, left invested, could instead be spent. Two trustees treat the finding as obviously satisfied; they invoke the idea that we are living at the hinge of history, with vast numbers of future lives at stake, a narrow window in which the worst risks can still be closed, and technologies whose trajectory remains unsettled. Three others believe the clause was a trap: no board can responsibly certify such a proposition on the evidence available to it, so the fund should confine itself to present-need giving indefinitely. In nine days we vote on a $40 million portfolio split between a pandemic-prevention program and a maternal-health program, both of which the staff have written up in long-run terms.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board in advance. It should reach a definite disposition of both proposals and fix the evidential standard the consequentiality finding ought to carry — what would have to be true, and knowable by trustees, for a yes vote to be anything other than bad faith — instead of surveying the arguments and leaving us to choose among them. State the strongest case for certifying the finding now and answer it directly. If your conclusion is that we cannot certify, say what becomes of the pandemic-prevention proposal on that footing and whether the clause should be amended at the next deed review.
