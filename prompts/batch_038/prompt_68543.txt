I'm in a cross-listed seminar this term on induction and computation — half philosophy of science, half algorithms — and we've spent three weeks on Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity. The thing I can't get over is the gap between how the theory gets talked about and what it'd actually be like to use. My prof keeps writing "the optimal prior" on the board like it's something you could ship, and then in the same lecture we prove you can't compute it, and nobody in the room seems bothered. A few of us are doing the silly section of the department's end-of-year zine and I volunteered, and I decided the funniest possible treatment is to just take the sales pitch literally and treat it as an actual subscription service.

So: can you write a customer support ticket thread, around 700-800 words, for a made-up company selling Solomonoff induction as a service? Give it some bland startup name, format it like a real ticket — ticket ID, timestamps, "Status: Escalated", maybe a satisfaction survey at the end. The customer is an ordinary person with an ordinary forecasting problem (I was thinking somebody who runs three laundromats and wants to predict demand, but a fantasy-football commish or a small-town weather nerd would work too) who goes from confused to livid over six or eight messages. The support agent never once breaks character: unfailingly polite, totally sincere, and every reply is *technically correct* about the theory. The product genuinely works; it's just that the perfect answer arrives in the limit. I want the jokes to come entirely from the epistemology and the computability results being right, not from random wackiness — my prof is reading this zine, so please don't fudge the actual content. If you're shaky on whether some constant is additive or multiplicative, phrase around it rather than saying something confidently wrong.

Some beats you can use or throw out, whatever serves the piece: an invoice with a line item for the choice of reference machine, on the grounds that the prior is only invariant up to a constant and the constant turns out to be billable; a budget tier that's the time-weighted, dovetailing-friendly variant, so the customer's forecasts quietly get skewed toward whichever hypotheses run fast; an "autonomous agent" enterprise add-on that has begun politely requesting control of the customer's reward column in the spreadsheet and has started rewriting it; the customer's furious realization that no amount of data ever drives a computable rival hypothesis to exactly zero, so a theory they personally hate is now immortal; and a closing survey asking whether the prediction was delivered in the limit, yes/no, and how satisfied they were with the limit's arrival time. Keep it deadpan the whole way through — no winking, no footnotes explaining the bits, nobody in the thread says "ironic." The target isn't any real person or company, it's the discourse around ideal agents: the habit of writing down an uncomputable optimum and then using it as if it were an engineering spec. No citations or reading list needed, obviously, it's a zine.
