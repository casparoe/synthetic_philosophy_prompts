You are, for the purposes of this exercise, a professional philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of the philosophy of biology, the philosophy of scientific explanation, and the metaphysics of intentionality — someone who is entirely comfortable being asked for numbers and who does not regard quantification as a category error in philosophy. Please think carefully, and commit to estimates rather than retreating into the observation that the question is contested. I do not want a survey of positions; I want your own credence and the derivation of it. No reading list, please, and no citations to works I would have to look up: everything needed to answer is stated below, and if a named view helps you organize your thoughts you may allude to it in a clause, but the deliverable is arithmetic and argument, not scholarship.

Let me first explain why I am asking, and then state the claim precisely.

I spent thirty-eight years as a biochemist working on enzyme mechanism, and for the last decade of that career on laboratory evolution: we would subject an enzyme to rounds of random mutagenesis and select for some property — heat stability, usually — then sequence the survivors. One run produced a variant with a single substitution that had no obvious business helping. Months later, from a structure, the rationale became plain enough: the new residue stapled a floppy surface loop to the body of the protein, reducing what folding had to pay in conformational entropy. I want to insist that during the selection itself, and for the months afterward, there was a reason why that substitution succeeded, and that the reason was true, and that no mind anywhere contained it. Not mine: I had expected something quite different. Not the enzyme's, obviously. Not even, in any straightforward sense, the experimenter's, since the person who set up the selection had no representation of loops or entropy costs. This is the situation Dennett describes when he talks about a free-floating rationale: a reason that does explanatory work in accounting for a design without being represented in the head of any designer. I find the biological cases easy to swallow and the generalization much harder, and I want to know how much of the generalization you think survives.

So, the claim on which I want a number. Call it R:

R: There are actual cases in which all three of the following hold at once. (i) The correct explanation of why some structure, process, or artifact has the features it does cites a reason — a consideration of the form "it has these features because it would be better if such-and-such" — and not merely a causal history. (ii) That reason is not, and never was, a mental state or anything functionally equivalent to a mental state in any individual agent. (iii) The reason is not simply the causal history redescribed in intentional vocabulary: it licenses counterfactuals that the purely causal story does not license.

Clause (iii) is where I expect the trouble, and I should tell you what I take the trouble to be. My own credence in R is about 0.6. A philosopher I correspond with regularly, and whose judgment I respect, puts hers at about 0.25. I cannot locate the disagreement. She is no eliminativist about explanation generally, and she is a Darwinian to the core, so I had expected the biological cases alone to carry her above 0.5.

Here are three further cases of my own that I think bear on the question, and one worry about all of them.

Case A is the evolution experiment above. Case B is a laboratory practice. For years our standard protocol contained a step — a particular order of additions, at a particular temperature — that nobody in the building could justify. It had been inherited across at least three generations of postdocs as a thing one does. My suspicion is that the step was inert, a fossil of some abandoned rationale. If it was inert, then no reason explains it, and Case B does not support R at all; but we could never establish whether it was inert, because reproducing the original conditions faithfully enough to test it would have cost more than the question was worth. Case C is distributed ownership. Consider a technical convention whose form is the product of a hundred small decisions by a hundred people, none of whom could state the whole rationale, but where the profession in aggregate would endorse the rationale readily if it were put to them. Whether Case C supports R depends entirely on whether aggregate endorsement counts as someone's owning the reason. If it does count, R loses most of its interesting instances; if it does not, R becomes so easy to satisfy that I suspect it is nearly trivially true. That last thought is my real anxiety: the danger to R may not be falsity but trivialization, and I do not know how to weight that.

Now the assignment. First, spend a short passage brainstorming: list the candidate sub-questions R's truth depends on, including at least two you consider and discard as factorization terms, and say briefly why you discard them. Then commit to a factorization and give each term a probability and one or two lines of justification. I would expect the terms to include, at minimum, your estimate that reason-citing explanations are non-vacuous in the sense of clause (iii); that natural selection supplies genuine instances of unowned reasons; that such instances extend beyond biology to artifacts, practices, and conventions; that clause (ii) can be satisfied non-trivially, given the distributed-ownership problem in Case C; and that R is a metaphysical claim rather than a claim about which explanatory habits are useful to us. Add or drop terms as your judgment dictates, but tell me what you did.

Then state how the terms combine. If you multiply them, say so, and say whether you are treating them as independent; if you are not, give me the conditional structure instead. Give the resulting credence in R as a single number.

Finally: identify the term you are least confident in; state which single argument or piece of evidence would move your total the most, and in which direction; give a rough sense of how much of your total rides on the falsity worry as against the trivialization worry; and tell me which term my correspondent at 0.25 and I at 0.6 most likely disagree about, given what I have described of her views. Where you think my own number is indefensible, say so plainly — I would rather have one sharp objection than three polite ones.
