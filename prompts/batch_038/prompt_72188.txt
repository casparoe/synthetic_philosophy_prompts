Our department is throwing a retirement dinner in June for a colleague I'll call Miriam — she's been here thirty-five years working on choice over time, and somehow I got volunteered to give the toast. Here's the awkward part. Miriam is known in a fairly small circle for a position that most people find either brave or silly: she doesn't think someone with cyclical preferences is automatically irrational. Her line is that the old money-pump story — you'd rather trade A for B, B for C, and C for A, there's a little handling fee each time, so you end up exactly where you started and poorer — only shows that *myopic* people can be pumped. Somebody who can see the whole sequence coming, she says, just refuses the first trade, and then nothing leaks out. Her critics answer that this is either empty (if she's ranking whole sequences, she has transitive preferences over sequences after all, so she's conceded the point) or it's a commitment device wearing a fancier hat. The position has a name, resolute choice, but I'd rather the toast didn't turn into a citation exersize — she's explicitly asked for no equations and no use of the word "utility," and I want to honor that.

The room is about forty people. Maybe twelve are decision theorists and economists, two of whom have published against her and will be grading every sentence; the rest are her husband, her two adult kids (one's an ICU nurse), a brother who farms, a pile of former students now in insurance and tech, and two emeritus professors who've gone hard of hearing. I'd like roughly four minutes read aloud, built around one concrete pump that a nurse and a farmer can follow in real time. Her own version, used in intro class for decades, involved a canoe, a kayak and a bicycle plus a broker's commission — and the canoe is genuinely still sitting in her garage, so her kids will clock that immediately. I'd like to use it, unless you think something else carries the leak better, in which case invent a third option and tell me why you swapped. The mechanics need to be laid out clearly enough that people can watch the money go round the circle, but the toast must never announce "and the philosophical point is..." — the twelve in the know should hear, underneath the joke, that the whole fight is about whether you're allowed to demand she justify each step on its own or whether you can only ask about the sequence as a whole. The other hard constraint: it can sound like it's on her side, but it can't actually assert she's right, because she'd hate a retirement toast that picked a fight with two people sitting at the front table. The last minute should be about Miriam as a person — she kept the same office chair for thirty years, she would never revise a grade once its given, she drove the identical route to campus every single day for three decades — and it should quietly connect the stubbornness in her work to something true about how she lived, without stating that connection either.

After the toast, give me the honest bit: if you actually think resolute choice collapses into a commitment device and I'm asking you to defend something indefensible, say so plainly, and say which of the two objections above you take to be the stronger one. Tell me what would change your mind about it — which argument or example would flip you — and where you think the toast itself is most likely to misrepresent the theory or to be leaning on a contested reading of it that her critics would reject.
