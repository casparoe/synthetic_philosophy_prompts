For several years I have been reading manuscripts with a comparative cognition group, and there is a classificatory practice of theirs I have not been able to get into focus. When the group codes a vocalization as functionally referential, three findings are required. Production must be narrowly tied to one class of external event, so that the call is given to aerial predators and not to ground predators, or to a fruiting tree and not to a rival group. Receivers, including individuals with no prior exposure to the event, must respond with a differentiated pattern appropriate to it — scanning overhead and pressing into cover rather than climbing and looking down. And the differentiation must survive playback of the call on its own, with no sight of the caller and nothing else to go on, so that it cannot be attributed to graded arousal leaking into pitch or tempo. The same group also maintains a second list, invoked in other papers: production varies with who is present and whether they are attending, callers alternate gaze between the event and the audience, and they repeat or modify the call when the first attempt gets no response. Those are treated as evidence that the caller means to tell somebody something. And there is a standing exclusion that puzzles me most. A call that reliably tracks the caller's own state — hunger, distress, a wish for contact — and that produces differentiated, appropriate responses in receivers is coded as expressive or motivational, never referential, even though it appears to clear the production and response conditions just as easily. A dispute in the lab has made the tension concrete: a graduate student wants to code a food-associated call as referential because it satisfies all three tests, and her supervisor refuses, on the grounds that there is no audience effect and that the call is about the caller rather than about the food.

Tell me what theory of mental content this practice presupposes. Is it teleosemantic, so that the call means whatever it was selected or learned to indicate; a bare informational account, on which lawful covariance plus reliable uptake does all the work; or something frankly deflationary, where the vocabulary of reference is shorthand for a pattern of causes and responses and nobody supposes the monkey has a thought about the eagle? Distinguish what the coding rules strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, and in particular say whether any of the three tests forces the conclusion that the signaler represents anything at all. Then assess whether the first list can be combined with the audience-and-gaze list, or whether the group is equivocating, treating a causal coupling and a communicative intention as though they were evidence of the same thing; if they cannot be combined, say which should be dropped and how the food-call dispute resolves once the choice is made. I am most interested in the standing exclusion. Since a hunger call can be given when the caller is not hungry, misrepresentation looks available in the internal case as well, so I want to know what is really doing the work: a requirement that content be truth-evaluable, that the referent be publicly available to both parties, that the receiver stand to gain information it could not otherwise have, or nothing deeper than taxonomic habit. Finally, indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — which rival accounts of content would license exactly the same coding decisions — and what would have to change, in the criteria and in the design of playback studies alike, if the group adopted an interpretivist account on which content is fixed by the role a signal plays across an entire behavioral repertoire, or the more austere view on which these signals carry no content and merely cause responses.

Please answer in continuous prose, as one would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
