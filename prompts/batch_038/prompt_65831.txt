I'm on a hiring-analytics committee at a mid-size company and we got into an arguement at lunch that I still can't let go of. We were looking at promotion rates and found one of those textbook reversals: overall, men get promoted at a noticeably higher rate than women, but inside every single job family — engineering, sales, ops, support — women get promoted at a slightly *higher* rate than men. Our outside consultant said this is actually good news: once you hold job family constant the gap disappears and even flips, so there's no discrimination left to find, and the headline number is just an artifact of women being concentrated in families that promote less often overall. Everyone at the table nodded. I was the only one uneasy and I couldn't say why beyond "that feels like it's hiding something," which is not something you can bring to a meeting.

So here's what I want from you: argue that the consultant is wrong. Make the strongest case you can that breaking the numbers down by job family in this situation can *mask* discrimination instead of ruling it out, and that the aggregate reversal is sometimes the more informative number for the question we actually care about — which is "does this company treat equally good employees equally well?" I'm not looking for a hedge. Don't tell me it depends on the underlying structure and both readings can be legitimate; I know that's the standard line and I think it's doing way too much work here. Take a side. And in particular I want you to attack the piece everyone treated as neutral background: "women just happen to be concentrated in the job families that promote less." Why is that sentence itself suspicious rather than a fact we can condition on and move past?

Some details about our setup, since I think they matter. Nobody assigns people to a job family — they sort into it themselves, through internal transfers and which openings they apply for. For roughly a decade, moving into engineering from any other family required a sign-off from your current manager, a requirement that got dropped in 2016. Promotion rates within a family track pretty closely with how much revenue and headcount that family controls. And the overall split of men and women across families has been remarkably stable for about fifteen years, longer than any of the current managers have been in their seats. Use whatever conceptual machinery you think does the work, but keep it in plain English: I have to explain this to people who've never had a stats course past intro and who would glaze over at any jargon. No references or suggested reading, just the argument.
