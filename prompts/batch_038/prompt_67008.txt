I was moderating a festival panel last month — I cover science and ideas for a living — and my two panelists got into it over Galileo's tied-stones thought experiment, the one in the 1632 Dialogue where Salviati asks you to imagine a heavy stone that falls fast strapped to a light one that falls slow. Dev, a physicist, opened like this:

1. "No new fact goes in. You just notice the Aristotelian is committed both ways — the pair is heavier than either stone, so it should fall faster, but the light one should drag the heavy one down, so it should fall slower. Galileo never had to drop anything off a tower; he just showed the other side can't hold its own view together."

Ana, a historian of science, said: you can flatten the whole thing into numbered premises and two inference steps, and when you do, it turns out to be an ordinary argument with a picture wrapped around it — there's no special mental faculty doing the work.

2. Dev: "Sure, you can always rewrite it that way. That's not the interesting question. The interesting question is whether the picture gets you somewhere the list doesn't."

3. Ana: "Then look at which premises the picture is smuggling. The reductio only bites if you've already granted that a moving body is slowed by encountering something slower than itself. Deny Aristotle's weight-to-speed law and you can just as happily read the tied pair as speeding up, since it's heavier. Same setup, opposite conclusion. That's not a demonstration, it's a mirror."

4. Dev: "Fair — that premise isn't logic, it's physics. But Galileo had it from the inclined-plane work, so the thought experiment is just where the ramps cash out."

5. Ana: "Which concedes my point. If everything the thought experiment delivers traces back to experiments plus deduction, there's nothing left to credit it with, and we should stop talking as if it's a source of knowledge."

Flag where you're genuinely unsure too, and say what both of them missed about what the tied stones are supposed to prove.
