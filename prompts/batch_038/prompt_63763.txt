Next month I'm presenting at a two-day workshop on agency in organizations — a mixed crowd of philosophers, legal theorists, and a handful of people from business schools — and the organizers have asked each participant to circulate in advance a one-page list of the commitments they are prepared to defend, so that the sessions can be spent attacking the list rather than summarizing positions. I also sit on the seven-person board of a housing cooperative, which is where most of my intuitions in this area come from, and I've tried to write the list honestly rather than diplomatically. Before I send it I'd like a careful consistency check, because I have a nagging sense that several of these items do not belong in the same document, and I can't tell which ones are doing the damage.

1. I'm a reductionist about collective attitudes in the Bratman style. When it is true that some people intend to do something together, that truth is made true entirely by facts about the personal attitudes of those people: interlocking intentions directed at our acting together, mutual responsiveness, and a commitment to meshing sub-plans. I don't think we need, and I don't think there is, an irreducible we-mode ingredient in the mind; talk of a plural subject strikes me as a façon de parler for a pattern of individual psychology.

2. Nonetheless I think organized collectives are agents in their own right. A board, a firm, or a municipality can hold beliefs and intentions that no member holds, can revise them by its own procedures, and can retain them through the replacement of every last member. What fixes such a collective's attitudes, on my view, is its decision procedure together with its records — minutes, standing policies, previously ratified resolutions — and not any snapshot of what is currently in its members' heads. I don't see this as in conflict with item 1, since item 1 is an account of people acting together and item 2 is an account of structured organizations.

3. Here is the case that motivates item 2. Last spring our board had to decide whether to accept a municipal grant conditioned on a set of occupancy rules. Following our standing procedure, we voted separately on three questions: whether the conditions were acceptable in principle, whether we could realistically comply with them, and whether to accept the grant. Each of the first two carried five votes to two; the motion to accept failed four to three. Every member's own three votes were entirely coherent — each of the four who voted against accepting had voted in favor of one of the two premises and against the other. My judgment is that the cooperative was committed to accepting the grant by the deliverances of its own procedure, that it is open to criticism for the resulting incoherence, and that the criticism lands on the cooperative rather than on any member.

4. For the small-scale case I'm a flat individualist. When our house runs its annual workday or agrees to replace the roof, I'm content to say there is nothing there beyond interlocking personal intentions; item 1 explains it completely and I see no need to posit an entity. And when someone drops out after the plan has been settled, I think the others have standing to complain — but that standing comes, on my view, from the fact that in joining the plan the person licensed the others to rely on them, not from any commitment that is irreducibly joint.

5. I hold a supervenience constraint as a guard against letting group agency float free: two collectives whose members have exactly the same personal attitudes, in exactly the same interlocking pattern, cannot differ with respect to what they collectively believe or intend.

6. Blame and credit are not symmetric at the group level. An organization can be blameworthy for something no member intended, foresaw, or endorsed, because its procedures and its records can make the thing its own doing; but an organization can be credited for something only if at least one member actually endorsed it and was moved by it, since credit requires that somebody's motives be good, and motives exist only in people.

7. I deny group feeling altogether. No committee, firm, or state has a phenomenology; experience requires a single unified subject, and I don't see how one could be assembled out of many. At the same time I am perfectly happy to say that a landlord company is contemptuous of its tenants, that a firm's apology was performed rather than felt, and that such ascriptions are apt objects of moral assessment.

8. Methodologically, I prefer accounts that explain group talk by deriving it from independently available facts over accounts that buy explanatory power by enlarging the furniture, and I am especially suspicious of views on which a theory's own favorite entity turns out to be indispensable for making ordinary moral criticism of organizations work. That usually seems to me a sign that the moral work was being done by something else all along.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do, and rank the real tensions by severity instead of presenting them as a flat list of worries. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give — which item, or which part of it — and what the cheapest available revision costs me. I'd particularly like to know whether items 2, 5, and 6 can be held together without abandoning item 1, and whether item 3 and item 4 can be held together without my owing two different accounts of where the standing to criticize comes from; I would much rather repair the package than defect to a plural-subject view or to a view that takes we-intentions as primitive. Then tell me which single revision would eliminate the most trouble overall. Please don't simply announce that the list is inconsistent and stop there, and please don't hedge by flagging every pair as problematic — if some of these are compatible on inspection, I want to hear that as well, and why.
