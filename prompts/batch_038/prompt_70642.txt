I am in my final year of high school, and for an independent-study essay I have been teaching myself how the set-theoretic paradoxes are supposed to be resolved, working from a library copy of an introduction plus whatever I could follow of the logic. I think I understand the main move: you cannot simply gather up everything satisfying a condition into a set, because the condition of not containing itself gives you a contradiction; instead you may only pick out, from inside a set you already have, the members of it that satisfy the condition. That much I can state. What I cannot state is a second thing, a pattern I keep running into once the restriction is in place, and I want to know whether it has an established concept attached to it, or whether what I think I have noticed is really just something older that I have misdescribed to myself.

The pattern is this. Each paradox seems to be answered by a prohibition, and the only warrant I can ever find for the prohibition is that it blocks the paradox in question. Nothing in the theory warns me in advance which totalities are going to blow up; I find out afterwards, by having already derived the contradiction. So with the ordinals: if I try to line up all of them, I get Burali-Forti, an ordinal greater than every ordinal, and the reply is that the collection was too big — but the theory never told me it was too big before I tried. And the people who do offer a general criterion say something like: a collection is legitimate provided it is smaller than the whole universe of sets, which seems to me to presuppose the very thing we were trying to avoid treating as a finished object. Then, separately, every line that gets drawn appears to create something new standing on the far side of it. Rule out the set of all sets and you are left with a hierarchy built level by level, and now you can ask about the collection of all levels; rule that out and you can ask about the whole process. When I raised this with my teacher, the answer was that the honest position is that the universe of sets is never a completed thing, so asking for it is the mistake. But that response strikes me as making the original prohibition not a claim at all, and I cannot tell whether I have hold of a real criticism of the standard fix or of a confusion about what the fix was ever meant to say.

My friends have offered me two labels already, and neither seems right. One said it is simply moving the goalposts; another said it is like adding epicycles to rescue a theory that should have been abandoned. Both miss something: the restrictions are not obviously false, they generate a vast amount of mathematics that everyone agrees works, and no one claimed the theory was finished. What I am trying to pick out is narrower — a rule justified only by the construction it excludes, discovered after the fact, inside a theory that is otherwise doing well, combined with the fact that each such rule seems to define a larger collection out of which the next paradox can be built. So: is there an established term for this, either in the philosophy of mathematics or in the more general literature on how theories absorb counterexamples? If there are several near neighbours, please set each of them against what I have described — what it is, where my case falls outside it, and which of them fits best — and tell me plainly whether what I have noticed is a genuine objection or a mistake. If nothing fits, then coin something for it, with a definition, and give me a way of telling it apart from ordinary after-the-fact repair, including what a case that looks like this but is not one would be. I care much more about the boundary than about the word, because I need to know exactly what I am entitled to claim in my essay and exactly what I am not. Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, since I will be reading it at my desk and copying sentences into a draft.
