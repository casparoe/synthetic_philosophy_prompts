So — I write fiction, and Thursday nights I run a drop-in workshop out of the back room of a bookshop in my town: 14 people, wildly mixed levels (two are retired, one is 19 and writes fanfic, one has an MFA and won't shut up about craft), and I get two hours, 6:30 to 8:30. Lately I've been marking up other people's dialogue with two highlighters — green for a line where the character could take back the unstated bit without sounding like a liar, red for a line where the unstated bit is welded to the actual words so that any take-back reads as backpedaling. I've got a private name for the beat in between, the retraction seam, and a hunch that green lines make readers warm to a character while red lines make them distrust whoever's talking — but the hunch isn't what I need from you. What I need is for my workshop people to feel that difference in their own mouths before anyone explains it to them. No lecture up front about Grice or speaker meaning or cancelation or any of it; half of them have never heard of the guy and the other half would just recite him back at me.

Give me a runnable game for that room, those people, those two hours. Index cards and pens, no money, nothing to print beyond what I can photocopy at the shop. I want them writing lines and then trying to retract them out loud to a partner, and I want the asymmetry to fall out of the scoring rather than out of my mouth — so build in a second phase where you strip the shared context away from the same lines and let them watch which unstated claims survive that and which evaporate the moment the context goes. I want the actual rules, the roles, how points work, what happens on a tie, and a minute-by-minute timeline including the exact moment I'm finally allowed to say the word "implicature." If you can make the winner's prize something a fiction workshop would genuinely want rather than a sticker, do that.

Then the debrief, which is the part I care about most. Map what happened in each round onto the real machinery — what a sentence encodes versus what a speaker means by it, and the difference between an inference the surrounding context licenses and one a particular word drags in behind it whether or not the context asks for it — and tell me where my green/red system is going to misfire. Especially: handle the case where a retraction fails not because the meaning is welded in but because insisting would be rude, i.e. social face doing work that looks exactly like semantics; how do I seperate those two live in the room, and what do I ask to find out? Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I can guess a couple already — someone scoring for good prose instead of retractability, someone producing sarcasm or irony and blowing up the round, the MFA guy naming the theory in minute four — but I want the ones I won't see coming, with a cheap fix for each. And be frank about what the game simply cannot reproduce, the thing I'll have to supply in my own words at the end. My guess is it's something about the hearer's inference being a reconstruction of what it'd be reasonable to take the speaker to mean rather than a claim about how anyone's head actually works, plus the fact that assuming cooperation carries normative weight in a real conversation that a card game can't fake — but correct me if I'm worrying about the wrong feature.
