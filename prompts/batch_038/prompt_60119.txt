So I've talked myself into a slightly deflationary view of the Principal Principle and I want it properly stress tested before I defend it in a draft.

The view: PP isn't a substantive constraint on rationality that could have turned out false. It's what you get by combining (a) the mundane norm that says if you take someone to be better informed than you about how some outcome will go, you adopt their confidence in that outcome absent special reasons not to, with (b) the claim that objective chance is just whatever plays that role in the world. Chance is a job description rather than a thing, and PP is analytic on the job description. On this reading the usual headaches — the endless fight over what counts as admissible evidence, and the undermining worry people press after Thau and Hall, where the actual long run frequencies look like they bear on what the chances are — aren't counterexamples to PP at all. They're symptoms of treating PP as a requirement imposed on chance from the outside instead of something read off of what chance is.

What would count against this? I want a map of its exposure, not a defense of it. Specifically:

- which considerations are decisive and which are merely awkward, ranked fatal to cosmetic
- separate the damage a defender of this view would themselves concede from the stuff only a hostile critic presses
- am I insulated to the point of vacuity? can I describe any possible world where something is a genuine chance but doesn't deserve chance-level credence — and if I can't, is that a cost, given chance is supposed to be an a posteriori feature of the world
- the rival-expert problem: suppose we build a device that prints a number for any process we feed it, and the outcomes match up perfectly over time, but we then discover its outputs are caused by a deterministic simulation reading off the future. am I committed to calling its readouts chances? and if a theory posits 0.3 for a decay while frequencies converge on 0.4, do I have to say the theory misidentified the chance, or that there never was one, and does the difference matter?
- accessibility: does my view rule out chances nobody could ever identify — one-shot unrepeatable events, early-universe processes, anything behind an epistemic horizon? is losing those a real loss or just bookkeeping?
- collapse: does this end up being subjectivism about chance with a hat on it, chance as some idealized agent's credence, in which case I've stopped talking about objective chance altogether?
- Everett stress test: if PP is analytic, does the problem of deriving the Born rule dissolve by stipulation, and would dissolving that easily be a reductio on me rather than a win?
- what does my view predict that a realist-about-chance rival doesn't — anything, even in principle, that could be checked

What would most move me personally is one concrete, non-ad-hoc case where an agent who satisfies every ordinary deference norm and knows the chance should still not match her credence to it. Not the Big Bad, I already think I can absorb that.

And end with a blunt verdict: have I just defined my way out of the debate, and if so is that fatal or is it what a conceptual-analysis-flavored thesis is supposed to look like?
