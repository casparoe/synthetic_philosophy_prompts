I'm a novelist, not an archaeologist, so bear with me — half of what follows is invented on purpose and I don't want you fact-checking it against any real site. Here's the setup I'm working with for a long scene in the middle of the book:

A small team is excavating a pit cut into the floor of a burned mudbrick house, somewhere vaguely Bronze-Age-ish. The pit's contents, from bottom to top: a ceramic jar smashed on purpose and refitted from something like forty sherds, all of them present, no gaps; two spindle whorls, complete, no wear at all on the perforations; a dog, laid out pretty much articulated, with its skull placed seperately maybe ten centimetres off to one side; a lens of charred grain that came back from the lab about a hundred and fifty years *later* than the date for the house burning down; and, mixed in throughout, a scatter of worked flint that typologically belongs to a period a good deal earlier than the house.

The two site directors hate each other and I want their fight to be an intellectual one, not a personality one. So what I actually need from you is examples of the conceptual problem — the general problem, not this pit — that an assemblage like this exposes. Give me five, and make sure they're five genuinely different problems, not five rephrasings of "the evidence is thin." For each one I want:

- the problem stated generally, in a way that would still bite if the pit were somewhere else entirely;
- which specific detail above triggers it, and why that detail does the work;
- the best reply a trained archaeologist has to it;
- and then the part I care about most: whether that reply actually dissolves the problem or just moves it somewhere else, and if it moves it, where it lands.

Two of the five should be about the ritual-versus-refuse fork and why that fork might be the wrong instrument rather than a hard question. One should be about the dating anomaly and what it does to the idea that a deposit is a single event. And at least one should be about something I probably haven't thought of — I'd rather be surprised than flattered.

Please don't give me a reading list or name real sites or papers, I can't chase them and they'd just slow me down. And keep the language concrete: I'm going to be turning these into two people shouting at each other over a tray of finds in a hot tent, so anything that only makes sense in a journal abstract is no use to me.
