I retired four years ago from a chair in statistical epidemiology, having spent most of my working life fitting transmission models and then, less happily, explaining to clinicians why an association in their data was not a mechanism. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in a desultory but earnest way, and I have joined a Tuesday-evening reading group — mostly retired people like myself, plus one overworked postdoc who tolerates us. In six weeks it is my turn to present something of my own rather than someone else's chapter, and I have drafted a short essay on the case that Gibbard and Harper introduced under the name Death in Damascus. I would be grateful for blunt comments on the draft below. I have no library access at present and no wish to be sent off to look things up: I want your analysis, not a list of places to read. If you think I have misdescribed the case itself, please correct the description before you correct the argument.

**Draft: "Death in Damascus as a Confounding Problem"**

*1. The case.* A traveler meets Death in a market in Damascus. Death appears startled and remarks that they are due to meet again tomorrow — in Aleppo. The traveler rides through the night, arrives in Aleppo, and dies there the following day. The decision-theoretic use of the story asks what the traveler ought to do at the moment of the encounter, assuming the appointment cannot be cancelled and that Death's foreknowledge is exact.

*2. My thesis.* In my former profession we taught one distinction until it became reflexive: a variable that tracks an outcome is not a lever that changes it. Conditional association is a claim about evidence; intervention is a claim about consequences; and the besetting error of the observational epidemiologist is to treat a marker as though it were a mechanism. I want to suggest that the Damascus case is that error in Oriental dress. Evidential decision theory asks the traveler to conditionalize on his own act — to treat his choice as a symptom from which the location of the appointment may be inferred. Causal decision theory asks him to treat the choice as a lever. Because the appointment is fixed in advance, the lever is disconnected: nothing the traveler does now can move Death. But the choice remains an excellent symptom: given that he flees, Death is waiting in Aleppo. What we feel at the story, on my reading, is the discomfort of a man who has learned from his own intention where his death is standing.

*3. What follows.* Both acts are fatal, so on any sane accounting the traveler is indifferent between them. This is why I think the case is routinely oversold. Nothing in the description gives the two theories different rankings here: whichever act he settles on, Death is there. The evidential reading is not recommending the worse act; it is reporting bad news about the act already chosen. The case is therefore a counterexample to nothing at all. It is a parable about conditioning, and its moral is that symptoms must not be mistaken for levers.

*4. A variant, which I think does the real work.* Replace Death's infallible foreknowledge with an underworld clerk who books each evening's appointment on the strength of a forecast about where the traveler will be the next day. The clerk is good but not perfect: right nine times in ten. Suppose further that the traveler has some independent, weaker evidence about the booking — an innkeeper's remark that suggests without establishing that the ledger lists Aleppo. My claim is that this is where the interesting asymmetry lives, because the two styles of reasoning now come apart numerically. Causally, flight cannot alter a booking already entered, so what governs the decision is the background risk, and the clerk's reliability is spread evenly across both acts. Evidentially, the traveler's own choice is now a ninety-percent indicator of the ledger, and that should swamp the innkeeper's testimony. I expect a genuine disagreement about what to do, and I expect the evidential answer to be the one that kills the traveler more often across repeated playings of the same situation.

*5. A proposed criterion.* I am inclined to think the business is better handled as a control engineer would handle it. A policy is admissible only if it is stable: having adopted it, and having updated on the fact of having adopted it, the agent should not wish to adopt another. We would call this a fixed point of the updating map, and we would decline to deploy a controller that lacks one. Applied to Damascus: the traveler who resolves to stay, then updates on his resolution, learns that Death is in Damascus and wants to flee; the traveler who resolves to flee updates into wanting to stay. There is no fixed point. My suggestion is that this — and not any disagreement between the two decision theories — is the correct statement of what has gone wrong. The problem is ill-posed, in that its decision procedure has no fixed point, and an ill-posed problem should be reported as such rather than solved.

My requests, in order of importance to me:

(i) *Accuracy triage.* Which specific claims in §§2–5 are false, or would strike someone who works on this material as a confusion rather than a difference of emphasis? Please rank the problems by severity rather than listing them indiscriminately, and for each say whether the repair is local — a rewording — or whether the section has to come out. I am most concerned about §3, where I assert that the two theories do not rank the acts differently in the canonical case. If that assertion is right, I should like to know whether anything is left for the rest of the essay to bite on; if it is wrong, I should like to know exactly where the ranking diverges.

(ii) *The variant in §4.* Please check my arithmetic rather than my prose. Does a fallible clerk who books in advance actually generate a divergence between the evidential and the causal calculation, given that his accuracy attaches equally to staying and to fleeing? If it does not, say plainly that §4 is dead and tell me what minimal change to the setup — an asymmetric predictor, a second appointment, testimony that is itself influenced by the choice, something else — would make a divergence appear. And please tell me separately whether the instability described in §5 survives the introduction of noise, since I have implicitly assumed in §4 that infallibility was doing the work.

(iii) *The gloss.* Does the observational/interventional analogy in §2 earn its place, or is it decorative — a restatement, in my own vocabulary, of a distinction the case already displays? I would rather hear the unflattering answer. My own suspicion is that if the analogy were genuinely load-bearing it would yield a prediction about the case that the case does not have, and I have not been able to find one.

(iv) *The fixed-point criterion in §5.* If this turns out to be a familiar proposal that goes by another name, tell me so in a single sentence and do not send me hunting for it; I want only to know whether I have arrived somewhere already occupied. What matters more to me is whether the criterion is usable. If a problem admits no fixed point, is declaring it ill-posed a substantive verdict, or a respectable way of declining to answer? And is there something the traveler can rationally do that my criterion fails to capture — a sense in which the decision can be made well even though it cannot be made stably?

(v) *Overall.* Is there an essay here worth finishing, or is the honest advice to abandon it and present someone else's argument instead? If it is worth finishing, tell me which single claim I should stake it on, and what would make it interesting to a person who has thought about this case for twenty years rather than for three months. I am not looking to be reassured; I have three decades of practice at being told that my model is misspecified, and I find it much less painful than the alternative of presenting something confused to a room of patient friends.
