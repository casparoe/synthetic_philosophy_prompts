I'm a doctoral student in political theory, and next term I'm moderating a departmental colloquium Q&A with a political theorist who defends what she calls a deliberative jury theorem: on her account, deliberation simultaneously raises each participant's probability of voting correctly above one-half and generates positive correlation among their votes, and the correlation is benign precisely because it is transmitted by shared exposure to good reasons. I'd like six to eight questions that go past a restatement of that picture. At minimum, please cover the inversion case, in which competence below one-half makes large deliberating groups confidently wrong, and what that implies about where the real burden of proof sits; whether correlation caused by good reasons can be told apart from correlation caused by shared bias without already knowing the answer; which independence assumption, unconditional or conditional on the truth, actually survives once votes are causally interdependent through communication; how her account relates to the Hong–Page style result that diverse groups of problem-solvers can outperform homogeneous groups of the most able, given that deliberation homogenizes; and what institutional recommendation her version of the theorem supports that the classical one does not. For each question, add one follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and close with a question about which empirical finding would force her to give the view up. Begin directly with the questions themselves, with no preamble or framing remarks.
