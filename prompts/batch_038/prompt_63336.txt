I sit on the AI governance committee of a mid-size hospital network, and next month I owe my colleagues a short written justification for a budget line I have come to find philosophically embarrassing. Our vendor's model flags possible sepsis on the inpatient wards, and on the validation data it sold us, it outperforms the median hospitalist on both sensitivity and specificity. The deployment nevertheless requires that a licensed clinician open every flagged chart, review the flag, and record an affirmative sign-off before the alert is escalated to the bedside team. The median sign-off takes under a minute, and our own internal audit of eleven months of alerts found that the clinician concurred with the model in 99.1% of cases; among the overrides, the clinician was wrong roughly twice as often as right. When I ask what the sign-off is actually for, I get three answers that do not obviously fit together: it keeps a human accountable, it catches the rare case the model gets badly wrong, and the clinicians would never accept a system without it. Meanwhile my brother-in-law flies freight for a cargo carrier and describes a job in which the automation does nearly everything and the crew's best-documented risk is that their own manual skills have decayed to the point of being a liability during the few minutes a year they are genuinely needed; regulators nonetheless require two humans on the flight deck and insist that one of them be in command. European data protection law, for its part, gives people a right to demand human review of decisions taken solely by automated processing that significantly affect them, and that right is presented as a substantive protection rather than a formality.

Set against those cases, the same institutions are strikingly relaxed elsewhere. Our network screens applicants with a vendor tool that ranks resumes before any person sees them, and nobody on my committee has proposed mandated human review of a rejection; shift scheduling, formulary pricing, and even the sorting of the patient portal's inbox run end to end on software. Beyond the hospital, feed ranking and real-time ad pricing involve no human sign-off whatsoever, though their aggregate effects dwarf those of a single sepsis alert. The pattern, as far as I can tell, is that a human loop is treated as non-negotiable precisely where the machine is demonstrably better than the person and where the loop itself is eroding the person's competence, and as optional where the machine is worse and the harm is diffuse rather than attributable to one identifiable patient. The loop also manufactures a distinctive kind of worker: someone who carries legal and moral responsibility for an output they did not produce, whose dissent is statistically discouraged and slow, and who is the name that appears in the incident report afterward, usually under a finding of human error.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, not a survey. Which account of work, and of agency, makes the sign-off requirement intelligible at all: is it a claim that only a person can answer for an outcome and therefore a person must be attached to it, a claim that people need to be needed and that employment is a good independently of its output, a claim about legitimacy and consent, a claim about epistemic humility toward a system whose failure modes we cannot enumerate in advance, or a claim about the appearance of control? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and would a view on which a job is justified solely by its contribution to outcomes permit paying someone to concur with a machine eleven hundred times a month? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and flag the places where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same requirement. Then tell me whether any single theory can cover both halves of the pattern, the mandated loop in the cockpit and the ICU and the absent one in hiring and ranking; if none can, say which half is the aberration and why. I would also like you to take seriously the difference between a role justified by the 0.9% of cases in which it might matter and a role justified by routine contribution: what does being kept on for the tail do to a worker's own understanding of their contribution, and is this role a fair instance of the widespread charge that many modern jobs are pointless and known to be so by the people who hold them, or does the accountability function confer meaning on work that is causally inert? Finally, spell out what would have to change in practice if we swapped the presupposed view for one of its rivals, in both directions: abolishing the loop and spending the money where it buys outcomes, and conceding that decisions of that kind then have no human author to whom a bereaved family can address a complaint; or, conversely, admitting that the loop is employment and solidarity policy and staffing and scaling it as such rather than defending it on safety grounds. I am not after references or a list of who has said what, and you do not need to know anything about my hospital beyond what I have described; I need the argument laid out and the tensions named.
