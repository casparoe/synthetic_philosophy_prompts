For three years our research group has run a seminar that keeps returning to the same unresolved quarrel, and I want an outsider's diagnosis of its structure rather than a referee's verdict.

The quarrel concerns how far the strategy-stealing argument travels. Everyone in the group knows it in its home setting: in a symmetric, finite, deterministic positional game in which extra marks of your own colour can never hurt you, a first player who is assumed to be losing may make an arbitrary opening move, adopt the second player's supposed winning strategy, and, whenever that strategy calls for a cell already occupied by the arbitrary stone, waste a move elsewhere. The conclusion is that the second player cannot have had a winning strategy after all; the first player can force at least a draw, and in a game admitting no draws, a win.

Call the two camps the generalizers and the localists.

The generalizers hold that this is a general schema about advantage rather than a fact about boards: whenever an agent holds a resource its rival lacks, and the resource is one the rival could simply decline to use, the resource cannot be a genuine source of disadvantage, so any claim of first-mover advantage in a symmetric setting is suspect. They press it well beyond games — to a party in a competitive technological race that could always adopt the more cautious plan its careful rivals would have followed; to a committee chair granted an extra casting vote; to a negotiator handed an additional option, on the grounds that an option one need not exercise cannot make one worse off.

The localists hold that strategy-stealing is a proof trick with a long premise list and no content outside it. Their counterexamples: misère variants, where completing a line loses and an extra stone is a liability; zugzwang positions, where being obliged to move is precisely what loses the game; games in which occupying a cell forecloses your own later possibilities; and, most seriously, settings where the resource is publicly observable, so that holding it changes what the other side does, and where the rival possesses no unconditional winning strategy to steal but only a comparative edge that the extra resource may erase.

The stalemate has a fixed shape. Every counterexample the localists produce, the generalizers dismiss as a case in which the premises simply are not met — which is true, and beside the point, they are told. Every export the generalizers propose, the localists redescribe as a different argument wearing the same name. Neither side believes the other has made a mistake; each believes the other is confused about what the argument is.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed, is that the dispute is verbal: the phrase strategy-stealing argument names, for one camp, a theorem schema with explicit premises, and, for the other, a family of analogies about the fungibility of advantages, so the two are answering different questions and no counterexample can bear on both. But I may be wrong, and there may be substantive disagreement hidden inside — perhaps about whether monotonicity of resources and the fixity of the opponent's plan are one premise or two, or about whether the schema requires an unconditional guarantee rather than an expected-utility comparison.

So: diagnose the disagreement, working from the positions as I have stated them. Decompose the schema into its premises, and say for each of the four export cases and each of the four localist counterexamples which premise is actually doing the work, and whether the failures, where there are failures, are of the same kind. Say whether a case is conceivable in which every premise holds and the conclusion nevertheless fails — a genuine refutation rather than a change of subject — or whether that is impossible in principle, and why. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a shared case study, a reformulation of the question, or nothing at all. I want the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of positional game theory and not a list of further examples.
