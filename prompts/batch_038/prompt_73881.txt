I am three years into a doctoral project on Henry Sidgwick's engagement with psychical research — the chapter began as a study of Newnham College and drifted, as chapters do — and the interpretive debate I have walked into has stopped producing arguments and started producing restatements. Roughly, there are four positions. The continuity reading holds that the Society for Psychical Research, which Sidgwick helped found and first presided over in 1882, was an extension of his moral philosophy by other means: *The Methods of Ethics* closes on a conflict between rational egoism and universal benevolence that ethics alone cannot settle, and only some guarantee that virtue and happiness ultimately coincide would settle it, so a search for evidence of personal survival was for him the one empirically respectable route to a postulate his own system required but could not supply. The symptom reading treats the same material as biography rather than philosophy — the scrupulous man who gave up his fellowship in 1869 rather than declare himself a member of the Church of England, who spent his life negotiating between destructive criticism and a need for consolation, and whose parapsychology was an outlet for a wish his published work refused to theorise; on this view, reading the Society back into the ethics is a straightforward biographical fallacy. The institutional reading says both miss the point: what interested him was method, not survival — building a body that would bring base rates, cross-examination and the exposure of fraud to a domain the scientific establishment had abandoned, which is why the Census of Hallucinations published in 1894 argues from the reported experience of some seventeen thousand people rather than from séance testimony. And a fourth, deflationary camp maintains that he never believed any of it, and that attributing to him a faith his ethics needed but never claimed is the projection of a vocabulary onto a lifelong agnostic who pointedly declined to use it.

What frustrates me is that the evidence is not in dispute. Everyone works from the same Proceedings, the same correspondence, the same séances — including the sittings with Eusapia Palladino in Milan in the mid-1890s, after which Sidgwick's report stressed what looked like trickery while Myers and Lodge, on my reading of their exchanges, pressed for the inquiry to continue — and yet the camps have not converged, and each accuses the others of the identical vice: deciding in advance what Sidgwick could have believed. That gives me a hunch I would like you to test rather than endorse. My suspicion is that the stalemate is not about Sidgwick at all but about the interpreters' own priors on whether paranormal claims can be true, so that the principle of charity is doing covert metaphysical work: to read a subject charitably is to read him as rational, and what counts as rational is being supplied by the interpreter's view of psi. If that is right, the question of what Sidgwick believed has quietly become the question of what he could have believed. There are rival diagnoses I cannot dismiss, though — that the camps use belief univocally for what are distinct attitudes, namely assent, disposition to fund an inquiry, and willingness to stake a career's credibility, and are therefore answering different questions under one heading; that the real disagreement is methodological, about whether a philosopher's institutional activity is admissible evidence about his doctrines at all; or that the question is ill-posed when asked of a man whose published ethics refuses to demand certainty where certainty is unavailable.

So tell me which of these the dispute actually is, or whether it is something I have not listed. Argue from what the camps characteristically say and from the shape of the material itself, not from a survey of Sidgwick's life or of the Society's history, and do not give me a reading list, since I have the literature and want the analysis. Then say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction to be drawn, an archival or statistical test whose result would discriminate between the readings, a formal reconstruction of what a census of hallucinations could and could not have licensed for a philosopher of Sidgwick's standards, or a change of question entirely. Be candid if your answer is that no progress should be expected, and explain why that would be so. Please write in British English.
