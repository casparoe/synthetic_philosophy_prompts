I'm setting the take-home final for a small upper-level course on population ethics and very-large-scale cases (infinite populations, astronomical stuff). Students get 3 hours, 1200 words of prose plus as much computation as they want, and the question is worth 25 marks. I want ONE essay question on comparing infinite worlds that they cant answer by reciting the usual complaint — that cardinal arithmetic makes any two infinite worlds come out the same size, so you need extra machinery. The point I want them to land on is that every piece of extra machinery adds structure that wasn't in the description of the world (a discount rate, a limit process, a choice of ultrafilter), and then to argue about whether that's a fatal flaw or just what comparing infinite things costs.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 1200 words with the computations set out compactly, and a marking scheme.

Here's the apparatus I want the question to hand them explicitly, so nobody loses marks over undefined terms. A world is a function from locations n = 0, 1, 2, ... to welfare values in [0, 10] (bounded so the discounted sums below always converge); S_n(W) is the partial sum through location n. Four ranking rules:

R1, δ-discounting: W is better than V iff Σ δⁿ u_W(n) > Σ δⁿ u_V(n), for some fixed δ in (0,1).
R2, limit overtaking: W is better than V iff lim (S_n(W) − S_n(V)) exists and is positive; if the limit doesn't exist the worlds are incomparable.
R3, limsup overtaking: same as R2 but with limsup. Nobody endorses this, but it's roughly what half the class will invent on their own, so I want it in there to be destroyed.
R4, ultrafilter: fix a free ultrafilter U on the naturals; W is better than V iff {n : S_n(W) > S_n(V)} ∈ U.

Six worlds to rank: A is 1 everywhere. B is 2 at location 0 and 1 everywhere else. C is 2 everywhere. D is 1 at even locations and 0 at odd. E is 0 at even and 1 at odd. F is 1 everywhere except 0 at locations 2^k.

Five conditions, also stated in the question: strong Pareto; anonymity (if one world is a permutation of another under some bijection of the locations, they're equally good); completeness; transitivity; and a fifth I want treated as a real condition rather than a grumble — determinacy: the ranking of two worlds is settled by the welfare assignment alone, with no free choice of an extra mathematical object doing the work.

Three parts. (a) Rank the six worlds under each of the four rules, showing the work. (b) For each rule, say which conditions it meets and which it breaks, with an explicit counterexample for every break. I want the model answer to point out that the D-vs-F ranking under R1 flips as δ varies — check it at δ = 1/2 and δ = 0.9 — so R1 isnt a single rule but a family, which is already a determinacy failure before you get to the anonymity problems. (c) The essay part: R2 gives up completeness, R3 gives up transitivity, R1 gives up anonymity, so R4 looks like the survivor — complete, transitive, strong Pareto. Does it actually solve the problem or just relocate the arbitrariness? A top answer has to work out what R4 says about D and E and notice that it depends on whether the set of even numbers belongs to U, so anonymity isn't delivered, it's made optional. It should also notice that R3 settles D over E with no parameter in sight at all, which makes its location-bias harder to see than R1's, and say something about whether hidden arbitrariness is worse than advertised arbitrariness. And it should be careful that "incomparable" under R2 is not the same as "equally good" for D and E — students slide between those constantly.

Two extra constructions I want built into part (b) or (c). A trio of worlds that makes R3 cycle, and a pair where R3 says each is better than the other. I've got drafts of both: X = (2, 3, 3, 3, ...), Y = (1, 6, 0, 6, 0, 6, ...), Z = (3, 3, 3, 3, ...) for the cycle, and W = (4, 0, 6, 0, 6, 0, ...) against V = (3, 3, 3, 3, ...) for the two-way pair. Verify them, and if you can build tidier ones with less extreme values go ahead, but I'd much rather have correct constructions than pretty ones.

For the marking scheme: 25 marks, broken into named must-hit moves with the marks each is worth and one line on how students typically botch it. Then separately, list the three or four answers that look competent and aren't — "just let δ go to 1", "R4 is fine because the ultrafilter is part of the theory, not the world", "R2 respects anonymity because it doesn't rank D above E" — and say what each one costs.
