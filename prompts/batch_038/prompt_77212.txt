I am a third-year doctoral student in the history and philosophy of computing, and I am in the middle of fixing the architecture of my dissertation. The subject is not the imitation game as it is usually discussed — I have nothing to add to the literature on whether the game is a criterion of thought — but rather the child-programme proposal and the surrounding pedagogy in Turing's 1950 paper: the suggestion that one should build a machine whose mind begins as very little mechanism and a great deal of blank paper, and then educate it; the teacher who shapes it by what Turing describes in broadly reward-and-punishment terms; the deliberate admission of a random element into the process; and the analogy he draws between the whole teaching arrangement and biological evolution, in which the machine's initial structure plays roughly the part of hereditary material, its changes the part of mutations, and the experimenter's judgement something like the part of natural selection. My methods are textual and conceptual: close reading, reconstruction of arguments, and comparison with what the mid-century behavioural sciences assumed about teaching. I cannot run experiments, I have no machine-learning collaborators, and I have no desire to spend two years writing a paper whose thesis is that Turing anticipated reinforcement learning.

I have a list of candidate questions that could anchor the two central chapters, and what I need from you is not a survey but a defended agenda: which two of these I should build those chapters around, which deserves a section at most, and which is a trap. My candidates, in no order of merit:

1. Whether Turing's teacher is essentially selective or essentially instructive. If the teacher merely accepts or rejects whatever the machine's own variation produces, the pedagogical vocabulary does no real work and the child programme is a search procedure wearing a schoolmaster's coat. If the teacher genuinely instructs — contributes structure the machine could not have generated from its own variation — then the evolutionary analogy he offers fails, since natural selection does not instruct. Which reading the text supports, and what each implies about whether the imitation game assesses an achieved competence or a capacity-to-be-taught.

2. The teacher's epistemic position. Ordinary pedagogy assumes the teacher understands both the subject and the learner well enough to judge progress. Turing's teacher-experimenter judges outputs without needing to know what is going on inside. Whether that makes the learning machine of 1950 a behaviourist commitment rather than a methodological convenience, and whether it matters for the coherence of the child programme with the imitation game.

3. Whether the child programme forces a dispositional reading of the imitation game, on which a machine passes not by what it currently does but by being the sort of thing that can be educated. Whether that reading is coherent, and whether it defuses the objection that a sufficiently large pre-tabulated responder could pass — on the grounds that a lookup table cannot be educated — or whether it instead makes passing depend on facts about the machine's future that the game itself cannot reveal.

4. What the random element is for. Whether it is an acknowledgment that the space of behaviours cannot be searched systematically, a piece of psychological speculation, or a hedge against the Gödelian objection, on the grounds that a machine incorporating genuine indeterminacy is not the kind of object the incompleteness argument targets. Whether settling this changes how we should read his reply to the mathematical objection.

5. Whether the proposal survives the removal of the human teacher. If the teacher can itself be a machine — an automatic evaluator generating its own curriculum — the arrangement stops being education and becomes design, and the question of what counts as intelligent behaviour is settled by whoever built the evaluator. Whether that is a deepening of Turing's view or a collapse of it.

6. Whether the pedagogy is load-bearing at all. Whether the education metaphor imports specific commitments from the drill, discipline, and examination culture of Turing's own schooling that philosophers have been treating as decorative, or whether it is scaffolding and the child programme should be read as a purely engineering heuristic that supports no conclusions about mind.

Please state the criteria you are ranking by and how you weight them, because I expect you and I weight neglectedness differently: I care a great deal about whether a question's answer is already assumed by everyone who writes in the area, since an obvious answer is a poor use of two years, whereas you may be inclined to reward a question for being central even if it is familiar. Say where the trade-offs actually bite — which candidate is most important but least tractable given my tools, and which is most tractable but least consequential. Say plainly which one is the trap: the question that looks profound but either collapses into a point everyone already makes or cannot be answered from what Turing wrote, and I want to know which failure mode it suffers from. Then tell me what would change your ordering over the next eighteen months — what argument, or what development in how people read the 1950 paper, would move an item up or down the list. Finally, describe the failure mode of my preferred approach from the inside: how I would know, about a year in, that I had anchored the chapters on the wrong question, and what the early warning signs of that would look like in my own drafts.

Two constraints on your reply. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I have the texts and I am asking for your judgement, not your sources, and if you are unsure whether someone has argued something, say so rather than inventing an attribution. And write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. You may refer to my candidates by number in running prose. Aim for somewhere around twelve hundred words.
