I am circulating a compact statement of my position on the limits of markets ahead of a policy-ethics workshop, and before it goes out I would like the package audited for internal coherence — not defended, not situated in the literature, just checked against itself. Here is what I hold.

1. Default presumption: where an exchange is voluntary, leaves both parties better off by their own lights, and imposes no uncompensated costs on third parties, the law ought to permit it. The justificatory burden falls on prohibition, never on permission.

2. Some markets are nonetheless properly blocked. I support the prohibition on paid living kidney donation and the prohibition on selling one's vote, and I regard these as two instances of one phenomenon rather than as unrelated taboos that happen to sit near each other on a list.

3. I reject any inference from the intensity of a reaction to the wrongness of what provokes it. Revulsion is not evidence, and I want every claim in my account statable to someone who feels no revulsion at all. At the same time, I think the theorist who treats widespread aversion to a market as simply one further preference to be fed into a cost-benefit calculation has missed something, and that the persistence of such aversion is a social fact which anyone designing or defending a market ignores at their peril.

4. The best account I have of what goes wrong in the blocked cases is expressive rather than distributive: certain goods are degraded, or have their social meaning corrupted, by being priced and traded, because market treatment of them expresses an attitude that is incompatible with the attitude a society ought to take. I want this stated without the vocabulary of dignity, sanctity, or intrinsic worth — those terms have never done any work for me — so it must be a claim about the meaning a practice carries in a given society, not about a property the good possesses.

5. I do not think appeal to unjust background conditions is sufficient. Even in a society with a decent floor, adequate information, and no duress, I would oppose a legal market in kidneys. Inequality explains why actually existing organ markets are worse than they need to be; it does not explain what is wrong with them.

6. I would accept a public monopsony: a single governmental purchaser paying living donors a fixed, non-negotiable sum, with organs allocated by the medical criteria already in use rather than by willingness to pay. I regard that arrangement as a genuine improvement on both the status quo and an open market, and I would not oppose it. What I object to is organs going to the highest bidder, and the emergence of an organ trade as a line of work — not the transfer of money to a donor as such.

7. I oppose price-gouging statutes. Caps on the price of generators, fuel, water, and hotel rooms after a disaster suppress supply precisely when it is most wanted, and the resulting shortages fall hardest on those least able to queue or to pay a side premium. When people press me on the morality of charging what the traffic will bear from someone whose basement is flooding, my answer is the economics. I do not think there is a moral theory of the price to be had in that case.

8. I accept the empirical claim that prohibition has pushed the organ trade underground and abroad, and that donors in unregulated markets fare considerably worse than they would under any legal scheme. I would still oppose legalization in the form of an open market, and I would do so even if I were fully persuaded by that claim.

What I want is an audit. Specifically:

- Identify which of these genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do. I expect at least one pair that reads like a contradiction to turn out to be perfectly stable once the terms are pinned down, and I would rather be told which than have every friction point flagged with equal gravity.
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity. If one of them is curable by a distinction I have not drawn, say whether the distinction is available, and whether I can afford it given the rest of the package — in particular given the constraint in item 4.
- For each tension, state what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs downstream: which further commitments it weakens, and whether the resulting position is one I could still recognize as mine.
- Close with the single revision that would restore the most coherence across the whole set, and say explicitly what I would lose by making it.

I have no prior about which item is the weak one, and I would rather not have my own phrasing treated as a hint about where I expect the trouble to be. Several of these are positions I have argued for publicly, so please commit to a ranking instead of reporting that a number of tensions are arguable; and where you are genuinely uncertain — above all about whether a distinction I am relying on is real rather than verbal — say so, and say what would settle it. Finally, if you think the request itself is misframed, tell me that instead of complying: if one of these items is not a coherent thesis, if two of them are not rival explanations but claims pitched at different levels that cannot conflict, or if a consistency audit is simply the wrong instrument for a position of this kind, say so plainly and explain what would be the right instrument.
