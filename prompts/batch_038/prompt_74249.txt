I build bidding agents. My day job is training RL policies that bid in repeated open auctions for GPU capacity — you know the setup, we submit bids, get allocated slices, observe the fills, update. Last quarter one of our agents got into a bidding war with a competitor over a block of H100 hours and kept raising past what the block was worth to us by a wide margin, ended up paying maybe 3x fair value and the deal fell apart anyway. A colleague of mine who reads pop behavioral econ looked at the logs and said "this is literally Shubik's dollar auction, it's escalation of commitment, you need to bolt on a hard stop-loss or some precommitment device so the policy physically can't keep raising." He also mentioned Barry Staw's studies where people who had personally chosen a failing project later doubled down on it, and something about self-justification. I don't have an econ or psych background — I think in MDPs, policies, value functions, latent states, beliefs, regret, non-stationarity, credit assignment.

So: can you restate that whole cluster of claims in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report? The specific claims I want translated are (1) the game-theoretic one, that the dollar auction has no pure-strategy equilibrium and that a fully rational player shouldn't even open the bidding; (2) an equilibrium fact I half-remember, that in the symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium the probability your opponent folds doesn't depend on how high the bids have already gotten; (3) the "escalation can be rational" defense, i.e. that continuing to pour money into a failing project is fine when you're updating on new information about how expensive it's going to be to finish; and (4) the psychological account — self-justification, ego threat, not wanting to look inconsistent in front of the people who watched you start. For each of the four, tell me what maps cleanly onto state/action/reward/belief terms, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in that framework. Here's the part I actually want you to weigh in on. My own working theory is that how much you've already sunk is only a legitimate part of the state if it's correlated with something you can't observe — the opponent's budget, their resolve, how much they value the item — and otherwise any policy that conditions its action on sunk spend is by construction leaving value on the table. Is that right, or is the game-theoretic analysis saying something my formulation can't capture?
