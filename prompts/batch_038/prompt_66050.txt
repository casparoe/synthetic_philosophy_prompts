I am auditing a clinical ethics seminar that has been asked to advise a regional hospice network on a guidance document about the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment. Nearly every session drifts back to a question that is logically prior to any of the practical recommendations the network actually wants: whether the distinction between doing harm and merely allowing harm carries independent moral weight, or whether it functions only as a proxy for something else — the agent's intentions, the duties her role imposes, the expectations she has created in her patient, or the baseline against which her conduct happens to be described. I would like an essay that takes a definite position on that question. Please do not write a survey or a catalogue of positions. I want the piece to weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, and then to commit in terms to a verdict about the moral status of the doing/allowing distinction, defending that verdict against at least three specific objections.

To keep the argument anchored, please build it around a trio of cases that a member of the seminar constructed and that I have not seen discussed anywhere in the literature. In all three, the physical movement performed is the same. Case One, the idle valve: Marta is a dialysis technician whose patient Elias has end-stage renal failure. The machine filters his blood continuously, and without it he will die of uremia in roughly four days. During a scheduled inspection Marta finds a hairline crack in a filter cartridge, and ward protocol requires her to isolate the machine until a replacement arrives six days later. She closes the arterial valve, blood stops entering the circuit, and Elias dies on the fourth day. His underlying condition is exactly what it was an hour earlier, and nothing new has been introduced into his body. Case Two, the contaminated bath: six months later, same ward, same patient, same valve. A batch error has left a hemolytic agent in the dialysate, and it is now crossing the membrane into Elias's bloodstream; on the current trajectory he will die within forty minutes. There is no way to swap the dialysate or bypass the cartridge in time. The only way to stop the poisoning is to close the arterial valve, which ends dialysis as well, so that Elias dies of uremia in four days rather than of hemolysis in forty minutes. Marta closes the valve. Case Three, the mislabeled bag: identical to Case Two in every respect save one — the hemolytic agent is in the line because Marta herself mislabeled a bag earlier that morning, and she now recognizes her error with thirty seconds in which to act. The movement of her hand, the two available futures, and Elias's death on the fourth day are all the same; the only difference is that the lethal process now underway is one she started. My difficulty is this: on most causal accounts of doing and allowing, the three cases look alike, since in each of them Marta intervenes in an ongoing physical process and Elias dies sooner than he would have died had she never walked onto the ward. Yet they do not seem to have the same moral standing, and — more awkwardly — they do not seem to differ along the doing/allowing axis at all. The case in which Marta interrupts a harmful process already in motion is plausibly the one where her conduct is most active, while the case in which she declines to sustain a benefit is where it is least, and yet our reactions run in the opposite direction.

Here is what I would like the essay to deliver. State the general principle you are defending explicitly enough that it can be applied mechanically, then apply it to all three cases and give a verdict on the permissibility of Marta's conduct in each, explaining where the principle earns its keep and where it needs auxiliary machinery. Engage the familiar pressure points along the way: Rachels's paired bathtub examples, which are meant to show that once intentions and outcomes are held fixed, killing is no worse than letting die; Foot's account in terms of positive and negative rights and the different duties they generate; the suspicion that double-prevention cases like the second and third are artifacts of describing a single event twice, once as the stopping of a harm and once as the removal of a benefit; and the baseline problem pressed by Bennett and others, namely that there may be no non-arbitrary way of specifying what would have happened had the agent not acted, so that whether a piece of conduct counts as doing or as allowing depends on which counterfactual we happen to select. Address separately whether the third case differs morally from the second, given that the sole difference is who started the lethal process, and whether an agent responsible for a threat thereby acquires a stronger duty to remove it even at the price of withdrawing an aid she had been supplying. Two housekeeping constraints: no bibliography, no footnotes, no page numbers, and no attempt at precise citation — invoke authors and positions only insofar as the argument needs them, and treat each such invocation as something you would defend on its merits rather than on its authority. And do not close on a balanced, non-committal note. If you conclude that the doing/allowing distinction is morally significant only derivatively, say what it derives from; if you conclude that it is spurious, say so; if you conclude that it is irreducible, explain what makes it irreducible rather than restating intuitions about cases. Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words, written for readers who already know the standard examples, so that trolley-style cases and the drowning child in the shallow pond need not be introduced at all.
