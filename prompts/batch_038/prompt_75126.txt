> Dennett's idea is that evolution built minds in four steps, and each step is strictly smarter than the one below it. Darwinian creatures are the simplest: everything they can do was installed by natural selection, so they can't learn anything at all — a plant, a sea squirt, an insect. Their whole repertoire is fixed at birth. Next come Skinnerian creatures, which can learn, but only in the dumbest possible way: they try things more or less at random until one of them pays off, and then they repeat it. Popperian creatures are the real breakthrough, because they carry a model of the world around inside them. Now the bad options can be killed off in the head before they're tried, instead of letting the world kill off the animal that tried them — Dennett's whole point is that these creatures let their hypotheses die in their place. This is just what believing and predicting amount to, so anything that navigates by an internal map, caches food for later, or works out a detour counts as Popperian. Finally there are Gregorian creatures, named after the psychologist Richard Gregory. These don't get smarter by having better brains — they get smarter by plugging into tools other people already designed: language, writing, arithmetic, the internet. It's essentially the extended-mind idea, and it means a Gregorian creature's intelligence isn't located in its skull at all. So the ladder runs reflex → habit → thought → culture, and each rung contains the ones below it: you can't be Gregorian without also being Popperian, though you can be Popperian and stop there. That's why Dennett thinks most animals are parked a rung or two below us, and it's why a chatbot is such an odd case — it uses language but was never selected by anything and has no body, so it looks like a Gregorian creature with no Darwinian creature underneath it.
