I'm 300 pages into a novel and I need a sanity check on a character, because I've been living inside one dead German's digestive tract for a year and I can't tell anymore whether I'm writing him or ventriloquizing my own opinions about wellness culture.

The character is a philosopher with a ruined stomach who treats food the way other people treat salvation. He's basically built out of Nietzsche's late stuff, especially the autobiography, where he insists the question of nutrition matters more to humanity than anything the theologians have come up with, and frames the whole thing as: how do you feed yourself so as to reach your maximum of force. So before I put words in his mouth I want his actual track record, as reported by the man himself:

- German cooking wrecked him. Soup before the meal, meat boiled to shreds, vegetables in fat and flour, puddings like paperweights. He blames it for the German spirit, which he calls an indigestion.
- The English diet, all that meat, gives the spirit heavy feet. Best cuisine in the world: Piedmont.
- Alcohol: one glass of wine or beer a day was enough to turn life into a vale of tears for him. Water suffices. He likes towns where you can drink from running fountains, and he advises the more spiritual types to abstain absolutely. But — and this is the part everyone skips — small, well-diluted doses put him out of sorts while strong ones turned him almost into a sailor, and as a boy he wrote a long Latin essay through the night on heavy grog with no ill effect at all.
- Vegetarianism: against it, from experience, and he credits Wagner with converting him back to meat.
- Meal size: a big meal is easier to digest than a small one, because the stomach gets going as a whole, and the entire art is knowing the size of your own stomach.
- Movement: somewhere in the late books he tells you not to trust a thought that wasn't born outdoors while you were moving about freely, with the muscles on holiday.
- Tea, if I'm remembering this right and not inventing it: only in the morning, small quantity but strong rather than plentiful.

And the complication I can't reconcile: from the letters, so far as I can tell, he was on chloral hydrate and opium more or less continuously for the migraines and the sleeplessness, right up against the abstain-absolutely line.

So here's the new case. In my draft he's moved to a city where everyone is on a GLP-1. He gets a prescription — not to get thin, he doesn't care about his body in that way, but because digestion was eating four hours of his writing day and he wants those hours back. He injects it once a week, the hunger simply goes quiet, he eats one substantial meal in the late afternoon, he's producing more than he has in a decade, and he's pleased about it in a way that embarrasses him slightly, because the drug was handed to him rather than earned by the kind of self-experiment he thinks is the only honest method. His building has a cold plunge on the roof and half the neighbors use it. He has tried one nicotine pouch, found the tiny dose intolerable, and thrown the tin away.

What I want from you:

1. Name the candidate rules running underneath that track record — I can think of at least four (conserve force and spend nothing on digestion that could go into thinking; refuse anything that arrives pre-wrapped in morality, purity or self-denial; refuse anything that is currently the mass fashion of the herd he despises; and trust only what your own body reports after careful self-observation, so there are no universal answers at all). Tell me which of those the text actually supports, which I'm projecting, and whether the odd dose-response business with alcohol is its own seperate rule — that non-monotonic thing, where the moderate sip is worse than the swig — because that's the one I keep tripping over.

2. Run each rule against the injection and tell me what verdict it delivers. I want to see where they collide rather than where they agree, and I specifically want to know whether the opium-and-chloral record counts as a precedent for it or against it. A drug that quietly removes appetite, taken by half the country, prescribed by a doctor, and welcomed because it buys thinking time — I can hear three different Nietzsches saying three different things.

3. Give me your best single guess at what he'd say, with the reason you prefer that rule over the others. Then seperate what he'd write in public from what he'd actually do in private, because I think those come apart for him and I need to know which one my chapter is in.

4. Name the case that would settle it between your top two rules. I have one in mind — put the same injection in a world where it's unfashionable, hard to get, and faintly scandalous, and see if his verdict moves — but tell me whether that actually discriminates, or whether it just moves two of the rules together, and if there's a better test case than mine.
