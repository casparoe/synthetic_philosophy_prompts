Next Thursday I run a fifty-minute discussion section in a first-year philosophy-of-time course, and the assigned topic is the argument that the relativity of simultaneity forces us to treat past, present, and future events as equally real — the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Twenty students, whiteboard only. The room has no projector, and I will not be drawing Minkowski diagrams: I cannot produce legible ones under time pressure, and for the students who most need this argument the diagram would substitute a picture for the reasoning.

The complication is what the students will already be carrying in. Half of them are concurrently enrolled in the physics department's introductory relativity module, which has given them time dilation and length contraction by way of light clocks but has never presented simultaneity as a convention with a synchronization procedure behind it. Their lecturer has said, in so many words, that which distant events count as simultaneous is a matter of coordinate choice rather than of physics, and at least one student will repeat that to me. The other half have met the argument through a popular video built around the Andromeda version — two pedestrians passing on the street, one of whom an alien fleet has already launched — and therefore believe they already know the argument and that it proves the future is fixed.

I want a plan for how I should teach this, not a script I can read aloud. Please give me:

1. A dependency-ordered sequence with minute allocations, each step annotated with what must already be in place for it and what it licenses next. Mark explicitly the point at which the physics stops and the metaphysics begins; I expect to blur the two there and lose the physicists in the room.

2. The single example that carries the argument, with an account of what it must have and what it must lack. Constraints: it must not be the Andromeda story; it must be stateable in prose plus at most one simple sketch of an apparatus; it must be poseable to the class as a question they can attempt before I reveal what follows; it must involve nothing more exotic than constant-velocity motion; and the disagreement between the two observers' presents must come out in hours or days rather than microseconds, at a relative speed they would recognize as mundane. Tell me why the numbers work out that way, in one sentence I could say aloud.

3. A misconception map as a table with the columns *misconception* | *where in the sequence it must be headed off* | *the line I say there*. Cover at minimum: block universe read as determinism or fatalism; the involvement of observers, minds, or consciousness; conflation with light-travel delay and with what we can see; the belief that time dilation is doing the work; and the "just coordinates" line from the physics module. For that last one, tell me how to treat it honestly — I do not want to contradict another department, but I also cannot concede a point the argument depends on refusing to concede.

4. Five minutes near the end for the strongest objection, chosen and defended: which one, why that rather than the others available, and how to state it so that it functions as a real objection and not a straw man. I am myself unpersuaded by the argument, so flag the two or three places in the plan where my skepticism is most likely to leak into my phrasing and supply neutral wording for those moments.

5. One closing question to put to the class, plus a description of what a competent answer, a slogan-only answer, and a diagnostic failure would each sound like.

6. What I should refuse to cover at this level and announce that I am refusing it, with the sentence to say out loud when I decline.

Do not recommend readings, papers, or outside resources; the plan must be executable without my looking anything up. End your reply with a single line in exactly the form KEY MISCONCEPTION: <short phrase>, naming the one misconception the entire sequence is engineered to prevent.
