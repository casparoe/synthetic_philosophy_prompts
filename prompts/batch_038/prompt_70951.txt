I am a fifth-year doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind, and our consciousness working group has asked me to circulate a two-page précis ahead of a return visit by a speaker whose talk ended on a sentence that no one in the room could afterward agree about. Her argument was empirical: she moved through change blindness, inattentional blindness, and studies in which subjects confidently report features of a scene that were never present or fail to report features that were, and concluded that first-person access to the workings of one's own visual experience is far thinner than our self-description of it implies. A question from the floor asked whether reports of that kind could serve as data for a theory of experience at all, and her answer was the last slide: consciousness is a user illusion — a line that has circulated in popular treatments of the mind since the early 1990s and that, in that room, immediately split into four incompatible readings, one of which deflated qualia, one of which impeached introspective report, one of which was about the self rather than about experience, and one of which was taken to vindicate the reality of phenomenal character. She never disambiguated, and I have to say in writing which commitment she actually took on.

Please give me a systematic inventory of the distinct theses that sentence can carry, laid out side by side rather than resolved into a single favored reading. Specifically:

(a) Enumerate at least six readings, each stated as a self-contained claim in which the operative senses of "user," "illusion," and "consciousness" are explicit. Two axes should generate the spread: what kind of failing "illusion" names — a property that is not instantiated at all, a property that is instantiated but systematically misdescribed by its subject, or a real functional structure whose presentation is a deliberate simplification in the way a graphical desktop simplifies file operations — and who the user is supposed to be: an inner subject to whom an interface is presented, the organism as a whole, or no user whatsoever.

(b) Classify each reading as a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a contentious one, or a self-undermining one; say what evidence or argument would bear on it; and flag any reading on which the speaker's own use of the sentence would be unwarranted.

(c) Say which readings her empirical premises could actually support and which she could assert only by supplying premises she never argued for. In particular, address whether anything follows from the introspective-access failures alone, given that those leave open whether what is misdescribed is the presence of phenomenal character or its nature.

(d) Isolate the homunculus problem for readings on which consciousness is an interface presented to someone, and explain how a reading can avoid it without collapsing into a claim about the mere usefulness of a particular vocabulary.

(e) Identify which readings remain compatible with holding that phenomenal character is real but transparent to itself, and whether any reading is strong enough to dissolve the intuition that a further question remains about why such an interface is accompanied by seeming at all.

Format the result as a numbered inventory: for each reading, a label of no more than a few words, the precisified thesis, the classification, and one line on what would settle it, followed by a short closing section answering (c) through (e). Please leave out bibliographies, source lists, and citations; I need the analysis itself, not pointers to it.
