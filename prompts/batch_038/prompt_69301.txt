I write numerical simulation code for a living, no philosophy background at all, but I've accumulated a bunch of gut calls about when a system counts as predictable and I want to know what principle they add up to — if there is one.

My verdicts:

- Double pendulum, exact equations, initial state written down to 15 digits: you lose it after maybe 20–30 seconds. Unpredictable.
- Same pendulum, but I only ask whether the energy stays bounded and whether it's swinging wildly: predictable indefinitely.
- Logistic map at r=4 with the seed known exactly: predictable, I just run the loop.
- Rule 30 cellular automaton, rule and starting row known exactly: predictable for the same reason.
- A PRNG whose seed I dont have: unpredictable.
- Weather three days out: predictable. Forty days out: nope. And I suspect there's no principled line between those, just a knob.
- Satellite orbit where my drag model is slightly off: the error creeps up linearly, not exponentially, and I'd still call it unpredictable past about a year.
- One uranium atom decaying: unpredictable. A gram of the stuff: about as predictable as anything gets.

The pair I can't make consistent is logistic-map-at-r=4 versus the satellite. The first amplifies errors exponentially and I call it predictable; the second grows them politely and linearly and I call it unpredictable. So whatever the principle is, it can't just be about growth rates — it has to involve how much uncertainty I start with and how tight my tolerance is. Give me the principle, or two competing versions if one can't cover everything, state it precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts each version has to break. Then give me its verdict on a case I left out: a chaotic system whose initial state I know to infinite precision.

Also flag which parts of this are settled math and physics and which are live arguments among people who actually work on it — I don't want to walk away thinking a contested position is the consensus. Keep the whole answer under 500 words.
