I run ensemble forecast systems for a national weather service, so my working life is spent inside the technical machinery of sensitive dependence: perturbed initial conditions, ensemble spread, Lyapunov times, and the distinction between a system that amplifies small errors and one that is structurally stable. On weekends I read a great deal of narrative history, and I have become bothered by the gap between what that machinery seems to promise and what I actually believe about particular historical counterfactuals. Here are my verdicts. Sarajevo, June 1914: the motorcade takes a wrong turn, the driver stops to reverse in front of a delicatessen, and one of the conspirators whose attempt had already failed that morning happens to be standing on the pavement there. Contingent — that is a nail. Yet I also think a continental war within a few years was very likely with or without it, and I hold both thoughts without feeling any strain. The Armada of 1588: the storms that wrecked so many ships on the homeward passage around Scotland and Ireland are not a nail in my book, because the invasion had already been defeated by the fireships off Calais and by the Dutch boats keeping Parma's army pinned in shallow water; take away the storms and the outcome is the same, only tidier. Waterloo, 1815: Grouchy hears the guns late in the morning, Gérard urges him to march toward them, and he does not. Contingent at the level of the battle, and still I do not think Napoleon's regime survives the year, because the coalition was raising several times what he could put in the field. Curiously I feel the Grouchy case is a more real nail than the Sarajevo one, and I cannot articulate why. Florida, 2000: a few hundred votes decide the presidency. Genuinely contingent, but I am reluctant to call it a nail at all, on the ground that a procedure designed to turn on single ballots is sensitive by construction rather than by accident — and I do not know whether that is a distinction or a prejudice. The typhoons that destroyed the Mongol invasion fleets in 1274 and 1281: contingent in the strongest sense available, and yet they explain nothing to me, because I cannot trace the divergent world more than a few decades forward. There again I seem to be separating two things I normally run together, namely whether the world would have been different and whether I can say anything about the difference.

The pair I cannot reconcile is in the 1940s. The transistor: robust — several groups in different countries were converging on the same device, and had Bell Labs not produced it in 1947 something equivalent would have appeared within a few years. The decision to bomb cities in August 1945: contingent — a different person in the White House after that April, or a demonstration over uninhabited ground of the kind some of the Chicago physicists urged in June, and no city is destroyed. But the reason I give for calling the transistor robust is that the machinery was already built and the institutional momentum overwhelming, which is precisely a description of the bomb project in the summer of 1945, with its committee, its target list, and its invasion planning. Relatedly, I think killing a backbench MP in a car accident changes nothing while killing a head of state changes a great deal, and my explanation is redundancy: the role has a spare, the person does not. That explanation predicts the opposite verdict about the American presidency in 1945, since by then it was the most heavily staffed and most smoothly substitutable decision point in the world. My tentative principle is that an event is genuinely contingent just in case there is a small, physically and epistemically accessible perturbation of the antecedent state such that the outcome differs, where differing is assessed at the description level at which the question was asked. I am fairly confident that the final clause is a cheat, since it lets me rescue any verdict by re-leveling the description.

So: state the principle, or the two or three candidates worth having, precisely, and tie each clause to the case that motivated it. Tell me whether the description-level clause can be made non-ad-hoc — whether anything independent of my question fixes the level, or whether it must always be fixed by the question, and if the latter, what work the principle is still doing. Say which of my verdicts the best candidate forces me to abandon; I suspect it costs me either the Armada or the transistor-and-bomb pair, and I want to be told which and why. Then give me its verdicts on cases I have not raised, including at least one where the answer makes me uncomfortable. Finally, tell me how much of my professional vocabulary transfers at all: whether structural stability, basins of attraction, and ensemble spread mean anything when the system in question is a set of human institutions, or whether sensitive dependence and it-could-have-gone-otherwise are different kinds of claim entirely, the first being a property of a model and the second a property of the world. I am not after a survey of what writers on historical contingency have said; I want your own reconstruction, argued for.
