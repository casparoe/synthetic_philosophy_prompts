I teach a third-and-fourth-year seminar called "Cooperation, Democracy, and the Limits of Expected Utility" (co-taught with someone from the politics department, who is convinced the whole debate about voter rationality was settled empirically by turnout studies and gets annoyed when I keep bringing up normative questions). I'm writing the closed-book final now — two hours, no notes, and I've told the students in advance that they won't be expected to recall any citations, so the question has to carry everything it needs inside itself. I want one essay question on the paradox of voting, worth 30 marks, and my big constraint is that it must not be answerable by reciting the Downsian calculus, noting that p is astronomically small in a national election, and then bolting on the citizen-duty term that Riker and Ordeshok added as if that were a solution rather than a redescription. Every single one of last year's answers did exactly that and I couldn't tell the strong students from the ones who'd memorized a Wikipedia summary.

So here's the seed material I want you to build the question out of — please use it more or less as given, since the point is that the numbers are tuned to take away the easy outs. **Case: the Mill Road levy.** A town of 12,000 registered voters decides by referendum whether to fund a flood barrier; it passes iff at least 6,000 vote yes. 3,000 of the town's households sit in the flood zone. A poll released three days before the vote, which is stipulated to be accurate and to be common knowledge, puts support at a dead heat, such that each individual flood-zone voter's probability of casting the decisive yes vote is 1/40. Cost of voting is about 45 minutes and €12 in parking, so call it €15; a flood-zone household's expected avoided loss if the levy passes is €6,000 over twenty years. So p×B = €150 against a cost of €15, and the purely instrumental arithmetic says vote — decisively. But there's a twist I want the question to hinge on: every one of the 3,000 flood-zone voters faces the very same numbers and knows the others do too, and nobody can coordinate, communicate, or be selectively rewarded. If they all reason their way to voting, turnout is no longer marginal, the race stops being close, and p collapses toward zero for everyone — the consideration that made voting rational destroys itself once it's普遍 acted on. And here's the second seed: one of my grad students has proposed what she calls the **Divisibility Principle** — when the value of an outcome depends only on how many people contribute, each contributor's reason to contribute equals the total value of the outcome divided by the number who contribute, whether or not their own contribution makes the difference between the outcome's obtaining and its not obtaining. She offers DP as a rescue of the instrumentalist story. I want the question to force students to say precisely what DP does and doesn't buy you: is it still an instrumental account at all, or expressivism/rule-consequentialism wearing its clothes; what it has to say about voters with unequal costs (the one for whom voting costs €200 rather than €15); what it implies in the limit where you're certain you're not decisive; and whether the fact that everyone's shares sum neatly back to the total value is a virtue or a symptom that DP needs an independent account of why reasons should be divided up that way in the first place.

Act as a political philosopher who is also comfortable enough with participation games to formalize a little bit — but keep any formalism to what a third-year with one term of decision theory can follow on paper, and don't expect students to know that literature by name. Before you draft, brainstorm four or five candidate cruxes the case opens up and pick the sharpest; think carefully about which ones a well-prepared student could reach in two hours versus which ones need a whole dissertation. Then give me: (a) the exam question itself, with the case stated compactly enough to read in five minutes and a clear instruction about what the essay must decide; (b) a model answer of about 900 words that actually takes a position rather than surveying options; (c) a marking scheme built on six must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth summing to 30 and a one-line note on how students typically botch each; and (d) a short separate list of moves that should actively lose marks — I'd want that list to include, at minimum, confusing the voting paradox with rational ignorance about political information, treating the D-term as an explanation, and gesturing at Parfit's harmless-torturers style no-difference cases without saying how a threshold outcome with identical contributions differs from a case of imperceptible harms. One more thing I care about alot: the tuned p of 1/40 is supposed to break the standard move of saying "expressive voting solves it," because expressive benefits are cheap for a voter precisely when the chance of affecting the outcome is negligible — at 1/40 you can no longer cheer for your side while ignoring what your ballot does. Make sure the marking scheme rewards a student who spots that, and be explicit about how many marks it's worth.
