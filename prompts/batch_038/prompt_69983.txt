I am a junior in high school and I have no formal training in philosophy. My serious extracurricular is poker: I have logged something on the order of forty thousand hands of small-stakes no-limit hold'em, I read solver output more often than I read anything else, and I habitually think in terms of ranges, pot odds, expected value, variance, exploitative versus game-theory-optimal play, bankroll management, tilt, and playing the player. For my European history class we read Machiavelli's Prince in English translation, and the two chapters that actually held my attention were the one on what power fortune has over human affairs and the one on whether princes should keep their word.

Our assignment is to write about Machiavelli on fortune and virtù, and most of my classmates seem to be writing about whether he is a cynic. I would rather do something more exact. So: please restate his account of fortuna and virtù as faithfully as you can into the vocabulary I use, and then tell me what did not survive the translation.

Please handle these specific claims rather than giving me an overview: the image of fortune as a flooding river against which men can still build dikes and barriers in fair weather; the assertion that fortune arbitrates roughly half of our actions and leaves us the other half, or perhaps a little less; the argument that the impetuous succeed better than the coldly cautious, together with the claim that men are undone because their natures do not change when the times change, with Pope Julius II as his case; the instruction to be both fox and lion and to appear merciful, faithful, humane, and religious rather than to be so unconditionally; and Cesare Borgia, who on Machiavelli's telling possessed extraordinary capacity and still lost everything.

I also want you to test a thesis of mine instead of simply flattering it. I suspect that virtù is essentially exploitative play — reading the table and deviating from a default line — rather than a fixed optimal strategy, and that Machiavelli's contempt for the cautious man is the same point a coach makes about a regular who cannot adjust. Tell me where that reading holds and where it breaks.

Be honest about the translation in both directions. Do not present expected value, variance, or equilibrium reasoning as though Machiavelli possessed them, and flag every place where my vocabulary makes his claims look more quantitative or more predictive than they are. In particular, I want to know whether the claim that fortune governs half of our actions is a statistical estimate in any recognizable sense or a rhetorical way of saying a great deal but not everything, and whether my instinct to hear it as a statement about the share of variance in outcomes is simply an error.

Then give me the residue report in three lists: claims that translate cleanly, naming the poker term that carries each one; claims that translate only with distortion, stating precisely what bends; and claims with no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For each item in the second and third lists, say whether the failure is a limitation of poker thinking or evidence that Machiavelli's claim was less clear than it first appeared.

Two candidates I expect to land in the third list, which I would like you to confirm or correct. First, poker is played under a fixed rulebook, in finite hands, against opponents whose only objective is to win chips, with a transcript large enough that skill and luck can in principle be separated; a prince can alter the game itself, faces a population that must be made to love or fear him rather than merely beaten, and has a sample size of one. Does that difference destroy the analogy or only bound it? Second, Machiavelli consistently personifies fortune as a woman, and the register of that personification seems to be doing argumentative work; I have no term for it. Is that a defect in my framework or a sign that the passage is ornament?

Finally, I do not want a summary of The Prince, and I do not want to be told that his Italian resists translation. Assume I have read the book once, carefully, in English.
