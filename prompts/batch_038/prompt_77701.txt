I have no training in philosophy or law; I run a regional warehouse and do most of my reading in the evenings. Two years ago our city council spent four consecutive meetings on whether to stop enforcing an ordinance, on the books since the 1930s, that bars most retail trading on Sundays. One councilman argued that the rule exists because this community once felt strongly that Sunday trading was a desecration, that enough residents still feel some version of that, and that the city is therefore entitled to keep enforcing it even though no one is hurt by a hardware store opening its doors; his opponent answered that morals legislation of that kind is only a photograph of whatever the local majority happened to feel when the ordinance passed, so it cannot be argued with, only outlived. I went looking for the reasoning underneath both speeches and ended up in the mid-century British exchange between Patrick Devlin and H. L. A. Hart, which I have been working through slowly with a friend who is a nurse and no better trained than I am. Trying to settle which councilman had the better of it, I wrote out the following chain, and I do not care for the place it arrives at.

(1) What makes a collection of people one society rather than a crowd that happens to share an address is a common morality: a body of convictions about how people ought to behave that most members hold and expect one another to hold. (2) A society whose common morality dissolves stops being a society at all, since nothing then binds its members to one another but force and convenience, and it comes apart. (3) So a society is entitled to defend its common morality by law for the same reason and in the same way it is entitled to defend itself against invasion or treason: self-preservation is self-preservation. (4) The content of that common morality is not set by philosophers or by scripture but read off the ordinary reasonable person, as a jury of such people would stand for them; strong disgust, indignation, and intolerance felt toward a practice is how one identifies a practice as offending the common morality. (5) It follows that the law may prohibit whatever reliably provokes those feelings in the ordinary reasonable person, harmed or not, because the thing to be prevented is the fraying of the common morality, and (2) says fraying is what destroys a society. (6) But public feeling drifts: conduct that disgusted one generation leaves the next indifferent, and conduct once tolerated comes in time to be found disgusting. (7) By (4) through (6), then, whether conduct is criminal depends only on how the ordinary person's stomach happens to be turning at the time, and the same principle that required prohibition in one decade permits the conduct in the next. (8) A principle on which the criminal law is a register of shifting sentiment, and on which no moral criticism of the law is ever available because whatever a society feels strongly is by definition what it may enforce, is not a principle of political morality at all; therefore (1) is false, and a common morality is not what holds a society together.

I would much rather keep (1) and (2): I cannot shake the sense that a place where nobody shares any expectations about conduct is not a community in any sense I care about, and (8) throws that away to win an argument about Sunday trading. So tell me where the chain should be broken before (8), which step you would reject first, why that step looked respectable when I wrote it down, and what the rejection costs whoever makes it. If instead you think the reasoning is valid and the only problem is that some premise is simply untrue, say so plainly and name the premise a defender of the judge's position could surrender with the least damage, plus the one he must not surrender. Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing remarks. In those five points, keep straight what is now settled about this exchange among people who work on it and what remains genuinely contested, and please do not hand me a disputed position dressed up as the consensus.
