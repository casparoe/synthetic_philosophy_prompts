Question as set:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems are routinely invoked outside mathematics — in philosophy of mind, in theology, in literary and social theory, in popular science — as showing that formal systems cannot capture all truth, that minds outrun machines, or that no system of thought can ground itself. Candidate's answer (paragraphs numbered for reference):

1. Both results depend on the fact that a theory of arithmetic can talk about its own syntax. Assigning a code to each symbol, term, formula and proof lets the theory express, by an ordinary arithmetical formula, the relation "x codes a proof of the formula with code y." Because the coding is recursive, the theory can represent that relation provided it is strong enough to represent the recursive functions at all, and Robinson arithmetic already suffices. The diagonal lemma then guarantees that for any formula φ(x) there is a sentence G provably equivalent to φ(⌜G⌝). Taking φ(x) to be "x is not the code of a provable sentence" gives the Gödel sentence, which says of itself that it is not provable in the theory.

2. The first incompleteness theorem: any consistent, recursively axiomatized theory strong enough to represent the recursive functions is incomplete — there is a sentence G such that the theory proves neither G nor its negation. Gödel's own 1931 proof assumed the stronger hypothesis of ω-consistency; Rosser showed that plain consistency is enough by complicating the fixed point, so that the sentence says in effect that if it has a proof then its negation has a shorter one. Two clarifications are worth making. "Undecidable" here means neither provable nor refutable, not "no algorithm settles it," which is a different result about different objects. And incompleteness is a property of theories rather than of logic: first-order logic is sound and complete, in the sense that every valid formula is provable, so nothing here suggests the logical machinery is defective.

3. Because G is equivalent to a Π₁ claim — that no number has a certain recursive property — its truth follows from consistency alone. If G were false, some standard number would code a proof of it; the theory would then prove both that this number codes a proof of G, since it proves every true Σ₁ sentence, and G itself, hence ¬G by the fixed point, and so would be inconsistent. We can therefore see from outside that G is true and unprovable. This is what people mean by "true but unprovable," and it is also where the significance of the result is usually misstated. What Gödel's argument really shows is that arithmetic cannot define its own truth: if a theory could define a truth predicate for its own language, the same diagonal construction would produce a liar sentence and the theory would be inconsistent. Incompleteness is thus fundamentally about truth rather than proof, and G is a truth that escapes the expressive reach of the theory altogether.

4. The second theorem says that such a theory, if consistent, cannot prove the arithmetical sentence expressing its own consistency. This is what bears on Hilbert's programme, which sought a finitary proof of the consistency of strong theories, ideally one carried out in a weak and unimpeachable fragment. Since a theory cannot certify itself, the programme failed outright, and I think the conclusion can be put more strongly still: we can never have a positive reason to believe that Peano arithmetic is consistent. Any consistency proof available to us must be conducted in a theory that we cannot certify either, so the regress never terminates, and confidence in arithmetic ultimately rests on trust rather than on proof.

5. I want to evaluate the mind-and-machine claim, which I take to be the most interesting of the extra-mathematical appeals. The argument runs as follows. Suppose my mathematical output is produced by some Turing-equivalent programme, that is, by a recursively axiomatized theory T. If T is sound, then T is consistent, so by the second theorem T cannot prove Con(T). Yet I can see that Con(T) holds, because I know that T faithfully reproduces my reasoning and that my reasoning is sound. So I can establish something T cannot, and I am therefore not T. This strikes me as decisive, and the familiar objections beg the question. To suggest that I might be unsound, or inconsistent, is to concede that the objector cannot produce a complete specification of me, which is only to admit that no such specification exists.

6. Finally, the general moral. The hypotheses are technical, but what they amount to is that a system must be able to represent its own reasoning. Any body of doctrine that can talk about its own sentences — a legal system reasoning about its statutes, a moral theory reasoning about its principles, a physical theory reasoning about its models — meets that condition, and is therefore subject to incompleteness. That is why these theorems have been so generative outside mathematics: they show that no system of thought can be at once self-contained and self-grounding.
