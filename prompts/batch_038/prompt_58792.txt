ok so I've been down a rabbit hole on societal collapse, and the thing that's bugging me isn't any one case — it's that I don't seem to apply the word consistently, and I want to know what I'm actually committed to. Here are my gut calls, and I've tried to say why each one feels the way it does:

- Norse Greenland: collapse, obviously. Last record is a wedding at Hvalsey in 1408 and then nothing, and a century later nobody's living there. No continuity of anything, not even the lifeway.
- Classic Maya: collapse — even though I know perfectly well that Maya people and Maya languages are alive today. The dated monuments just stop (last Long Count date I've seen cited is 909 CE, at Toniná), the kings-carved-in-stone business is finished, the southern lowland cities empty out.
- Easter Island: not collapse, just an ugly change. The palm forest goes, moai carving stops, but the islanders stay and keep fishing and gardening, and the newer work — including the ancient DNA stuff — seems to say the population was more or less stable until Europeans arrived, and *then* it was a catastrophe. Pre-contact looks like adaptation to me.
- Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean: collapse. Palaces burned, Linear B never comes back, the Hittite state is gone. But the Greeks are obviously still there and much of the Levant carries on, so maybe only a palatial system collapsed and no society did. I'm uneasy here.
- Angkor: not collapse. The capital drains away in the 1400s, but the court relocates south, and the religion that takes over is the one still practiced there. That's a transformation with a very high body count for the old elite.
- Cahokia: not collapse. It reads like people voting with their feet and dispersing into the countryside. Except I notice I'm not willing to say the same thing about some of the others, and I can't articulate why.
- Roman Britain after 400: collapse. Villa economy gone, towns hollowed out, coinage stops, the Latin-speaking elite becomes archaeologically invisible within a couple of generations. And yet there's no mass die-off and the farms keep farming.

My problem pair is Maya vs. Easter Island. On paper they're nearly the same event: a monument-building theocratic system stops building monuments, the environment is degraded, most of the population and thier language survive. One I call collapse and one I don't. Roman Britain vs. Cahokia is the same shape of problem.

My tentative principle is something like: collapse is a society dropping a level of sociopolitical complexity it was previously maintaining — states, specialists, redistribution, monumentality — regardless of whether the people die or their descendants flourish. But that seems to make Easter Island a collapse too (no more moai, no more chiefly competitive display), which I don't want, and it also makes a peaceful voluntary downsizing a "collapse," which sounds wrong.

So: is there a principle, or a couple of competing ones, that generates my verdicts or gets most of them at an honest cost? I want it stated precisely enough that it could come out wrong on a case — some threshold on complexity levels, or a distinction between elite institutions and subsistence systems, or losing a state versus losing a lifeway, or reversibility. Tell me which clause does the work for which case, then tell me straight up which of my calls it cannot save and which one I should drop. Then run it on three cases I haven't given verdicts on: the Indus cities being abandoned while the population drifts east toward the Ganges plain; Chaco Canyon's great houses being walked away from; and the Soviet Union. Two more things I want folded in: does it matter whether the word is being used by the people who lived through it or by us afterwards, and does my list show that I'm smuggling in a moral — they wrecked their environment and paid for it — even in the cases where I insist I'm only describing institutional change?
