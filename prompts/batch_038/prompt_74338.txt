I spent fourty years doing effective theories and renormalization, mostly condensed matter and critical phenomena, and since retiring I've fallen down a hole reading philosophy of mathematics — specifically the platonist/nominalist fight over whether numbers, sets, and Hilbert spaces are things that are out there or just ways of talking. Trouble is I can't stop hearing the whole debate in my old vocabulary, and I'm worried I'm fooling myself with a bad analogy that happens to feel comfortable. So here's what I'd like you to do.

Take these five: (a) the platonist position, (b) the nominalist position, (c) the argument that we ought to believe in mathematical objects because our best physical theories quantify over them ineliminably, (d) the nominalist counter-program that rewrites enough of physics to recover the same empirical content without that quantification, and (e) the worry about how we could ever have knowledge of, or any contact with, causally inert abstract objects. Translate each one into the language I actually think in: degrees of freedom, cutoff and scale, what's universal versus what's scheme-dependent, running couplings, effective descriptions, redundant parameterizations, gauge fixing, what a theory is committed to at a given resolution. Be honest about it — don't quietly import an assumption from my side of the fence and then hand it back to me labelled as a translation.

Then the residue, which is the part I actually care about. For each of the five, say whether it came across clean, whether it only came across with distortion (and exactly what you had to bend to make it fit), or whether there is no counterpart for it in my vocabulary at all. And tell me which way to read each failure: places where the residue shows my framework is missing a distinction the philosophers need, versus places where it suggests their question was less definite than it looked.

One extra thing, my own little test, which you're welcome to tell me is useless. I've been calling it the scheme-invariance test: a claim about mathematical objects counts as substantive only if it comes out the same under a change of what I'd call the descriptive scheme, the way a statement isn't real physics unless it survives a change of regularization. Run the debate through it and say which side, if either, has claims that fail it — and whether failing it is fatal or just means the claim is doing something other than describing.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per lettered thing above, two sentences each at most — the translation, the verdict on how much of it survived, and a clause about the scheme-invariance test. No introduction, no conclusion, no wrap-up paragraph. If you think one of the five is a trap, or is secretly the same claim as another one, still give it its own item and say so inside it.
