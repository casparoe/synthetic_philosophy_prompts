Question: Among the maxims Kant considers in the Groundwork are that of a man who borrows money by promising to repay it while knowing that he never will, and that of a person with a natural talent who resolves to give himself over to amusement rather than cultivate it. Explain how the Formula of Universal Law is supposed to generate different results in these two cases. In your answer, say what work is being done by the idea of a contradiction in conception and by the idea of a contradiction in the will, and explain what the contrast between the two cases is meant to show about the difference between perfect and imperfect duties. (20 marks)

Answer (J.M.): The Formula of Universal Law says we may act only on a maxim we can at the same time will to become a universal law. What is being tested is the maxim, that is the principle the agent acts on, rather than the action or its effects: the question is not whether everyone doing it would produce a worse world, but whether the maxim can be thought as a universal law at all. So it is a test of consistency in willing, not of cost and benefit. Take the borrower first. His maxim cannot even be conceived as a universal law. If everyone who needed money made a promise they knew they would not keep, the practice of promising would collapse, since no one would believe such a promise and the borrower could not achieve by promising what he is trying to achieve. The maxim destroys itself when universalised, and that is a contradiction in conception. Because it fails at this first stage it generates a perfect duty, here a perfect duty to others, and perfect duties admit of no exceptions: there is no circumstances in which I may borrow on a false promise. The talented person is different. A world in which everyone lets their abilities rust is perfectly conceivable; nothing about it is self-defeating in the way promising is, since people can amuse themselves quite happily without ever developing anything. So the maxim survives the first stage and the contradiction has to appear in the will instead. The reason she cannot will that universal law is that she may one day need the products of other people's cultivated talents — a surgeon, an engineer, somebody who bothered to learn enough to keep a farm going — and in willing a world where nobody develops their capacities she deprives herself of exactly the help she may later want. The maxim of never assisting anyone fails for the same reason: universalising it would leave you without the aid you might one day need. So contradictions in the will always have this shape, where the agent wills something now that she could not want if it came back round on her universally applied. Since she fails only at the second stage her duty is an imperfect one, and imperfect duties are wide, in that she may choose which talents to develop and when. But that latitude concerns only the form her compliance takes, not how much of it there is: an imperfect duty still requires you to do as much as you can, because doing less would be making an exception for yourself, and making an exception of yourself is what the whole test exists to catch. The difference between the two duties is therefore not that one binds less strongly — both bind absolutely — but that the first tells you precisely what to refrain from, while the second leaves you to work out what developing your talents amounts to in your own case.
