I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so I'll try to describe the setup precisely and flag where I'm unsure I'm using the right terms.

I own a service that picks one candidate action from a small set under uncertainty — roughly, choose a routing policy or a retry budget, where each candidate has a probability distribution over outcomes that we estimate from a model and then score. For a long time we scored candidates by expected value. After an incident in which the highest-EV candidate carried a small probability of a catastrophic outcome, we replaced it with a risk-adjusted score: expected value minus a penalty proportional to the estimated variance, plus a hard disqualification for any candidate whose estimated probability of a catastrophic outcome exceeds a fixed threshold. Two properties of this score seem relevant. First, it is a single fixed function of the outcome distribution, applied identically at every call, so its output is a total order and we have never seen a cycle. Second, because the penalty depends on the whole distribution and not just the mean, the ordering between two candidates can flip as our estimates get sharper, even when no new candidate has appeared and nothing about the candidates themselves has changed.

In the design review, a colleague who reads a lot of rationality material objected with something like: 'if you're not maximizing expected utility, you're leaving money on the table — and someone will come along and take it.' When I asked him to describe the transaction in which we lose, he said the exploit was a theorem, mentioned Dutch books, and the discussion moved on. I want to answer him properly next time rather than be talked past.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making? For each reading I'd like to know: exactly what it asserts; what is being called coherent or incoherent (our beliefs, our preferences over outcomes, our preferences over acts, or our observed choice behavior); whether it is a claim about one decision or about a sequence of decisions; whether it is a mathematical theorem, a substantive but defensible thesis, a platitude that does no argumentative work, or simply false; and what assumptions it depends on — that an agent will accept any individually favorable offer, that stakes add up without bound or transaction cost, that the sequence of offers cannot be anticipated, that our estimates are fixed rather than updated — together with a judgment about which of those assumptions are likely to fail in a deployed system making repeated decisions against counterparties who are trying to win.
