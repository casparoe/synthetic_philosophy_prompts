Our reading group spans a philosophy department and a cognitive-science laboratory, and we have reached an impasse that I suspect turns on an analogy rather than on any evidence either side could produce.

The analogy runs as follows. In the philosophy of science it is a commonplace that observation is theory-laden: the radiologist sees a lesion in the shadow where the student sees mottling, the crystallographer sees a lattice in the diffraction pattern, and the convert to a new paradigm is said to see a different world altogether on the far side of a gestalt shift. A chapter that one of our members is defending moves from that commonplace to a substantive thesis about perception — if trained, theory-soaked seeing of this kind is genuinely perceptual, so that the radiologist's experience itself and not merely her description of it differs from the student's, then perception is cognitively penetrable, and experience therefore cannot function as an independent check upon the beliefs that shape it. The analogy is doing real work: it is being used to license a sceptical claim about the epistemic standing of perceptual justification, and my friend says that licence is exactly what it cannot confer.

The objector in our group holds that the comparison equivocates at every joint: that what is theory-laden in science is judgement, description, and the interpretation of instrument readouts rather than the sensory episode; that the expertise cases are perceptual learning, which is not penetration by belief at all; and that scientific observation is a trained, norm-governed, social practice in a way that ordinary seeing is not.

For concreteness, our own stock case: after a week of being taught field marks, my flatmate reports that the warblers in the hedge now look different to her. She sees the wing bars she was told to look for, where previously she saw only small brown birds.

Please audit the analogy, addressing the following in order:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly. For each element of the source — the theory or paradigm, the training, the apparatus or representation, the datum, the expert's seeing, the novice's seeing, the community's norms for what counts as a correct sighting — state what, if anything, plays that role in the target.
2. Triage the disanalogies: which of them bear on the inference from theory-laden scientific observation to cognitive penetration, and which are true of the comparison but irrelevant to the use being made of it.
3. Identify what the analogy quietly imports into perception that ordinary seeing may lack — for instance, that the object of seeing in the scientific case is itself a representation produced by an apparatus, or that the relevant vocabulary is acquired rather than recognised.
4. Rule on the flatmate case. Is it penetration by belief, perceptual learning, attentional selection, or a change in report, and does whichever verdict you reach generalise to the radiologist, or does the radiologist differ in a way that matters?
5. Deliver a verdict on the conclusion: does the sceptical claim about perceptual justification survive the disanalogies, and if it survives only in weakened form, state precisely the form it can bear and what further premises would be needed to restore the full version.
6. Offer a better analogy for the thesis the chapter wants — or say that none is available — and specify how much of the argumentative weight it can actually carry.
7. Say whether the distinction between phenomenal character and perceptual judgement resolves the dispute or merely relocates it, and which side that distinction favours if it does any work at all.

Two constraints on the response. First, please write in American English. Second, do not supply references, bibliographies, or a survey of who holds which position; I want the audit conducted from the structure of the cases themselves, and it should not presuppose acquaintance with any particular paper. Something in the region of 1,200 words would be appropriate.
