Every year the same thing happens on my second-year archaeology-of-collapse module. They do the Tainter reading — complexity as a problem-solving strategy, investment in sociopolitical complexity subject to diminishing marginal returns — and they nod along, and then six weeks later the essays are full of "short-sighted elites", "failure to adapt", "mismanagement". The idea goes in as a proposition and comes out as a moral judgement. Nobody has actually felt what it's like to be the polity watching the returns soften underneath you while every individual decision still looks locally sensible.

So I want a game rather than another case study. 18 students, one two-hour seminar slot (call it 100 usable minutes), second years who've had a single week of lecture on this and nothing else. Teams of three, so six polities. Paper and pencil only — no app, no money, no laptops for the players.

Core loop: each team runs a small agrarian polity for eight or nine rounds. Every round they take a harvest, pay upkeep, and decide whether to add a layer of complexity — an irrigation scheme, a scribal administration, a standing garrison, a redistributive temple store, a road and waystation network, a tribute-collection apparatus, that sort of thing. Each layer raises the yield but adds a permanent upkeep cost, and I want the marginal return to genuinely decline: layer one might net +4 surplus, layer two +3, layer three +2, layer four +1, layer five zero or negative. By round six the team that kept building should be visibly worse off than the team that stopped at three, and it should get there with no disaster at all. That's the whole point — the curve does the damage on a calm day.

Things to build in, roughly in order of importance:

1. A buffer. Surplus carries forward, and it's the buffer rather than the harvest that keeps a polity alive, so the harm from over-building is deferred and invisible until something draws it down.

2. Shock rounds. Two or three rounds carry a random shock — drought, a raid, a failed harvest — drawn by dice or from a deck, survivable early and lethal late purely because the buffer's gone. Don't mention shocks in the briefing at all. The first one should land as a surprise in round three or four.

3. The constituency mechanic. This is the bit I can never get across in a lecture and I want you to design it properly. When a team builds a layer, a named interest group inside the polity attaches itself to it (temple households, garrison officers, the scribes, the muleteers on the road). Dismantling a layer then costs political capital in proportion to how long that group has existed and how much it has grown. So simplification is arithmetically correct and politically expensive, and teams find out for themselves that the reason polities don't downsize isn't stupidity. Give political capital its own seperate little economy — earned by feeding the groups, spent on overriding them — and make running out of it consequential, e.g. a layer you can't pay for gets removed by somebody else's decision instead of yours.

4. A "strip the fixtures" move. Once, maybe twice, a team should be able to consume a layer outright — burn the roof beams, quarry the temple for stone, eat the seed grain, melt down the garrison's bronze — for a large one-off surplus that buys them a round or two, at the cost of the layer immediately and permanently. I've seen this called catabolic collapse, a society eating its own capital, and it's what lets a doomed team stagger on rather than go under. It should feel grim and tempting at the same time.

5. It has to be winnable by downsizing. This is the make-or-break condition: if the best line is just "build sensibly and stop at three", the game teaches nothing and I'll bin it. Rank teams at the end on something other than raw complexity — I'd suggest a combination of surviving population, final surplus, and layers still functioning — tuned so that a team which deliberately dismantles two layers in round seven and finishes lean and alive beats a team that held on to everything and got eaten by upkeep in round nine. I want at least one table to arrive at the decision to make their polity smaller, and then to notice afterwards that they experienced it as a success rather than a death.

Give me: the full rules text as something I can hand out on one side of A4; the layer table with build costs, yields and upkeep; the shock deck or table; the scoring formula worked through with a sample game so I can check the numbers actually behave; a minute-by-minute run sheet including where the break goes; what to print or bring; and a short facilitator script for the round transitions that gives nothing away.

Then the debrief. I want to map specific moments onto the concepts — be able to say "table four, what happened to you in round seven" and have it land as an instance of diminishing marginal returns, of complexity as a strategy rather than a virtue, of collapse as economising. Give me the line that introduces the distinction between collapse-as-failure and collapse-as-downsizing only at that point, and keep the vocabulary out of the handout entirely: they should meet the terms as labels for something they've just done.

The objection comes up every year and it will come up again — that the game is a cheat because real polities didn't get to sit in a room and choose to simplify, they were dissolved by other people's decisions. Answer it honestly rather than waving it away, and I'd like the answer to include the point that a polity hemmed in by other complex societies may not have the option of collapsing at all, since a neighbour will absorb or occupy the vacancy, whereas one on a frontier or an island might. If you can build that into the rules rather than leaving it to the debrief — some tables geographically boxed in and unable to downsize even when they want to, others not — so much the better, but only if it doesn't make the scoring unfair between teams.

And two frank bits at the end. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I'd guess a team hoards surplus and refuses to engage, or the political-capital bookkeeping is too fiddly to maintain while they're arguing, or one sharp student reverse-engineers the optimal stopping rule in round two and announces it to the room, killing the discovery. Tell me which of those actually bites and how to design against it, plus whatever else you can foresee. And what the game structurally cannot reproduce — I already suspect two things: real complexity buys capacities that don't show up in a grain ledger (information processing, prestige, the ability to mobilise at all, the plain fact that some polities could feed cities), and my teams are playing against each other and a deck of cards rather than against a landscape that degrades under them. Say what those absences cost me and exactly what I have to supply myself in the debrief to fill them in.
