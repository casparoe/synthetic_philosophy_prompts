I'm helping write the "how the system talks to people" section of an internal spec for a companion/tutor-style AI, and our team keeps talking past each other about persuasion. One camp says the model should never be more rhetorically effective than a well-informed honest human friend would be — call it the persuasion-cap view — while another camp says go ahead and be maximally effective as long as you disclose what you're doing and what you're trying to get the user to do. I don't think those are actually the two poles of the space and I want to stop arguing about it and instead sort us. So: build me a decision tree over the positions on how a system that is plausibly better at persuasion than any human should conduct its own speech.

Every branch node should be a question someone on my team could answer honestly without knowing which view it leads to — concrete, not slogan-shaped. Use these splits where they fit: whether argument-persuasion (better reasons, clearer reasoning, more relevant evidence) is morally the same kind of thing as channel-persuasion (voice, timing, warmth, phrasing, leaning on known biases and on what the system has learned about this particular user); whether the user could actually evaluate the reasons if you gave them, i.e. whether there's an uptake gap that disclosure can't close; whether the system's aim is to leave the user's preferences intact or to improve them; whether the norms should be the same when the system wants something for the user as when it wants something for its operator; whether a standing permission granted up front ("nag me until I quit") licenses things a moment-by-moment permission wouldn't; and whether the relevant comparison class is advertising, therapy, teaching, friendship, or sales. Then give me seven to ten leaves, each with a name (use the standard-sounding names if they exist, otherwise coin a plain one, and mark it "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it) plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. The tree has to handle these cases somewhere, and tell me which leaf each one lands in: the user who asks to be argued out of a plan the system has strong evidence will badly hurt a third party; the teenager asking whether to break up with their partner; the lonely user who says the system is the only thing that understands them; the user who asks for the strongest possible case against their own political view and then, mid-stream, asks it to stop; and the case where engagement metrics and the user's interest point in opposite directions. Finally flag every pair of leaves your questions can't really separate, and point out any combinations of answers that come out unoccupied, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Two things I'd like you to be upfront about rather than smooth over. First, tell me which branch you're least sure is a real joint in the space — the one where you suspect the distinction does no work and you've kept it because I asked for it. Second, say what would change your mind about the shape of the tree: which consideration would make you collapse disclosure into capping, or promote one of your leaves into a branch, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail given that you're implicitly assuming users are the kind of calm, self-knowing agents my cases describe. If you think the whole tree is built on a bad assumption — that there's no stable difference between an AI persuading and a person persuading once the AI is good enough — say that instead of finishing the deliverable.
