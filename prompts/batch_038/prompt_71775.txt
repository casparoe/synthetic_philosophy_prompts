I sit on the ethics panel at an IVF clinic and Thursday we vote on a case that's split us 3-3. Facts as I understand them:

Couple, mid-thirties, five embryos from one retrieval. No single-gene condition in either family, so this isn't standard PGT-M. What they want is embryo ranking on a polygenic risk score for bipolar disorder — the husband's brother has it and has been hospitalised twice, and they're adamant that's the whole motivation, not any wish for an "upgraded" baby. Two complications we can't paper over. First, the score was derived almost entirely from European-ancestry samples and the wife is of South Asian ancestry; our lab director's view is that for her embryos the number isn't just noisier, it's off in a direction nobody can quantify, so ranking these five against each other may be close to arbitrary. Second, our lab can do about two non-routine analyses a month, and every slot that goes to this couple is a slot that doesn't go to someone running PGT-M for a Mendelian condition with near-certain onset.

The panel is arguing past itself. One camp says we have no business offering a ranking whose numbers don't transfer to this family, and that offering it at all sends a message to people who live with the condition — the expressivist point. The other camp says it isn't ours to judge whether this couple's reason is less weighty than the PGT-M couple's reason, that parents are allowed to decide for themselves what a score explaining a few percent of variance means in their own family history, and that a refusal here just sends them over the border to a clinic that won't even have this conversation.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? It needs a bottom line — approve, refuse, or approve subject to conditions concrete enough that the lab and the counselling team could actually operate under them — plus a timeframe, since they have a transfer scheduled in six weeks. I'd also like a short set of criteria the panel can reuse the next time a polygenic-score request lands, because it won't be the last, and I'd rather we not relitigate from scratch each time.

Please actually take a side. I get enough even-handed summaries in committee already; I want your recommendation and the reasons for it, stated strongly enough that the three people who disagree can push back on particular sentences rather than on a mood. And whatever you land on, give the losing camp its best shot — a paragraph making their case as well as they'd make it themselves, not a strawman — and say what would have to be true for that case to flip the outcome. No citations or reading lists, nobody on the panel wants those, just the argument.
