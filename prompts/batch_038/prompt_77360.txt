For thirty-six years I was a hydrogeologist, and for the last fifteen of those I worked on performance assessment for deep geological disposal of radioactive waste. That work depends almost entirely on quantities nobody has measured and, in several cases, nobody could measure in principle: how often a borehole intrudes a waste emplacement drift in the next ten thousand years, what fraction of infiltrating water reaches a repository horizon, how fast a particular radionuclide sorbs onto fractured tuff. So the agencies that fund such work run formal expert elicitation exercises, and I sat on or facilitated a good many of them. I retired three years ago and have since taken up philosophy seriously, and the procedure I know intimately from the inside now strikes me as philosophically odd in ways I could not have articulated at the time. I would like you to reconstruct what it presupposes.

The protocol, in the versions I know, runs roughly like this. Each panelist is asked, for each quantity, for a small set of quantiles: a value they judge there to be only a five percent chance the true quantity falls below, a median, and a value they judge there to be only a five percent chance it exceeds. Before the live questions they answer the same sort of question about quantities whose answers the facilitator already knows, so that the panel's calibration can be checked; nearly everyone's intervals turn out to be far too narrow, and the facilitator coaches them to widen. The individual judgments are then combined into a single distribution — usually by averaging, sometimes with weights derived from each member's calibration performance — and that one distribution is what enters the risk model and, through it, the regulatory decision. Where the panel disagrees substantially, the usual remedy I encountered was to widen the final distribution until it encompassed the spread of the members' views.

With that in view:

1. What conception of probability does the protocol presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that a single expert's belief about a physical quantity can always be captured exactly by one distribution, so that the only legitimate form of reported uncertainty is spread inside a distribution rather than a family of distributions? I have read a little about representing belief with a set of probability functions instead of one — I believe the phrase is imprecise probability — but I do not know whether that machinery is even applicable to a case like this, or whether it would simply be a notational indulgence.

2. The protocol widens intervals for two apparently different reasons: because the individual is overconfident, and because the panel disagrees. Are these the same kind of correction? One seems to concern a person's ignorance of the world; the other concerns the existence of competent rival opinions. Does using the same instrument — a wider interval — for both presuppose that they amount to the same thing, and if not, what is lost in the collapse?

3. On averaging: if one member says the annual frequency is on the order of one in a million and another says one in a thousand, the mean is a number neither would endorse, and in a model nonlinear in that input the average of the inputs is not the input that yields the average of the results. What does taking the mean presuppose about the character of the disagreement — that the members are noisy measurements of one true value, so that the spread is estimation error, rather than that the evidence underdetermines the value? Please also say what turns on averaging the numbers versus averaging the whole distributions.

4. Separate what the procedure strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Several accounts of probability — subjectivist, a propensity-flavored reading, a hypothetical-reference-class reading, something purely epistemic and single-case — all seem able to license the same operations. Identify where the protocol is genuinely underdetermined, and where it does quietly foreclose a view.

5. What would have to change if the panel were permitted to report a set of distributions, or lower and upper bounds, instead of one? Trace the consequences through the downstream machinery. A threshold test — is the frequency below one in ten thousand — can apparently be answered with a set, though sometimes only with a verdict of "undetermined"; an optimization or a benefit-cost ranking may not be answerable at all. I suspect the demand for a single number comes less from a theory of probability than from the shape of the decision procedure the number feeds, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives scrutiny or is too facile.

6. Finally, a consistency question about the institution. The same agencies that commission these carefully calibrated single distributions also require, elsewhere in the same assessment, deliberately conservative or bounding assumptions that everyone agrees are biased and are not best estimates. Does an organization that in one place insists on a single calibrated distribution and in another on a knowingly distorted number hold two incompatible views of what a probability is for? Or is there a coherent account on which both belong, and if so, what does that account say about which of the two numbers is really doing the deciding?

Two requests about how you answer. First, be as committed as the material honestly allows: tell me which parts of your reconstruction you would defend and which are speculative, and where a question has a settled or majority answer, give it rather than retreating into "it depends." Second, and more important, push back. If my framing rests on a confusion — if widening for overconfidence and widening for disagreement really are the same operation and I have manufactured a puzzle out of nothing, or if this practice presupposes no philosophical view at all because it is simply an administrative convention that has outlived its rationale — say so plainly at the outset rather than humoring me. I spent a career having colleagues tell me when my error bars were meaningless, and I found that far more useful than polite agreement.

Please write in American English. Where you introduce a technical term, gloss it in a sentence; I am not asking for a reference list or a reading list, and I would rather you reason the matter through than supply sources I cannot check.
