For the closing ten minutes of a graduate seminar on biological information — an audience split roughly evenly between philosophers of biology and working molecular biologists — I would like a poem about the genetic code, and more precisely about its wobble position.

The form should be eighteen lines in six tercets, unrhymed, in a steady free-verse line of around ten to twelve syllables, so that the ear settles into a regular gait before anything happens to disturb it. The governing constraint is this: the third line of each tercet must be a near-copy of the third line of the tercet before it, differing from it in exactly one word. The first tercet supplies the original line; each subsequent tercet alters it once, and only once. Nothing else in the poem may recur — no refrain elsewhere, no rhyme between stanzas, no repeated image announced as repeated — so that this drifting third line is the single thing the reader learns to listen for. If you give the poem a title, let the title be one further alteration of that line.

The content should be exact without ever becoming explanatory. It should accommodate the fact that the first two positions of a codon are read strictly while the third is read loosely; that most of the code's redundancy is gathered at that loose end; and that the substitutions this permits are "silent" only in the narrow sense that the amino acid comes out the same, since the cell registers differences the codon table does not record — pace of elongation, the folding of a protein as it is being made, the fate of the transcript. That last cluster should be felt rather than named: please do not list terms like codon usage bias or translational efficiency, and do not gloss them in a parenthetical. A reader who teaches this material should find nothing to correct; a reader who has never heard of it should find the poem coherent on its own terms.

The argument I want the form to carry is this: five of the wobbles should be perfectly absorbable — the sense survives the substitution, as it is supposed to — and the sixth should not be. In the final tercet the one changed word should alter what the line means outright, and should tip the poem toward the thought that the significance of a triplet was never a property of the triplet itself but of whoever is doing the reading, since in mitochondria, and in certain single-celled organisms, the same three letters cease to mean what they mean in the table on the seminar room wall. Someone who knows this should experience the last tercet as a click of recognition; someone who does not should experience it as a door opening.

Please resist ending on a verdict about whether DNA really is a code, or on any moral about meaning, convention, or nature's language. Stop where the form stops.
