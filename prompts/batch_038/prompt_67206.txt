I have come to accept a fairly strong reading of the looping-effects thesis about psychiatric classification. Not merely that diagnostic labels influence the people who receive them — that much seems undeniable — but the following three claims taken together. First, because psychiatric nosology is promulgated in explicit, operational, publicly available form to the very populations it sorts, the extension of a diagnostic category is never fixed independently of the category itself. Second, successive revisions of the manual therefore do not converge on a stable target in the way that revisions in somatic nosology plausibly do. Third, cross-era comparisons of prevalence, course, and outcome are consequently not strictly commensurable, since instrument and object drift together. On this reading, the apparent stability of a category across editions is as readily explained by diagnostic substitution and by the re-cutting of criteria as by the persistence of a kind — and the stability data accordingly lose the evidential force they are usually given.

I want an unsparing account of what would count against this position.

1. Which findings or developments would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Consider, though do not limit yourself to: statistical tests of measurement invariance across cohorts and administrations of the same instrument; family-aggregation and longitudinal-course results that survive a change of criteria; a heritable or biomarker signal that picks out the same category under successive criteria sets; a syndrome independently described in a population with no access to the manual, matching it on presentation, course, and familial loading; and a widely publicised category that nevertheless failed to drift.

2. What does the position predict that a natural-kinds realist about the same categories does not? If the two are observationally indistinguishable, say so plainly and tell me whether that indistinguishability is a defect in my position or in the rival's.

3. Engage my chief worry rather than setting it aside: published somatic nosologies appear to loop too — thresholds move, and populations respond to being told which side of a threshold they fall on. Does that refute my position by showing it proves too much, or merely narrow it? What would have to be true of psychiatric categories specifically for looping to be *distinctive* rather than a generic feature of any classification publicised to its own instances?

4. Which items above would a serious defender of the thesis concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as beside the point? I am especially interested in whether the thesis survives being read as a claim about objectivity rather than about ontology — and whether the originator of the distinction would have accepted the stronger version I have stated.

5. Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability? It seems able to absorb either outcome: instability as the loop operating, stability as the label not yet having propagated, or substitution masking the drift. Assess whether that symmetry is fatal, and whether unfalsifiability is a disqualifying defect for a claim of this kind or a tolerable feature of a framework-level thesis.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and close with your own verdict on how exposed the position actually is. I would rather be told that it risks very little and that this is a problem than be reassured.
