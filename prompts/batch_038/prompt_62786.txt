I'm writing the epistemology section of a design doc for a system that keeps explicit probabilistic beliefs and periodically proposes new hypotheses, and before I hand it over I want to know whether the principles I've committed to actually cohere. Here's the set:

(1) There are exactly two ways a credence function can rationally change: strict conditionalization when the incoming evidence is certain, and Jeffrey conditionalization when it isn't — you shift the probabilities over a perceptual partition and everything else follows from the old conditionals. Nothing else is an update; anything else is just drift.

(2) I'm an unapologetic logical-omniscience idealizer. Probability theory is a normative logic for ideal agents and actual agents are approximations of it. This is why I don't want Garber's way out: letting an agent "learn" something like *theory T entails datum E* only works if you give up logical omniscience, and I don't want to give it up.

(3) But I also refuse to explain away the old evidence problem. The anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was known data decades before 1915, and when GR turned up and implied it, that datum genuinely confirmed GR — its the textbook case of confirmation by already-known evidence, and I don't want a view on which nothing at all happened there epistemically.

(4) Confirmation is a static relation. Whether E confirms H supervenes on the probability function and the two propositions, with no times, agents or indexicals built into the definition. If P(H|E) > P(H) then E confirms H, full stop, and it doesn't matter who knew E when.

(5) I still want the prediction/accomodation asymmetry though: a theory that sticks its neck out and predicts something novel is better confirmed by it than a theory that was retrofitted to fit data somebody already had.

(6) Beliefs move only when evidence moves them. Reflection on its own — sitting there thinking about relations between propositions you already fully believed — cannot rationally raise your credence in anything.

(7) And I don't see that (2) is embarrassed by the fact that my credence in a forty-line derivation I did last night is maybe .8: the .8 is about my performance, whether I actually carried it through correctly, not about the theorem.

My own suspicion, for what its worth, is that (4) and (5) are the pair that can't both be true, and that everything else is fine. But I could easily be wrong about that, and I don't want to pre-judge it. So: go through the whole set and tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only look like they do, ordered by severity rather than dumped on me as a flat list. For each real conflict, say what has to be given up or reinterpreted and what I lose by doing it — and tell me which single revision would buy back the most consistency for the least cost. Please don't pad it out with every tension that can technically be phrased; I'd rather have three real problems than twelve marginal ones.
