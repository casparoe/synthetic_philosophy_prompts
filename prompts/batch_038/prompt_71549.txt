I'm 71, spent forty years doing flight biomechanics on insects — mostly wing allometry in dragonflies and hoverflies — and only got into philosophy of biology after retiring, which is how I fell into the adaptationism debate head first. I've written down where I actually stand so a reading group can attack it, but I'd rather know if it's even self-consistent first. Here's the list:

1. Gould and Lewontin are basically right: people pick a trait, tell a plausible story about what it's for, and then treat the plausibility of the story as evidence for it.
2. Spandrels are real and common. A fair number of the features I measured in my career were architectural byproducts of something else, not targets of selection in their own right.
3. Natural selection is the only process that produces genuine fit between organism and environment. Appeals to "self-organisation" or "internal constraints" as rivals to selection aren't really explanations of fit at all — at best they describe where selection had to work.
4. Optimality and engineering models are legitimate, and in my own field they've been repeatedly vindicatd: the wing loading the models predicted is the wing loading we measured.
5. Comparative phylogenetic methods largely settle the whole dispute, because once you control for shared ancestry you can see whether a trait tracks function or tracks architecture.
6. I don't think claims of perfect adaptation are testable, and I distrust anyone who calls a trait optimal without saying what the alternatives were.

Go through it and tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what the cheapest revision is for each real problem. Rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than listing them all as equally damaging, and commit — give me your verdict and the reasons for it, not a menu of ways I could reconcile things and be left to choose.
