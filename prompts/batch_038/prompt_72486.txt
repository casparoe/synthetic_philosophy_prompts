I'm in a history-of-political-economy reading group that meets on Thursdays, and this week we were working through a chapter of a popular trade-press book by a journalist on the Enlightenment's invention of economics. Most of it was fine, but one long aside about Adam Smith and empire set off an argument between me and a friend that we never resolved, and I'd like a careful outside judgment. The author writes: "Adam Smith was the first great anti-imperialist in the Western canon. In *The Wealth of Nations* he ran the numbers on Britain's American empire and found the colonies to be a dead loss, since the expense of defending and governing them exceeded every shilling Britain gained from their trade, which meant that colonial rule was self-harm even on Britain's own accounting. From this Smith drew the obvious moral conclusion — that empire is exploitation, and that no people is entitled to govern another for its own benefit — and he told Britain plainly to let its colonies go. He even sketched a scheme for seating Americans in Parliament at Westminster, not because he hoped to hold the empire together, but as a graceful way of presiding over its dissolution: a transitional arrangement on the road to independence. What makes Smith remarkable is that he rejected empire without rejecting free trade, arriving at a commerce-without-conquest cosmopolitanism that later liberals spent two centuries failing to reach."

I suspect a good deal of this is retrofitting a modern politics onto an eighteenth-century argument, but I can't say where the seams are, and my friend thinks I'm being defensive about a book she liked. Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a historian of Smith's political economy would accept without qualification, what is a defensible simplification of something he really argued, what inverts his purpose, and what simply isn't in the text? In particular I'd like you to separate three things the passage seems to run together: the fiscal claim about the balance of costs and benefits from the colonies, the critique of the mercantile system and of monopolizing the colonial trade, and whatever moral condemnation Smith does offer of European conduct toward colonized peoples and toward chartered companies ruling territory. Then tell me what the parliamentary-representation scheme was actually for, on what conditions it was offered, and what Smith recommended if those conditions were refused — the popularizer treats it as an exit ramp, and I have the impression it was meant as something else, but I can't reconstruct the alternative. I'd also like to know whether the closing portrait of Smith as an exceptionless free trader who wanted commerce without conquest survives his own treatment of the navigation laws and of the pace at which commercial restrictions should be removed.

Finally, does the author's headline conclusion — that Smith was the first great anti-imperialist in the canon and that he reached a position later liberals failed to reach — follow from anything in the passage that is actually accurate, or does it depend entirely on the parts that are wrong? Where the popularizer is right or at least partly vindicated, please say so explicitly rather than only cataloguing faults, because I want to give my friend a fair accounting rather than a demolition. I'm not looking for citations or a reading list (I have no library access this month and would only end up chasing references I can't check); what I want is your substantive judgment about Smith's arguments and how well this paragraph tracks them.
