I'm a freelance science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about how bad we are at reporting on our own minds, and my editor keeps pushing it toward the simple "you think you know yourself, you don't" story. I want to resist that, which means I need the conceptual distinctions sorted before I write anything.

Can you explain, for someone smart but not a philosopher, the difference between three things that seem to get smooshed together in this literature:

1. introspective *error* — there's a determinate fact about your experience and your report simply gets it wrong;
2. introspective *opacity* — the fact is there, but it isn't available to you in the way it's available to someone watching your behavior;
3. cases where the report partly *constitutes* the state, so there's no independent fact to miss.

The part I realy want pressed on is whether (3) collapses into (1) with a time delay. When someone swears they were calm the whole time while their voice was shaking, is it coherent to say they were partly making themselves calm by saying so — and from the inside, what would distinguish self-constitution from plain confabulation, given that both feel exactly like accurate reporting? Also: if introspection is as unreliable as the debunkers say, what's the epistemic status of the evidence for that claim? Is there a self-undermining problem here, or does it get avoided because the dramatic failures are all about one narrow class of states?

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I'll do that legwork myself. Just the explanation, in prose I could almost lift into a draft. Then at the end give me a table with these exact columns: `claim` | `what it says in one sentence` | `what would have to be true for it to be false` | `how much to hedge it in print`. Four or five rows covering the main positions a general reader should walk away with.
