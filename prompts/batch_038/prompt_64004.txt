I keep running into the Daschner case in the torture literature and I want to work out what it actually proves. Frankfurt, autumn 2002: Magnus Gäfgen has kidnapped 11-year-old Jakob von Metzler for ransom, the police have him in custody, and nobody knows where the boy is. From what I've read — tell me if I've got any of it wrong — Gäfgen had already collected the money and booked himself a holiday, the police psychologist judged his account of the boy's whereabouts to be a tissue of lies, and deputy police chief Wolfgang Daschner put his order into a written memo invoking proportionality and the duty to save the child's life. A doctor was kept on standby, an officer went in and told Gäfgen that a specially trained man was on his way to inflict pain he would not forget, he broke within minutes, and he led them to the body. The boy had been dead since the day he was taken.

Now run the ticking time bomb thought experiment against this case, carefully and in both directions. Start with the mapping. The scenario normally stipulates that there's a bomb, that this suspect knows where it is, that pain will get it out of him, and that there's still time. Which of those four does Frankfurt actually deliver, and which does it merely look like it delivers? Because I suspect the interesting move is that this is the case everybody cites as the real-life ticking bomb, and metaphysically it wasnt one at all. So tell me whether the argument needs the bomb to be real or only needs the interrogator to be justified in believing it is, and whether Daschner's decision was right, wrong, or neither once you separate those two questions — I think the answer isn't the same for both.

Then the other direction. He wrote it down, invoked a standard, designated an officer, held a doctor in reserve, and was prepared to carry the threat out if it failed. That isn't the lone hero of the thought experiment, that's the first draft of a procedure. Does the case end up supporting the Shue-style point that the idealized scenario systematically misdescribes how torture actually gets done, or does it undercut that point?

And the aftermath: he was convicted and fined, and the Strasbourg court later found a violation of the absolute prohibition on inhuman treatment while letting the conviction stand. Is that the absolutist's answer working as designed — go ahead, then take the punishment — or the prohibition being hollowed out from the inside?
