I teach a 300-level cross-listed course, half political philosophy half philosophy of social science, called something like Norms, Coercion and Cultural Variation, and I'm sitting in the library basement writing the final with about three hours of quiet left before I have to go collect my kid. The one unit the students actually got worked up about was the tightness/looseness material — the cross-national survey work that ranks societies by how strong their social norms are and how little deviance they tolerate, and the broad pattern that tight places show up with more order, more conformity, less crime and less tolerance, while loose places show up with more creativity and innovation and more mess. Trouble is half the class has decided this settles the normative question. They think the tradeoff curve is basically an answer to "how much rule-following should a society demand," and they keep handing in papers that pick a point on the curve and call it a theory of justice. I want an essay question that takes that move away from them without being unfair about it — they've read the empirical stuff, they just haven't thought about what it could possibly license.

So here's what I need. Write one exam essay question on this material that can't be answered by restating the findings or by announcing that you're a moderate. I want it to push on two things. First, whether a between-society correlation supports any prescription for a particular society at all: I want them to have to deal with the fact that tightness shows up downstream of threat, resource scarcity and population pressure, so recommending tightness to a society that isn't under threat is doing something strange with the explanation — you'd be importing the response while leaving out the cause. And the fact that some of the outcome measures look partly definitional rather than independent of the index, since tolerance of deviance is close to how looseness is scored in the first place, and "innovation" gets measured in ways loose societies would recognise and tight ones wouldn't. Second — and this is the part I want the question to actually turn on — give them a concrete case to decide. A loose society has to rule on whether to let a tight sub-community inside its borders enforce norms on its own adult members that the wider society prohibits outright: heavy social and material sanctions on leaving the faith, on cohabiting, on the way members dress in public. Whether tolerating deviance commits you to tolerating communities that don't. I want the question built so that the answer visibly comes out differently depending on whether the value of looseness is taken to be instrumental to autonomy, constitutive of it, or just a matter of genuine exit and informed consent — and so that students have to notice the reflexive wrinkle, that the enclave's tightness may itself depend on being able to punish defectors, which the wider society either permits or forbids, and can't do neither.

Deliverables, then. The question as it would actually appear on the paper, with a time limit and a mark total — call it 45 minutes, 25 marks. A model answer of about 700 words. And a marking scheme that spreads the 25 marks over five or six named moves and says explicitly what seperates a first from a solid B: I want the B described as an answer that does everything competently but treats the two halves as two seperate mini-essays, or that resolves the enclave case with an unargued appeal to harm without saying whose harm counts or why the enclave's own account of the good gets no weight. Also list three or four specific mistakes that should cost marks — assuming that because tightness correlates with order it causes order, thinking that internal tightness is automatically a violation of members' autonomy regardless of what they agreed to, or sliding from "these societies differ" to "we should be more like them." No bibliography, no citations and no reading list, I'll circulate the sources myself and the students will have had them in class. Keep the prose of the question itself plain enough that a tired third-year can parse it on the first read.
