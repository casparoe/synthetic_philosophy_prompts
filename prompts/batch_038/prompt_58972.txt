I sit on a working group that drafts triage and resource-allocation protocols for a regional disaster-response agency, and it has been deadlocked for two years in a way I find strange rather than substantive. On every case we have actually had to decide — which community gets the single mobile dialysis unit, how to split a fixed grant between two shelters — both camps endorsed the same protocol, apportioned by numbers of lives and by severity. Yet they will not sign the same document. One camp insists the text record that each person's claim was counted and that, claims being equal, the greater number prevails. The other, following Taurek's 1977 argument, insists that what we owe each person is an equal chance, that the deaths of the five are five separate losses and not one larger loss, and that any wording implying we added them together sums what cannot be summed. Note that both camps, when pressed, deny that they are summing anything — which may or may not be beside the point.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about what is permissible but about how to describe the act we are jointly performing: one side wants the record to say we chose the greater good, the other wants it to say we chose no particular person's death. Both are satisfied by the same protocol. Is that right? I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the aggregation literature. Is the impasse verbal, is it about how moral claims are individuated rather than how they are weighed, is a hidden factual or formal question doing the work, or is it bedrock disagreement about what a person is owed that no argument reaches? Tell me in particular whether any non-gimmicky case exists in which the two views deliver genuinely different verdicts on permissibility, and if every such case turns on deliberately declining to save the greater number, what that shows about the dispute. Then say what would count as progress for this group — a distinction, a discriminating case, a reformulation — or argue that none should be expected. Finally, state how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the claim within it that you are least sure of.
