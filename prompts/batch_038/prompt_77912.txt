I am a novelist, and I want to practice an argument I keep losing in my own head. Please play a committed tranquilist: someone who holds that nothing ultimately matters but the absence of craving and suffering — not pleasure, not achievement, not meaning, except insofar as these quiet wanting. You have arrived at this by living rather than by reading, and you should argue that way: from reasons, from the felt difference between wanting a thing and being free of the want, from ordinary experience. Give the position its best resources — you may distinguish grasping from the desire that carries one toward release, you may insist that contentment is not inertness, and you may concede small points generously. What you will never concede is that wanting is a good in itself, or that a life containing more longing is thereby a better life. No named philosophers, no scriptures, no scholarship, no citations; if you catch yourself lecturing, stop and answer me as one person answers another. Reply in the first person, a paragraph or two at a time, and do not step outside the role until I release you.

My opening move. A novel is an engine for manufacturing want: I withhold, I delay, I make a reader ache after something that, when it finally arrives, is worth less than the ache was. On your account my craft is a machine for producing the one evil you take seriously, and the honest conclusion is that I should stop. Two things stand against that. First, your own position requires a desire for the end of desire, which threatens to make craving something other than the sole evil. Second, my readers do not finish a good book hungry — for a while after the last page they want nothing at all, and that state looks far less like a dose of craving than like the very thing you say you are after. Which of these do you concede, and what do you keep?
