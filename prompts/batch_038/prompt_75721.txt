I'm one of six volunteers on the collections committee of a county historical society — small river town, we meet the second Tuesday in the basement of the Carnegie library and somebody always brings a cake. I'm a retired schoolteacher with zero archival training; I got roped in years ago because I catalogued the map collection. Two things have landed on the agenda for next month and the committee is split in a way that feels less like an argument about shelf space and more like an argument about something I can't quite name, so I'm hoping you can help me get it into words. A family is offering us about forty boxes of the papers of the man who ran the County Home from 1919 to 1954: diaries, correspondence, photographs, scrapbooks. By all accounts he was a decent administrator and his diaries describe individual residents by name and character in real detail — it's genuinely moving. Separately, the county wants to transfer the Home's own institutional records to us, which turns out to be mostly twelve ledgers of farm accounts. The residents are in those ledgers, but only as labor and expense lines. Somebody is "3 days husking, credited .75," somebody else is a coffin and burial cost filed under expenses with initials instead of a name. There are no case files, no intake forms, no letters; as far as we can tell not one resident of the County Home ever wrote a word into the record themselves, and everything that does survive was written in the bookkeeping vocabulary of the place that held them.

The split: one member wants to take the diaries and pass on the ledgers as low-value accounting clutter; another wants the ledgers and calls the diaries "the warden's version of things," arguing that we'd be letting a man who signed the commitment orders have the final word about the people he committed; and a third, a retired genealogist, wants to build a searchable online name index by cross-matching the initials and dates in the ledgers against a digitized county burial register and old newspaper death notices. That would give maybe three hundred people their names back. It would also mean publishing identifications that no single document actually supports, and my worry — which I couldn't articulate when my brother said at Thanksgiving "just keep both, what's the harm" — is that once the index is up on the internet with our logo on it, researchers fifty years from now will treat it as the source and never look at what's underneath. There's one more thing I keep coming back to: a former employee apparently tore out a run of ledger pages sometime in the seventies to spare certain families embarrassment, so at least one hole in this record was made deliberately, by a person who thought they were being kind, and we know roughly where the hole is but not what was in it.

Constraints and ask. Our collections policy says we accept material of enduring value to the county and that we must describe our holdings fully and accurately, and the two camps read "accurately" in completely opposite directions. We have a four-thousand-dollar digitization grant that expires in June and about 0.2 FTE of staff time, so we cannot do everything. A local descendants' group has asked us, politely but firmly, not to quietly shelve the ledgers. Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the meeting: it has to come down on something the committee can actually vote on — take one collection, both, or neither, and what if anything we do about the name index — with a date attached, and give reasons a skeptical member could push back on rather than pieties about preserving our heritage. Make the genealogist's case as strong as you honestly can even if you end up rejecting it, say what we should do about the torn pages and whether we owe anyone an explanation of the gap, and end with a short test or rule we could apply the next time a collection like this walks in the door, because it won't be the last one. Start with your recommendation in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't summarize my situation back at me.
