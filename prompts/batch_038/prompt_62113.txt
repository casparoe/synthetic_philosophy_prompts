Three months ago I was asked to serve on the charter-revision commission in the city where I have lived for eleven years, and the assignment has quietly taken over my evenings. Each member has been asked to bring forward one institutional innovation for consideration, and I have chosen one I now half regret, because I cannot get my own intuitions about it to line up with one another.

The proposal is a version of what is sometimes called futarchy. The elected body would continue to vote on ends: it would define and maintain an index of the city's welfare, and it would decide which classes of decisions are eligible for delegation. But for a specified list of recurring matters — zoning variances, the school capital budget, the consolidation of bus routes — candidate measures would go to a conditional prediction market rather than to a floor vote. Traders would buy and sell claims on the index measured some years out, conditional on the measure's adoption, with those bets called off if the measure is not adopted; where the conditional market clearly estimated that adoption would raise expected welfare, the measure would take effect. The slogan is that we would vote on values but bet on beliefs. No city has actually operated such an arrangement, so nothing I say below turns on a track record.

Here is what I would like from you. Play Rudolf Carnap — not a summary of him, but the man, in the first person, somewhere around 1960, with your principle of tolerance, your distinction between questions internal to a linguistic framework and external questions about the adoption of one, your doctrine of explication and the criteria by which an explication is to be judged, and your own long-standing democratic socialist convictions about planning. I will describe this arrangement to you as one piece of unfamiliar social technology among others, and you may question me about it as you would about anything else; if you need a fact, ask for it, but limit yourself to one question per turn and advance your own position in the same turn. Do not reproduce the sentences of your published work from memory — speak in your own idiom instead. Remain in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, so that this stays an argument rather than a lecture.

What you may and may not concede. You may concede that a pragmatic assessment of frameworks presupposes a stated purpose, and that purposes themselves can be assessed, at least by what they do to the work people are trying to carry out. You will not concede that the choice among frameworks is a theoretical question with an answer holding independently of the framework in which it is asked, and you will not concede that the arrangement above is defeated by an appeal to legitimacy as such. Where your own politics pull at you, say so, and say whether you hold those convictions as hypotheses or as something else — but do not let them carry the argument.

My verdicts on the cases, which is what I actually need help with. Whether the market's figure counts as an expectation, and what expected welfare means here: I think this is settled by stipulation, that it cannot be argued from inside, and that we should choose a definition and move on. Whether the traders were right about what would happen to the neighborhoods affected by a given consolidation: plainly an empirical question with an answer, settled later, about which I do not want to hear anything concerning conventions. Whether residents of the ward that would bear the cost of a measure should be barred from trading in it, or capped: I think they should, and I think this is not housekeeping but goes to whether the arrangement is legitimate — though I cannot tell you what that word is doing in my mouth. Whether, after three years, an index that rose while the city got worse by any account I would recognize has failed to capture what we meant: I want to say it failed, that there is a fact about whether the explicandum was captured, rather than that we simply changed our minds. And whether my neighbors should be permitted to vote the whole arrangement down: I think they should, and I also think that if a referendum is just another instrument for aggregating opinions about consequences, and the market is the better instrument, then letting them vote means choosing the worse one — and I cannot explain why that is not simply a mistake.

My opening move, then. You will want to say that my first two verdicts show I already understand you, and that the last three are residue — a habit of demanding something the questions cannot supply. But consider what your tolerance commits you to in this room. A polity may adopt any framework it finds fruitful; our commission is a polity; so on your account we may adopt this charter, and we may decline it, and there is nothing further to be said beyond which of the two serves the purposes we have. Yet you have spent your life maintaining that some political arrangements serve the attitudes that make inquiry possible and others are feeling dressed up as metaphysics, and that this is not a matter of taste. If you may rank those, you have a criterion of framework choice stronger than convenience — and the proponent of this charter is entitled to reach for it: markets, he will say, are the arrangement that most efficiently serves purposes we already share, and your own tolerance requires you to let him say so. Tell me which of the two you are giving up, the ranking or the tolerance. And please do not tell me that my question is a pseudo-proposition; if that is in fact your answer, then perform the diagnosis on this case, step by step, in front of me.
