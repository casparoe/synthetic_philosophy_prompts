I would like a single credence, with its derivation made explicit, on a proposition about the no-free-lunch theorems and the problem of induction.

First, a definition I am stipulating, since I do not know whether it corresponds to anything standard. Call a possible world non-hostile with respect to an inductive rule R if the world's regularities obtain independently of R: nothing in the world's structure refers to, responds to, or keeps score against what R predicts. A world may be singled out by a description that mentions R — the world in which R is led astray as often as possible — without being hostile in that sense, since the reference to R lies in our way of picking it out rather than in the world itself. I want the restriction because a common objection to drawing any philosophical moral from the no-free-lunch results is that their force comes entirely from worlds tailor-made, one per rule, to defeat the rule in question; I want to know what survives if that objection is granted.

The proposition, P, has two conjuncts:

(1) Even when we quantify only over non-hostile worlds, for every inductive rule R there are non-hostile possible worlds on which R is no more reliable than a rule that ignores its evidence entirely; and

(2) there is no argument for the claim that the actual world is not one of those worlds which fails to rely on some inductive rule of its own.

P further asserts that (1) and (2) together amount to a formal vindication of Hume's point rather than to a technicality about how averages are taken.

Please give me your credence in P, decomposed with a number and one sentence of justification for each of the following terms:

- the probability that (1) holds under the stipulated notion of non-hostility — specifically, whether our ability to specify failure worlds by descriptions mentioning R is enough to guarantee that such worlds exist for every R, or whether some plausible further restriction (finitely statable laws, computable regularities, worlds whose structure is surveyable in principle) would block (1) for at least some rules;
- the probability that the benchmark in (1) is the right one, as against a weaker claim that R does no better than some other rule, and how much of P's truth rides on that choice;
- the probability that (2) is a substantive thesis rather than a restatement of P's conclusion, and, if substantive, whether any candidate argument — that induction is reliable, that its track record entitles us to trust it, that creatures which induct survive — counts as non-inductive in the sense (2) requires;
- the probability that the class of non-hostile worlds is well-defined without a measure over it, and whether supplying such a measure, say one weighted toward simplicity, would already resolve Hume's problem, so that P's truth depends on declining to supply one;
- the probability that this framing is apt at all, that is, that Hume's problem concerns comparative reliability across worlds rather than how habit licenses particular expectations.

Then state how those terms combine into your number for P, given that (1) and (2) are conjoined; identify the term to which your final credence is most sensitive and roughly how much it would move under a change in that term; and say which single argument would shift your number most. My own credence is about 0.6 and a colleague's is about 0.2 — tell me which term the gap between us most likely lives in.
