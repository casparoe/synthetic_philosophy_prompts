Right — I need someone to actually argue a side of this rather than survey it.

Background as I understand it. Suppose someone's preferences over three options are cyclical: she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. A trader who knows this can sell her the switch from A to B for a pound, then B to C for a pound, then C back to A for a pound, and she finishes exactly where she started with three pounds less. The usual line is that this shows cyclical preferences are irrational, full stop. Defenders of cycles — typically people who think some options are neither better nor worse nor equally good but on a par, and that a small improvement across such a pair doesn't behave transitively — reply that the agent isn't an idiot: she can see the whole sequence coming, run the backwards induction, and turn down the very first trade. No pump, no problem.

Here's the position I want argued for: that this sophisticated-choice reply isn't a defence of cyclical preference so much as a surrender of it. Once the agent is allowed to foresee the sequence and plan around it, the cyclical ordering has stopped doing any work at all, and the only ordering actually governing her behaviour is a transitive one she's had to reconstruct on the hoof out of the leftovers of the cycle. The reply keeps the letter of intransitivity while giving up everything that made intransitivity worth believing in the first place.

Please make that case properly rather than gesturing at it. In particular:

- get clear on what it would even mean for a preference ordering to be idle in this way. What's the difference between an ordering that never issues in a choice and one that isn't really the agent's ordering? If the answer is "nothing", say so and carry on.
- handle the obvious comeback: at each single step taken in isolation the agent genuinely prefers the option she's being offered, so there's a real local cycle at every point in the sequence, and foresight doesn't change what she prefers, only what she does about it. If preferring isn't doing, is the cycle still there?
- say whether your argument depends on there being only three options, or on the special features of the on-a-par cases, or whether it bites on any view that predicts a cycle anywhere.
- and if you think there's a version of the reply that survives — the agent who genuinely can't bind herself in advance, or who gets the trades sprung on her one at a time — spell out what has to be true of her environment for that, and whether what you're left with is a defence of cyclical preferences or just an observation that some agents get unlucky in unfriendly worlds.
