Background for the dialogue you are to write: A certification authority is reviewing the autonomy stack of an uncrewed cargo aircraft that operates beyond visual line of sight. At the core of that stack is a machine-checked proof of a separation-assurance theorem: whenever the separation module is engaged and the vehicle's state lies inside the domain covered by the approved dynamics model, the aircraft will not enter the protected volume of another aircraft. The authority has re-verified the proof using its own installation of the same proof assistant. During a certification flight, the primary surveillance feed logs what appears to be a brief incursion into the protected volume of a manned helicopter, under conditions the approved model represents as covered.

Two people must now agree on a standing policy for cases of this kind: what the system should be built to do, and what its designers are epistemically entitled to do, whenever data that appears to conflict with a proposition the system counts as known arrives after the fact.

The manufacturer's Proof Steward defends dogmatic closure, and reasons as follows. The theorem is known. Whoever knows that p thereby knows that any future evidence against p would be misleading. So the rational response to the incursion log is to hunt for a fault in the sensing, the logging, or the encoding of physical assumptions into the formal model, and never to suspend the theorem or to treat the incursion as evidence that the theorem is false. The Proof Steward is aware that this reproduces, verbatim, the structure of the dogmatism paradox, and holds that the paradox's conclusion should be accepted rather than explained away.

The authority's Anomaly Reviewer holds that this policy is irrational, on grounds that go beyond the cost of a mid-air collision. The proof assistant's verdicts rest on a trusted computing base that cannot be audited by means of the same assistant, and the only signals the system will ever receive about the integrity of that base are precisely the anomalies that the closure policy is built to discard.

Write a dialogue between the Proof Steward and the Anomaly Reviewer in which each tries to bring the other around. Please observe the following requirements:

1. Both characters are equally competent, equally well informed about the verification regime, and equally scrupulous in argument. Neither may be a strawman, and neither may win by making a slip the other politely declines to point out.

2. There must be genuine back-and-forth. Every turn should engage the strongest point actually made in the preceding turn rather than restating its speaker's own position. Include at least one moment in which a character concedes something and states what the concession costs them.

3. The exchange must reach the general form of the problem, not just the aircraft. Each character should say explicitly what they think happens in the step from "I know that p" to "I may disregard future evidence against p": what licenses it, or what illicitly smuggles it in.

4. The Proof Steward must press this objection, and the Anomaly Reviewer must answer it rather than deflect it: re-opening a settled theorem accomplishes nothing, because any re-examination would have to be carried out by the same proof assistant whose verdicts are in question. Either the assistant is trusted, in which case re-opening returns the original verdict and changes nothing; or it is not trusted, in which case the system never possessed a verified theorem to re-open. The rejoinder to the Reviewer's answer should also be included.

5. Neither character is persuaded by the end. Close with each of them naming the single premise on which their disagreement now turns, stating whether they regard the disagreement as substantive or as a clash over how the word "know" is being deployed, and identifying one observation that would change their mind.

6. Keep the dialogue self-contained. The characters should argue from first principles and from the concrete features of the case, not from what particular philosophers have written. No citations, bibliography, or reading list.

7. Length: roughly 900 to 1,300 words.
