I am drafting the central chapter of my thesis on Adam Smith and want the strongest available case for a reading I hold but cannot yet argue cleanly. The reading is this: the impartial spectator is impartial only in an informational sense. What gives his verdict its authority, on Smith's own account, is that he is supposed to be free of the agent's stake in the matter and fully acquainted with the situation; what he is not free of is the evaluative outlook of the community out of whose actual sympathetic responses he was constructed. He is thus an idealised compatriot rather than a disinterested observer of the kind later ideal-observer theories require, and his verdicts can be systematically wrong wherever the settled sentiments of his society are wrong. Please argue for this from the mechanics of the theory — sympathy as the imagining of a change of situation, the calibration of the person principally concerned to what spectators can enter into, the formation of the man within the breast through habituated anticipation of others' responses — rather than from quotation, as I have the citations in hand. Then take up the sharpest objection on your own initiative: Smith's account of how our moral sentiments are corrupted by the disposition to admire the rich and the great and to neglect the poor looks like a case in which the idealised spectator corrects a settled but distorted sentiment, and if the mechanism can manage that correction, it can manage any. Either show that what does the correcting there is something other than the spectator's impartiality, or concede the objection and state precisely what survives of the thesis. Close with a short note on what would change your mind: which element of the machinery, if it worked differently from your description, would make the reading collapse.
