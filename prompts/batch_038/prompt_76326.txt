Spent 35 years as a sedimentologist, retired a couple of years back, and have fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole since. The archaeology dept at a nearby uni has invited me to give a seminar talk in the spring, becuase I wrote a little paper arguing that a lot of archaeological inference about past belief is unfalsifiable in a specific, fixable way.

My pitch: borrow the logic of diagnostic-test evaluation. Every interpretive claim — "these figurines are objects of a fertility cult", "this burnt deposit is a foundation sacrifice" — should be scored on two things I'm calling interpretive sensitivity and interpretive specificity: what fraction of sites where the practice really happened the reading would pick up, and what fraction of sites where it didn't happen the reading would still generate. A reading that any competent analyst would produce for almost any assemblage of small carved female figures has near-zero specificity and carries no information, however beautifully it's written up. I'd also argue for exposure controls: run your reading blind on assemblages from contexts where you have independent reason to think the practice was absent, and count how often it fires anyway.

The room will be maybe 25 people. Mostly material-culture specialists, several trained in the post-processual reaction and deeply suspicious of anything that smells like processual positivism — one of them does phenomenology of landscapes. There's a quantitative zooarchaeologist who'll broadly agree with me but thinks the medical analogy is naive. An Egyptologist who works on iconography in museum collections stripped of context. Two or three grad students, one of whom works on repatriation and will care a great deal about who gets to decide what an object meant. Nobody does statistics for a living.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this room actually raises, ordered by how much damage each does, phrased the way they'd phrase them (thier vocabulary, not mine), each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I should just concede. Tell me which of my own premises I should be willing to give up in order to survive the hour, and include the one question I'm praying nobody asks — I suspect it's the absence of any gold standard for belief, but I want you to put it to me worse than I can put it to myself.
