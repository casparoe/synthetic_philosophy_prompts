Thirty-five years I spent doing neutron scattering at a national lab, and I've only started reading philosophy properly since I retired, so please be patient with me — I think there's a piece I'm missing and I can't find it.

Here's the thing that's bugging me. Bertrand's chord problem: you want the chance that a random chord of a circle comes out longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on how you set up the "random," you get different numbers — two points chosen at random on the rim gives 1/3, a point chosen at random along a radius gives 1/2, a point chosen at random anywhere inside the disc as the midpoint gives 1/4. Fine.

But doesn't that settle it, namely that the principle of indifference isn't a constraint on anything at all? It lets you "derive" whatever you like depending on how you describe the space you're spreading the probability over. And if that's the case, I genuinely cannot see how the microcanonical ensemble survives contact with it. Statistical mechanics tells you to put equal weight on every microstate consistent with the energy you've fixed, which looks to me like exactly the same move as picking a parameterization of the disc and calling it uniform. Nobody ran an experiment that told them to do it.

So my question: why do the foundations people treat equiprobability over microstates as perfectly respectable while treating Bertrand's three answers as a scandal? Is physics quietly smuggling in some extra principle that the circle case just doesn't have available to it — and if so, wouldn't it be more honest to say so upfront instead of still teaching indifference as though it were a principle? Or is my whole framing wrong somehwere?
