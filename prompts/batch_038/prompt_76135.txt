My film class was watching a horror movie last week and afterwards our teacher said something that has been stuck in my head ever since. He said getting scared by a movie is exactly like a little kid playing make-believe where the couch is a lava monster: the kid knows perfectly well it's a couch, we know perfectly well it's actors and a script and a guy in a rubber suit, and in both cases what you feel isn't really fear, it's a sort of pretend-feeling you generate on purpose because it's fun. He said this comes from a philosopher named Kendall Walton who talks about props and games of make-believe, though he didnt go into the details. His point was that this makes the whole "how can we be scared by characters we know don't exist?" problem just evaporate — there's no paradox at all, we're all just playing a game and the screen is the toy, same category as the couch.

Two things bug me about it though. First, my friend Maya screamed at the jump scare in the basement scene even though she had already seen the movie twice and knew exactly when it was coming — nobody asked her to play, her body just did it, and she was shaking for like a minute after. Second, I play a VR horror game where I physically flinch, my palms sweat and I back away from things I know 100% are pixels, and honestly it doesn't feel like playing with my little cousins stuffed animals at all. It feels like fear, the same stuff I feel when a wasp is in my car. Maya also offered a rival analogy: she said reacting to fiction is more like being terrified inside a nightmare, where when you wake up you know nothing was ever chasing you but the terror was completely real while you were in it. Our teacher waved that off as just his couch-monster story in different clothes and I'm pretty sure its not the same claim at all.

So can you audit the couch-monster analogy properly for me? I want the actual correspondence table — what's playing the role of the child, the couch, the lava monster, the rule that makes it true in the game that the couch is a monster, the decision to start playing, and what counts as the pretend-feeling versus what real fear would look like — and then the places the mapping breaks, sorted into the breaks that actually matter for what my teacher is using it to do (dissolving the paradox) and the ones that are just surface differences nobody should care about. Specifically: can the make-believe account absorb Maya's jump scare and my VR flinching, or does it have to say something more complicated about reflex and physiology that weakens the neat story? And is the nightmare analogy genuinely equivalent to his, or do the two actually disagree about something substantive — like whether the feeling is real? If both analogies are bad, tell me what a better one would be and what work it can and can't carry. Please don't give me a reading list or citations, I just want to understand the argument, so explain it in your own words and be concrete about which features of the child's game are doing the heavy lifting and which ones get quietly imported even though fiction doesn't have them.
