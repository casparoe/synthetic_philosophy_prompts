I am at work on a novel two of whose characters are academics, and I want to stage an argument between them that neither of them loses cheaply. The exchange is drawn from one I overheard this spring at a writers' residency, between a woman who runs corporate resilience seminars built on Stoic practice and a man who teaches Hellenistic philosophy. I did not intervene, and I am not asking which of them I ought to have preferred; I want only to know which of their individual moves holds up, so that I can hand each of them the better part of the argument at the right moment.

The woman opened by saying the whole appeal of the modern revival reduces to the dichotomy of control: sort everything into what is up to you and what is not, and confine your concern to the first column. The man said this was a twentieth-century invention wearing an ancient name — that Epictetus divides things into what is ours in the sense of our capacity for choice, and body, property, reputation and office, which are not; and that the Stoics classed health, wealth and standing as preferred indifferents, to be selected though never counted as good, which is a long way from indifference as withdrawal. She replied that a contemporary popularizer has already repaired the difficulty by substituting a trichotomy — what is up to us, what is not, and what is partly up to us — together with the advice to internalize one's goals: aim not at winning the match but at playing it as well as you can. He answered that the third category either collapses into the second, since an outcome only partly up to you is still not up to you, or else undoes the internalization she was leaning on; and that the internalized goal is not itself wholly up to you, since a poor night's sleep or a strained hamstring degrades the performance. She said he was policing a word while she was describing an intervention with measurable effects, absorbed into cognitive-behavioral therapy decades ago. He said the effects were real but not the ones she advertised: a regimen directed at one's own dispositions trains people out of civic and political engagement, which the ancient version, bound up as it was with cosmopolitan obligation, did not.

Please adjudicate move by move, rating each of the six turns from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying each rating; say separately who wins on the trichotomy, on the meaning of what is up to us, and on the disengagement charge; and tell me what both of them are overlooking.
