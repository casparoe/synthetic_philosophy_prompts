I write literary fiction. Third novel, out next spring, and there's a long stretch of it set in a public high school in a district that's in the middle of one of those fights about what goes into the history curriculum. One of my characters teaches a senior elective — twelve kids, called something like "How the Story Gets Told" — and in chapter 19 he sets their final: a 45-minute in-class essay, 20 marks. The problem is the exam has to actually be good, because most of that chapter is him sitting at his kitchen table grading it, and I don't want to be faking the thing he's grading. So I need you to write it.

Here's the shape I've settled on. His question is about periodization, not about adjectives. Everybody in the public fight argues about wording — the 1619-versus-1776 stuff, the decades-long row over how Japanese textbooks describe the annexation of Korea and the 1930s — but I want his question to make the kids notice that the real arguement is carried by the chapter breaks and the start and end dates, which nobody pickets because they look like administrative housekeeping. Two tables of contents, same facts, different book.

So: the question should hand the kids two invented tables of contents — you make them up, I'd rather they weren't real textbooks and they don't have to be recognizable — covering the same stretch of history, where every single event appears in both, in the right order, and where the two still clearly say different things. Then it asks what the difference amounts to, and whether one could be *better* than the other rather than merely different. The outlines need to carry everything the students require; the question can't depend on any kid knowing some particular historian's thesis.

And here's the thing that has to be the crux. It cannot be answerable with "both sides have a point" or "all history is written from a perspective," because that's the answer he marks down. In the book there's a kid who writes a genuinely clever essay — clever, I want the reader to feel that — arguing that since every periodization is chosen, and every choice flatters somebody, no choice can be better than any other. My character gives it a C. The scene only works if the reader can see he's right to, so the marking scheme has to justify the C without my character having to give a speech about it.

What I need back:

- the exam question as the kids would see it on the page, including both tables of contents, plus a line on time allowed and how the marks are distributed;
- a model answer, about 600 words, that would actually earn the full 20;
- a marking scheme broken into named moves with marks attached, totalling 20, each with a one-line note on how students typically botch that particular move;
- a short list of answers that sound smart out loud and should get a C or worse, with a sentence each on what's wrong with them. The "it's all just framing" one has to be on there, and so does the kid who writes that one outline is simply more accurate than the other without ever saying accurate in what respect.

No bibliography, no further reading, no citations anywhere — the kid in my scene has 45 minutes and whatever got said in class, not a library.

One last thing, and this is the part I'm least sure how to ask for. I want the question to leave room for the possibility that some of the difference between the two outlines really isn't settleable by evidence — that part of it is about who the book is for, whose grandparents are sitting in the classroom reading it. But an A answer has to say how much of the dispute falls on that side and how much doesn't, and why it can tell. If you can build that in without turning it into two seperate questions, do it.
