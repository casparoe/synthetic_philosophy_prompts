I spent thirty-eight years in trace organic analysis — mostly environmental mass spectrometry, latterly method development for compounds present at parts per quadrillion — and since retiring four years ago I have been reading philosophy of science with the slightly disconcerting sensation of recognising the problems but not the vocabulary. I have been offered the chance to apply for an eighteen-month honorary senior research fellowship at the university whose laboratories I used to visit, and I should like your help turning a half-formed idea into a two-page proposal. The idea concerns the possibility of extraterrestrial life, and specifically the way in which both halves of that debate are conducted. In my own field a null result is never simply a null result: it is only meaningful relative to a stated limit of detection and a characterised selectivity, and a competent analyst distinguishes sharply between "the instrument could not have seen it", "the instrument saw something that was not the analyte", and "the analyte is not there". My suspicion is that discussions of life elsewhere routinely collapse these three, and that a great deal of the disagreement between optimists and pessimists — about the interpretation of an atmospheric disequilibrium such as coexisting oxygen and methane, about the much-argued-over putative trace gas in the spectrum of a temperate sub-Neptune, and equally about the evidential weight of our failure to detect any technosignature at all — consists of one side varying an assumed detection limit while the other varies an assumed selectivity, both reporting the result as though it were a single quantity, a probability that a search would succeed. There is also an asymmetry that I think has not been given its due: when the limitation is one of sensitivity, a better instrument is monotonically better, but when the limitation is one of selectivity, increased sensitivity can make the evidence worse, since it multiplies the false positives drawn from a large abiotic background. If that is right, there is a non-maximal optimal sensitivity for a given survey, and both a positive and a null result must be interpreted against it.

Please write the proposal itself. It should state the research question precisely enough that one could imagine it having a determinate answer, and should say what the project claims that the existing positions do not already give us — you may refer to those positions descriptively (the sceptics about biosignature inference, the enthusiasts for disequilibrium as a stand-alone criterion, those who treat our electromagnetic silence as informative and those who regard the searched volume as negligible), but do not attribute views to named individuals unless you are confident. Set out the method concretely: what I would actually do across the eighteen months, in stages or months, given that I am one person with a desk, no laboratory, and a working knowledge of formal methods but no desire to write a textbook on Bayesian statistics. Explain why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — in particular, why the project is not merely the observation that base rates matter, which a referee will say any statistician already knows, and why it is not simply a call for better reporting standards in astrobiology, which is a recommendation rather than a question. Say what a successful result would look like: I have in mind some explicit account of an evidential yield that keeps sensitivity and selectivity separate, together with a demonstration that at least one standing disagreement is dissolved or sharpened once the two are disentangled, and a workshop bringing philosophers of science together with people who actually design these searches. Say equally plainly what would count as failure — I am quite content for the proposal to concede that the distinction may turn out to be merely notational, and I should like that possibility stated rather than buried. Finally, give an honest assessment of scope: what must be cut for the project to be feasible for one retired chemist in eighteen months, and what I would answer if a referee asked whether this is philosophy at all or simply good analytical practice dressed up.

Two housekeeping matters. I shall supply the references myself, so please include no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations — and under no circumstances invent any; where a claim about the literature is needed, make it in prose and flag it for me to substantiate. Please write the whole proposal, and any commentary around it, in British English.
