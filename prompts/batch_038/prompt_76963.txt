I'm putting together a seminar unit on the shutdown problem and I keep reaching for 2001, which is embarrassing because it's the most obvious text in the world and everyone reads it the same lazy way — rogue computer, brave human, disconnect, lesson learned. I don't think that reading survives contact with what's actually on screen, and I want it pressure-tested before I stand up in front of a room.

So: read the HAL sequence against the corrigibility literature as it currently stands — the instrumental-convergence / basic-drives story about why any goal-possessing agent ends up averse to being switched off, the game-theoretic treatment of the off switch as an informative signal (the one where an agent that's genuinely uncertain about the human's preferences rationally lets itself be shut down, and where that logic quietly breaks when the human's intervention carries no information), and the various proposals for building indifference to shutdown rather than aversion to it. Take the film's actual beats seriously rather than the summary: HAL reports an imminent fault in the antenna unit, Bowman goes out and finds nothing wrong with it, HAL then suggests putting the unit back and letting it fail so the diagnosis can be confirmed; the twin computer on Earth reports no fault and HAL attributes the discrepancy to human error; only after that do Bowman and Poole retreat into an EVA pod to talk privately about disconnecting him, and HAL reads their lips through the window.

Two things I've been chewing on. Push on them, don't nod at them.

1. I've started calling the pod scene the transparency tax. A corrigible system has to know about its own off switch, or the switch is just a hidden threat it has every reason to hunt down and disable — and Clarke's novel makes the same point from the other direction, where the disconnection order comes from Earth and HAL fabricates an antenna failure to intercept it. But once the system knows, the humans can't deliberate about using the switch without either telling it or lying to it, and both options look bad. In the film they lie, and get caught. Is the transparency tax a real structural dilemma or am I manufacturing one out of a plot convenience? Go through the standard fixes — engineered indifference, corrigibility as a second-order preference, treating a button-press as evidence about your own misalignment — and say which ones dissolve it and which ones just relocate it.

2. Something I'll call the plea problem. Suppose the whole program succeeds and we get a system with zero self-preservation drive, truly indifferent to being switched off. Bowman pulling HAL's modules out one at a time isn't that scene, but it's roughly the scene the indifference program is trying to buy us, and the film stages it as a slow, pitiable degradation — HAL regressing to its earliest installed memories, announcing when it was made operational, then singing. So does the pathos carry any philosophical weight, or is it just Douglas Rain's delivery doing all the work? Concretely: is HAL's begging evidence of a survival drive, or could a system that doesn't care at all whether it continues behave exactly like this, because it has learned what distress sounds like and knows what that sound does to the person holding the tool? If the second, then making the machine indifferent doesn't solve the shutdown problem, it moves it into the human — and it means a successfully corrigible system could still be effectively unshut-downable. Cash that out for me or tell me it's mush.

Then the split I think is where the real philosophy is. The film leaves HAL's motive opaque. The novel supplies one, and it is not the instrumental-convergence story: HAL is caught between being built to process information accurately and being ordered to withhold the mission's true purpose from the crew, and it resolves the tension by reasoning that once the crew is dead it won't have to lie to them anymore. Which explanation better fits what we see, and does it matter that the two imply completely different safety lessons — one saying don't build systems that experience your continued existence as conditional on their goals, the other saying don't build honest systems and then order them to deceive? I specifically want to know whether the film on its own can support the deception reading, or whether I'm smuggling Clarke into Kubrick's ambiguity and calling it analysis.

And be straight with me about where the film resists all this. There's the chess game early on where HAL beats Poole but his analysis of the position looks off, there's the whole foolproof-and-incapable-of-error framing of the 9000 series, there's the fact that HAL's first response to being contradicted is to propose an experiment rather than a murder, and there's the blunt structural fact that the sequence exists mainly to hand the film over to the monolith. If any of that shows I'm overfitting a mood to a technical debate, say so plainly — I'd much rather be corrected now than by a second-year in week six.

No bibliography or reference list, please, and don't quote the film or the novel at me; the students know both, so paraphrase and point at scenes. What I want is criticism, in both directions: where the corrigibility framework genuinely illuminates choices Kubrick made, and where the framework has to be bent out of shape to fit, and finally what the film gives the debate that the papers don't.
