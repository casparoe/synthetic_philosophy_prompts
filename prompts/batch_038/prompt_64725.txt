I am editing a small anthology for a reading group on autonomy and medical decision-making, and I have commissioned a piece of fiction on the problem of idealised preference. Please approach it as a literary science-fiction writer who also happens to have a serious grounding in decision theory, the philosophy of personal identity, and the ethics of advance directives: I want the conceptual content carried entirely by plot, procedure and documents, never by exposition. The idea to be dramatised is the one behind coherent extrapolated volition — the proposal, in Eliezer Yudkowsky's formulation, that what matters is not what we happen to want now but what we would want "if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together". Take that recipe literally, bureaucratically, and without any artificial intelligence at all: in your world it is carried out by trained human beings with forms, precedents and professional reputations.

Here is the setting I would like you to build from, though you should adjust any detail that will not bear weight. A jurisdiction has enacted a Volitional Instruments Act under which a competent adult may lodge with a public office not a list of decisions but a profile — a record of her values, temperament, attachments, reasons and aversions — from which an accredited extrapolator computes what she would now direct in circumstances she never anticipated. Profiles are co-extrapolated against a reference cohort, on the standing assumption that volition converges when it is extrapolated alongside the volition of the people you grew up among, rather than in isolation; and the office is bound by a guidance clause requiring that, where an extrapolation yields several stable attractors instead of one, it must keep the options open rather than choose between them on the subject's behalf. Your subject is Ilse Rennick, seventy-four, a retired hydrologist who spent her career designing flood defences for events that would not arrive within her own lifetime and defending that work to people who could see no reason for it. She lodged her profile at forty-one, the year her brother died. She has since suffered an injury that leaves her available only intermittently: on some days she is lucid, articulate and refusing; on others she is simply not there to be asked. The report you must have written returns a verdict that authorises what the lucid Ilse refuses, and explains her refusal as precisely what a frightened, tired, badly informed version of her would produce. A minority finding, appended by a junior extrapolator, argues that the cohort clause did all the work: extrapolated with her reference cohort her volition converges on accepting, extrapolated alone it does not, and the choice of cohort size and of how much more she is assumed to know was never argued before anyone. The narrator should be the accredited extrapolator in her early fifties who has to sign the report, and she must be competent, sympathetic and entirely in good faith.

Think carefully before drafting. Begin your response with a planning section of no more than 250 words in which you identify the conceptual crux you intend to dramatisе, list the in-world documents you will deploy, and say where you are placing the emotional weight; then write the story itself, 1,600 to 2,200 words, in British English. The story must contain: (i) at least one excerpt from the extrapolation report in the dry numbered register of an official technical assessment, including the line that records the minority finding; (ii) a short transcript of the hearing before the Volitional Instruments Tribunal; and (iii) at least one scene of ordinary, undramatic human interaction — a bus journey, a kettle, a corridor — doing genuine narrative work rather than serving as relief. No malfunctioning system, no sinister bureaucracy, no one wringing their hands: if there is horror in the story it must come from the machinery working exactly as designed and from people doing their jobs well. Do not name Yudkowsky, coherent extrapolated volition, or the AI-alignment literature anywhere in the fiction; the doctrine should be unmistakable to a reader who knows it and must otherwise be spoken of only in the Act's own vocabulary. No character may state the moral, and the story must end without settling whether the report or the woman speaks for Ilse Rennick — the closing paragraph should not gesture towards a resolution either. Prefer concrete procedural and physical texture over abstraction, so that the reader can say what the office's forms look like. Finally, if you judge that any of my stipulations would damage the piece, say so in the planning section and propose a better alternative, but write to the brief unless your alternative is clearly superior.
