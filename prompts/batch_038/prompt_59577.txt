I run a reading group on political economy and philosophy for about 22 undergrads, mostly not econ majors, and we meet for 75 minutes a week in a room with a whiteboard and three movable tables. This week's topic is Moloch, multipolar traps, races to the bottom. Problem: I've already burnt the easy version. Earlier in the term we ran an iterated prisoner's dilemma and then a commons game, and it fell completely flat — half the group said "so just don't defect," and one of them said, memorably, that it's only a trap because I designed it to be, and that in real life people would simply agree not to. I want an activity that pair can't be dismissed that way.

So: design an in-class game where the bad outcome is produced by a run of choices that every single participant would happily defend at the moment they made it. Not defection against a cooperative alternative — I want moves that look like local improvements and are only bad in aggregate. Explicitly off the table: n-person prisoner's dilemma, any shared pool to overgraze, a dollar auction, any setup with a designated villain, and any version where the trap only fires because someone breaks a promise. The engine I have in mind is positional: each trade or bid gets evaluated against a baseline that the trade itself is quietly moving, so nobody ever sees the drift, and the scoreboard only looks bad if you compare the last round to the first one. It should also be true that the group's attempt to organize a pact either fails or is itself one more step down — but break the pact through the structure, not by fiat. My students smell "the facilitator vetoes it" instantly and it wrecks the whole thing. Points need to convert into something they actually care about, otherwise the ranking logic has no teeth; last term I used candy and it worked uncomfortably well.

Deliverables: full rules, materials list (no real money, index cards and the whiteboard are fine), and a round-by-round timeline that fits in 75 minutes including the debrief. The part I care about most is a worked example — pick 6 players over 5 rounds and show me the actual scoreline, round by round, so I can verify the trap fires instead of discovering live that it collapses. Tell me what the sensible-play path looks like, and separately what happens if two or three players refuse to participate in the drift: I want to know whether their resistance costs them, because that's the feeling I'm chasing. Since I have 22 people and three tables, either design it for parallel 5–6 player tables with some channel between tables, or for the whole room as one game — say which you chose and why, the room shape is genuinely a constraint for me. Then the debrief: map specific rounds onto the structural features I want them to end up naming themselves (multipolarity, no single decision-maker, each step individually rational, exit individually irrational, nobody to blame, a central planner would have refused the whole sequence at round one). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover mid-session, and be frank about which feature of a real race to the bottom this game just can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply by argument afterwards. They shouldn't hear the word Moloch until the very end.
