Okay, background first: I work about twenty hours a week doing processing at a county records office — rehousing stuff, writing finding aids, the usual — and last year our state board made every repository complete a "representational adequacy self-assessment," a forty-page compliance document in which you score yourself on how well your holdings cover the communities you serve. Reading ours was the strangest experience of my short archival career. It's completely sincere, and it's also, structurally, an institution attempting to measure it's own blind spots using the exact instruments that produced the blind spots, and nobody involved seems to notice. I want to turn that feeling into a piece of satire, and I think it has to be written from inside the genre rather than about it. So: write me a mock internal compliance report — call it something like Representational Adequacy Self-Assessment, Cycle 3 — from an entirely invented county archive, roughly 900–1,100 words, formatted like the real artifact: numbered sections, findings, two or three scored indicators with last cycle's number sitting next to this cycle's, a corrective action plan with named owners and target dates, a sign-off block, maybe a note about which previous cycle's numbers were restated. The voice is completely deadpan throughout — no winking, no asides to the reader, no sentence that sounds like a punchline about power. The humor has to come from the content being exactly right, so that an actual archivist laughs and winces at the accuracy rather than at the jokes.

What I want it to dramatize, without ever stating any of it as a thesis: that silencing happens at several distinct points — whether something gets written down in the first place, whether it gets kept, whether it gets described in a way that makes it findable, and whether anyone later decides it matters — and that a repository can only ever work on the last two, which is why its indicators keep showing tidy improvement. Also: that the records surviving about a poor, policed, enslaved, or institutionalized population are overwhelmingly records made *by* the people administering them — intake books, ledgers, day books, schedules, case files — so that "increasing representation" quietly means acquiring more surveillance paperwork and counting it as a win; that description conventions tell you to reproduce the creator's own arrangement and vocabulary and forbid editorializing, so the finding aid ends up speaking in the register of whoever made the file; that a community collecting drive asks donors to show up with provenance, dates, and clearable rights, which filters out precisely the people whose history never made it onto paper; that privacy restrictions meant to protect the recorded end up protecting the recorder from the descendants trying to find them; and that deaccessioning is legally impossible, so everything acquired stays and every remedial acquisition just lengthens the queue. The overall score should improve slightly, and the reason for the improvement should be something deflatingly administrative, like three boxes being recategorized or a series getting a new scope note. Constraints: invent everything — the county, the collections, the board, the staff — and dont cite anything or give me a reading list; I want a document, not a bibliography, and I want it to work for someone who has never heard the word provenance, so every concept should be legible purely from how the report uses it. Keep the internal logic tight and consistent, and if a line starts sounding like the quiet part said out loud, cut it and replace it with a drier procedural sentence.

Then, after the report, step out of the fiction for about 150 words and talk to me straight. Tell me which parts are exaggerations of real practice and which are things repositories genuinely do. And give me an actual verdict rather than a shrug: can a self-assessment of this kind ever do any good, or is it structurally doomed to become one more sedimentary layer in the very record it's auditing? Pick a side, give me your reasons, name the strongest thing someone on the other side would say and explain why you still land where you land. If you took a liberty with an archival principle for the sake of the joke, or leaned on a contested view about what description is even supposed to do, flag it and say why you thought it was worth it.
