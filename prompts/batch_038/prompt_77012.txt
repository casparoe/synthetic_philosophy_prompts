I run a small neighborhood association and I spend an embarassing amount of time in a group chat with three friends arguing about how people who disagree about what's good are supposed to get along with each other. Over a couple of years I've sort of built up a position on this and I want someone to pressure-test it before I write it up for the group, because I'm pretty sure there's a hole in it and I can't tell where. I don't have any philosophy training, I've just read around online and thought about the council meetings a lot, so please treat what's below as my actual views and not as a summary of some book.

Here's the list. (1) Compromises between different value systems aren't a sad second-best, they can be a real moral gain: if I care about the wetland staying wild way more than you care about the extra parking, and you care about the parking way more than I do, we swap and we're both genuinely better off — that's a good outcome, not just a truce. (2) The reason to trade doesn't depend on me being unsure about my values. I don't need to hedge with "maybe you're right about the good and I'm not." Even when I'm dead certain your priorities are bad, your gains still count, because what you care about matters to you and that's a fact in the world. (3) But there's a floor: I won't be an actual participant in something I'm sure is seriously wrong, no matter how good the deal on offer is. I call it the no-complicity rule — getting my thing by helping deliver your bad thing is off the table. (4) In practice I use a threshold I made up, the three-times rule: if you care at least three times as much as I do, I trade, even about things I mildly disapprove of. (5) On the council I vote for stuff I don't believe in all the time on the understanding that people vote for mine later, and I think that's the same thing as (4) and totally fine. (6) There are a few things I won't trade at any multiple — I wouldn't sell off a resident's right to speak at a meeting even at a hundred times, because that changes what kind of body the council is. (7) I'd rather build a system that lets people swap than try to get everybody to agree about what's good first.

Two hunches about where the crack is, and I'm honestly not sure either one is right: one is that (4) is just made-up arithmetic that only works if I've already assumed everybody's caring can be measured in the same units, which is exactly what (2) says I don't need to assume; the other is that my friends' objection — that this whole thing collapses into "people with more to spend can buy their way out of being decent" — lands, when I think it shouldn't. So: go through the list and tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only look like they do. Rank the real conflicts worst to least bad, don't hand me seven equally serious worries, and for each one say exactly which commitment has to bend and what it costs me to bend it. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most damage. I don't want a general essay on moral trade or compromise, I want an audit of mine. Please end with a line in exactly this form: Verdict: #N — [six words or fewer on what breaks].
