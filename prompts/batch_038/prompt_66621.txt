My roommate picked up a bit of signaling theory from pop science books over the summer and now he's unbeatable at any conversation, because everything anyone says to him is "cheap talk." He'll tell you a compliment isn't evidence you did good work, an apology isn't evidence of remorse, and a founder saying they'll ship on time isn't evidence of anything, and the argument underneath is always the same one. I've tried to write it out as a chain so I can see where it bends:

(1) Anyone who stands to gain from being trusted will try to seem trustworthy whether they actually are or not.
(2) So any way of seeming trustworthy that costs the signaler nothing gets flooded with fakes, and once its flooded it stops telling the receiver anything.
(3) So a signal only carries information if its cost is high enough that faking wouldn't pay — and for that you need the cost to outweigh the payoff of a successful fake, otherwise the fake is just a good investment.
(4) Which means every signal anyone can actually believe is one that destroys more value than it creates. That's the whole point of the tail on a peacock, he says — he's read enough Zahavi to know the tail is supposed to be a handicap.
(5) Words cost essentially nothing to produce.
(6) So nothing that consists only of words is evidence of anything. You have to ignore what people say entirely and watch what they burn.

The annoying part is that I half agree with him. He's right that a lot of talk is worthless, and I've caught myself making his exact move when I'm evaluating people. But (6) is obviously too strong — I get information out of conversations every day, including out of his. And when I try the reflexive move on him, that his whole argument is just words so by his own lights I should ignore it, he says that's cute but wrong, because *his* words are backed by the cost he'd pay if I found out he was bullshitting me, namely thier friendship. Which is an escape hatch you could drive any sentence in the English language through, if you're allowed to count the costs of getting caught later.

So: which step is broken? I want the actual fault located, not a general essay about how signaling is subtle. If more than one step is suspect, rank them and say what I'd have to give up to reject each — I'd rather lose (4) than (1), since (1) seems like the only reason any of this got started. And I want to know for each suspect step what the fixed version looks like and what the fixed version still gets you, because I assume some weakened cousin of his conclusion survives all this.
