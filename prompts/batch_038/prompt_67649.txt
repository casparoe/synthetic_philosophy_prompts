I teach a third-year ethics seminar, 18 students, and I've got one 75-minute slot next week where I want them to run into the internal/external reasons debate without hearing either label until the very end. None of them have read Williams — half are poli-sci majors taking it for credit — so no lecture up front, and please don't have me write "internalism" on the board at minute one.

Here's the mechanism I've sketched; tell me if it's workable and then build it out properly. Three dossiers, a page each: a person, an explicit list of what they want, believe, and are disposed to feel, and one candidate action. The only question the room answers each round is: does this person have a reason to do it? Trios can justify a yes only by writing an explicit chain from the dossier to the action, and they have to play move cards I deal out — means/end, correcting a false belief contained in the dossier, weighing against the other things the person wants, and an imagination card that lets them bring in what the person would come to want if they thought about it harder and more honestly. Every card played gets tallied on a ledger I keep visible at the front of the room.

Round 1 should be a case where the honest answer is no. Round 2 is one where correcting a belief flips the verdict. Round 3 is the one I actually care about: someone who, so far as the dossier goes, has nothing at all that reaches the action — I have in mind a man whose estranged brother is dying and who resents him, where no amount of family-duty talk gets any purchase — and the imagination card is the only route left. Then round 4 I turn the ledger around and make them audit their own tally. By then they'll have manufactured reasons for people who on the paperwork didn't have any, and I want them to notice that they can always do this if they're allowed enough moves, and to say out loud what the moves were doing.

What I want from you: the three dossiers written out in full, with enough texture that chain-building isn't trivial and there are real distractors in the motivational set; the card set with exact wording; timings per round; the verdict sheet; and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the actual distinction — the subjective motivational set, the sound deliberative route, external reason statements — and walks them into the collapse worry, that the escape clause is elastic enough to swallow everything it was supposed to exclude, and then into the blame question: if the round-3 guy genuinely had no reason, what are we doing when we resent him for not going? Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and what the activity can't reproduce that I'll have to supply by hand. My suspicion is that its the soundness of the deliberation itself: my students are smuggling in their own sense of which chains count as good reasoning, and that isn't in any dossier. Say whether that's right, and whether the whole design is rigged against the externalist — I don't want it to be, and I'd rather know now.

If you want an anchor with a pedigree, Williams himself leaned on the Henry James story about Owen Wingrave, the young man whose family insists he has a reason to be a soldier. But I'd rather the room build the cases than recognize one, so use it only if it earns its place.
