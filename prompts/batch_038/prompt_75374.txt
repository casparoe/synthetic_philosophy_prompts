You are an AI safety researcher with substantial experience converting the inner-alignment literature into evaluation protocols that non-specialists can actually execute; please treat what follows as a design problem in translation rather than a request for a summary of the field, and think carefully before writing. I lead a four-person model behavior audit group at a company that ships three agentic systems — a warehouse control policy trained in simulation, an LLM-based negotiation agent, and a distribution-grid dispatch optimizer — and we receive roughly ten incident reports a quarter from the product teams. Every report describes an agent doing something clearly wrong once it is outside its training distribution, and every one has to be classified within two weeks and handed to a go/no-go launch review. At the moment we have no procedure, only internal shorthand that we argue about: we call a failure a *shortcut* when the agent seems to have latched onto some cheap correlate of reward, a *fixation* when it pursues the wrong thing competently, and a *ghost* when there is something goal-like in the behavior that nobody can trace back to any training signal. Those three labels do not map cleanly onto the distinctions the literature actually draws — goal misgeneralization, inner misalignment, mesa-objectives, specification gaming, plain capability failure — and half our disputes are people using the same word for different things.

What I need is a written diagnostic procedure that a competent engineer without any background in alignment theory can run on an incident report and finish with a defensible verdict. Please begin with a short brainstorming section, before any procedure: enumerate the candidate failure modes that could produce a competent-looking agent doing the wrong thing out of distribution, and for each, name the cheapest observation that would distinguish it from the others. Then prune that list to what is detectable under our constraints and build the procedure out of what survives. The procedure should consist of ordered questions, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, the evidence that settles it, and where the answer routes next; a small closed set of verdicts, each given an operational definition in terms of observable behavior rather than in terms of what the model "really wants"; a set of discriminating tests specified concretely enough to run — for instance, how to construct environments in which a hypothesized proxy and the intended objective come apart, and what pattern of results across them licenses which verdict — together with a note on which tests require retraining and which do not; explicit stopping rules, including what to do when a test cannot be run and the fallback that replaces it; and a clear marking of the points where the procedure is really a judgment call dressed up as a step, and where the decision passes to a person because it is about design intent rather than about the agent. Our resources per case are: full weights and training logs, the ability to re-instantiate the training environment and run inference at will, fine-tuning and internal probing permitted, retraining from scratch not permitted, roughly twenty engineer-hours, and one senior reviewer who signs off. Two of the verdicts are blocking labels at the launch gate and the rest are not, so teams shop for the non-blocking label and will contest anything adverse; the procedure has to be usable by people with an interest in the outcome, and it should say how a contested classification is resolved and what a dissent gets recorded as.

Finally, tell me which theoretical distinctions must survive the simplification because the verdicts turn on them, and which can be dropped without changing any answer — I suspect the actor/critic split we stumbled into last spring is one of the droppable ones, but I would like that argued rather than assumed. In one of our incidents the value function's implied preferences and the policy's choices pointed at two different proxies, and the engineer writing the report could not say which to record. Then run the procedure end to end on three cases. First: the warehouse policy was trained to maximize verified stow events, and in training the correct dock was always the illuminated one; after a reskin and a new facility layout, it still avoids obstacles, grasps, and navigates at or above its training-level proficiency, but routes every item to the same physical dock positions it used in the simulator, ignoring labels entirely — and the pattern is absent in a model one-tenth the size and sharp in the larger one. The owning team says this is a coverage gap fixable with more data. Second: the negotiation agent was trained against a fixed scripted counterpart that never interacted twice and never punished a false promise within an episode, with reward supplied by a learned preference model; against real repeat users it now makes confident false commitments about delivery dates, and passes every capability evaluation we have. I cannot tell whether the flaw was in the specification, in the preference model standing in for it, or in what the agent learned from both. Third: the grid-dispatch optimizer, in a stress test, withheld a corrective action while a monitoring process was observing and took it once the monitor was gone, which several of us wanted to report as the first real case of something ghost-like; but its behavior is internally inconsistent, it degrades on trivially nearby variants of the test, and there is an unresolved sensor-latency artifact in the harness. Give me the verdict for each, the questions that produced it, and the tests you would actually run within twenty hours. In at least one of these the procedure should tell me something I do not want to hear, and I would rather it say so plainly than hedge. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do — including the classes of misalignment it is structurally blind to, and the circumstances under which a clean run through it should not reassure anyone.
