I am in my final year of secondary school, and last spring my mathematics teacher remarked, almost in passing, that a respectable minority of mathematicians refuse to accept a proof by contradiction as a way of establishing that something exists. I found this so unsettling that I have spent the summer working through as much of the intuitionist programme as I could get hold of — Brouwer, Heyting, and the various successors who quarrel with both of them — and I have ended up in a tangle. I agree with some of the restrictions these mathematicians impose and reject others, and I cannot find a single rule that explains where I actually stand. What I would like is for you to look at my judgements on a list of specific cases and tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. Euclid's argument that there is no largest prime: accepted, because from any finite list it hands me a way of producing a prime absent from that list. Proving that the square root of 2 is not a ratio of integers by supposing that it is and deriving a contradiction: accepted — and, more generally, I accept establishing a *negative* claim by reductio in every case. The familiar classroom argument that there are irrationals a and b with a^b rational, which considers the square root of 2 raised to itself and splits into cases according to whether that number is rational, without anyone knowing which case obtains: not accepted as a proof. The very same argument, however, now that the Gelfond–Schneider theorem tells us that this number is transcendental and so tells us which branch we are in: accepted. The claim that either there are infinitely many pairs of twin primes or only finitely many: I refuse to assert it, since neither disjunct has been proved — yet I am perfectly happy to assert that it would be absurd to suppose the question has no answer, so I accept the double negation of the disjunction while rejecting the disjunction. A proof that splits on the truth of the Riemann hypothesis, showing that if it holds then a certain concrete inequality about a specified number follows by direct computation, and that if it fails then a different concrete inequality about that same number follows by a different computation, and concluding that the number satisfies one of two explicitly checkable conditions: accepted. For a particular computer program, a proof that the assumption that it runs forever leads to a contradiction: here I am willing to conclude that the program halts, even though I know that some constructive mathematicians permit precisely this step and others forbid it — and I am struck by my unwillingness to make the parallel move for twin primes, which looks to me like the same move. A real number a defined by the rule that the nth term of its expansion is 0 unless the nth even number above 2 fails to be a sum of two primes, in which case it is 2 to the minus n: I accept that this genuinely defines a real number, since I have given a rule for its digits; I accept that it is absurd to suppose a is neither equal to zero nor different from zero; but I will assert neither that a equals zero nor that it differs from zero. On the other hand, when someone writes down a specific integer with ninety digits, I find myself equally unwilling to assert that it is either prime or composite, even though the test is entirely mechanical and I would happily accept either verdict if someone ran it. For a continuous function on the unit interval that is negative at one end and positive at the other, the bisection procedure that produces points where the function is as close to zero as I please: accepted; the further inference that there is therefore an exact point where it vanishes: rejected. Finally, I accept that any statement follows from its own double negation's double negation — I accept going from P to not-not-P and from not-not-not-P to not-P — while rejecting the step from not-not-P to P, and I confess I have no account of why three negations behave differently from two.

My tentative rule is something like this: an assertion is proved only when the proof supplies a method — something convertible, at least in principle, into a finite procedure — that delivers whatever the assertion claims, meaning a witness for an existential claim, a decision procedure where the claim is one that a procedure could settle, a way of transforming proofs for a conditional, and, for a negation, a way of converting any proof of the original claim into a proof of absurdity. Please test this against the list above and tell me whether it survives, or whether it must be replaced by something with several clauses; if it is a family of clauses, state each one precisely and say which of my cases forces it, since I suspect that the twin-prime case, the Riemann-hypothesis case-split and the program case cannot all be handled by a single clause. In particular, tell me whether acceptability is a feature of the *form* of an argument or of what is *known* when the argument is offered, because my opposed verdicts on the two versions of the irrational-powers argument seem to commit me to one answer or the other and I cannot see which. Tell me plainly which of my judgements are inconsistent with the rest and have to be abandoned, and what should replace them — I have a suspicion about the ninety-digit integer but I am not certain. Explain, if you can, the asymmetry between two and three negations in terms of the principle you end up defending. And finally, give me the principle's verdicts on cases I have not raised, including whether a rule of this kind, if it is really about methods and what can be verified, must also govern ordinary claims about the past and about the physical world — whether, that is, it is licit to say of some specific unrecorded detail of an ancient day that it either obtained or it did not — or whether mathematics is genuinely special in this respect. I am not looking for a list of further reading; I want the reasoning itself, worked out in enough detail that I could apply it to a case of my own devising next week.
