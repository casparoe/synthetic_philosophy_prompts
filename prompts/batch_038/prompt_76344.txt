Here is the passage, with its section heading stripped:

"Libet fitted subjects with scalp electrodes, asked them to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it, and had them watch a dot sweeping around a clock face much faster than a second hand, so that they could report where the dot stood when various things happened. Two timings mattered. The readiness potential — a slow negative shift over the motor areas that marks the brain getting ready to move — began roughly half a second before the muscles fired. But when subjects marked the dot's position at the moment they first became aware of an urge to move, they placed it only about a fifth of a second before the movement. That leaves some three hundred milliseconds in which the brain has already committed to the act while the person is still, so to speak, waiting to be told. Libet's conclusion was that the experience of deciding is not the beginning of an action but a late report on an action already begun: consciousness is informed rather than in charge. The one opening he left was a veto — in the final hundred milliseconds or so the conscious mind can still abort an act under way — so what we have on his account is not free will but free won't. Later scanning work has strengthened the case, since activity at the front of the brain lets researchers guess which button a subject is about to press up to ten seconds before the subject reports having chosen. What none of this addresses is whether the same applies to decisions that matter — a verdict, a resignation, a marriage proposal — because those unfold over minutes and consist in weighing reasons rather than flicking a wrist. But there is no principled reason to expect them to work differently, only the practical difficulty of putting a deliberating juror in a scanner."
