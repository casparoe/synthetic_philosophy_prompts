Retired four years ago after 34 years in a fire research lab, most of it on how textiles ignite — cellulose pyrolysis, char formation, that sort of thing. Since then I've been going to a reading group at the public library, and this term we've been working through al-Ghazali's Incoherence of the Philosophers in a used paperback one of the members had. Naturally the causality chapter is where we got stuck, because the fire-and-cotton business is more or less my entire professional life.

Here's what happened. I said the cotton example struck me as a weak way to make his point, because in practice we don't just know that flame near cellulose is followed by charring — we have a fairly detailed account of what goes on in between (heat transfer, the decomposition products, the ignition chemistry), and that account lets you say something about materials nobody has ever set light to. So "all you observed is the one thing after the other" looked to me like a serious understatement of what's on the table.

The fellow running the group waved it away with: al-Ghazali was making Hume's point six hundred years before Hume, and Hume's point isn't the sort of thing more mechanism could answer. Everybody nodded and we moved on to miracles. I've been chewing on it since and Im not happy, partly because I half suspect he's right and partly because I suspect that one sentence did a lot of work it hadn't earned.

So what I want is the whole menu of precise claims that sentence could be standing for, sorted roughly from the true-but-empty ("both of them wrote about causation, in different centuries") through the substantive-and-defensible to the ones that are simply false. For each reading, tell me whether it actually holds up given what the two men were doing. My own guess is that al-Ghazali's quarry is the philosophers' claim that causal connections are necessary in a way reason can demonstrate — the chapter is literally about whether the disruption of causality is impossible — whereas Hume is after our idea of necessary connection and where it comes from, and I'm not convinced those are the same animal. Also: do they agree about what's left standing once the necessity goes? My reading is that al-Ghazali wants to keep the regularities perfectly reliable, because God keeps up a habit, while Hume leaves you with a determination of the mind and no deity in the machinery. If that's right, does "same point" survive? And where does the theology sit — one of them is protecting the possibility of miracles and the other isn't obviously protecting anything.

Related, somebody else said the label for this is occasionalism, but I noticed he seems willing to allow that God could create a power in the fire and let the fire do the burning, so I don't know that he's a full-blooded one. Fold that in if it matters for the comparison, because if he isn't a strict occasionalist then whatever he shares with Hume might be narrower than the slogan suggests.

Then the bit I actually care about: which of these readings, if true, would license using the line the way it was used — to make my mechanism objection beside the point? I can see there might be a version on which more mechanism genuinely cannot help, and a version on which it can and the Hume comparison is just a conversation-stopper in period costume. Tell me which is which. And if there's a version of the claim that is both non-trivial and correct, spell out exactly what it commits you to and what it leaves my objection doing.

Don't bother with citations or a bibliography, I'm not writing anything up. I just want to walk back into that room knowing what I'm talking about.
