So quick background — I'm a novelist, and my last book has a main character whose wife exists as an NPC on a game server that gets shut down in chapter nine; the whole back half is her grief played totally straight, no "she was only pixels" wink at the reader. Some philosophy-and-literature outfit is putting me on a panel in April to defend the position the book takes, which roughly is: virtual objects and virtual harms are real, and the reason people flinch at that is that virtual harms are non-rivalrous — they don't compete with harms out here for anyone's attention or resources, so they never land on a ledger, and it's easy to pretend they aren't happening. Grief, on my view, tracks the shape of a relationship rather than the ontological grade of its object.

The room'll be maybe forty people: mostly analytic metaphysicians who work on fiction — a couple of Meinongians, most of them happy to say fictional characters are abstract artifacts or nothing at all — one guy who's spent years arguing virtual objects are real but digital, who'll think I'm on his side only sloppier, and two or three ethicists who hold that harm requires a subject with a welfare. No gamers, no lit critics, which is weird but there you go.

Give me the five objections I'll actually get from that room, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd phrase them — I want their vocabulary, not mine. Numbered list, exactly five, nothing before or after it, and keep each item to about three sentences: the objection, my two-minute reply squeezed into one line, and where that reply goes soft. Make number five the one I'm praying nobody raises.
