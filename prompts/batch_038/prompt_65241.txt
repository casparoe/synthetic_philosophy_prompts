Our department is running a two-day workshop whose stated purpose is an adversarial collaboration between two positions that have, by their own participants' admission, spent three decades talking past one another. On one side sits a biological naturalist in Searle's sense: some intentionality is original or intrinsic, it is a causal product of certain biological processes, and every case of derived intentionality presupposes minds that have it originally. On the other side sits an intentional-stance theorist in Dennett's sense: the original/derived distinction does no explanatory work, aboutness is a matter of how reliably a system's states support prediction when interpreted as beliefs and desires, and no additional non-dispositional property is ever detectably present where we say content is. Please draft the joint statement these two parties should be able to sign at the end of the workshop.

To keep the document from degenerating into a restatement of familiar ground, both parties have agreed in advance to adjudicate one stipulated case, referred to in the statement as the Isolated Register.

The Isolated Register is a sealed module M containing a single bistable element R with exactly two physically stable states, s0 and s1, plus a transducer chain that drives R to s1 when an inserted coin matches a fixed physical profile (mass, diameter, thickness, electromagnetic signature) and to s0 otherwise. M has no memory, no self-monitoring, and no access to information about its surroundings or its own history; its only output is the state of R. M is built to be slotted into either of two hosts: host A, a vending machine operating in a city where every coin matching that profile is a genuine quarter; host B, a physically identical machine operating where coins matching that profile are half the time legal-tender tokens that are not quarters and that the transducer cannot discriminate. M is molecule-for-molecule the same in both hosts, and nothing in M records which host it occupies.

Three further stipulations are part of the agreed case. First: in one scenario M leaves the factory, is never installed, never powered, never inspected by anyone, and is incinerated a week later. Second: in another scenario there exist two physically indistinguishable modules, M-eng, built to a specification by engineers who documented what its states were for, and M-luck, produced by a fabrication accident in a plant that was immediately abandoned, with no designer, no documentation, and no user, then or ever. Third: in a third scenario the sensing and the downstream mechanism are moved inside M, so that sensor, coin store, and sorting actuator are all internal and the coupling between R's states and the coins is closed within M; this closure can be enriched in stages — finer sensing, memory of past coins, error correction, flexible re-weighting of features — with no change whatever to the physical substrate of R.

The statement should contain four parts.

Part I, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, each followed by one sentence on why each side can accept it given the rest of its view, and, where a claim is signed only with a rider, the rider itself. Do not pad this with platitudes. Aim at claims that genuinely constrain the debate: what the relocation of a two-state coin discriminator between host A and host B does and does not establish; whether any adequate account must make room for misrepresentation and for content that outruns actual causal covariance; whether an abstract syntactic description, considered apart from any implementation, could suffice for semantics; whether content attributions are answerable to a system's dispositions or free of them; and whether either party is committed to the existence of a determinate answer to the question of what s1 is about in host B.

Part II, a table of residual disagreements with columns for topic, the formulation as the biological naturalist would sign it, the formulation as the intentional-stance theorist would sign it, and a classification as substantive or verbal with a one-line reason. Each party's column must state its own view in words it recognizes as its own, not in a caricature chosen by the other side.

Part III, the cruxes: those sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each, give a case, experiment, or argument that both agree bears on it, drawing on the three stipulated scenarios wherever they can do work — the uninstalled and unobserved module, the engineered module beside the accidental one, and the progressive internal closure of the coupling — and say explicitly what result would be informative rather than a restatement of the disagreement. Then record each party's prediction, written down in advance, about how it will respond to that result. Where the two predict that they will respond identically, flag this, since it is evidence about whether the crux is genuine. Include at least one crux on whether phenomenal consciousness does any content-fixing work, and one on whether a regress of derived content must terminate in an actual mind or can terminate in a design or selection process in which no mind ever appears.

Part IV, a short section on which portions of the dispute are verbal. Address specifically whether original and derived are being used to mark a difference in the number of argument places of the content relation — content as a one-place property of a state versus content as a relation holding between a state, an interpreting practice, and perhaps a history — and whether the two parties' talk of real intentionality picks out the same property at all. Propose a shared technical vocabulary in which the surviving disagreement can be stated without either party winning by definitional fiat; if you judge that no such vocabulary is available, argue that rather than glossing over it.

Two constraints on execution. Do not adjudicate the debate or award a verdict to either side; the deliverable is a text both could put their names to. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or references to specific publications, and do not put any wording into either philosopher's mouth as a verbatim quotation — paraphrase throughout, and where a paraphrase is one that its target might contest, mark it as contested.
