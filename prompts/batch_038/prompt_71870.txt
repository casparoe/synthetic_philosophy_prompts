One camp says obviously not — syntax can't buy you aboutness. The other says the fight is empty, that once you take Dennett's two-bitser seriously there's no such thing as original intentionality, its just a question of which interpretation earns its keep. I'm running a 45-minute internal workshop and I want to settle it by making people commit to cases instead of slogans.

So: write me a "which theory of mental content do you actually hold?" quiz. Nine concrete cases, each with two or three options, drawn from stuff like a quarter-detecting internal state that gets shipped somewhere crawling with slugs, tree rings, a frog's bug-detector cells firing at BBs, a fossilized footprint, a word in a language nobody alive can read, our spam classifier, a phantom limb. No case may name a theory or ask anything as on-the-nose as "can machines mean things" — the point is to sort people by their verdicts, not by which label they'd pick.

Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least Searlean intrinsic intentionality, Dennett-style instrumentalism, Dretskean indicator semantics, teleosemantics, the radical-enactivist no-content-at-the-basic-level option, and eliminativism. Tell me which combinations of answers are unstable, which pairs of positions the quiz cant actually separate, and which corners of the space end up empty — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?
