The heuristic-argument move in number theory always gets me: you say a random n near x is prime with probability 1/log x, then you just multiply out the odds for n and n+2 as though they were unrelated coin flips, sum it up, and out pops a twin-prime density that actually tracks the data. Cramér's random model is that presumption of independence with a formal name on it, and everyone quietly knows the independence isn't earned — parity is the tell, sieves can't distinguish a number with an even number of prime factors from one with an odd number, so there are known holes in the whole business.

Write me a joke about this. The laugh has to come from the presumption of independence actually failing somewhere specific, not from 'math is hard' or 'the numbers lie to you.' The shape I keep reaching for is: two arguments everybody treats as independent corroboration of the same conjecture turn out to be the same argument — but find your own image for that, I don't want to hand you the bit. Tellable out loud in under a minute, and it should land for someone who's skimmed the heuristic literature without knowing what a sieve is.
