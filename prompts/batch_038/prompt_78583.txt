I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of economics, and I'm running an optional reading group for our MSc cohort on norms of choice under risk. Roughly ten people. Half have done a bit of poker or spread-betting maths, half have never taken a logarithm outside school, and all of them turn up already convinced either that the answer is obviously to maximise expected value or that it's obviously reckless to bet the lot. I want to spend week one sorting them — by what they say about specific gambles, not by what they call themselves — and then use the resulting groups to drive the rest of term.

Can you write me the sorting quiz? Ten or eleven questions, each one a concrete numeric gamble with actual stakes and payoffs, two or three answer options per question, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. No question is allowed to be a label question — nothing like "do you think you should maximise growth rate?", and nothing that mentions utility functions, ergodicity or the Kelly formula in the question text itself. The whole point is that a student can answer every item without knowing which camp their answers put them in.

Here's the angle I care about, and it's the bit that makes this more than a recap of a Wikipedia entry. My working hypothesis is that these positions mostly come apart over which of three features of a gamble an agent treats as fixed: whether the bet repeats, whether winnings get reinvested and compound, and whether there's an absorbing state at zero. So I want at least four of your items to be near-identical gambles where exactly one of those three parameters is switched, and the scoring key should tell me, for each such pair, which positions are supposed to flip their answer and which shouldn't. If your tree predicts that two positions never diverge on any of my items, say so explicitly and either give me an item that does separate them or admit it can't be done behaviourally and separate them on justification instead.

Some raw material, numbers are rough — please redo the arithmetic and adjust the payoffs if they don't produce the tension I'm after. (A) A fair coin; if you stake a fraction of your pot, heads multiplies that stake by 1.6 and tails by 0.6, and you can restake whatever's left every week. (B) The same coin, but you'll only ever see it once in your life. (C) One person playing (A) a thousand times versus a thousand people each playing it once. (D) Something like (A) but where a single loss wipes the pot out completely rather than shrinking it. (E) A positive-expected-value bet whose downside correlates with your salary, so you're already long the same risk. (F) A bet you take in the last year of your life with no bequest motive and no further wagers. (G) The same bet as (A) but you're staking other people's money and they've each hired ten managers like you. For each item I'd like you to state the arithmetic expected value per round and the expected growth rate, so I can check that the case actually pulls in the directions I think it does, and flag any item where the two come apart by less than I'd need to make it diagnostic.

Leaves that must appear, in whatever names you prefer: full-Kelly staking; fractional Kelly as a robustness hedge rather than a mistake; the log-utility expected-utility maximiser who thinks the growth rate only matters because it happens to be what a concave utility function delivers; Peters-style ergodicity economics, where the time average is the only thing that's actually true of a single agent; an unconstrained expected-value maximiser who accepts ruin; a mean-variance or Sharpe-ratio type for whom compounding isn't the issue, dispersion is; and a quietist who thinks the whole dispute is about modelling assumptions and no genuine normative disagreement survives. That's seven — if you want an eighth, add one, and please tell me Samuelson's old objection to treating the geometric mean as a rational requirement lands on which of these or whether it needs its own leaf.

Two more things. First, build the space before you build the leaves: give me the questions and the answer combinations they permit, then locate the positions in it, and for every combination nobody occupies, say whether that's because the view is incoherent, because it's live but unattractive, or because nobody's bothered yet. I'd bet real money there's an unoccupied cell corresponding to "accepts the ergodicity maths, denies it has any normative force" and I want to know whether you agree or whether that cell collapses into my quietist. Second, at the end list any pairs of leaves your instrument can't actually distinguish, and say what a reader in the group should do about it — my worry is that half the cohort ends up in one undifferentiated blob.

British English throughout please, and keep the register informal enough that I can hand it straight to the students as a worksheet without rewriting it.
