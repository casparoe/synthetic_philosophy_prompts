I co-organize a reading group that sits awkwardly between our statistics department and our philosophy department, and last Thursday the same proverb got deployed twice, for opposite purposes, by two people I respect. First, a visiting philosopher of science argued that the no-free-lunch result — the one Wolpert proved in the mid-nineties for supervised learning, and the companion theorem he and Macready proved for search and optimization — finally gives Hume a formal backbone: if every learning algorithm has identical expected performance once you average over all possible target functions, then no inductive method is better than guessing, so the problem of induction is not merely unsolved but provably unsolvable, and any appeal to simplicity or uniformity is naked faith. An hour later, my own advisor used the same words in a completely different direction, to shut down a junior colleague's proposal to default to a particular model family for a new client's data: there's no such thing as a free lunch, so you owe it to the client to benchmark on their data rather than import assumptions. I was struck by how little the two arguments share, and by how neither of them seemed to notice that the prover of the theorem wrote a companion piece arguing there are perfectly good senses in which algorithms *are* distinguishable a priori.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims that "there's no such thing as a free lunch" could be standing for in this context? For each reading I want (i) the exact proposition being asserted, including which quantity is being averaged over and under what measure, (ii) a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, true-but-vacuous-in-the-actual-case, or simply false — and (iii) whether it would support the philosopher's Humean conclusion, my advisor's benchmarking injunction, both, or neither. Please be attentive to the distinctions I suspect are getting run together: the theorem's statement about expectation over a permutation-closed set of problems versus any claim about a single fixed problem; the assumption that the data are sampled i.i.d. from one unchanging distribution, which is itself an inductive assumption rather than a conclusion; the difference between "you cannot get better-than-chance performance without empirical assumptions" and "you cannot do better than another method"; whether the uniform measure over all input-output mappings is a legitimate candidate for "the prior over worlds" that Hume's skeptic needs, given that the worlds it weights most heavily are ones no one believes we inhabit; and the "conservation" gloss, on which whatever one method gains on some problems is exactly offset by losses elsewhere — a gloss that sounds skeptical but may be doing no work at all.

Two constraints. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; treat me as someone who has read the statements of the theorems but has never been given a clean taxonomy of what people claim they entail, and assume I cannot look anything up. And I would rather have your reasoned verdicts than diplomatic hedging: if the Humean use is a category error, say so and say precisely where the inference breaks; if my advisor's use is a sensible methodological point that merely borrows the slogan's authority without needing it, say that too. Roughly five to seven readings, numbered, each with a one-line verdict tag I could put on a slide.
