I am a metrologist at a national measurement institute. My working life is the traceability chain: primary standards, reference standards, working standards, calibration certificates, uncertainty budgets, dissemination to end users, and the surveillance of drift. I am fluent in the standard vocabulary of measurement science — measurand, realisation of a unit, traceability, metrological compatibility, Type A and Type B evaluation, and the distinction between the definition of a unit and its realisation in practice — and I have essentially no philosophical training beyond having read The Structure of Scientific Revolutions twice, once as an undergraduate and again last year.

Kuhn borrowed his central term from a mathematical usage I understand better than the philosophical one: two magnitudes are incommensurable when they admit no common measure, as with the side and the diagonal of a square. That is my native territory — finding, realising, and disseminating common measures — which is why I suspect the borrowing is either more exact than philosophers admit or a flat equivocation. I want to know which, and I want to know it by the only method I trust: attempt the translation and report the failures.

Please restate Kuhn's doctrine of incommensurability in my vocabulary, working with the later, more disciplined formulations rather than the 1962 rhetoric. Take these five claims in turn:

(a) incommensurability is a failure of one-to-one mapping between the taxonomies two groups use to sort the same objects, so that some terms on one side have no single counterpart on the other, and the overlap is local rather than global;
(b) incommensurability does not entail incomparability — comparison survives where translation fails;
(c) there is no neutral algorithm for theory choice: shared values such as accuracy, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness are weighed rather than applied as rules, and weighed differently by different people;
(d) some statements are formulable only once the new lexicon exists — beforehand they are not false but unavailable;
(e) the remedy is learning the other lexicon by immersion, and acquiring it changes the learner rather than merely informing them.

For each claim, construct the closest metrological counterpart you can — drift, redefinition, replacement of a primary standard, an artefact that no longer agrees with its own official copies, a measurand specified differently by two laboratories, a calibration undertaken without an agreed reference — and assign it to one of three categories: translates cleanly; translates only with a distortion you must describe; or has no counterpart at all. Alongside this, keep a running ledger of every point where you had to import an assumption from either side to make the mapping hold. The ledger matters more than the mapping. If you get the translation through by assuming the measurand stays fixed, or by assuming that Kuhn's talk of scientists responding to a different world after a revolution is loose speech, say so on the record rather than letting it hide inside a definition. I am as suspicious of my own framework's presuppositions as I am of his, and I would rather see a failed translation than a rigged one.

Then answer four questions I cannot settle myself.

1. The kilogram was redefined in 2019 by fixing an exact numerical value for the Planck constant, and the whole exercise was engineered so that the size of the unit did not change: continuity was a design requirement, adopted after a century in which the prototype and its official copies had slowly come to disagree with one another. Is that the nearest thing in my discipline to a Kuhnian revolution, or is it precisely a change arranged so that no revolution can occur? Describe what a redefinition would have to look like for continuity to be impossible to engineer in advance, and say whether that description is coherent or empty.

2. Accuracy in metrology is defined relative to a standard that is itself a matter of convention and international agreement. Does that give me a way to express Kuhn's claim that science changes away from anomalies rather than toward a fixed target, or does it merely collapse his position into a conventionalism he would reject?

3. Claim (c) says values are weighed without a determinate rule. Is that analogous to an uncertainty budget that cannot be closed, to a Type B evaluation for which no model is agreed, or to neither? State what is lost in whichever analogy you choose.

4. When I send a travelling standard to another institute and bring it home, I return with a calibration coefficient, not with a converted outlook. Does that asymmetry show that claim (e) is inexpressible in my vocabulary, or that my discipline has simply never been forced into the situation Kuhn describes?

Close with your verdict on the residue. Two readings are available: either metrological language is structurally incapable of representing conceptual change of this kind, because it presupposes a stable world and a stable measurand and therefore cannot describe their revision; or the residue shows that much of what Kuhn asserts was less determinate than it sounded, and my instinct that the business reduces to poorly specified measurands is defensible. Argue for one, or explain why neither can be established from the translation alone — and tell me where you think I am most likely to be wrong.
