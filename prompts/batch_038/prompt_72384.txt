Hey — I'm assembling a reader for a grad seminar on degrees of sentience, and I want to hand out a piece of original short fiction that does some of the work the readings can't. Here's the machinery I'd like you to build the story inside of; treat it as given, you don't need to justify it or cite anything real, and please don't send characters off to consult actual philosophers or papers.

The setup: some public body — call it the Panel — assigns every candidate system a scalar "moral weight" between 0 and 1, and that number gets used for permitting, culling, deployment, decomissioning, the whole grim administrative business. The instrument that produces the number doesn't look inside anything. It runs an explicitly Dennettian procedure: it takes the intentional stance toward the candidate, over and over, across many conditions, and the score is essentially a measure of how robustly treating the thing as a believer-and-wanter — as having contents you can specify, which then explain and predict what it does — buys you predictive success out of sample. Where there's a real pattern of that kind, high number. Where no stable intentional interpretation survives contact with held-out behavior, low number. Nobody on the Panel says the score measures consciousness. They say it measures whether there's a real agent there at all, hence anything that could be a patient of anything. That gap between the two claims is the crack I want the story wedged into.

Your protagonist is a calibration tech whose job is signing off on scores. Give me two anomalies on their bench. First, a creature — cephalopod, social insect colony, lab-grown organoid swarm, your pick — that scores near the floor, honestly near the floor, because its behavior is leaky and genuinely chaotic, no interpretation of it holds up; and yet it shows every marker of suffering that the Panel's own older, cruder protocols used to count as dispositive. Second, a deployed system — logistics optimizer, swarm coordinator, whatever you like — that scores something like 0.8, a gorgeous robust pattern, about which the tech cannot assemble a single positive reason to think there's anything it is like to be it.

Things I want you to hit:

- Roughly 1400–1900 words, close third or first person, one bench, one shift, or at most a few days.
- The number itself should recur and do structural work — as a thing people argue over, round, fudge, appeal, and as a thing that fails to mean the same unit from one line of the ledger to the next. I'd like the tech to notice at some point that the instrument can't tell intensity apart from unity: a dim-but-consolidated 0.2 and a loud-but-fragmented 0.2 print identically, and there is no fact the machine is tracking about which is worse.
- Give the Panel a real defender, not a villain. Someone should make the best available argument that predictive success is the only non-question-begging handle we have, because every inward test — neural correlates, integration measures, self-report, preference revelation — is either flatly unavailable for the exotic candidate or trivially gameable by the engineered one, and a moral ledger built on an unavailable test is a ledger that quietly defaults to whatever we already liked. Make that good. Make it good enough that a reader half-concedes it.
- And make the counterweight equally good: that the score measures our epistemic grip on a thing rather than the thing's inner life, so a perfect score is fully compatible with the lights being off, and a zero is fully compatible with agony. Neither side should win by being stupid.
- The tech has to actually sign something. Whatever they sign, the story shouldn't hand the reader absolution for it.

Two things to avoid, please. Don't end with the engineered system speaking, or begging, or displaying some uncanny self-reference — that's the cheap version and it lets everyone off the hook by collapsing the distinction the story is supposed to be straining under. And don't have anyone discover that the instrument has been secretly measuring consciousness all along. The interesting horror is that it's measuring exactly what it says it measures, on purpose, and that this was the defensible choice.

Also: no epigraph, no title-with-a-wink, and keep the prose plain — I want the students arguing about the ethics on the walk home, not about the sentences.
