i TA a survey course on medieval political thought and this term i've been trying to get the students to actually apply Aquinas on tyranny rather than just summarize him. Last year's discussion exercise died because I used a lame case — some unnamed king who taxes too much — and everyone just repeated the "tyranny is directed at private advantage, kingship at the common good" line and went home. So this year I made up something more annoying and i want to sanity-check what a strong answer would look like before I hand it out.

The case: President Marek of Karsan (fictional) was elected legitimately about twelve years ago, then rewrote the electoral law, jailed the opposition leadership, and now rules by decree. The independent press is gone and there are credable reports of torture in the security services. Crucially, there's no domestic institution left with standing to remove him — courts are packed, the legislature is a rubber stamp, and elections are still scheduled but the results get pre-anounced. Two options are on the table. Option A: a foreign intelligence service covertly reprograms the pacemaker in Marek's chest so that he dies of what will look like a heart attack; the service's own lawyers argue it's an act of national defense, because Karsani forces have been shelling across the border for two years. Option B: Marek's personal nurse, a Karsani woman whose brother died in custody, drugs his infusion. She acts entirely alone, tells no one, and has zero institutional backing, though she's read the underground pamphlets and knows her country is desperate.

So what would Aquinas actually say about each of these? I want the reasoning, not a verdict followed by a vague gesture at double effect. A few things i'm specifically curious about: does the fact that Marek was originally installed lawfully help him or hurt him — is the "the community that made him king can unmake him" idea even usable when that community no longer has any organ capable of acting? Would a foreign intelligence service count as public authority in the sense he needs, and does it matter that the two states are in something we'd now call an armed conflict — and does anything in his treatment of ambushes and stratagems make the covert, deniable character of option A better or worse than a plain drone strike would be? Where does his "unless the disturbance would do the subjects more harm than the tyranny itself" proviso bite, and how would he apply it when nobody can reliably predict the succession — is that a counsel of patience or a counsel of paralysis? And then tell me which of his distinctions you think has to be stretched hardest to cover this case at all, and whether the nurse is committing something he'd flatly call murder even if he'd agree the world is better off. Please don't give me a bibliography or a list of where to look — just argue it out with me in plain english.
