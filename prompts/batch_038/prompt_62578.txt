When people argue about who to blame for the First World War, they don't all seem to be working with the same idea of blame, and I'd like the difference spelled out. One is the guilt-flavoured notion that Article 231 of Versailles got read as expressing — the treaty text says responsibility, but Germans branded it the war-guilt clause almost immediately — where a state brought the war about by its own aggression and can properly be held to account for it. The other is the air-crash-investigator notion: nobody decided the crash, so you map the causal chain (alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, a handful of botched decisons in July 1914) and studiously avoid saying who's at fault.

Compare the two properly: what does each take as its object, what would count as settling a dispute inside each frame, and is it even possible to move from one to the other without smuggling something in? Then use the Fischer controversy as the test case — Fischer's claim that Germany deliberately used the July Crisis to launch a war, with the annexationist war aims as evidence, looks like it converts a crash-report finding into a guilt finding. Is that a genuine conceptual move, or is he just pointing at a longer causal chain and reaching for a loaded word?
