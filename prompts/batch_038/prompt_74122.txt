I am a graduate teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophical foundations of welfare economics; my discussion section has sixteen students, meets once a week for eighty minutes, and none of them has encountered the primary literature, although all have had a semester of intermediate microeconomics and are comfortable with the notion of maximizing expected utility. Next week we cover Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and my instructor has handed me the entire session to run rather than to lecture. I would like a complete, runnable in-class activity that makes the theorem land as an experience before anyone hears its name. What I specifically want them to feel is the pressure the premises exert: once a group has committed both to respecting unanimous preference and to ranking uncertain prospects by their expected value, it discovers that its self-styled fair procedure has quietly become a positive weighted sum of individual utilities with the weights fixed once and for all — and that it can therefore no longer respond to anything it learns afterward, including the arrival of a new member whose preferences are far more intense than the rest. I want them to walk into that impasse on their own feet and to be visibly irritated by it.

Please specify the following. A fictional decision problem concrete enough to be argued about: I have been assuming something like a town council dividing a fixed annual fund among three programs, where the prospects on the table are genuinely uncertain, so that the group must rank lotteries rather than outcomes — feel free to substitute something better if it generates cleaner numbers. A deck of utility cards with actual integers I can print at an office printer, not placeholders, including at least one profile on which the rule the group is most likely to invent for itself (averaging ranks, taking the median, capping anyone's gain, letting the worst-off member veto) demonstrably violates unanimity or the expected-value requirement, so that I can refute it with their own cards. A committee structure in which students take turns holding the deciding role rather than sitting as an undifferentiated crowd. A scoring scheme that gives them something to lose without involving real money. A minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty minutes. And the pivot, roughly a third of the way in, at which the composition of the group changes and the weights they have already committed to begin to bite.

Then the debrief. Map each phase of the game onto a particular premise of the theorem and onto a particular sentence students will have said out loud; name what they were doing at the moment they compared one cardholder's utility to another's, and what license they had for it; and be explicit about the further assumption someone needs in order to move from "a positive weighted sum" to "equal weights," since I want that gap to be the last thing they hear rather than the first. I would also like a candid section on what will go wrong when I run this. At least one student in my section is a mathematics concentrator who will see within five minutes that the aggregator has to be additive and will announce it, and I need a way to keep that from deflating the room; a second predictable failure is that the group simply refuses to treat the printed numbers as interpersonally meaningful, at which point the game stalls. Finally, tell me which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce no matter how carefully I run it — I suspect it has to do with the difference between fixing weights before a preference profile is known and fixing them after — and what I, as facilitator, will have to supply verbally to cover the shortfall.
