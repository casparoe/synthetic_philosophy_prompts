Write a poem of four stanzas, twenty-two to twenty-eight lines in total, whose architecture is Schopenhauer's account of music and whose last movement turns on a difficulty that account leaves behind. The poem must work as a poem first: a reader who has never heard of him should encounter a strange, coherent piece about listening, and a reader who knows the argument should be able to sort the stanzas without being told.

Specifications:

1. The first three stanzas correspond, in order, to the three strata of his hierarchy of voices. The first is the fundamental bass and what it stands for: the crude mineral mass, the lowest grade of the one striving underlying everything, out of which all the rest arises. The second is the inner voices between bass and melody, and the plant and animal kingdoms they stand for, the intermediate levels through which the inert becomes the living. The third is the high leading voice, the one that moves with unrestrained freedom from thought to thought, holds the separated parts into a single unbroken connection, and stands for the intellectual life and effort of a human being. The fourth stanza is not part of the hierarchy; it is the listener, and it is where the poem stops mapping levels.

2. Enact rather than state the musical rule he insists is no arbitrary convention: that the bass must keep a wide interval from the voices above it and may not take part in their quick, fragmentary motion, but only move in long slow steps. Let line length, syntax, and repetition carry this. The first stanza should be the longest and the slowest, heavy-breathed and turning back on itself; the second quicker and more varied; the third the shortest in bulk and the fastest, free to leap, since it is the voice that leads.

3. Rhyme scheme as ontology: the first stanza holds a single rhyme throughout, the mass that everything else is generated from. The second loosens it. The third is unrhymed entirely, because the leading voice is not bound to return the way the ground is.

4. His account of melody is of a line that wanders away from its home note through unresolved tension and delayed resolution, comes back, and finds that the arrival is only the starting point of a fresh departure. Build this in literally: the third stanza must end on the poem's very first word, and the fourth stanza must open on entirely new ground. The return should read as an arrival and not as a conclusion.

5. He holds that music does not give us this or that particular joy or grief along with the occasion that produced it, but the thing itself, abstracted from every motive and circumstance. Accordingly, the poem must contain no story: no named person, no scene, no cause, no object for any feeling it renders. Grief must appear without a source and longing without a target. If a reader could supply a narrative that explains the feelings, the specification has been missed.

6. Do not use the words will, representation, Idea, noumenon, phenomenon, metaphysics, or Schopenhauer anywhere in the poem, and do not name any composer, piece, or instrument. The contrast he draws between the other arts, which deliver copies of copies, the shapes things happen to take, and music, which delivers the inner thing itself, should nonetheless be unmistakably present in the fourth stanza, in plain concrete language.

7. The fourth stanza must hold two claims in unresolved tension. What the listener hears is nothing other than the same blind striving she is made of; and yet hearing it produces a release from striving. One art whose entire content is desire has the effect of quieting desire. Let that be the poem's final note rather than a problem the last line solves. No consolation, no uplift, no admonition, no archaic diction or inverted syntax.

8. After the poem, add a note of no more than eighty words identifying which formal choice answers which claim. No citations, titles, quotations, page numbers, or references of any kind, and the poem must be able to stand without the note.
