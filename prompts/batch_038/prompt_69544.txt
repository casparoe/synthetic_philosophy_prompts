I spent thirty-seven years in the chemistry of disordered solids at a national laboratory — diffraction work mostly, some calorimetry — and retired three years ago. Philosophy of mind has become my late-in-life vice, pursued with the help of a Tuesday reading group that meets in a bakery, whose other members are a retired structural engineer and two former graduate students of mine. At the estate sale of an old colleague last spring I bought a box of German-language pamphlets, and among them was an undated lithographed lecture handout, section-numbered, with the title page torn away; the paper and the orthography suggest the 1870s, and the author is nowhere named. It turns out to be a polemic against what it calls the "mind-dust man." My German is rusty, but I have translated the section that interests me as literally as I can manage, keeping even the author's more awkward images. You will not find this text and I do not expect you to; I want to work through it on its own terms rather than be told what its genre usually says.

Here is § 14, "On the composition of the psychical":

> It is much in fashion now to say that the soul is a powder: that consciousness, wherever it is met with in any quantity, has been built up out of consciousnesses in the dust, each of which feels somewhat, and feels no more than that somewhat. The chemist is invoked, and I confess the invocation is handsomely made. Sodium is a fury in water, chlorine is a suffocating vapour, and the two together are the salt that lies upon the table; the compound has what the components have not, and no one calls this a marvel. Why, then, should it be thought a marvel that a hundred grains of feeling, each the feeling of a trifle, should compound into the feeling of a symphony?
>
> But look closely, for the analogy is not merely weak; it is inverted. In the salt the new property is a property which the salt has *for others*: it is discovered by the tongue that tastes it or by the balance that weighs it. The gift of the compound is a gift to a witness. What the mind-dust man requires is otherwise in kind: he requires that the compound be its own witness, that the salt taste itself. And here the arithmetic fails him at the first step. Take a hundred dust-feelings and lay them side by side. Each is shut in its own skin, without co-feeling for its neighbours, and the hundred-and-first, which is to feel them all together, is nowhere in the heap; for the heap is only the hundred, and the hundred are only themselves. Say that they fuse into one, and you have not compounded them but abolished them, and the one which remains is simple and not composite — which was not the thing to be shown. Say that they do not fuse, and you have a hundred feelings and no feeling of the hundred — which was again not the thing to be shown. The dilemma is complete: the compound is either nothing, or it is not a compound.
>
> It is therefore not that we cannot see how the many become one; it is that we cannot see how the many could ever have been other than one.

Two notes on my rendering. Where I write "shut in its own skin," the original reads *eingeschlossen in ihre eigene Haut*, which I have kept literally because the image seems to be doing work. Where I write "co-feeling," the original says *Mitgefühl*, which in ordinary German means compassion or sympathy; I chose a technical-sounding English word because the author appears to want a term for a feeling's feeling of another feeling, but I may be over-reading him. The engineer in our group declares the whole section nonsense on the grounds that it proves too much, and I could not tell him why he is wrong.

What I would like is help with the following, in this order:

1. Reconstruct § 14 as one or more numbered arguments — premises, suppressed premises, conclusions. I suspect there are three arguments here rather than one (the analogy, the dilemma, and whatever the final sentence is doing), and I would like to know where each begins and ends and whether the later ones depend on the earlier ones.
2. Take up the distinction between a property a compound has "for others" and a property it has "for itself." Is that distinction coherent, and what would have to be true of a physical system for it to have a property in the second way? My own worry is that the author has not defeated the chemical analogy at all but has merely restated his difficulty in chemical language: sodium and chlorine do not have to taste anything for the salt to acquire a new property, so why should the dust-feelings have to feel anything for the whole to acquire a new feeling? Tell me whether that objection lands or whether § 14 has an answer to it.
3. State both horns of the dilemma precisely and test their exhaustiveness. Give me at least one third option the author has not listed — a way of holding that the many become one without fusion and without a mere heap — and say whether it escapes both horns; if it does not, show exactly where it collapses into one of them.
4. Tell me whether the argument begs the question. Specifically: does "each is shut in its own skin, without co-feeling for its neighbours" already assume the conclusion, namely that a part-feeling cannot be a feeling of the whole it helps compose? Then steelman the mind-dust man: what is the most he can concede about the parts and still avoid the first horn, where concession to fusion is treated as abolition?
5. The section seems to me to slide between two complaints that need not go together — that many subjects cannot add up to one subject, and that many thin qualitative characters cannot add up to one rich qualitative character (that is what the symphony example suggests). Are these separable? Does § 14 succeed against both, or only against one? If only one, does that leave the author with less than the final sentence claims?
6. Consider a case of my own devising, which is what the engineer and I actually argued about. A hundred photodiodes, each with its own register and each — granted purely for the sake of argument — with some micro-experience of local contrast. Downstream sits an integrator with recurrent connections which emits a single report, "a face is present," a report no diode can produce and which is not the report of any one of them. Does § 14 rule this out? If it does not, identify the precise place where the case slips through the argument, and say whether the integrator counts as a "witness" in the author's sense or as the forbidden hundred-and-first feeler. I want an honest answer to a further question, which is whether the difference between my array and his heap is a difference in kind or only a difference in how much we happen to know about the wiring.
7. The final sentence. Has the author changed sides — from denying that composition yields a subject to treating unity as the primitive? Give me the two most plausible readings, say which is consistent with the rest of § 14, and tell me which reading makes the section worth arguing with today.

Please do not send me references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have no way to chase them down and they are not what I am after. I would rather you flag any place where you think my translation is distorting the argument — "co-feeling," "property for itself," and "abolished" are the three I distrust — and tell me what to go back and re-check against the German.
