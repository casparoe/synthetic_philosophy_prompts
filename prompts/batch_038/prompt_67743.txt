I have no training in this — I read philosophy on my own, mostly at night — and I have been turning one argument over for the better part of two winters. It is about voting, and it ends somewhere I do not believe. Here is the version I finally got straight:

1. I ought to do something only if it is an effective means to an end I actually want — that is, only if doing it makes that end likelier, or makes it better when it arrives.
2. In an election with tens of millions of voters, the chance that my single ballot changes who wins is vanishingly small. Call it one in ten million.
3. So the expected contribution of my ballot is one ten-millionth of the difference between the two sides — a quantity so small that, for anything I can do with it, it is no quantity at all.
4. If the outcome were really what I cared about, I would spend my evening on things with a much larger expected effect: donating, canvassing, wearing down my brother-in-law, writing letters.
5. Therefore voting is the least effective of all the ways to pursue the goal voters say they are pursuing, and anyone serious about the outcome should skip it.
6. And yet skipping it feels like a dereliction in a way that skipping the donation does not, and no amount of arithmetic makes that feeling go away.
7. So the obligation to vote cannot come from the outcome at all; it must come from somewhere else — participation, solidarity, a duty owed to the practice.

Step (7) is where I think it broke, but I cannot say so confidently, because the reasoning proves far too much. Run it over the small contributions I make to large goods and everything becomes a gesture. My recycling bin does not measurably change the climate; the fraction of an illness I fail to pass on by staying home sick is one ten-millionth of a curve; the dollar I put in a food-bank tin is one ten-millionth of its budget. If (3) holds, all of it is symbolism wearing an arithmetic excuse, and I would rather believe that some of these things actually matter than believe my own argument.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I have managed four replies on my own and cannot tell which of them actually breaks the chain and which only moves the problem. (a) The figure in (2) is too small, because the chance of a dead heat does not fall off nearly as fast as one in the number of voters. (b) Step (3) quietly treats a tiny fraction of an enormous thing as though it were a tiny thing. (c) Step (4) is empty, since canvassing ten people is still only ten times one ten-millionth, so the argument condemns every political act and not merely voting. (d) Step (2) assumes the closeness of the race is settled in advance and unaffected by how many people reason the way I am reasoning. Take each in turn, say what the person who adopts it has to give up elsewhere, and tell me whether the "if everyone thought that way" move behind (d) is a real principle or a bludgeon.

I would most like to keep (1), because without it I do not see how to think about any of my choices — but I am not attached to it if it is the thing that has to go, and I would rather be told plainly than reassured.

Please write in American English. Give me the diagnosis in prose first, working through the steps in order. Then follow it with a table, one row per numbered step, with these columns exactly: Step (the number); Status (sound, flawed, or salvageable-at-a-cost); Why it looked fine (the intuition that made it seem obvious at the time); Best repair (what a defender could replace it with, or none); What the repair costs (what has to be surrendered elsewhere to keep it). Close with a single sentence naming the one step you would drop if forced to drop exactly one.
