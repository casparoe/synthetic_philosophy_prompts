I am the instructor for a third-year political philosophy course and would like an independent assessment of a take-home essay before I finalize the mark. The question as set, and the student's answer in full, are reproduced below without corrections or annotations of any kind on my part.

**The question**

*Readings for the week: Weber on politics as a vocation and the two ethics; Walzer's essay on the problem of dirty hands; and the assigned secondary literature on responsibility in large organizations.*

For the purposes of this essay, stipulate the following case and the following terminology. A minister of the interior receives credible information that a detained suspect knows the location of a device that will kill a great many people unless it is found. She issues a written instruction to a subordinate that is deliberately worded so as to remain within the letter of the regulations governing interrogation, while making unmistakably clear to the subordinate what is expected of him; the subordinate, understanding the instruction, employs methods that the minister believes to be wrongful. The information is obtained and the device is found. The minister then publicly denies having authorized anything of the kind, and does not intervene when the subordinate is prosecuted. Let us call a case *laundered* when an official procures a wrongful act through a subordinate in this way: the official's own conduct stays inside the letter of the rules, while the wrong is committed by someone else, on her authority and for her reasons.

(a) Does the laundered case fall within the problem of dirty hands as Walzer understands it, or does the fact that the minister's own conduct was rule-compliant take it outside that problem altogether?

(b) What work, if any, does Weber's distinction between an ethic of responsibility and an ethic of ultimate ends do in answering (a)? Does that distinction resolve the minister's situation, leave it unresolved, or is it simply the wrong instrument for a case of this kind?

(c) Are the subsequent denial and the prosecution of the subordinate a further wrong, a mitigating factor, or morally irrelevant to the assessment of the original authorization?

*Word limit: 600.*

**The student's answer**

Dirty hands cases have a distinctive shape. An agent occupying a public role does something that remains wrong even though, given the circumstances, she had most reason to do it. The wrong is not cancelled by the good that it produces; on Walzer's account it survives the justification and must be acknowledged as a genuine moral violation by the person who committed it. That is what separates dirty hands from an ordinary hard choice between two legitimate goods, and from the case of an agent who merely brings about a worse outcome than she might have. In the latter there is nothing that has to be owned as a wrong. In the former there is: the politician has crossed a line that she would want everyone, herself included, to respect, and she cannot appeal to the value of the result in order to erase the crossing. This is why Walzer thinks that the dirty-handed politician who emerges untroubled is not an improved politician but a worse one. The acknowledgment is part of what distinguishes the tragic use of power from simple ruthlessness.

Applied to the minister, the analysis seems to me to divide in two. First, on the authorization itself. The minister did not perform the wrongful act, and the instruction she issued was framed so as to stay inside the letter of the regulations. She is therefore one of a number of contributors to an outcome for which no single person is clearly answerable. Because the wrong is dispersed across the ministry rather than located in one pair of hands, this is precisely the dirty hands problem, which in the organizational literature is sometimes called the problem of many hands. If that is right, the delegation does not introduce a new difficulty into the case; it merely makes vivid a feature that was present all along, namely that political wrongdoing is typically shared out among people whose individual contributions look innocuous.

Second, on the denial. This is where I think the minister's position becomes indefensible rather than merely tragic. Her denial is an attempt to keep the benefit of the wrongdoing while refusing to be identified with it, and the prosecution of her subordinate completes the attempt by installing someone else in the position of the person who must answer for what was done. Even on Weber's view she would have no defence here. Weber distinguishes an ethic of responsibility, which requires the agent to answer for the foreseeable consequences of her conduct, from an ethic of ultimate ends, which requires only that the agent's intention be right and leaves the outcome to the world; the distinction is essentially the familiar contrast between consequentialism and deontology. Weber also holds that in politics the ethic of responsibility ranks above the ethic of ultimate ends, with the result that the politician who acts on it has nothing left to acknowledge: the responsible politician's hands come out clean. That ranking is not available to a minister who conceals the connection between her own instruction and its consequences. She declined to answer for consequences that she foresaw and directed, which is a failure of responsibility by the responsibility ethic's own standards, and it is a failure that no appeal to the lives saved can repair.

So the case is a dirty hands case with respect to the authorization and a straightforward case of bad faith with respect to the denial.
