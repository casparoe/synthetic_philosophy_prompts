I've got 30 minutes plus Q&A at a workshop on the politics of infrastructure. The room is mostly STS people trained on social construction of technology, two historians of engineering who basically only trust archival evidence about what designers actually intended, one political theorist who's convinced the whole "artifacts have politics" line is structural explanation wearing a costume, and a handful of design researchers who want to leave with something usable.

My talk argues that the interesting sense in which a thing has politics isn't the Winner/Moses move, where someone bakes an exclusion into the artifact on purpose. That's real but its the special case. The general mechanism is foreclosure: once an artifact stabilizes, it narrows the option set available to later decisionmakers *before* those decisions ever get framed, so the politics lives in what stops being up for grabs rather than in what got deliberately built in. My case is a municipal permitting system I've spent two years on — the whole stack was built decades ago around a data model in which any residential unit that isn't a detached single-family house can only exist as a variance, so a zoning reform the council passed last year still can't be represented without a special exception. Nobody chose that. It's just what the schema can express.

Give me the prep sheet for the question period: the objections this particular room will actually raise, sorted by how bad each would be for me, phrased the way each camp would phrase it — the constructivist and the political theorist won't put it the same way. For each one, a reply I can get out in about a minute, what I should just concede up front, and where the reply is thinnest. Then tell me which of them I can afford to lose and which one I can't, plus the question I'm praying nobody asks.
