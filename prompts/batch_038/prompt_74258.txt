so, bit of background first. I work in public health — vaccine outreach, mostly cost-per-outcome spreadsheets all day — and about eighteen months ago I started sitting with a local dharma group, originally just for the meditation. These two halves of my head have started fighting. My teacher keeps saying things like "the karma is in the intention," which sounds like outcomes don't matter at all, but she's also the one who organizes the food drives and who pushed me to give away more of my salary, which sounds extremely outcome-focused. Then last month I tried to explain why I send money to bednet distribution rather than to the animal shelter down the road, and she said "you're counting beings," as if the counting were the problem. I left more confused than I arrived.

Our group is doing a session on this in a few weeks and I volunteered to make the handout, which I now regret. What I want isn't an essay. I want a sorting device — a decision tree / "which position do you actually hold?" thing — that walks someone from their gut reactions about cases to a named spot in the argument over whether Buddhist ethics is a species of consequentialism, a virtue ethics, an agent-relative thing built on karma and rebirth, or none of those categories.

What I need:

- 8 to 10 questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options. No question may contain the words consequentialism, deontology, or virtue ethics — people should be sorted by what they say about the cases, not by what they call themselves.

- Build the space first and then locate views in it. I definately want branch points on: whether an act can be wrong when it produces good results but springs from greed or ill will; whether a well-intended act that backfires through no fault of yours leaves any moral residue you ought to feel bad about; whether the agent's own future — this life or later ones — sits inside the calculation or beside it as a separate prudential matter; whether the thing being aimed at is the reduction of suffering, the absence of craving, or something that isn't a state of affairs at all; whether animals, hungry ghosts and hell beings count the same as humans when you're adding things up; and whether the precepts are exceptionless or are reliable guides a compassionate person can override. For that last one use the old story about the ship's captain who kills the would-be murderer on board to save the merchants and to spare the killer the hell his deed would have earned him — our group will half-remember it already and it's the one case that makes everyone shift in their seat.

- At least seven leaves, each labeled by the claim that actually defines it, plus the standard name for the view if it has one. If you're not sure the name is right, write "unnamed" — and no invented citations or page numbers, I have no way to check them and a wrong one is worse than none.

- Then the honest part: tell me which pairs of leaves your questions can't really separate. My guess is that the virtue-ethics-ish leaf and the intention-is-everything leaf come out identical on nearly every case, but you might be able to pry them apart and I'd want to know how. Also flag any combination of answers that's logically available but that nobody seems to defend, and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unappealing, or just because no one has bothered.

- Finish with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves, and one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment, written for people who are new to this.

Audience is about fifteen people, half of whom have never read philosophy and half of whom think they have, so it should fit on two printed pages. One of them is my teacher, so please don't make the "none of these categories" option look like the smart answer by default.
