here's the question i assigned (20 points). note that the three-way taxonomy is mine, something i built for the course, so judge the answer against these definitions rather than against how the words are usually used in the literature:

QUESTION: For this course we distinguish three slots for explaining perpetrator behaviour. SITUATION: features of the immediate context that push on anyone standing in that context regardless of what they believe — orders and sanctions, peer pressure, visibility to comrades, how the task is organised, timing. CULTURE: socially transmitted belief systems and categories shared across a population — what is thinkable, who counts as what, what a normal person takes for granted. Culture operates through what agents believe, but the beliefs come from outside them and are held in common. CHARACTER: stable individual dispositions — temperament, personal history, conscience, the kind of person someone is. Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) both study the same unit, Reserve Police Battalion 101. (a) Say which slot each historian primarily fills and why. (b) Take one piece of evidence about the battalion's first mass shooting that both historians discuss and show how each reads it differently — not merely which one emphasises it more. (c) Is the Browning/Goldhagen dispute ultimately a SITUATION versus CHARACTER dispute, or is that the wrong way to carve it? Take a position and argue it. 400–600 words.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Browning and Goldhagen get taught as opposites but they are working from the same archive and the same unit, Reserve Police Battalion 101, a Hamburg order police battalion made up mostly of middle-aged reservists who were past the age for front-line service. Ordinary Men came out in 1992 and was built largely out of the testimony battalion members gave at Nuremberg and in later West German proceedings. Hitler's Willing Executioners came out four years later and was written substantially as a reply to Browning, who Goldhagen thought had explained the killing without explaining the Germans.

(a) Browning fills SITUATION. His account is multi-causal — deference to authority, peer pressure and the fear of looking weak in front of the men beside you, careerism, the dehumanising effect of propaganda and of the war in the east — but each of those is a feature of the context the men were standing in, and Browning leans on the Milgram obedience experiments to argue that ordinary people dropped into that context would have done roughly what these people did. Goldhagen fills CULTURE. His claim is that German antisemitism was not merely more intense than antisemitism elsewhere but different in kind, eliminationist, meaning that getting rid of the Jews was already available in German political culture as an option long before the Nazis turned it into policy. On his account the killers were not being pushed by their situation so much as carrying their situation around with them.

(b) The evidence they fight over is the offer Major Trapp made to the battalion before its first mass shooting, at Józefów, when he told the men that anyone who did not feel up to it could step out of the ranks. A dozen or so of roughly five hundred did. Browning's reading is that this shows the men were coerced in every sense that matters: the offer came once the unit was already deployed, refusal was publicly visible, and stepping out branded you as weak, so the choice Trapp handed them was not a real choice. Goldhagen reads the identical scene the other way — nobody was going to shoot a man for refusing, most of them killed anyway, and then kept killing for months, so the explanation cannot be pressure and has to be belief.

(c) I think it really is a SITUATION versus CHARACTER dispute and that the CULTURE slot is doing less work than it appears to. A belief system held by essentially everybody in a population is not a third kind of explanation, it is character at the population level: Goldhagen's Germans all have the same disposition, so what he is asserting is dispositional, and what Browning is asserting is that the disposition is beside the point because the situation does the work. On this reading Arendt had the shape of the problem right — the killers were unthinking — and Browning and Goldhagen are just arguing about what filled the empty head, conformity or antisemitism. What complicates the framing is that Browning's own list is not purely situational: careerism, and the brutalisation of the men over two years in the east, are things that happened to them across time, so to that extent Browning is also giving a developmental account of who the men became, which is closer to CHARACTER than he admits.
