I'm a third-year doctoral student in a programme that lets me straddle metaphysics and theoretical biology, and the sentence "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts" has now turned up twice in my life this term, doing two very different jobs. It is printed on a framed poster above the coffee machine in our systems-biology lab — nobody remembers who bought it — and last month a postdoc invoked it, more or less in the poster's words, to shut down an argument about reductionism: if you had a complete inventory of the intrinsic properties of every cell in an organism, he said, you still could not predict the organism's behaviour, because the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, and that is simply what "greater" means in this context. Then, separately, in the metaphysics reading group I belong to, which has spent the term on the metaphysics of material objects, a fellow student used the very same sentence against mereological nihilism. When one of us defended the nihilist line that there are no tables, only simples arranged tablewise, she said that the nihilist must therefore be denying that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, that this denial is manifestly false, and that nihilism accordingly collapses in a single move. Everyone laughed and we moved on, and I went away feeling that something had gone badly wrong in that exchange but unable to say what.

My suspicion is that the sentence is being made to carry several incompatible theses at once, and that at least one of the uses rests on an equivocation — but I would like a proper disambiguation rather than my inarticulate unease. Could you lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to next week's meeting, the distinct precise claims this one sentence could be making? I'd like the readings distinguished along at least three axes: what "the whole" is supposed to pick out (a mereological sum, a structured composite, an organised system with a functional boundary); what "the sum of its parts" is supposed to mean (the parts taken collectively, the aggregate of their intrinsic properties, or literally an additive quantity such as mass); and what sort of "greater" is in play (having more properties, having properties not had by any part, having properties that are not determined by or deducible from the parts, having novel causal powers, failing to be identical to its parts, or being something over and above them). For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude that nobody disputes, whether it is substantive but defensible, whether it is contested or flatly false, and who in the literature would want to deny it — including whether a nihilist, or someone who holds that composition is identity, can accept the plausible readings by paraphrase, for instance by insisting that it is the simples arranged tablewise, collectively, that have the interesting properties.

Finally, and this is the part I most need help with: which reading, if any, actually bears on the special composition question — the question of when, if ever, several things compose some further thing? Some of us think the slogan, if it says anything at all, points toward a restricted answer like van Inwagen's, on which composition occurs only when the resulting activity constitutes a life, while others in the group think it is equally congenial to universalism and hostile to both. Please say whether either side can claim it, and whether the sentence, on its most natural reading, is even the kind of claim that could settle when composition occurs, or whether it is orthogonal to that question — and then tell me, in one or two sentences, what exactly was wrong with my colleague's one-move refutation, assuming something was.
