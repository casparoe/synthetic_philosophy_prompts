I am a staff writer at a British magazine of science and ideas, where I cover the history of science and of ideas and also commission two or three long reads a month from outside contributors — mostly working academics and freelancers who have never met a page of historiographical method and would be puzzled if I sent them one. The anniversary cycle governs a great deal of what we publish: the two-hundred-and-fiftieth year of an expedition, the centenary of a paper, the fiftieth of an institutional scandal. After a decade of this I have come to suspect that nearly everything we run in that genre commits the same fault in slightly different clothes. It treats the past as a rehearsal for the present; it congratulates dead investigators for having got things right, meaning for having agreed with us; it converts their opponents into foils who contributed nothing to anything; and it smuggles inevitability into the syntax — that the field was 'finally ready', that it was 'only a matter of time'. Last month we ran a 1,400-word feature on a nineteenth-century naturalist whose work was neglected in his lifetime, and an academic historian wrote in to say, quite fairly, that we had turned a man who asked questions nobody now asks into a man who had answered ours. My commissioning editor keeps Herbert Butterfield's remark about the whig interpretation of history, that it 'studies the past with reference to the present', on a card above her desk; she takes it as a warning, though I suspect she mostly likes it as a phrase. What I need from you is not another essay on the problem but something a desk can run.

Please write a decision procedure — a sequenced set of tests that can be applied to a pitch in about fifteen minutes and to a draft in about half an hour — for deciding whether a piece of journalism about the past is whiggish in the damaging sense, in which particular way, and what would have to change for it to be defensible. Ground it in whatever the historiographical critique of progress narratives actually says, but write it for contributors who have never encountered the argument: no technical vocabulary unless you define it in a subordinate clause. For each test, give the question to be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and what the procedure does on failure — whether that is a repairable sentence, a structural rewrite, or a kill. Repairs must be achievable inside 1,200 to 1,800 words with no room for a hedging paragraph or a footnote, so please be concrete about what gets cut rather than what gets qualified. Mark plainly which steps are editorial judgment rather than matters of fact, since I do not want a false precision that a commissioner can hide behind; I would rather be told where the procedure runs out than given a score. And the procedure must not overcorrect into a ban on saying that a past claim was simply wrong, that some things really did improve, or that we know more than the people we are writing about — a magazine that could not say so would be useless, and I think dishonest.

Then run it on three cases. First, a commissioned anniversary feature on a forgotten precursor said to have 'anticipated' a modern theory, arriving with a strong narrative spine and exclusive access to family papers. Second, a profile of a researcher whose correct idea was rejected by contemporaries and who is now being rehabilitated as a martyr; here I expect the procedure to hurt, because the martyr frame is precisely what makes the piece publishable, and I want you to say plainly whether it has to go and whether anything can be salvaged in its place. Third, a piece on the collapse of a now-discredited nineteenth-century medical framework, where I want to say without embarrassment that its replacement was an improvement: tell me whether the procedure permits that, and on what conditions. Finish by stating what the procedure cannot settle — which questions it can raise but not answer, where it depends on a distinction (between our questions and theirs, for instance) that historians themselves dispute, and what a commissioner should do when the two errors, flattery of the present and an antiquarian refusal to judge, pull a single draft in opposite directions. Please write the whole thing in British English.
