Background first, since it probably explains the shape of the question. I'm a retired petrologist — about thirty-five years in front of an electron microprobe and a powder diffractometer, mostly igneous stuff. In the last few years I've started reading philosophy of science properly, which is both humbling and a bit irritating, because a lot of the examples are toys.

I've settled on a view about natural kinds that I'd like stress-tested rather than encouraged. Roughly: kinds are real, and what makes them real is that there's a shared underlying structure doing the causal work, so the world does a good deal of the sorting and our classifications either track it or don't. My own gloss on this — I don't think I got it from anybody, so it may be bad — is a convergence test. A candidate kind is natural to the degree that measurement routes developed independently, with uncorrelated failure modes, land on the same partition. Composition says these crystals are one thing, the diffraction pattern says the same thing, the optical behaviour says the same thing, and the three techniques go wrong in unrelated ways, so the agreement isn't an artifact of any one of them. That's what makes the kind mind-independent for me: not that we chose the partition well, but that we couldn't easily have chosen otherwise and still gotten the same answer out of the samples.

What I lean on: the silica polymorphs, where the composition is identical and the structure is what makes them different species, right up to the high-pressure one where the silicon changes coordination; the fact that ordering the elements by atomic weight came out backwards in a couple of places and got fixed by switching to atomic number, which looks to me like underlying structure winning an argument against a convenient observable; and the old business where jade turned out to be two completely unrelated minerals that happen to look and work the same, which I read as the world correcting a folk category.

Objections I think I've already got answers to, so please don't stop at these: the solid-solution series. Plagioclase runs continuously from the sodium end-member to the calcium one, and the named intermediate members are basically agreed cut points on a continuum; olivine is similar. My answer is that the boundaries are conventional while the continuum is real, and vagueness isn't unreality — same move for emerald being a gem variety of beryl rather than a species of its own. Interest-relative does not mean arbitrary. And I know about the philosopher-of-language thought experiments with twin planets and lookalike liquids; I just think they're designed so that the microstructural answer wins by construction, which makes them useless as tests.

The case that actually worries me, and I'd like you to engage it head-on: rock names. Granite is effectively a region on a modal classification diagram, boundaries drawn by committee, and the diagram is about proportions of minerals you can see under a microscope, not about any essence. Yet granite-ish rocks do behave alike and do support inferences. If a kind can be a box on a chart we drew and still be projectible, is my convergence test describing good instrument practice rather than a fact about the world? Relatedly, I think ice counts as an approved mineral species, which seems to make the criteria about natural occurrence and ordered structure rather than chemistry, and I'm not sure that helps me.

One more thing. Robin Hendry has a paper arguing, in effect, that water is not H2O. As far as I followed it the point is that what chemists actually mean by water isn't pinned down by a fixed molecular formula in the way the essentialist story needs. Tell me if I've garbled it, and tell me whether it lands against my view or only against a cruder version of it.

What I want back:

1. The defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want the ranking to be argued, not asserted.
2. Which of these a committed microessentialist would concede as counting against the view, and which they'd wave off — plus where the waving off starts to look like relocating the goalposts rather than answering.
3. What my position risks that the rivals don't: the homeostatic property cluster accounts, the pluralist line that says the same domain supports many equally legitimate kind systems, and the biologist's claim that species are individuals rather than kinds at all. Is there any case where one of them comes out ahead on exactly the same evidence I've cited?
4. Whether the convergence criterion can be prised apart from the microstructure claim — convergence with no essence underneath, or essence that never shows up as convergence — and if so, which half of my view is doing the work.
5. An honest verdict on whether I've insulated the view into unfalsifiability, and if I have, whether that's a defect for a thesis of this kind or a perfectly acceptable feature of a metaphysical one.

And please seperate out what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. Don't hand me the deflationary or pragmatist line as though it were the consensus just because it's currently fashionable, and don't hand me my own line back as consensus either. If there's no consensus, say so and tell me where the real fault lines run. Where you're reconstructing an argument rather than reporting a position somebody holds, flag it — I'd much rather know that than read a confident summary of something nobody ever said.
