**Gradual enough? Rate-relativity and the replacement argument**

The neuron-by-neuron replacement case is usually run as a sorites. Chalmers argues that if swapping neurons for silicon chips left your experience fading or flickering, you'd get fading qualia or dancing qualia, and the subject would be in no position to notice the flicker even though flicker is exactly what there would be; since that's intolerable, functional organization must carry experience with it. Parfit's descendants say something different but structurally similar: what matters is Relation R, psychological continuity and connectedness, and identity itself drops out as the thing we were really worried about. Both, I want to claim, share a premise they never argue for — that a replacement process comes in steps, and that questions about it are questions about what happens at a step.

Notice how the arguments run. At step n, nothing bad happened: you still remember your childhood, the chips behave like the neurons they replaced. At step n+1, same. So by induction nothing bad ever happens, and the endpoint is you. Or, going the other way: at some step the light goes out, and the argument's job is to say you can't locate it. Either way you've already conceded that the process is indexed, that there's a count of replacements, and that the count is what the metaphysics hangs on.

That concession is unwarranted. A neuron isn't a thing that is either present or absent. Replacing one takes hours: the cell is disabled, the local circuit compensates, synapses on the substitute are trained up against a moving background, and the substitute itself drifts as the tissue around it changes. Nobody, including the surgeon, can say how many replacements have been completed by Tuesday afternoon. So the induction that drives both versions of the argument has no fixed sequence to run along.

Here's the point I want to build on. "Gradual" is not a one-place property of a process; it's a relation between two rates, and it's empty until you say what the second rate is. A million-neuron turnover spread over a year is instantaneous relative to the timescale on which memories are consolidated and reconsolidated, and glacial relative to a single saccade. Whether a given replacement is slow or fast is therefore not a question about the replacement. It's a question about which other process you measure it against. The standard question — is gradual replacement survivable — is only well-posed relative to a chosen comparison process, and the literature picks one silently, usually something like the rate at which the person's own psychology can absorb the change.

So pick it explicitly. I propose the relevant comparison process is the one by which a subject keeps track of itself: call it self-tracking, the ongoing operation of maintaining a model of which subject one is, updated against incoming evidence about one's own states and history. The proposal, then: a replacement preserves the subject just in case the rate of replacement is slow relative to the rate at which self-tracking can revise its model. Not psychological continuity in Parfit's sense — memory, intention, character — but the continuity of the tracking relation itself, which can persist even when the contents being tracked change wholesale.

The account buys a few things. A scan-and-copy upload fails, not because of a break in causal history, but because the copy's self-tracking registers itself as the continuing subject without ever having tracked the transition; the jump is in the tracker, not in the substrate. A single-neuron swap passes trivially. Fast whole-brain replacement followed by a slow retraining period is a genuinely borderline case, which is what we want, since intuitions about it are borderline too. And consciousness drops out of the analysis entirely: the proposal is compatible with Chalmers' conclusion while not needing the fading qualia premise at all, which is a nice division of labor. Anesthesia and dreamless sleep have to be treated as pauses in tracking rather than revisions of it, which I'll need a principled way of stating.
