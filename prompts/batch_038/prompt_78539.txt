Just spent like four hours arguing with my friend about free will and punishment and my head is still spinning, so I want to dump where I've landed and get an audit rather than a lecture. Here's the package:

1. Nobody ever basically deserves to suffer for what they did. I'm a hard incompatibilist about it: if determinism is true the causal chain started before I was born, and if it's false then there's just randomness in there, and randomness doesn't get me ultimate authorship either. The covert manipulation cases are what sell it for me — if a neuroscientist is quietly pulling the strings, that guy isn't responsible, and I cant see any relevant difference between him and an ordinarily caused person.

2. But I don't do the free-will-is-an-illusion thing. On this I'm with Dennett against the skeptics' rhetoric: the capacity to see consequences coming and adjust your behavior in the light of them is real, it evolved, it comes in degrees (kids have less of it, some people with certain injuries have less of it, some animals have a bit of it), and it's the only thing that was ever worth wanting. Competence, not contra-causal freedom.

3. I also think holding people responsible is part of the causal machinery that builds that competence. People get better at taking reasons partly because they're treated as answerable — praised, blamed, sanctioned. Sanctions aren't brute conditioning; they land on a creature that can take them as reasons.

4. So punishment should be forward-looking only. Roughly the quarantine idea: you may restrain the dangerous, only as much as the threat requires, in the least harmful way that actually works, and you owe them treatment and a real path back out. I don't think quarantine licenses making an example of somebody. That's just using him.

5. And I think we should say the quiet part loud: strip retributive language out of the law and out of everyday moral talk, because the belief in desert is false and that belief is what makes our system cruel. Plus the public health side — put the energy into lead and poverty and sleep instead of reacting after the fact.

6. Case verdict: the guy who knows he turns violent when he drinks, drinks anyway, and beats someone up is more blameworthy than the guy who snapped once out of nowhere. The first one's history is his own in a way the second one's isnt.

My friend's charge is that the whole thing collapses: that I want Dennett's agency without any metaphysics to make it answerable, and Caruso's humane prison without the one belief that made holding people answerable do any work in the first place. I honestly can't tell if he's got me or if he's just sliding between two senses of responsible.

So: go through the set and rank the tensions by severity, worst first. For each real one, say which commitment has to go or get weakened, and what that costs me — I want the price tag, not just the diagnosis. And flag the pairs that only look bad and dissolve once you pull the senses apart; I don't want everything graded as equally broken. Finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most.

One more thing: keep what's settled seperate from what's genuinely contested. If I've taken a side on something people who work on this disagree hard about — the force of the manipulation argument, whether a quarantine model can really carry a whole system of criminal justice — say so explicitly instead of presenting it as the consensus view.
