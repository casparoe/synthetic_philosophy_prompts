I work in manufacturing quality control, and I came to this subject sideways: my job is deciding whether parts that differ by microns are acceptable, and I eventually realized that asking where a tolerance band really ends is the sorites problem with a price tag. I have no academic training, so I have been working through the literature on vagueness on my own, mostly the dispute between the supervaluationists and the epistemicists. I have reached a point where I cannot tell what the dispute is actually about, and I want a diagnosis of it rather than a verdict on which side is right, and rather than a tour of the field.

First, the shared ground as I understand it. Both sides accept the sorites data: in a series of collections differing by one grain, nobody can say where heaps stop, and nobody can accept the step-by-step reasoning that no single grain ever matters. Both accept that vague predicates have borderline cases, that ordinary use leaves a great deal unsettled, and that speakers who use these predicates are not thereby confused. Most strikingly to me, the two camps seem to agree about the truth-value of every ordinary English sentence I can construct: neither dissents from the claim that a man of 2.1 metres is tall, and both are happy with the disjunction that either ten thousand grains make a heap or they do not, since the supervaluationist keeps excluded middle and the epistemicist keeps all of classical logic.

So where do they diverge? As far as I can see, in only two places. One is sentences containing "definitely": the supervaluationist gives the determinacy operator genuine work to do, while the epistemicist empties it out, because on their account every vague sentence is simply true or false with no gap to police. The other is a claim no ordinary speaker ever makes — that there is some number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 do not. The epistemicist says this is true yet unknowable even in principle, arguing that knowledge requires that one could not easily have been wrong, and that knowing the cutoff would require knowing that the very next case is on the same side of it. The supervaluationist says it is not true at all, because our practice never selected one of the many admissible precisifications over the rest.

I tried to design an observation that would separate the two views and failed. My best attempt was an informant with no limits on his knowledge: the epistemicist says he could name the number, the supervaluationist says he would be confused or would be changing the subject. But that difference is invisible from where we stand, because both sides agree we could never have evidence bearing on it. In my own work something similar happens: a part either passes the gauge or it does not, which suggests a sharp boundary is there; yet the boundary was fixed by a committee choosing among several equally defensible specifications, which suggests nothing sharper than a range was ever determined. Those two descriptions do not contradict each other, and I cannot feel any pressure to pick one.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than endorsed, is that this is not a disagreement about the world but about theoretical bookkeeping — about what the word "true" is supposed to be doing when nobody could ever say which way things are, and about what a semantics of vague language owes us. If that is right, the live question underneath is metasemantic: whether a community's practice can fix a precise extension without fixing one that its members could know.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive but mislocated, or two different projects travelling under one name — one about the logic and semantics of vague discourse, one about the metaphysics of boundaries? Is there any substantive question left once everything the two sides agree on is subtracted, and if so, state it as sharply as you can? Use what each side actually argues, including the standing complaints that epistemicism is mysterious or arbitrary about how the cutoff comes to be fixed, and that the supervaluationist's determinacy operator is a philosopher's invention that does not track how people use "definitely." Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an argument about whether meaning must be available to competent speakers, a formal result, a reformulation of the question — or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why. Finally, say whether the existence of a third camp, which locates the vagueness in the world rather than in our language, supports or undercuts your diagnosis. I am not looking for a reading list, only for the structure of the dispute.
