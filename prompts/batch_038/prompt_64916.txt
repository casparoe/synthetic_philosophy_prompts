Reading group next week, topic is dreams, and I've been asked to write down my actual position beforehand so people can attack it. Here it is, roughly:

(1) Dreaming isn't an experience. I'm with Malcolm here: what's true when I say "I had a vivid dream about my old school" isn't a report of a conscious episode I was undergoing while asleep, and making judgements while asleep is something I'm not willing to grant sense to.
(2) I'm a realist about phenomenal consciousness — when there's something it's like to be in a state, that's a real fact and not just a functional story about it.
(3) Lucid dreaming is genuine: people sometimes know they're dreaming mid-dream and steer things deliberately, and I find the lab work on this credible.
(4) Dream reports are unreliable as to content — the narrative, who was in it, how long things took.
(5) There's no screen in the head: no single moment or place where dream imagery gets presented to an inner witness.
(6) Whether someone had a conscious episode during sleep is a plain factual question that better neuroimaging could in principle settle, report or no report.

Two things I actually want to know: which of these genuinly can't all be true, and which pairs only look bad until you spell them out. Not a survey of the literature — an audit of my set. Also flag anywhere I'm leaning on a premise (1) needs but never states; I suspect something about there being no criterion of identity for dream events is hiding in there and I don't know how much weight (1) can carry without it.

"Where it breaks" — the ones that can't both survive, worst first, argument spelled out, ~400 words. "Where it only looks broken" — the dissolutions, ~200 words. "Cheapest repair" — what I give up, what that costs me elsewhere in the list, and one sentence I'd have to stop saying out loud, ~150 words.
