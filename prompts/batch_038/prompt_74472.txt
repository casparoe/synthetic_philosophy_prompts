I teach a second-year political theory seminar — nineteen students, seventy-five minutes, one room with a single long table, and a no-devices rule for the session. This week they have read Arendt's essay on truth and politics and a two-page excerpt from her essay on the Pentagon Papers. Last night I spent three hours arguing about those pieces with a friend who works in political communications, and I have come to think the argument cannot be settled by exposition.

His position is that there is no categorical difference between the old political lie and the new one. Concealment, spin, and image management differ, he says, only in professionalism and volume, and the claim that contemporary lying aims at something more radical than producing disbelief is a flattering story intellectuals tell about their own moment. My position is that the difference lies in the object of the lie. The traditional liar wants a particular fact suppressed, which leaves the fact recoverable in principle and the lie permanently liable to be undone by testimony or by documents; the newer practice wants the recoverability itself eliminated, so that even a successful exposure changes nothing, because there is no settled version left for it to restore. I also pressed the related Arendtian point that factual truth is uniquely fragile precisely because its denial carries no intrinsic implausibility — the world could always have been otherwise — so its only support is the willingness of people to testify and of institutions to keep a record. Neither of us budged, and I suspect a definition would not have moved either of us.

So I want the class to arrive at the question from inside their own behaviour. Please design the exercise in full, and include:

- The scenario and the record mechanism: what the class is documenting, and how. I want it invented and historically remote — a municipal incident in a small unnamed European town in the late 1950s would suit — so that the session does not turn into a referendum on this week's news, and so that no student is asked to fabricate anything about a real person or a live controversy.
- Roles: how many students carry the fabricating brief, how they are selected without marking them out as the villains, and what makes the brief genuinely attractive to carry rather than merely assigned.
- Rules and materials, paper only.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes, including the reveal and the debrief.
- A reconstruction test at the end that decides whether the record survived. This matters: I want the lie to lose when it is actually tested, so that what the exercise exposes is not the class's gullibility but how rarely anyone is willing to pay the cost of testing, and how quickly testing comes to look pointless or partisan.
- The debrief, mapping particular moments in the session onto the distinction above, with the essays left unnamed until the end.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including the student who declines on principle to fabricate, and the real possibility that the class simply reconstructs the facts from memory in ninety seconds — and what I do in each case.
- A candid statement of which features of the position the exercise cannot reproduce: the stakes, the asymmetry between an image-maker with institutional resources and an ordinary citizen, and the fact that participants can step outside a game whose real analogue no one can step outside of. Tell me what I will have to supply in words that the activity will fail to deliver.

One design decision I want you to make and defend rather than hedge: whether the event the class records should be something that genuinely happens in the room unscripted, which makes the facts real and recoverable and makes fabrication feel like an assault on what they witnessed, or a paper case, which is safer and easier to control. Pick one, justify it, and tell me what the choice costs.

I would rather you were committed than even-handed: give me the exercise you believe will work, not a menu of options. But tell me how confident you are that it produces the experience rather than a dramatization of it, and if your design quietly takes my friend's side at any point — if you think the distinction I have drawn is one of degree after all — say which point that is. And if you think my framing is mistaken, either because the distinction is not one Arendt would recognize or because a paper exercise of this length cannot in principle do what I want, say so at the top instead of building me something elegant on a premise you reject.
