I work in the strategy office of a group of district hospitals, and for the last eighteen months I have been reading the management and evaluation literature in the evenings in an attempt to put a name to something we did here that went wrong in a way I cannot find described anywhere. The history is unremarkable. We used to run one headline figure — median time from referral to first appointment — and it was corrupted precisely as one would predict: clinics began recording referral dates later, splitting pathways into shorter segments, and counting a telephone triage call as an appointment. I have read enough about Goodhart's law, and about Campbell's warning that the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decision-making the more it tends to corrupt the process it is meant to monitor, to know that this is a familiar story and that it is not a story about dishonest people. So the reform, which came from above and which I helped draft, was to retire the single number and replace it with a broad assessment: eleven domains, each scored from one to five by a panel that includes senior clinicians from the units being assessed, with narrative justifications attached to every score, and with weightings that the panel may revise each cycle "to reflect local priorities". No single domain is gameable, because no single domain decides anything, and the panel's deliberation is meant to capture what the old number missed.

What happened instead is the thing I have no word for. Within a year the score distribution had compressed into the top two bands; every unit was good, and the median referral-to-appointment time got worse by roughly a fifth. When a panel did surface something genuinely bad, the same meeting would reweight two domains, or agree that a criterion had been read too narrowly last cycle, or attach a caveat about case-mix, and the aggregate would emerge acceptable — and each of those moves was individually reasonable, which is exactly why nobody objected. The distinctive failure, as I see it, is not that the assessment came apart from the underlying reality; it is that the assessment lost the capacity to come out badly. There was no observable state of the hospitals that the scoring procedure would have recorded as failure, and the panel could have discovered this only by inventing a result to feed it. I have two smaller instances that I think show the same shape. A university I consulted for replaced a count of research outputs with a portfolio review across six headings with negotiable weightings, and for three consecutive cycles no department scored below the institutional mean, an arithmetically curious fact that nobody remarked upon. A software firm I know retired a bug-closure target in favour of a quarterly engineering-health rubric with a dozen sub-scores set by the team leads themselves; releases slowed, rubric scores rose, and when an engineer proposed reintroducing a hard delivery measure the answer was that delivery was already represented in the rubric and the weightings could be revisited next quarter. A colleague says I am describing self-assessment bias, and another says I am describing bureaucracy, and I am sure both are ingredients, but neither seems to me to be the thing: a panel of wholly disinterested outsiders with no stake in the outcome would, I suspect, have produced exactly the same immunity, because the immunity lives in the flexibility of the instrument rather than in the motives of the people using it. I have also wondered whether it is a measurement-shaped version of the point that a theory can always be rescued by tinkering with its auxiliary assumptions, but that feels like an analogy I am reaching for rather than a concept I have found.

So I should like to know whether this has an established name, and I want to be clear that a label alone will not satisfy me — I want the carving. Which are the nearest established concepts, how does the phenomenon I have described differ from each of them, and which of them, if any, does it fall under as a special case? If nothing fits, coin and define a term for it, and give me the boundary conditions: an instance of a rich, qualitative, panel-run assessment that would *not* count as this, and an instance of a crude single number that *would*, so that I can test the definition against cases I have not yet described. Please also be explicit about the confidence of your own verdict rather than presenting it as settled: tell me which of your candidate concepts you would drop first under pressure, what consideration would change your mind about whether this is one phenomenon rather than two — the loss of any possibility of failure, and the drift in what the criteria are taken to mean — and where you think my description is most likely to have led you astray, since I selected these three examples to fit a pattern I already half believe in. Do not trouble yourself with references or a reading list; I am after the analysis, not the sources. Please write the whole response in British English.
