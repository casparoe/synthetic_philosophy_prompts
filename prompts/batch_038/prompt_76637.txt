I co-teach a graduate seminar on the normative foundations of constitutional design, and last week a colleague from the law school reached for a very old image in defense of a proposal I oppose. The proposal would place a structural fiscal rule — a ceiling on the structural deficit, monitored by an independent council whose mandate and membership could be altered only by a two-thirds vote of both houses — beyond the reach of ordinary legislative revision. His defense was that a polity entrenching such a rule does precisely what Odysseus did when he had himself lashed to the mast: foreseeing a moment at which it will be tempted to act against its own considered judgment, the demos disables itself in advance, and its freedom is enlarged rather than diminished by the ropes. He then claimed the image also disposes of two standard objections, on the grounds that the people who do the binding and the people who are bound are the same people, and that the constraint is self-imposed rather than externally imposed — so neither the counter-majoritarian difficulty nor the charge of rule by a dead hand can get a grip.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the fiscal rule as policy. I want an audit of the image, and I would like it in four parts.

First, set out the correspondence explicitly; a table will serve. Identify what, in the entrenchment case, plays the role of the sirens, the ropes, the mast, the wax in the sailors' ears, the standing instruction not to release the bound man however much he pleads, and the sailors who row on regardless.

Second, note that the episode deploys two quite different devices: one that leaves perception and judgment wholly intact while removing the capacity to act on them, and another that removes perception itself. Assess whether my colleague has collapsed these, what turns on the difference, and which constitutional instruments correspond to each — entrenchment and supermajority amendment rules on one side, delegation to insulated bodies, standing exclusions of certain considerations from deliberation, and restrictions on what information or pressure reaches the decision-maker on the other.

Third, separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his inference from the merely cosmetic ones. Take seriously at least the following: the asymmetry between the binding self and the bound self, and whether the later agent's reversal of judgment is a failure of will or a revision of assessment; the fact that the threat in the poem is exogenous, precisely described in advance, and of known and brief duration, whereas the temptations an entrenched rule is meant to forestall are typically neither describable in advance nor datable; and the fact that the sailors who refuse to untie Odysseus have an interest in his survival that is independent of his command, which is not obviously true of whoever is charged with enforcing an entrenched rule against a legislature that wants it relaxed.

Fourth, tell me whether his two additional claims survive — same people, self-imposed — or whether they hold only for a constitutional community bounded enough in time and membership that the identity of binder and bound is not vacuous, and say what remains of the precommitment defense if it holds only there.

If you conclude that the image cannot carry the weight he puts on it, say what it can carry, and propose a better source domain for this kind of entrenchment, together with the conclusion the better image would license instead of the one he drew.

Please keep the whole response within 900 words, table included. Where you must economize, cut the retelling of the episode rather than the audit of the inference.
