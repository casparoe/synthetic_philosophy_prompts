I am in my final year of high school and writing a long essay for our school's philosophy review on a dispute I have not managed to resolve: whether Buddhist ethics is a form of consequentialism. In the secondary literature I have been working through, two camps have hardened, and they increasingly seem to be arguing past each other rather than at each other.

On one side are the consequentialist readers — the version I know best is Charles Goodman's — who hold that the tradition's ethical reasoning is best reconstructed as aiming at the reduction of duḥkha across sentient beings generally, and that this reconstruction is textually supported rather than merely charitable. Their exhibits: the Jātaka tale in which the Buddha, in a past life as a sea captain, kills a would-be mass murderer in order to save five hundred merchants, and is commended rather than blamed; the bodhisattva's willingness to accept unfavourable rebirths for the sake of others; the argument in Śāntideva's meditation chapter that suffering has no owner, so it makes no difference whose suffering it is and all of it is to be prevented; and the doctrine of no-self, which they take to remove the metaphysical footing for agent-centred constraints.

On the other side are the virtue-ethical readers, following Damien Keown, who argue that Buddhist ethics is structured around the cultivation of character and the transformation of intention rather than around states of affairs to be promoted. Their exhibits: the centrality of cetanā in the assessment of karma, which they read as making moral quality a function of the agent's motive rather than of aggregate outcomes; the apparently exceptionless status of the precepts as training rules rather than as summaries of past calculations; nirvāṇa functioning as the telos of the whole path rather than as one further good to be maximized; and the charge that consequentialist readers import modern welfarist vocabulary and then congratulate the canon for containing it.

Please draft the joint statement such a collaboration would produce. I would like it to contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — things both camps can sign about the texts and about what any adequate interpretation must explain. I expect some of these to be methodological, such as that the tradition nowhere formulates an aggregative calculus, and that intention is treated as the primary determinant of karmic quality. Please do not pad this section with trivialities; every claim should be one that does real work in constraining the two readings.

2. A disagreements table with the following columns: the dispute; the consequentialist statement of it, worded so that a consequentialist reader would accept it as their own view; the virtue-ethical statement of it, worded so that a Keown-style reader would accept it as their own view; and then, separately, whether the disagreement is substantive or turns on how generously the label consequentialism is being stretched.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. I suspect at least three: whether the intentionalism of karma entails agent-relativity in the modern sense or is compatible with an impartial evaluative standard applied to motives; whether the sea captain case shows a precept overridden for the sake of aggregate welfare, or shows something else entirely, such as a story about the purity of a compassionate motive that happens to save people and about upāya; and whether the no-self doctrine does the work the consequentialists assign it, or supports a rather different point about the ownership of suffering. Add any I have missed, and drop any that turn out to be merely verbal.

4. For each crux, a concrete textual case, argument, or survey that both camps would accept as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction written down in advance — what the virtue-ethicist expects to find in the Vinaya's rule-casuistry and the Abhidharma's analysis of wholesome and unwholesome roots if she is right, and what the consequentialist expects to find there if he is right.

Finally, since neither camp has signed anything yet, I want your own assessment appended: which of the formulations above you think is most likely to be rejected by one side as slanted, where your reasoning is most exposed, and what consideration or textual pattern would lead you to recast the disagreement differently — for instance to demote a crux you have listed to a merely verbal dispute, or to promote one I dismissed. If you think I have mischaracterized either camp in setting this up, say so before drafting rather than silently correcting me.
