I'm writing a paper for a seminar on the philosophy of punishment, and I want to pin down what Norway's prison system actually commits us to rather than simply describing how humane it looks. Consider three features held together inside a single system. First, the governing principle that the loss of liberty is itself the punishment and nothing further should be added to it: at Halden, officers are unarmed, the windows have no bars, prisoners shop for groceries and cook their own meals with real knives, and inmates receive a small daily payment as an incentive to leave their cells — yet the perimeter wall is deliberately kept visible from nearly everywhere, so that the deprivation is continuously felt even though the surroundings are comfortable. Second, the same legal system caps determinate sentences at twenty-one years but permits a preventive detention order with a court-set minimum term, extendable five years at a time without limit on the ground that the prisoner remains dangerous, so that release turns on a forward-looking risk judgment rather than on the offence. Third, when the man convicted of the 2011 attacks litigated his conditions of confinement, a Norwegian court found in his favour not because of material hardship — he held several rooms, a television and exercise equipment — but because of his isolation from other human beings; that ruling was later overturned on appeal.

What theory of the harm of imprisonment does this bundle presuppose? In particular: why should a view that locates the punishment in lost liberty end up meticulously rebuilding ordinary life inside, and what work is the visible wall doing that amenities cannot? Reconstruct the implicit account of what imprisonment takes from a person, and say whether the risk-based extension is consistent with that account or quietly substitutes a different view of persons — one on which dangerousness is a standing property to be tracked rather than something for which an agent is answerable. I'd also like to know which rival theories the practice rules out (retributivism that requires hard treatment, deterrence that needs the threat to be genuinely unpleasant, communicative accounts on which punishment must be a burdensome expression of censure), and where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would equally license it. Finally: if the real harm of confinement lies in severed social ties rather than in liberty as such, what follows for sentence length — does that view license longer terms in socially rich prisons, shorter ones for the isolated, or something else?
