Next month I'm running week four of a second-year applied ethics module, and the assigned question is whether the numbers should count when we have to choose whom to save. Twenty-four students, one seventy-five-minute seminar, standard room with a whiteboard and enough index cards and dice if I order them in advance. They've already had a week on aggregate welfare and a week on distributive fairness, so they arrive fluent in the vocabulary of maximizing and reasonably confident that saving five is simply better than saving one. My aim for this session is the opposite of fluency: I want them to allocate scarce help under rules that make them do something they cannot afterwards defend, and to notice the indefensibility themselves before I have said a word about Taurek. Please design the activity around a scenario of your own invention — not his divided supply of the drug, not a lifeboat, not anything with a trolley in it, none of the transplant cases. Something institutional and slightly unfamiliar, where the claimants differ in ways the students will initially think are relevant and later suspect aren't.

Three design commitments I'd like you to build in, and one I'd like you to argue against if you think it's wrong. First, run at least two rounds in which the location of the numbers is reversed relative to the location of the vivid, individually voiced claims, so that whichever principle a student reaches for in round one embarrasses them in round two. Second, include a round where the allocation procedure is a lottery, and engineer the setup so that the class confronts the difference between giving each group an equal chance and giving each person an equal chance; my own suspicion is that a naive group lottery quietly smuggles counting back in through the weighting of the draw, and I'd like the students to catch that rather than be told it. Third, represent the larger group as a set of individuals who each state their own loss in their own voice, rather than as a single bloc with a spokesperson, so that the moment someone tries to add those losses together feels like a strange thing to be doing. The commitment I'm unsure about: whether the activity needs a scoring currency at all. I assume you'll want tokens or points to make choices consequential without using real money, but any scoring scheme seems to presuppose the very comparability of losses that the argument denies. Either show me how to get consequence without comparison, or concede the point and tell me what I'll have to supply by hand afterwards. Also note that two of the students are pre-med and will want to relitigate everything as hospital triage protocol; build in something that channels that energy into the game instead of letting it hijack the room.

What I need back: the full rules, a materials list, and a minute-by-minute timeline for seventy-five minutes including the debrief; the debrief itself, mapping specific things that will have happened at specific points in the activity onto the pieces of the argument — what is being denied, what the aggregating reply says, and where the equal-chance intuition is supposed to sit between them; a candid account of what will go wrong the first time I run this with twenty-four people, including the failure modes that are most likely in the lottery round and what I should do in the moment; what I'm permitted to say before the activity starts, given that neither the name nor the thesis should appear until the end; and finally an honest limit statement identifying which feature of the argument no game of this kind can reproduce and the facilitator has to lecture in. No reading list, please — the readings are set and I don't want suggestions about them; I want something I could hand to a colleague to run in my place.
