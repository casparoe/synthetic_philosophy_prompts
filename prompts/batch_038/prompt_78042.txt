Last month the board of the health system I work for put me on a three-person committee charged with recommending whether our new children's wing should keep the name of its founding benefactor, an industrialist whose fortune in the 1890s came partly from company towns, private police, and the breaking of strikes. I am the only member with any training in ethics — I direct our clinical ethics service — and I have three months to produce a report that will be read, quoted, and resented. What has unsettled me is not the benefactor. It is that in our second meeting two colleagues reached, independently and without embarrassment, for the line that he was a man of his time, and I noticed that I wanted to reach for it too, and also that I refuse to reach for it in other cases where it looks like exactly the same move. So, rather than argue from instinct in front of a board, I sat down over a weekend with the historical cases everyone argues about, wrote out my verdicts before reading anyone else's, and discovered that they do not fit together. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here is the list. A Virginia planter-statesman who wrote the era's most quoted sentences about liberty, held several hundred people across his adult life, freed a handful at most, treated people as assets to be sold or given away when his finances required it, and acknowledged in his own private notes that the relation of master to slave trains one side in habits of tyranny and the other in habits of submission — I do not excuse him, even though the economy, the law, his family, and his entire class were built on it, because petitioners to Congress were telling the republic in his lifetime that the thing was a monstrous contradiction and he knew it. A navigator whose arrival inaugurated forced labour and mass death on the islands he reached, and who was arrested and shipped home in chains by the crown that had commissioned him, on charges arising from how he governed — condemned outright, and note that he was condemned by the standards of his own society's officials, so no appeal to the times is available at all. A European king who administered an African territory as personal property, whose government's own commission of inquiry documented the atrocities, and who carried on afterward — fully blameworthy, and the publication of the inquiry seems to me to aggravate rather than mitigate. A wartime leader credited with holding the line against invasion whose government declined, during a colonial famine that killed a million or more people, to divert shipping to the starving region, and whose disparaging remarks about that colony's population were recorded by colleagues at the time — here my verdict is odd and I want it examined: I think he is blameworthy for the famine decisions and I do not think the wartime leadership cancels or discounts that blame by any amount, because I don't believe there is a ledger on which credits and debits of that kind are entered against one another. A sixteenth-century friar who argued before his monarch's councils for the full humanity of the peoples of the Americas, and who for a time proposed importing enslaved Africans in their place, a proposal he later repented — I want to give him enormous credit and real blame simultaneously, which I suppose is consistent with the ledger denial above, though I can't see how. And then two cases that are supposed to look alike and don't. A surgeon in the 1940s who performed a psychosurgical operation that was then standard and celebrated on patients whose consent amounted to a signature, wrecking some of them: excused, thoroughly, because nothing better was visible to him and the profession's authorities were handing out prizes for it. And the same shape, now, with me inside it — the industrial confinement of animals for food, which people in 2080 will, I think, look back on the way I look back on that surgeon, and which I participate in three meals a day: not excused. I am blameworthy now. I cannot make those two verdicts consistent, because from inside each life the epistemic situations look identical to me, and the only difference is which end of history I happen to be standing at.

My own tentative repair, which I want you to break rather than admire, is what I have started calling the menu rule: a person is blameworthy for failing to do X only if X was, at the time, actually on the menu — articulated as a live option by someone in the person's own society, in terms the person could have understood without information or concepts that did not yet exist — and the degree of blame then scales with what acting on that option would have cost them, except that refusing to pay any cost whatsoever is itself the failing being judged. Two things go wrong. The first is that the cost clause threatens to swallow the availability clause: if social ruin, impoverishment, or exile counts as a sufficient price, nearly everyone in every era is excused, and if it never does, the clause does nothing. The second is that the menu rule makes moral luck in one's neighbours decisive, so that two planters of identical character, cruelty, and conviction are judged differently because a Quaker happened to live in one county and not the other, and that strikes me as intolerable in a principle about blame even though I keep wanting to say it is true. So, specifically: state the best principle you can — one candidate, or a short list of genuinely competing ones — precisely enough that it could be applied by someone who disagrees with me, and tell me which of my cases motivates each of its clauses. Say whether the principle is fundamentally epistemic, about what the person could have known, or practical, about what they could have done at what price, and show me a case where those two come apart. Then rule on cases I have not listed: the person whose society had no word at all for the wrong but who could have derived it in two steps from principles he explicitly endorsed and wrote down; the person whose society did articulate the alternative but whose government made articulating it a capital offence; and an institution rather than a person — my own health system, say — which inherited an endowment from blame that is now settled, and which must decide whether keeping the name is a fresh act with a fresh menu of its own. Finally, be blunt about the cost: which of my verdicts can your principle not accommodate, and if you had to choose between saving the 1940s surgeon and saving the refusal to let wartime leadership offset a famine, which do you cut, and what does the surviving principle imply about the asymmetry between being judged by the past and being judged by the future?
