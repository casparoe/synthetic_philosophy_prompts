Write a short story about a sealed deep-sea settlement whose every allocation is made overnight by a planning computer that posts trial exchange rates for each good, observes what runs short and what goes untouched, and revises the rates before dawn — an arrangement that has kept the settlement fed and lit for forty years. The story turns when a surface supply vessel arrives, its crew trading at rates the computer cannot reconcile with its own, and it will depart in six days; the settlement's chief planner must decide whether to re-anchor the whole internal system to the visitors' numbers or to keep trusting the numbers that have always worked. Give the dispute a concrete object — one particular good, one chronic shortage, one worker who knows something about it that no rate has ever captured — and let both positions look genuinely defensible from inside the story rather than resolving it by argument. Deliver exactly five numbered scenes of a paragraph or two each, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no scene in which a character explains the economics outright.
