You're a philosopher of medicine who's spent way too long inside the death-determination literature — Bernat vs. Truog vs. the higher-brain crowd, the whole mess — and I need you to reason like that for a working committee, not for a journal article. I chair the determination-of-death consult service at a mid-size academic hospital, and most of what we do is procedural (apnea tests done right, ruling out sedatives and hypothermia, mediating with families), but every so often we get something that actually forces us to notice that we've never pinned down what our own criteria mean. Our record so far, for reference:

1. Massive hemorrhage, no brainstem reflexes, apnea test done properly, ventilator-dependent, but still making antidiuretic hormone so no diabetes insipidus, warm, no sedatives on board — we declared death.
2. Eight months of permanent vegetative state, no awareness on any testing, brainstem reflexes intact, breathing on her own through a trach — we said not dead, and told the family that withdrawing support was a separate question with its own answer.
3. Controlled donation after circulatory determination: support withdrawn after a non-survivable injury, asystole, five-minute hands-off window, no return of circulation, then procurement — we upheld the determination.
4. Cold-water submersion, core temp about 25.4, no pulse, fixed dilated pupils, roughly an hour of CPR by the time we were called — we refused to let anyone make a determination until she was rewarmed and reversible causes were excluded. She ended up surviving with deficits, which people here still bring up.
5. Anencephalic newborn — we said not dead.
6. A donation case where about three minutes after asystole the monitor showed an organized rhythm for maybe twenty seconds with no pulse and no output — we said the determination stands, because there was electrical activity but no circulation actually happening.
7. Continuous-flow LVAD patient, no palpable pulse, blood pressure not measurable by cuff, awake and doing rehab while waiting for a transplant — we wrote in the note that pulselessness and unrecordable pressure are not by themselves grounds for a circulatory determination.
8. A family that insisted our whole-brain patient was still alive and demanded continued ventilation — we said the determination isn't a matter of family consensus, but granted a 72-hour accommodation and helped arrange a transfer.

Now the case in front of us. A 34-year-old woman whose heart and lungs both failed in the same illness three weeks ago; echo shows no contractility at all and the lungs have essentially no gas-exchange function left, so all of her perfusion and all of her oxygenation are being done by a centrifugal-pump extracorporeal circuit with non-pulsatile flow. She's on continuous dialysis too, and the circuit's heat exchanger does a lot of her temperature regulation. She is extubated, awake, oriented, talking, doing physical therapy on a mobile console, has every brainstem reflex, a normal EEG, and she makes spontaneous breathing efforts that accomplish nothing. She's a candidate for a double transplant in a few months. She's also miserable and has asked us to turn the circuit off, and both her attending and our counsel want to know, in writing, whether under our own framework she is a living patient from whom we'd be withdrawing life-sustaining treatment or somebody who is already dead. Nobody on the committee has a clean intuition, and the two prongs of the uniform act most states have adopted seem to point in opposite directions here, which is not something we've ever had to admit out loud.
