A colleague and I spent most of yesterday evening on a disagreement about safety methodology that I now suspect was partly verbal, and I would like an instrument that settles it rather than a third round of argument.

The shared starting point, which neither of us wants questioned and which your tree should simply assume: the deployed system's goals may be wrong, and a measure is not to be dismissed merely because it does nothing to fix them. Our dispute is about a particular kind of measure.

My colleague's favored scheme runs roughly as follows. Before any externally consequential action is executed, the frontier model must submit a natural-language account of its intended course of action together with its working notes to a separate verifier model; the verifier is trained to flag instrumental reasoning, self-preservation concern, and any apparent attempt to model the audit itself; and the whole apparatus is load-bearing on the thought that a misaligned system would find it more expensive to deceive the verifier consistently than to comply with it. My objection is that this is alignment work in control work's clothes: nothing is constrained by construction, and every guarantee offered is a guarantee about what the system would choose, which is precisely what we agreed not to rely on. His reply is that my own preferred package — restrictive tool access, air-gapped deployment, a hard ceiling on the system's ability to acquire resources outside the sandbox — is only affordable because we quietly assume the system will not spend much effort working around it, so the difference between us is one of degree rather than kind. I no longer trust my intuitions about where the line falls.

Please write a decision tree, to be handed out to our reading group (mixed audience: some work on deployed systems, others have only read around the topic), that takes a reader from the shared commitment above to a named position on the control-versus-alignment question.

Requirements:

- Six to eight branch nodes; no more than twelve leaves.
- Every node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer from the description of a proposed safety measure alone, without knowing which position the answer leads to and without needing empirical facts about any particular model. Questions of the form "do you think control or alignment matters more?" are banned, as are nodes that use any position's name.
- Build the tree from at least these axes: whether the measure's efficacy depends on the system's dispositions rather than only on its capabilities and access; whether it presumes the system would not resist even if it fully understood the measure; whether failure is detected before harm or prevented by construction; whether the guarantee lives in the weights, in the deployment environment, or in the interaction protocol; and whether the threat model includes deliberate deception as opposed to merely misgeneralized behavior. Add, drop, or reorder axes as the logical space demands, but say why.
- Each leaf carries a name — the field's ordinary vocabulary where one exists, "unnamed" where none does — plus one sentence naming the single assumption about the system on which that leaf most depends.
- Construct the space first and populate it second. After the tree, list every combination of answers your nodes permit that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply tried it yet.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate — that is, any two positions that answer every node identically. Name the failure; do not smooth it over.
- Render the tree as indented plain text with each node's answer options labeled, so that it can be printed without redrawing.

Then apply the tree twice: once to my colleague's verifier scheme, once to my restrictive-deployment package, and report the leaf each reaches. If either reaches no leaf, or reaches two at once, say so explicitly and identify the additional node that would be needed to place it.

End with one line in exactly this form, with no punctuation other than the separators:

Verdict: <leaf label for the verifier scheme> ; <leaf label for my restrictive package> ; <SUBSTANTIVE or VERBAL>
