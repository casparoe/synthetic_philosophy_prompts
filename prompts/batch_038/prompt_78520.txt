So I run a small reading group that's half anthropologists and half philosophers, and we've been circling the "was the agricultural revolution a mistake?" question for months and getting absolutely nowhere. I want you to act as a senior paleodemographer who has also done a lot of formal work on welfare aggregation and who has run several adversarial collaborations before — someone whose job is to draft the document, not to win the argument. Think carefully, and before you draft anything, spend a short brainstorming section (bullet points are fine) working out where the two camps are actually talking past each other versus genuinely opposed. Then produce the joint statement.

The two camps, as I'll define them for you:

**Camp A — the affluence/decline camp.** They hold that the shift from foraging to farming made the typical human life worse: shorter, sicker, more nutritionally narrow, more exposed to epidemic disease and zoonoses, more violent, and far more unequal. They take this to be documented rather than romantic — the ethnographic picture of foragers with modest needs and lots of leisure time, plus the skeletal record after the transition (falling stature, dental decay and enamel defects, markers of infectious burden and nutritional stress, widening grave-good inequality). They think the popular essay tradition that calls farming the worst mistake in human history is directionally right.

**Camp B — the inference-skeptics.** They mostly *accept* the lesion data and reject the verdict. Their objections: skeletal assemblages are samples of the dead, not of the living, so lesion frequencies are confounded by differential frailty and by the fact that a high-mortality population can look *healthier* in bone precisely because the frailest died before lesions had time to form (the thing usually called the osteological paradox — demographic nonstationarity, selective mortality, hidden heterogeneity in frailty). Add to that that farmers are sedentary, dense, and get excavated, while foragers are scattered and under-sampled; that agriculture supported orders of magnitude more human lives; that twentieth-century ethnographic foragers were pushed onto marginal refugia and so aren't a fair baseline; and that the honest counterfactual may not be "everyone stays an affluent forager" but "carrying capacity was already saturated, so fewer people exist at a comparable or worse condition."

What I want in the deliverable:

1. **A numbered list of claims both camps can sign.** Not mush — each claim specific enough that a member of either camp would recognize it as theirs, and each labeled either unqualified agreement or agreement-with-a-qualifier, with the qualifier stated. Include claims about the raw evidence itself, about what needs explaining, and about what the ethnographic and archaeological records do and don't license.

2. **A disagreements table**, columns: (a) # , (b) the disagreement stated neutrally, (c) Camp A's own preferred wording of their side, (d) Camp B's own preferred wording of their side — each phrased so that side would accept it and not feel caricatured, (e) whether the disagreement is verbal, factual, methodological, or evaluative. Be honest about which rows are really just people using "better off" to mean different things, and say which meaning each side has in mind.

3. **The cruxes.** I want at least four, and I suspect one of them is the aggregation question — whether the verdict is governed by average wellbeing per life, total wellbeing across lives, reproductive fitness, or something like freedom/capabilities — and another is how much of the health gap survives correction for frailty selection and preservation bias. For each crux give: the sub-question, phrased so that settling it would actually move at least one camp; the specific test, case type, or argument *both* camps agree would bear on it (for example, what a well-designed paired-site comparison in one region would need to look like, or what independent non-skeletal health proxies would count); each camp's prediction written down **in advance**, before the result; and which result would move which camp and by how much. If a crux has no test either side would accept, say so plainly rather than inventing one.

4. **A short residual joint statement** — the paragraph both camps would put their names to, followed by a precise statement of what still separates them. And then your own assessment of whether anything could actually end this debate, or whether it's structurally permanent.

Ground rules: American English, please. **No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no page numbers** — I'm reading this offline and a made-up reference is worse than none, so argue from the shape of the evidence instead. If you name a site, a region, a species, or a number, only do it if you're confident it's right; otherwise describe the kind of evidence and flag that you're doing so. Wherever you're on shaky empirical ground, mark it explicitly with something like "I'm not sure about this" rather than dressing it up — the whole point is a document two hostile camps could sign, and they'll pounce on anything inflated. Also, don't just split the difference: if you think one camp's objection is stronger than the other's, the joint statement should show that in what each side concedes.
