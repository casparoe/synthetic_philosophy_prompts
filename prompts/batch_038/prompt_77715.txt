In moral psychology there is a well-documented tendency for people to judge a harm that is brought about as worse than an equally bad harm that is merely allowed, often labeled omission bias. The prevailing interpretation in that literature is that this is a bias: a systematic distortion of moral judgment which should ideally be corrected, or at least corrected for when designing institutions and questionnaires. In normative ethics, meanwhile, a substantial body of work defends the opposing verdict — that the distinction between doing and allowing is morally real, that there is a genuine difference in significance between producing a harm and failing to prevent one, and that it is the theory which collapses the two that stands in need of revision.

The two literatures appear to be describing the same behavioral pattern and assigning it opposite normative statuses. I would like a careful explanation of the conceptual issue this generates, for which I will stipulate a name: the baseline problem for moral debiasing. Please address the following, in order.

1. Say precisely what an attribution of bias presupposes. A judgment can only be biased relative to some standard it deviates from, and in statistical or perceptual contexts the standard is supplied relatively uncontroversially — the world, or the axioms of the calculus. Lay out the candidate baselines available in the moral case: a coherence baseline, on which the standard is the subject's own other, more considered judgments; a substantive baseline, on which the standard is correct moral truth; and an outcome baseline, on which the standard is whatever choice actually promotes value. Explain why each of these does normative work that the word "bias" is then quietly borrowing, and why the choice among them cannot itself be made on the basis of data about how people judge.

2. Explain why the disagreement between the psychologist and the ethicist is therefore not an empirical one waiting for better experiments, and yet is not simply a verbal dispute either. What is it about the description of the phenomenon that already encodes one of the baselines?

3. Consider this proposal, which I would like you to evaluate rather than merely report. Suppose someone offers a decision procedure: strip the case of its real-world context and present it to subjects as a bare hypothetical; if the effect survives this bracketing, it reflects a preference, and if it disappears, it was a bias generated by contextual noise. Explain why the procedure begs the question in both directions — why bracketing can manufacture an apparent preference out of a bias, and equally can dissolve a genuine moral consideration by removing exactly the features that give it weight.

4. To keep the discussion anchored, work through the following case. Subjects are asked to allocate a fixed disaster-relief budget between two towns. In one condition, the allocation is described as the one already in force since last year, and subjects are asked whether to alter it. In the other, a numerically identical allocation is described as one of several competing proposals, and subjects are asked which to adopt. Many subjects who leave the incumbent allocation untouched in the first condition select a different allocation in the second, though the distributions at issue are the same. Explain what the two available readings of this result are — that the second condition exposes a framing artifact, or that the first condition preserves something the second one deletes, namely the moral weight of existing entitlements and legitimate expectations — and why the result itself cannot arbitrate between them.

5. Finally, explain what survives of the concept of a moral bias once the baseline problem is granted. Is there a formulation of the attribution that is weaker than a commitment to substantive moral truth, yet stronger than the empty claim that this judgment differs from mine? If so, state what it licenses us to say about the doing/allowing case in particular; if not, explain what the concept was doing all along in the debiasing literature.

Please do not offer a bibliography, references, or named attributions; argue entirely from the concepts. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,100 words, pitched at a reader who is at home with the trolley problem and with reflective equilibrium but has never encountered the heuristics-and-biases literature. Resist the temptation to dissolve the tension by declaring one of the two literatures confused: the interest lies in explaining why the same pattern can be coherently described as a defect and as an insight, and what that tells us about the structure of the concept of bias.
