I write a science-and-ideas column and I've been shadowing a philosopher of probability for a profile — I'll call her Miriam, she's not on the record yet — and across three very long interviews she handed down rulings on basically every one of those classic "so which number is it, then" puzzles I could think to throw at her. She's off the grid untill Thursday and I've got a new case to put to her, so I want to know beforehand where she'll land and why. Don't send me to the literature, I've read the literature — I want your read on her.

Her verdicts, more or less as they appear in my notes:

1. Bertrand's chord problem, the one about a chord longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle: no answer. A fraud as written. She says there's three defensible numbers — a third, a half, a quarter — and I have to tell her how the chord gets thrown before she'll commit to any of them.

2. Buffon's needle, needle shorter than the gap between the floorboard lines, and I deliberately didn't mention any protocol at all: she answered instantly, 2L over pi-d, and got genuinely irritated when I said hold on, didn't you just tell me the chord problem has no answer? Her line was that sliding and rotating the floor changes nothing, so there's only one distribution on offer and nothing left to choose between.

3. The wine-and-water bottle, where all you're told is that the wine-to-water ratio is somewhere between 1 and 2: no answer, and she thinks this one's worse than the chord problem, because the restatement — the water-to-wine ratio is between a half and 1 — describes the identical physical situation and can't also be uniform, so pointing at a machine doesn't rescue anything until somebody says which quantity the machine is uniform in.

4. The cube factory, edges between 0 and 1 cm, what's the chance the next cube's edge is under a half: no answer as stated. But she volunteered that if I told her the machine was engineered to make volumes uniform, or engineered to make edges uniform, she'd give me a number in five seconds flat.

5. Borel's sphere case — a point chosen uniform on the globe, then you condition on it landing on one particular meridian: this one's fine, she says, actually well-posed, and the latitude distribution along that meridian is not flat, the polar ends come out thinner than people expect. Her explanation was that "uniform on the sphere" already fixes a measure, so the famous paradox is about a sloppy limit and not about a missing answer.

Okay, the new one, which is the reason I'm asking. A cosmologist keeps pitching me a version of the doomsday/Copernican argument for a sidebar, and his pitch goes: treat yourself as a random draw from all the humans who will ever live, so your birth rank is uniform between 1 and N, where N is the total; you're roughly the hundred-billionth human; so N is unlikely to be vastly larger than that, and the species doesn't have much of a future ahead of it. When I said random draw from what, exactly, and who's doing the drawing, he said the principle of indifference is doing exactly the same work there that it does in the geometric puzzles, which is why it's legitimate, and that I'm just being squeamish.

So — what does Miriam say? I want the machinery, not just the guess. What rule, or what handful of rules that fit those five verdicts equally well, could she actually be running? Which of them makes the cosmologist's argument come out well-posed, which makes it come out empty, and which would you bet on and why? Is there anything in her track record that supports the scale-invariance move he's implicitly making — uniform in N versus uniform in log N — or does her own wine-and-water ruling blow that up? And critically: are her needle ruling and her chord ruling compatible, or is one of them doing violence to the other, because if they can't both stand that's my lede and not the sidebar.

If her verdicts genuinely don't pin the new case down, say so plainly, tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to with each possible answer, and name the one further puzzle I should put to her on Thursday that would split the surviving rules apart.
