I'm a civil engineer by training and not an academic in any sense, but for about three years now I have been reading the evolutionary and cognitive literature on religion on my own — Hume and Durkheim first, then the more recent work on hypersensitive agency detection, minimally counterintuitive concepts, costly signaling, and multilevel selection. I also help moderate a monthly discussion group at my local library, mostly retirees, a couple of nurses, and one or two graduate students from the state university, and we have now spent something like four sessions on a question that has not moved an inch: whether religion is an adaptation or a by-product. One camp points to the apparent near-universality of religious belief, the costliness and rigid regularity of ritual, and the reported correlations between religious commitment and group survival or fertility, and concludes that religious behaviour was shaped by selection. The other camp insists that those same facts are exactly what you would expect if religion were a spandrel — a culturally elaborated exploitation of independently adaptive machinery for agency detection, social learning, and coalition signalling — and adds that universality proves nothing about adaptation, since music is found in every human society and almost nobody in this debate is willing to treat music as an adaptation. Everyone has read the same two or three books, everyone concedes the same empirical facts, and every session ends with both sides restating their original position more confidently than before.

My working hypothesis, which I have not been able to defend properly and which the group mostly ignores, is that the dispute is not really about adaptation versus by-product at all, but about what the explanandum is. Both sides seem to assume that religion names a single natural kind with a single evolutionary history, and then argue about what that history was. Yet the components they actually quarrel over — agency detection, costly ritual, doctrinal transmission, moralizing high gods, dietary and sexual taboos, the afterlife imagination — plausibly have quite different origins and quite different timescales, and some of them look like cultural inventions far too recent for natural selection to have done much with. On that reading, the adaptationist says selected about one bundle of features while the by-product theorist says not selected about a different bundle, and the two of them are never straightforwardly contradicting each other. In my own notes I have started calling this the trait-first assumption, and my suspicion is that both camps hold it without ever arguing for it, because it is built into the way the question gets asked in the first place.

Please diagnose this stalemate for me. Is my hypothesis right — are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading, so that the disagreement is partly verbal and partly a matter of which features get bundled under the word religion? Or am I being too clever about it, and is there a substantive disagreement about selection that survives the disaggregation, something the two camps would still fight about even after agreeing precisely which traits are on the table? I want the diagnosis tested against what each side actually argues: the appeals to universality, to demographic and group-level survival data, to costly signaling as a mechanism, to cross-cultural and historical comparisons of moralizing gods and social complexity, and to whatever each side draws from archaeology, comparative primatology, or the heritability of religious attitudes. Tell me which of those are hidden empirical questions with a determinate answer in principle, which are disputes about standards of evidence — about what could ever demonstrate selection for a behaviour that leaves no fossils — and which are really arguments about how to carve up the category. And then, given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress in a room like ours: a distinction to be drawn, a kind of study to be run, a formal result, a change of question, or perhaps the frank conclusion that no progress should be expected and why. I am not looking for a survey of the positions; I have done that myself and it is not what has gone wrong.
