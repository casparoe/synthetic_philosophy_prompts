I spent thirty-eight years as an experimental physicist, mostly in condensed matter, and only took up philosophy seriously after retiring. My mathematics is serviceable rather than professional: I can follow a diagonal argument if the steps are written out, but I have no instinct for which formulations logicians regard as careful and which as loose. A chapter in a popular book I recently read, written by someone clearly competent and sympathetic to the anti-mechanist side, summarizes the Lucas–Penrose argument as follows:

"Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that any consistent formal system strong enough to do arithmetic contains sentences that the system can neither prove nor refute. Lucas saw in 1961 that this yields a direct test of mechanism: given any such system F, one can construct its Gödel sentence G(F), step outside F, and see that G(F) is true — a feat F itself cannot perform. Hence no formal system can be a complete model of the mathematician's reasoning. Penrose pressed the same point at length a generation later, adding that the conclusion survives even if the machine is allowed to work probabilistically or heuristically, since those remain mechanical procedures; and he drew the further inference that the brain must be doing something non-computable, for which he proposed a physical basis in gravitationally induced state reduction within neuronal microtubules. The stock objection — urged by Putnam, Feferman, and Chalmers among others — is that we cannot know we are consistent, and so cannot apply the theorem to ourselves; but this trades on a confusion, since the argument nowhere requires the mathematician to prove his own consistency. It requires only that he is in fact consistent, a hypothesis the mechanist can hardly deny while claiming to model a working mathematician. Because Gödel's theorem also tells us that there are arithmetical truths which are true yet unprovable by any means whatever, the upshot is decisive: no computer, however fast or however large, could duplicate human mathematical understanding."

I find the passage persuasive on a first reading and slightly slippery on a second, and I cannot tell which of my reactions to trust. Please go through it claim by claim rather than as a whole, and for each claim tell me whether a working logician, or a defender of the Lucas–Penrose line who knew the literature well, would sign off on it, regard it as an acceptable popular simplification, or regard it as subtly wrong. In particular I would like the following sorted out.

First, what premises does the argument actually need: mere consistency, soundness, or knowable soundness? The passage seems to slide between these, and I suspect that matters. Second, is the "stock objection" stated as its proponents would state it, and does the reply — that only being consistent, not proving consistency, is required — in fact meet it, or does it answer a different objection than the one logicians press? Please say explicitly what the second incompleteness theorem does and does not license here, since I cannot tell whether it helps the mechanist or the anti-mechanist. Third, does the claim that some arithmetical truths are unprovable "by any means whatever" follow from Gödel's theorem at all, or is unprovability always relative to a system? Fourth, does the non-computability conclusion, even if granted, carry any weight toward the specific neurophysical conjecture about microtubules, or are these two independent claims that the passage has fused? Fifth, what exactly is being claimed to be beyond a machine — behavior, or the algorithmic explicability of mathematical insight — and is "however fast or however large" relevant to an argument of this kind?

Finally, state for me what the argument, correctly formulated, does establish, how much of the passage's conclusion survives the corrections, and whether the residue is a decisive refutation, an open problem, or something weaker still. I am inclined to believe the conclusion may well be true, which is precisely why I want to know whether this passage earns it.
