On 21 April 2002 the first round of the French presidential election produced an outcome that no poll and no commentator had forecast. Sixteen candidates stood, and for the first time in the Fifth Republic not one of them cleared twenty percent. The incumbent Jacques Chirac led with 19.9 percent; Jean-Marie Le Pen of the National Front came second with 16.9; and the Socialist prime minister, Lionel Jospin, came third with 16.2 — behind Le Pen by roughly 195,000 votes, or about seven-tenths of a percentage point. The left was simply not short of candidates: Chevènement, who had broken with the Socialists, plus the Greens, the Communists, and two Trotskyist tickets, together took more than a quarter of the vote, each of them campaigning on the understanding that their supporters would consolidate behind Jospin in a runoff that Jospin would reach. He did not, and he announced his withdrawal from political life that evening. In the second round on 5 May, virtually every party, union, and newspaper called on its members to vote for Chirac under the banner of a republican front; Chirac won 82.2 percent, the largest margin in the history of French presidential elections, and turnout rose from 71.6 to 79.7 percent. Chirac declined to debate Le Pen. Between the two rounds blank and spoiled ballots nearly doubled, from about one million to about 1.8 million, and on 1 May somewhere between 900,000 and 1.3 million people marched against Le Pen, some of them wearing shirts that instructed voters to prefer the crook to the fascist.

I am assembling a case study for a seminar in democratic theory, and I want this episode used to test three accounts of the ethics of voting — in both directions: what each account implies the voters of 21 April ought to have done, and what 21 April does to each account.

The three accounts, stated as their defenders would state them. First, the instrumental account: a ballot is an act with consequences, its moral weight a function of the probability that it alters the outcome times the value of the difference it makes, and since in a national election that probability is negligible, voting cannot be obligatory and the interesting questions concern what we owe as participants in a collective decision rather than as causal agents. Second, the expressive account: a ballot is chiefly a public declaration of who the voter is and what she stands with, its worth independent of its causal contribution, and the instrumental account mistakes the nature of the activity rather as a theory of conversation as information transfer mistakes the nature of conversation. Third, the lesser-evil account: where every option is bad, choosing the least bad is permissible and sometimes required, and the charge that it implicates the voter in the lesser option's own wrongs can be answered by distinguishing what she does from what she permits.

Here is the part I would like you to develop and then attack, because I do not think it exists in the literature and I am not attached to it. Call it the decisiveness discount: the proposal that a citizen holds something like a fixed expressive budget and spends it wherever she believes the outcome is insulated from her, so that expressive license is inversely proportional to perceived decisiveness. On this account a two-round system is structurally a machine for rationing that budget — the first round is where citizens spend, the second where they cash in — and the machine runs on the belief that first-round spending is free. April 21 is the occasion on which the price came due: a voter for a Trotskyist or Green candidate spent her budget believing the cost was capped at zero, and the cost was Le Pen in the runoff. Tell me whether this is a genuine explanatory posit or an artifact invented after the fact; what in the episode supports or undercuts it (the turnout shift, the blank-ballot surge, the fact that the small left candidates had polled at comparable levels for months); what observation would falsify it; and whether, if it is merely a description of a psychology, any of the three accounts gives the voter a reason to abandon it.

Then work through these:

1. Map the agents. Not only the individual voter: Jospin, who would not ask allied candidates to withdraw; Chevènement; the Trotskyist tickets; Chirac, who refused the debate; the press; and the abstainers, of whom there were more than eight million in round one. Say what each account says about each of them, and flag every point at which it says nothing at all.

2. Take seriously the arithmetic. If two hundred thousand ballots flipped which of two candidates faced Chirac, was the probability of a single vote being decisive in fact negligible, as the instrumental account assumes? Does the episode supply evidence against the account's central premise, or does it merely illustrate how small probabilities can attach to very large stakes?

3. The problem of timing. The act of the Jospin-adjacent left voter looked identical on 20 April, on 21 April, and on 6 May; only its consequences differed. Assess its wrongness ex ante and ex post and say whether the two assessments come apart, and if they do, whether the ethics of voting requires a doctrine of luck that none of the three accounts has room for. Address the epistemic side honestly: Le Pen had been rising in the final week, and the fragmentation was visible to anyone reading the polls.

4. The second round as a stress test. Eighty-two percent of the electorate voted for a man most of them did not want, many simultaneously registering refusal via a blank ballot, and the whole event was named a republican front rather than an endorsement. Does this show that lesser-evil voting is not a variety of instrumental voting but a third thing — blocking — that the taxonomy is missing? Does the blank-ballot surge refute the expressive account, confirm it, or expose a category the account never distinguished, namely expression aimed at one's own side rather than at the winner?

5. Scope. Say where the case escapes the literature entirely: the ethics of the candidates, of the institutions, of a two-round plurality rule that translates individual sincerity into collective disaster. Is any of that within an ethics of voting, or does the episode suggest the individual ballot is the wrong unit of analysis?

I am not looking for attributions or a reading list — I know the standard names — and I do not want the theories made to speak where they are silent. Where a theory has no resources for a feature of the case, say so plainly and move on.
