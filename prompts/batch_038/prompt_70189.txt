I manage freight contracts for a mid-sized retailer, and I have to pick a carrier for our busiest lane. Carrier Alpha has run the lane for eleven years, and its record puts late arrivals at 12%, a figure that has barely moved. Carrier Beta began operating last year; it has hauled a scattering of loads for us, none of them on this lane, and there is no industry record for a firm of its kind. If pressed, I would put Beta's late rate somewhere between 5% and 30%, though I would not bet on those endpoints either. Beta is quoting 9% cheaper than Alpha.

Here is what troubles me. If you asked me to price insurance on a single Beta load, I would charge exactly what I would charge for an Alpha load — I do not, in fact, think Beta's loads are more likely to arrive late. My analyst says this makes my position incoherent: since I refuse to treat Beta as worse in expectation, turning down the cheaper quote is nothing but a surcharge on numbers I cannot see, and it costs the company real money to buy myself a feeling of prudence. The detail he discounts is that I have to defend this choice to a board that reads postmortems, and only one of these carriers comes with eleven years of documented performance.

Is my analyst right that I am inconsistent? If not, what is my preference tracking that his argument leaves out — and how would I recognize the point at which that consideration stops carrying any weight?
