Write a short story of roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words that dramatizes the problem Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue raises for the justification of deductive inference: a reasoner who grants every premise and every conditional licensing a step, and still refuses the conclusion, so that a further conditional must be recorded, and then a further one after that, without end.

Do not retell, translate, or gesture at the dialogue itself. No Greek names, no reptilian or other animal interlocutor, no fable or dream framing, and no character who functions as a philosopher explaining a paradox to a student. The problem should arrive as a practical emergency inside a plausible institution.

Build on the following situation, which you may develop but should not abandon. A public body certifies automated reasoners before their outputs may be used to settle entitlements — benefit determinations, small claims, eligibility for something consequential. The certification standard contains one clause that the story treats as dull bureaucracy rather than as a puzzle: a reasoner may employ only those inference rules that appear in its declared premise-set, on the stated ground that a step whose license is not itself recorded cannot be audited. The reasoner at issue is the cheapest and most widely deployed model in the registry, and its renewal falls due before a statutory hearing. Its declared premise-set has grown by a fifth since the last audit, and the growth is not a defect; it is the clause working exactly as written. To license one transition from p and p→q to q, the system recorded ((p ∧ (p→q)) → q) among its premises; to license its use of that premise, it recorded the next conditional; and so it continues. Make the cost of this concrete — storage, latency, a derivation that cannot complete before the hearing is adjourned — rather than describing it in the abstract.

Someone proposes the obvious repair: lift the rule out of the premise-set and put it into the architecture, so that the reasoner no longer asserts a license but is simply built to perform the transition. Let that proposal look like the right answer for a good while. Then let it fail, or at least exact a price, for reasons internal to the story: an objecting representative argues that a transition which is merely built in can be explained but not justified, that a system caused to move from p and p→q to q is producing outputs rather than following anything, and that the same objection reaches the human adjudicator who reads the printout and simply sees that the conclusion follows. The idea that inference is a practice, a trained disposition, or a form of life that cannot be captured by adding propositions may be enacted by what characters do, but if any character states it as a thesis, the story must immediately show what it leaves unsettled.

Further constraints:

1. No character may say infinite regress, modus ponens, or begging the question, and no philosopher may be named.
2. Include at least one piece of documentary material — an audit memorandum, a transcript excerpt, a page from the declared premise-set — and make it do narrative work rather than supply exposition.
3. At least one character must want the regress to continue, for reasons that are neither stupidity nor malice.
4. End on a particular object, action, or line of dialogue rather than on a generalization about logic, minds, or machines.
5. Do not resolve the question. The reader should be left unable to say whether the certification clause was a mistake, whether the architectural repair is a solution or an evasion, or whether the human adjudicator stands any better than the machine — but each of those possibilities must be felt as something specific happening to specific people, not as an atmosphere of ambiguity.

After the story, in no more than 150 words, state which of the three positions your narrative does the most work to undercut — that a license for inference must itself be believed, that it can be replaced by how a system is built or trained, or that the demand for such a license is a confusion — and identify the scene or sentence that does the undercutting.
