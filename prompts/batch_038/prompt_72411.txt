Ok so I keep seeing Hofstadter's Person Paper cited as the definitive takedown of the generic "man," and I want to actually understand what the arguement is doing instead of just nodding along with it. Here's the thing that bugs me.

When the piece swaps man→white it coins stuff like chairwhite, freshwhite, whitekind — but also whitepower and whiteslaughter, standing in for manpower and manslaughter. Those last two land completely differently from the others. Whitepower reads as an actual white-supremacist slogan; nobody then or now could hear it as a harmless race-neutral compound. So is that a crack in the analogy, or is the crack the point? What I want explained is the machinery underneath: why some of the substitutions generate exactly the intended offense and others blow past it, and what that tells you about what the objection to generic "man" was ever really an objection to. My hunch is it has to do with whether the -man element is still semantically alive or has been bleached into a frozen chunk of the word, and that the narrator's aside about how you don't pronounce the suffix with a long "i" but more like "wit" is a clue that Hofstadter knew this and was hiding the real argument in a joke about phonetics. Lay that distinction out properly for me and tell me whether the reductio actually depends on it, or whether it would work just as well without it.

And then the harder bit. People sometimes say the whole essay begs the question: there's no independent reason to treat race and sex as parallel here, because generic "man" leans on a social default that takes men to be the paradigm humans, and no equivalent default ever existed for "white" — so the satire smuggles in its conclusion by just stipulating the parallel. Explain what that objection amounts to, whether you think it sticks, and what work is left for the piece to do if it does. Specifically: does it still succeed as an attack on the three defenses the narrator keeps reaching for — that the old phrasing is more elegant and poetic, that context makes the inclusive sense obvious anyway, and that the reformers' proposals are redundant — even if the race/sex analogy itself is alot looser than it looks? I've read the thing but never thought hard about it, so walk me through it slowly.
