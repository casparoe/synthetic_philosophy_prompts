My calligraphy teacher has been cheerfully handing out verdicts about which marks count as an a, and I'm now pretty sure they encode at least two different theories she'd never separate. The set so far:

- a kid's wobbly single-story a whose bowl doesn't quite close at the bottom: "still an a, obviously."
- a Caslon two-story a with the ear snipped off: "an a, slightly rude."
- a coffee ring on a page that everybody reads as an a: "that's not a letter, that's a stain."
- the observation that a single-story a and a double-story a share basicly no strokes: "so? that's not what makes them the same letter."
- a Futura a set down in the middle of a Caslon word: "that's an a, but it isn't one of ours."
- a mark on a wall with no other letters near it that could be an a or a badly drawn o: "then I'd have to guess, wouldn't I."

Two cases she hasn't seen. One: someone runs an interpolation script that morphs a Caslon a halfway into a Futura a and installs the result as a font's a, deliberately, as that font's a — readers call it an a about 95% of the time in isolation, and next to its own b, c and d it looks like a car crash. Two, hypothetical: a manuscript a from around the year 800 that scribes and readers of the day all took for an a, but which shares no strokes with either form we know and which people today reliably call something else.

What does she say about each, and why? Then tell me what rule she's actually running, what other rules fit all six of the earlier verdicts just as well but split on these two, whether her judgements are even yes/no or graded, and which single further case would best tell the candidates apart.
