I sit on the appeals panel at a UK income-protection insurer — we decide whether a claimant's situation counts as a 'medical condition' under the policy. Our own rulings so far, for the record:

- type 1 diabetes, well controlled, no complications: counts
- a 3mm unruptured aneurysm picked up incidentally after a head bump, no symptoms, consultant recommending no intervention: doesn't count — we called it a risk, not a condition
- BP 148/92, never treated, no symptoms, found at a work health check: counts
- elite distance runner, resting pulse 38, everything else normal: doesn't count, even though that pulse would get anyone else investigated
- woman aged 52, no periods: doesn't count. Same picture at 27: counts
- two years off work with widespread pain, scans and bloods all clear: counts
- mild lisp, never caused him any trouble, no treatment sought: doesn't count

Now we've got this one. A warehouse picker who's done fixed nights for nine years. Off shift he sleeps perfectly — normal nights, no insomnia, nothing on the sleep study that looks like a mechanism failing. He just cant sleep by day or stay alert at night, and it's costing him his job and his health. Which way should we rule to be consistent with ourselves? Tell me which of our precedents is the closest analogue, what theory we're actually running — something Boorse-ish, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction, Nordenfelt's ability-to-reach-your-vital-goals view, or just plain normativism — and whether any of the earlier cases already commit us one way or the other. If the precedents genuinely run out here, say what principle we'd be adopting with each option, and name a further case that would tell those theories apart.

One thing: I know this area is a live argument, so please separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on it, and don't sell me one side of a dispute as though it were the consensus. British English, thanks.
