I ran a low-temperature lab for thirty-odd years, mostly calibrating thermometry and arguing about error bars with grad students, and since I retired I've been reading epistemology, which turns out to be the same subject with worse notation. I got as far as the KK business and the Williamson argument against luminosity — the one where a man is cold at dawn and not cold by midday and there's a last moment at which he feels cold but isn't in a position to know it. I found that convincing. So I tried to rebuild it out of premises I'd already have signed my name to in a calibaration report, and it went somewhere I really don't want to go. I'd like to know which step is bad.

My own terms, so I'm not borrowing anybody's machinery. Two situations are neighbours when nothing in my evidence, my instruments or my head separates them. A judgement has a grain if there are neighbour pairs straddling it, i.e. my discrimination bottoms out. The safe region of p is how far out from the actual situation, counted in grains, p stays true. A condition is luminous when, whenever it obtains, one is in a position to know that it obtains.

1. Every judgement I make, from a reading or from how things seem, has a grain. Nothing I do is infinitely sharp.
2. Safety: if I know p on that kind of basis, p is true at every neighbour of the actual situation. Otherwise there's a situation I'd have judged identically and been wrong, and I don't see how that was knowledge.
3. So if a condition C comes in degrees and crosses my grain somewhere — neighbours with C on one side, not-C on the other — C isn't luminous. At the last C-case before the switch, knowing C would require C next door too, by 2. Contradiction, so no knowledge there. This is just the cold argument and I buy it.
4. Knowing p is itself such a condition for any empirical p: evidence, alertness, counting statistics, drift of the cryostat, all graded. So knowing isn't luminous, and KK fails. Fine by me — people have defended KK, Hintikka for one, and I never saw the appeal.
5. Now the step I'm least sure of, and where the thing starts to run away. Suppose I do know p and its safe region is m grains. For me to know that I know p, that second-order judgement has to come out true at every neighbour; at each neighbour I have to know p; and knowing p there means p holding m grains out from there, which is m+1 grains out from here. So each trip up the hierarchy costs a grain: m−1 for one iteration, m−2 for two, and so on.
6. m is finite for anything empirical — the sensor's range, the stability of the bath, whatever fixes it. So at some finite level n the safe region for the n-fold iteration is empty, and I don't know that I know ... that p.
7. And then I did the damage. I told myself that knowledge you can't recognise isn't knowledge you can use, and knowledge you can't use isn't knowledge — call it the usability principle, it's roughly what an error bar is for. By 6 there's a level at which I can't recognise it. So the tower comes down all the way to the bottom and I don't know the ordinary empirical things either.
8. Scepticism about everything I ever measured, from premises I'd have put in a paper.

Concretely: whether the sample is below its transition temperature, thermometer good to a millikelvin, transition itself about a millikelvin wide. Step 3 hands me a case where the sample is below the transition and I can't know it, and I accept that without much pain. Steps 5 to 8 tell me I never knew it at all, which I don't accept, because I spent a working life knowing exactly that kind of thing.

Which step goes? I want to keep 2 and 3 if I possibly can, since 3 is the argument I came here for, and I'll cheerfully throw KK under the bus. My instinct says 5 is doing something shady with units but I can't put my finger on it; a friend of mine, also retired and no more use than I am, says the whole thing turns on 7. Take 2, 5, 6 and 7 in turn: for each, say why it looked innocent to me and what it actually costs to give up, and then tell me which one you'd cut and what I'm left with. Two more things I want answered. Is 5 to 6 just a sorites in a lab coat, and should that worry me given that I'm perfectly happy with 3, which looks like the same trick pointed the other way? And can the usability principle survive in some weaker form — because if something like it isn't true, I honestly don't know what I was doing all those years with error bars.
