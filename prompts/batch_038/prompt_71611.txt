Retired four years ago after 38 years of electrophysiology — locust flight circuits, mostly, plus a decade of graduate students asking me why we should believe the animal's behavior at all if we can record from the nerve instead. Since I stopped going in to the lab I've been reading philosophy of mind, and I've gotten properly stuck on one problem: if a conscious experience doesn't causally do anything, how come we manage to talk about it accurately? I gather this is called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Its the one thing I keep going back to, partly because I spent my whole career treating a report as just another output trace you could take at face value, and now I'm not sure that was ever justified.

I run a reading group of ex-lab types — two chemists, a structural geologist, me — and I want to hand them a decision tree that sorts us by what we actually believe about this, not by which -ism we like the sound of. Here's what I need.

**Fixed starting node:** "Verbal reports about experience are produced entirely by ordinary physical processes in the brain, and at least some of those reports are true about what the experience is like." Everyone in my group grants this, so the tree starts downstream of it. If you think granting both halves at once already rigs things toward particular leaves, say so loudly — I'd want a referee to tell me that.

**Structure:** seven to nine nodes, depth no more than five, and no more than eight leaves. Every node must be a question someone could answer without knowing any of the labels — a concrete case or a claim about a case, never "do you accept epiphenomenalism?" Please build at least three nodes out of material I'd recognize: a blindsight subject who denies seeing anything and still guesses above chance; the experiments where people confidently narrate reasons for a choice they demonstrably didn't make; a split-brain patient; and the everyday case of a pain you only notice once your attention swings back to it. Use whichever of those genuinely discriminate, and tell me which ones don't.

**Leaves that must appear, somewhere:** the version that just eats the cost and says the match between report and experience is a brute coincidence; the version that says we know our own experiences by acquaintance and the report's accuracy comes from there, not from causation; the constitution move, where the experience isn't a separate cause of the report but part of what realizes it; the phenomenal-concept line, where the concept you report under is itself partly made of the experience so accuracy is built in; the view that the report is really tracking the underlying neural state and the experience isn't what's being reported at all; interactionism, i.e. denying causal closure; and illusionism, where there's no accurate phenomenal report left to explain. Give each its real name if it has one.

**Two tags I want on every leaf.** First, what I've been calling the *coincidence bill* — how much brute luck that position has to swallow to get the match between report and experience. Rate it none / small / large / unpayable. Second, whether the leaf actually solves what I've been calling *content-locking*, i.e. explains why the report's content is fixed by the experience rather than merely happening to agree with it — or whether it just relocates that problem somewhere else and calls it solved. Both terms are my own coinage from scribbling in the margins, not anything I lifted; if there's standard vocabulary for either of them, tell me what it is, because I'd like to know what the field actually calls these.

**Then step back from the tree.** Give me the logical space as a coordinate table: nodes as axes, and which combinations of answers are occupied by a real position and which are empty. For each empty cell, say whether it's empty because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has bothered yet. And flag every pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I'd rather see the instrument's resolution limits than have them papered over.

One last thing, and it matters to me as someone who spent a career knowing which measurements were solid and which were one grad student's enthusiasm: separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. At each node, tell me whether the framing itself is agreed or whether the question is one that experts would set up differently. At each leaf, say plainly how populated it is — mainstream, serious minority, or nearly empty — and don't let a contested resolution wear the clothes of a consensus. Where you're making a judgment call about how the space divides, mark it as your judgment call rather than as the way the literature slices it.
