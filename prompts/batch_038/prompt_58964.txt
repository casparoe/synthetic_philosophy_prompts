For the past year I have been meeting weekly with three classmates to work through philosophy papers, and last month I wrote up the following argument as a handout on causation by omission. My intention was only to show how quickly omission-talk gets out of hand. Instead the argument seems to run past that target and end somewhere I cannot accept, and after three meetings none of us can locate the step where it goes over the edge. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) An omission causes an outcome if and only if the outcome would not have occurred had the omitted act been performed.
(2) When we evaluate such a counterfactual we hold fixed the actual past and the actual laws, including what everyone else actually did.
(3) Last month a child I will call Amara died of malaria in a region where an insecticide-treated bed net costs roughly what a lunch costs here.
(4) I did not send the price of a net to the agency working in that region. Had I sent it, the net would have been bought and hung over Amara's bed, and she would not have died.
(5) So my omission caused Amara's death.
(6) Nothing in (1) through (5) mentions me in particular. The same reasoning goes through for anyone who did not send the money, and there are roughly eight billion such people.
(7) So roughly eight billion omissions each caused Amara's death.
(8) Causation is all-or-nothing. Either c caused e or it did not; there is no causing e slightly, or causing it less than somebody else did.
(9) Since each of the eight billion stands in exactly the same causal relation to the death, and since responsibility for an outcome tracks that causal relation and nothing else, each of the eight billion is exactly as responsible for Amara's death as a lone assassin is for his victim's.
(10) That is unacceptable. Most of the eight billion are small children, or cannot spare the money, or have no route to that agency; and even for those who could have paid, we do not classify a forgotten transfer as a killing.
(11) Therefore (1) is false. Omissions are never causes.
(12) But then the sleeping lifeguard did not cause the swimmer's death, the nurse who skipped the double-check did not cause the overdose, and the missing sprinkler did not cause the fire to spread.
(13) And if the agent's contribution drops out of the causal story in every letting-die, then killing and letting die are causally indistinguishable, since in neither case does anything the agent did or failed to do figure among the causes.
(14) So the argument ends by erasing the doing-and-allowing distinction, which is precisely what I sat down to defend.

Tell me which premise or which inference you would reject, and then explain why it looked harmless when I wrote it, since it certainly did. I do not need the argument rescued; I want the repair costs compared. Three candidates keep coming up in our meetings and I would like each of them priced: (8), because some of us have read that causation admits of degrees while others insist that being a difference-maker and being worth citing as the cause come apart; (2), because holding the others' non-donation fixed may not be available for the person who could never have donated in the first place; and (6), because it may quietly trade a claim about my omission for a claim about everybody's. One member also thinks the trouble is further down, at (9), and that the real question is whether eight billion causes is itself an objection or just an unfamiliar picture. For what it is worth, I would rather surrender (10) than (1), and I am not willing to live with (12).

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, stating the step you reject, and then give the reasoning that gets you there. No preamble and no restating my question back to me.
