I cover science and ideas for a monthly, and for the past year I've been allowed to sit in as a fly on the wall at the governance committee of a mid-size private foundation (real org, names and details changed). Two factions on that committee have been arguing in circles for so long the chair has given up refereeing.

**The traders:** broadly consequentialist. Their view is that when two groups with different values can each come out better off by their own lights, and nobody outside the deal is made worse off, there's a genuine reason to strike the deal — and that declining it to protect your own clean hands is vanity dressed up as principle.

**The integrity camp:** their view is that you can never deliberately act on something you believe is wrong, so a compromise in which you vote for a thing you're convinced is unjust isn't a price you paid for permission, it's just the unjust thing with extra paperwork. They also reject bargaining over intensity of conviction outright, on the grounds that it hands the deal to whoever can inflate how strongly they feel.

**The live proposal,** as neutrally as I can render it. The foundation would endow a program of human challenge trials for a respiratory pathogen — healthy adult volunteers deliberately infected under close medical supervision, to compress the timeline on vaccines and antivirals. The traders want it funded this cycle. The integrity camp objects on two grounds they say the other side keeps collapsing into one: (a) the volunteers' household contacts and the surrounding community carry transmission risk they never agreed to, and (b) even if the consent were perfect, funding it is the foundation doing it, and they won't be among the people who did it. They insist (a) and (b) are seperate objections with seperate force.

**The drafted compromise.** The integrity camp agrees to stop blocking the challenge program, and to accept a standing rule that diffuse, small, uncompensated risks to unidentifiable future people get cleared by staff without a case-by-case board vote. In exchange the traders agree to a hard cap on the share of any single year's spending that can go to one high-variance program, an absolute integrity-camp veto over anything involving people incapable of consenting, and a doubled budget for less glamorous alternatives to challenge trials. Each side concedes where it feels least strongly and buys protection where it feels most strongly. The integrity camp calls this selling a veto. The traders call the refusal narcissism.

Write the document they should have produced a year ago: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps. Begin with the statement itself — no preface, no description of how you're approaching it, no recap of the above, and no bibliography or suggested reading; everything has to be argued from the case.

Structure:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Rule for what counts: each claim must be one at least one of the two camps would have rejected going in, and specific enough that a reader could check whether the real committee honors it. "Both sides want to do good" is filler; no filler. Include what they can agree on about the structure of the situation — that this is repeated voting over decades rather than a one-shot deal, what a veto is worth relative to a vote share, what logrolling does when its iterated, and whether the household contacts sit inside or outside the "both better off" clause.

2. A disagreements table, one row per real disagreement, with columns: the issue; the traders' formulation in words the traders would sign; the integrity camp's formulation in words they would sign; whether the row is a genuine disagreement or a verbal one (and if verbal, the shared content underneath); the crux — the sub-question whose settlement would actually move at least one side; the case or argument both sides agree bears on that crux; and each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance.

3. Outside the table: which part of this fight you now think was about words, and why. Include at least one row where the honest finding is that no crux exists, neither camp should expect to convert the other, and the only live question is what to do about that — and say what you'd tell them to do.

Last thing, and it's the part I actually need: on the analysis the joint statement has developed, is the drafted compromise something both camps could sign? If not, what's the smallest edit to the deal that makes it signable, and which clause was doing the damage? If your answer is that it can't be made signable in principle, say that plainly and say what the two camps should do instead.
