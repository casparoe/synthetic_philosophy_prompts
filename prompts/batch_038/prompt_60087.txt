I write the ideas column for a weekly science desk, and I have a feature coming up on why time seems to have a direction. My spine is an analogy I keep meeting in the wild, and before I commit it to print I want to know how much weight it can actually bear.

Here is the analogy, as the curator of a new physics exhibit put it to me on the record: the second law is like a teenager's bedroom. Left to itself it goes from tidy to messy and never the other way; you only ever get order back by spending effort and exporting the mess somewhere else. Her placard text pushes the comparison further. It says that the reason we remember yesterday rather than tomorrow, the reason a dropped egg scatters but never reassembles, and the reason the universe will end as a lukewarm, featureless haze are all one and the same fact — that there are vastly more messy arrangements of things than tidy ones. And her conclusion, which she was emphatic about, is that once you grant this there is nothing left to explain: the arrow of time is not a feature the world possesses but an artifact of counting, and anyone who thinks physics still owes us an account of why time points somewhere is demanding a mystery that was never on the books.

A philosopher of physics I interviewed disputes this, and gave me only two things to work with, which I've written down as best I can. First, she said, the microscopic laws do not care which way the film runs, so any explanation built purely on counting has to answer the question of what happens if you take the messy state and reverse every motion in it. Second, she said, the bedroom is doing a great deal of unacknowledged work: it presumes a room with walls, a standard of tidiness that somebody happens to care about, and — crucially — a bedroom that was tidy on Monday morning for reasons the story never gives. On her account the analogy does not merely fail to explain the arrow of time; it hides the one thing that would have to be explained.

There is a second use I've run into, which is why I care about getting this right. An op-ed in my own paper's business section borrowed the same picture to argue that data centers are entropy machines: because any local gain in order must be paid for by a larger disorder elsewhere, the heat load and resource draw of large-scale computing are a thermodynamic necessity rather than a policy choice, and no amount of regulation will change that.

So: audit the analogy for me. Lay out the correspondences explicitly — what in the bedroom plays the role of the microstate, the macrostate, the entropy increase, the effort spent tidying, the mess exported elsewhere, and the unexplained tidiness of Monday morning. Then tell me which of the places where it breaks are load-bearing for the curator's conclusion and which are harmless decoration, and be precise about which features of a bedroom the comparison imports that the universe as a whole lacks. Assess whether her "nothing left to explain" claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether the philosopher's two objections are as damaging as she thinks or whether the curator has a decent reply. Finally, tell me whether the op-ed is a legitimate extension of the same picture, a different picture altogether, or a straight abuse of it — and if the analogy cannot be repaired for either purpose, propose a better one that keeps the genuine statistical insight without smuggling in the parts doing the illegitimate work. This has to go in front of a fact-checker, so please mark clearly where you are restating physics as it is standardly understood and where you are reporting a live dispute.
