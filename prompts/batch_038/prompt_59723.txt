I got talked onto my city's pest-management review panel this spring — six of us, and nothing gets sprayed, baited, drenched, or fogged on city property until we sign off. Our decisions go into the minutes as precedents that later panels are supposed to follow, which sounds tidy until you read them, because every entry comes with a stated reason and the reasons don't line up with each other. Voting Tuesday and I want to vote consistently with us rather than with my gut, and honestly I can't yet tell what rule the panel is running. Here's the record so far, roughly in order. Approved: a sterile-insect-technique release of irradiated male fruit flies after traps in a residential block confirmed a small infestation — no insecticide involved at all. Approved: physical removal of a paper wasp nest over a playground gate, done after dark, forty-ish adults killed. Rejected: parks' standing request to blanket-spray a pyrethroid over every park and median on a calendar schedule each spring. Approved with conditions: gel bait stations for Argentine ants around a school perimeter, low-dose slow-acting bait, placed only where their monitoring showed activity — that one collapses whole colonies, so presumably millions of ants. Rejected: neighborhood-wide mosquito fogging after complaint calls about nuisance biting, when the county's traps hadn't found any virus. Approved: the same fogging in the same neighborhood maybe six weeks later, once the county confirmed virus-positive mosquitoes and a human case three blocks away. Rejected: a surface oil on the decorative pond to knock out midge larvae that don't bite and never come indoors, on the grounds that the pond has dragonfly larvae and a frog population people are attached to. Rejected: a slow-acting insect growth regulator broadcast through the municipal warehouse for stored-product moths, when a heat treatment that finishes every life stage within hours was available for about the same money. At orientation someone told me the panel basically "counts insects," meaning it minimizes how many die, but I don't see how that survives the ant decision sitting next to the pond one. My own guesses are some version of: only act when there's documented serious harm — disease, structural or crop damage, real injury risk — rather than complaints or cosmetic loss; and then use the least-harmful method that will actually do the job, where "harmful" might mean non-target and ecological fallout, or might mean how the target insects die, or both. Those readings come apart on what's in front of us.

The new proposal is Japanese beetles in the community garden on 4th and the rose beds beside it. The extension scout's counts are way over threshold: beans and raspberries getting stripped, and something like a third of that garden's produce goes to a food pantry, so it isn't only ugly. No disease angle, no structure at risk, no one's been hurt. Grounds crew has offered three options. (1) A systemic soil drench on the roses and along the bean row's edge, timed so nothing is in bloom when it goes in — the city's own pollinator policy would technically allow it, and they say its the only thing that holds the line this season, though it kills anything that chews treated leaves, slowly, over days to weeks. (2) Hand-jarring the adults into soapy water at dawn, plus milky spore and nematodes for the grubs in the turf next door — extremely targeted, quick death for the adults, but grub suppression takes a couple of years to build and it probably won't save this season's pantry yield. (3) A dusk pyrethroid fog over the garden and adjacent turf — fast knockdown, broad, would also hit whatever roosts in the hedge, and the frog pond is two properties down.

So what should I vote for if I want to be consistent with our own record: approve one option outright, approve with conditions, or reject all three and send it back for a fourth? Which of the eight precedents is the closest analog, and which one is doing the most work — I keep circling back to the fogging pair, since it's the only place where we approved something broad-spectrum, and I can't tell if that means severity overrides specificity for us or just that we panicked about a virus. Where the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, I want it spelled out what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each choice, because that's the sentence that ends up in the minutes and gets quoted back at us in a year. And then tell me which upcoming case would settle which rule we're actually on. I have one in mind: the golf course two miles over, under a city maintenance contract, wants an identical drench for an identical beetle infestation, and the only real differences are that nobody's eating anything there and the turf is ornamental. My instinct is that how we treat those two proposals relative to each other is diagnostic, but I don't know in which direction — if your candidate rules disagree about the golf course too, say so, and if there's a cleaner discriminating case I should be pushing onto the agenda instead, propose it.
