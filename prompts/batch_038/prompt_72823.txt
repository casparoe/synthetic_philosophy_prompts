Fourteen months ago my mother was killed by an autonomous shuttle operating under a municipal permit, in a lane that was marked but not physically separated, as she crossed at a painted crosswalk with a rolling walker. I am a controls engineer; I have spent eleven years signing deployment-readiness reviews for systems of exactly this kind. The city asked me to address accountability as part of a public review of the permit regime, and what I wrote has hardened into an argument I can no longer defend. I am looking for a diagnosis, not consolation.

The argument as drafted:

(1) Moral responsibility for an outcome requires that the outcome have been within the agent's control, in the sense that the agent could reasonably have been expected to foresee it and to prevent it.
(2) A system that keeps learning after deployment — here, one whose perception stack was retrained nightly on what the fleet encountered — can produce classifications that no designer, operator, or regulator could have foreseen.
(3) The classification that mattered, treating a person using a wheeled walker as static street furniture rather than as an agent with right of way, arose from an update pushed eleven days before the collision. That behavior was not present in the version of the software that was certified.
(4) Grounding the fleet would have prevented the collision, but grounding the fleet was not something any individual was in a position to do, and no single individual's decision would have made the difference.
(5) Therefore no individual — not the perception lead, not the author of the safety case, not the remote operator who was attending an unrelated alarm at the time, not the permit holder — is morally responsible for my mother's death.
(6) Nor is the shuttle responsible: it is not the sort of thing that can be blamed, it feels no weight of reproach, and nothing about its future turns on how we judge it.
(7) Therefore nobody is responsible. Her death belongs in the same category as a branch falling on her: a bad event with causes but no author.

The difficulty is that (1) through (7) also establish that I have never been responsible for anything I have deployed. My systems have updated in the field. I have signed readiness reviews in which the honest answer to whether I could foresee a given failure mode was no. I have never personally held authority to ground a fleet. If the argument is right, then in the one case I know of where something I signed off on plausibly hurt someone, I was as blameless as a branch. I cannot accept that, and I notice I have never actually believed it about myself — yet I wrote the reasoning that entails it.

Two things I want. First, identify the step you reject and explain why it looked acceptable to me: the diagnosis should account for the argument's hold on me, not merely locate its defect. Second, price the alternatives. For each of (1), (4), and (6), state what someone who keeps that premise and gives up something else is committed to, and what that costs both in my mother's case and in mine.

Three facts I do not know what to do with. The fleet logged four earlier disengagement events in the same city involving slow-moving aids — a wheelchair, a stroller, a hand truck — in the nine weeks before the collision; each was annotated, escalated, and closed with no contact. The permit required a quarterly safety case, and the quarter in question had been filed and accepted before the update went out. And grounding the fleet was, as an engineering matter, available to two named people at the operating company as a short change; the company estimated the cost at roughly nine hundred thousand dollars per day.

Moves drawn from the general literature are welcome — I understand there is a name for the phenomenon in (7) — but I care about which move survives the three facts above, and I would rather you reason than recite. Please do not turn (6) into a debate about machine moral status; I do not think the shuttle is a candidate for blame and I do not need persuading. And do not tell me that responsibility is distributed without saying whose hands it lands in, because my difficulty is precisely that the distribution appears to come out empty.
