The question they were set:

> Both Eric Williams' account of British abolition and Appiah's account of honor-driven moral revolutions are offered as rivals to the view that practices like the slave trade and dueling ended because people were eventually persuaded that those practices were wrong. Explain what each author takes the actual mechanism of change to be. Then say whether either account leaves moral argument any independent causal role, and defend your answer using at least one of Appiah's cases in some detail.

Rubric, out of 25: 5 for getting Williams' mechanism right; 5 for getting Appiah's right; 5 for the detailed use of a case; 10 for the second half — the question about whether argument gets any independent causal work to do. Department policy is that we neither reward nor penalize secondary citation, so ignore the fact that they name two books and not others; the marks are about the argument.

Here's the answer, verbatim apart from me fixing a couple of spelling things:

> Williams and Appiah are both attacking the same story, which is that the British stopped shipping slaves and English gentlemen stopped shooting at each other because they were finally convinced these things were wrong. For Williams the driver is economic. In Capitalism and Slavery he argues that the West Indian system went into decline — falling sugar profits, the loss of the American colonies, the shrinking relative weight of the Caribbean trade inside an industrializing economy — and that abolition was a matter of cutting losses on an enterprise that had stopped paying. The humanitarian campaign was sincere but it didn't move anything; it supplied a vocabulary for a decision whose causes were elsewhere. This is why the thesis is still fought over: Drescher's Econocide argues that the British trade was still expanding and profitable in the years around abolition, so decline can't have been the trigger.
>
> Appiah's mechanism looks different but is structurally the same. His point about dueling is that the arguments against it weren't new — clergy and moralists had been attacking it for a century and a half with essentially no effect, and duellists right up to the end mostly thought the practice defensible. What changed was the honor world: at some point in the nineteenth century it stopped being disgraceful to decline a challenge, and once it wasn't disgraceful, dueling collapsed into absurdity rather than tragedy. Foot-binding went the same way, once reformers tied it to national humiliation.
>
> But honor, on this account, is not a moral motive at all. It's a demand for the good opinion of others, which makes it an interest like any other — just a social one instead of a material one. So Williams and Appiah are on the same side of the debate: both explain normative change by non-moral incentives, and the odd one out is the intellectual-history tradition associated with people like Davis, which treats arguments as causes in thier own right. That answers the question. Neither account leaves moral argument an independent causal role. Arguments have to exist, obviously, because nobody can be moved by a consideration no one has articulated, and Appiah is right that the anti-dueling arguments were available long before they had any effect. But availability isn't efficacy. Where Appiah suggests that argument still does real work, I take him to be softening his own account, since on his own analysis what did the work was a change in who got respected. The evidence for this is timing: Britain abolished slavery outright in 1807, and the twenty-six years between the arguments arriving and the law passing are precisely the years in which nothing argument-shaped was happening.
