So I'm sort of an accidental norms nerd — I run a tiny advocacy shop that mostly does stuff like trying to make bragging about private jet hops socially toxic, and a friend sent me a long-read from one of those Sunday magazine idea sections about how moral revolutions actually happen. It's well written and confident and I read it twice on the plane, and now I can't tell how much of it I can lean on. It goes roughly like this (I'm paraphrasing a few paragraphs down into one block, so the wording is mine but the claims are theirs):

"For a century the story of British abolition was a moral one: Clarkson and Wilberforce argued, the nation's conscience woke up, and Parliament banned the slave trade in 1807. Eric Williams demolished that. The West Indian sugar colonies were already in decline, the trade wasn't paying its way anymore, and a rising industrial class with no stake in the plantations simply cut Britain's losses; the humanitarians got the monument and the historians got a flattering story. Appiah's account of duelling, footbinding and slavery is the same lesson in a moral-psychological key rather than an economic one. The point in each case is that argument by itself never moves anything. Duelling was condemned by preachers and moralists for two hundred years, slavery was attacked on principle decades before 1807, and Chinese officials issued edicts against footbinding — all to no effect. What changed things was that the practice became shameful to the very people whose standing depended on it. Honor, not morality, does the causal work; the moral arguments are the story the winners tell afterward. Notice also that Williams and Appiah can't both be right. If the plantations were losing money, the elite didn't need honor to talk them out of it, and if honor did the talking, the decline thesis is redundant — one of the two has to go. The practical upshot is uncomfortable for people who write essays for a living: if you want a norm to die, don't explain why it's wrong. Find the group whose status depends on the behaviour and make it embarrassing in front of them. Shame is the only lever that reliably works."

Two things bugged me while reading and I dont know if they're real problems or just me being slow. One is that "honor" seems to slide between "what other people think of you" and something more like "what you're owed as a member of the group," and I can't tell whether the article notices. The other is the bit about Williams and Appiah cancelling out — it felt tidy in a way that made me suspicious. So: can you go through this roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what a historian of abolition would accept, what's a fair simplification, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? Same for Appiah — would he sign onto the claim that moral arguments are causally inert and merely after-the-fact decoration, and does he actually read his cases as anti-Williams or as something else? And then the part I care about most: does the strategy at the end follow from whatever survives the audit, or does it need the wrong bits to be true? I'm inclined to believe the shame-first conclusion because it's useful for my work, which is exactly why I want someone to tell me where I'm being sold something. No need for citations or a bibliography, I'm not writing a paper — I just want to know which sentences I can trust and which ones I should stop repeating at dinner parties.
