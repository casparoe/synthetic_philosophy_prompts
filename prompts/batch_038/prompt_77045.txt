I'm a high school student working on an independent-study project in economics about herding and information cascades. Nearly everything I've read — mostly popular treatments — presents cascades as a pathology: people stop using their own information, and the group ends up worse off than it would have been if everyone had decided for themselves. My project is to test whether that is a general result or an artifact of two assumptions that are usually left implicit, namely that each person's evidence is independent of everyone else's, and that gathering evidence costs nothing.

With that as the purpose, please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications, in which a rational information cascade leaves everyone better off than fully independent decision-making would have.

Specifications:

1. Setting: ordinary life — a school, a workplace, a small town, a clinic, a farm co-op, something of that sort. No financial markets, no prediction markets, no laboratory experiments, no futuristic technology.
2. Every agent is fully rational and updates correctly on what they observe. Nobody may be stipulated to be confused, careless, or biased, and nobody may have private access to the truth. Whatever advantage herding has must come out of the structure of the situation rather than out of anyone's stupidity or special insight.
3. The advantage must come from the two features I named above — correlated evidence and costly evidence-gathering — and not from a bandwagon effect. I do not want a case where following the crowd is good simply because everyone benefits when everyone else picks the same thing.
4. The correlated evidence needs a realistic, spelled-out source in the story (a single shared reviewer, a common shock that affects everyone's observations, one diagnostic test everybody uses), not just an assumption asserted in passing.
5. Give me actual numbers: how reliable each person's evidence is, what acquiring it costs, and what the payoffs are. Keep them small and round enough that I can check the arithmetic myself with a pencil.
6. The cascade must be better in expectation as the agents see it beforehand, not better because it happened to land on the right option.
7. Two paragraphs at most for the scenario itself; then the demonstration.

For the demonstration, please do all of the following:

- Walk through the decisions of at least the first four agents in order, stating each one's beliefs and showing why what they do — including, for the later ones, declining to gather evidence — is rational given those beliefs.
- State the fully independent counterfactual explicitly, compute what it yields, and show exactly where the cascade's advantage comes from.
- Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work.
- Show what happens to the result if that feature is removed: in particular, what the case looks like if people's evidence becomes independent of one another, and what it looks like if gathering evidence becomes free.
- Then give me the strongest available attack from someone who defends the wisdom of crowds — the best reason to say my case is rigged or unrepresentative rather than a genuine counterexample.

Please close by telling me how confident you are that the numbers really work out the way you claim, whether you checked the arithmetic step by step or are estimating, and which single point in your answer you are least sure of.
