Thirty-eight years in a physiology department, most of it on anaesthetics and a fair bit of it on the ethics committee that signed off the animal work, and I've only started reading philosophy properly since I stopped. So I may well be coming at this sideways. I'm giving a talk to out local U3A group in the spring and I want it built round one episode rather than a survey of everybody's opinions.

The episode is the Cruel Treatment of Cattle Act of 1822 — Martin's Act, after Richard Martin — and the society that grew out of it two years later. The bits I think matter, and which I'd like you to take as given rather than correct me on: there had been earlier tries, including Lord Erskine's bill in 1809 which got through the Lords and died in the Commons, and Erskine had argued it in a mixture of rights-and-trusteeship language and biblical appeals. The 1822 Act protected oxen, cows, heifers, steers, sheep and "other cattle", and its list was later held not to cover bulls, which took the 1835 Act to put right. In June 1824 the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals was founded at a coffee house in St Martin's Lane, with a clergyman, Arthur Broome, as its first secretary and Thomas Fowell Buxton in the chair — and William Wilberforce among the founders, which is the fact I keep coming back to. The society brought 63 offenders before the courts in its first year, Broome paid an inspector to watch Smithfield Market out of his own pocket, went to prison for debt as the society's guarantor, and by 1832 the committee had resolved to stop employing inspectors at all.

The lens I want is Singer's expanding circle — the Lecky-derived picture in which the set of beings whose interests count gets wider over time, driven by reason universalising our originally tribal altruism, with the stopping point being everything capable of pleasure and pain.

So, four things:

1. Do the mapping honestly. Who or what is the circle's edge here, and what exactly moved in 1822? My worry is that the sentiment wasn't new — people already found it revolting to beat a cart horse to death in the street — and the Act only gave an existing sentiment teeth. Does the expanding-circle story need the feeling to be new, or can it count enforcement as expansion? And tell me what the bulls episode means: the edge of the moral universe being set by a schedule of nouns and then adjusted by a drafting amendment is a strange thing for a theory that says reason does the work.

2. The engine. Singer has consistency pressing outward. But this episode looks driven by pity, by disgust at visible cruelty in a horse-drawn city, and by clergymen quoting scripture. Is that a real anomaly for the rationalist account, or something it can absorb — reason using sentiment as its vehicle? Say what evidence would actually distinguish the two, if anything could.

3. The ragged edges. Slave trade abolished 1807, cattle protected 1822, slavery itself not until 1833, and the same men — Buxton, Wilberforce — pushing on both fronts at once. Does the theory cope with a circle that widens for sheep while hundreds of thousands of human beings are still property, and does it even make sense to talk of one circle rather than several sectors on independent timetables? And here's a related question I can't settle: did the Act give the animals standing in their own right, or did it protect the bystander's decency? Both readings were available at the time. Tell me what would decide it.

4. A hobby-horse of my own, which I dont think anyone has written up, so treat it as a proposal and pull it apart if it's rubbish. Call it dilution: I suspect circles expand in circumference while getting thinner, the same fixed budget of moral attention spread over more beings, so what changes is density rather than radius. On that view expansion can look like progress whilst delivering almost nothing to the new members — 63 prosecutions a year, aimed mostly at poor carters, with the hunt and the shooting party untouched, and the inspectorate abandoned within a decade for want of money. Does the 1822 case support dilution or genuine added concern? And is dilution falsifiable at all, or is it just a gloomy redescription of any case you like?

End by telling me where the expanding circle is simply silent on this episode — I'd rather have the silences marked than filled in — and whether 1822 confirms Singer, embarrasses him, or is a case his model never contemplated. No reading list, please; I can find my own way to the books. I want the argument.
