I'm a first-year PhD student, and next month I have to lead a session in an interdisciplinary reading group on the nature of inference. I've been assigned Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895). My background is in cognitive science rather than logic or epistemology, and I've read the piece four times; I can follow the surface of the regress, but every time I try to say what exactly goes wrong at a particular step, my sentence turns out to be either trivial or question-begging. I want help getting past that. I'm not looking for a summary — I've got the summary. I'm looking for the load-bearing details.

Here is the stretch I care about. A, B, and Z have already been fixed as, respectively, "Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other," "The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same," and "The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other," and the Tortoise has asked Achilles to force him, logically, to accept Z:

> "And if some reader had not yet accepted A and B as true, he might still accept the sequence as a valid one, I suppose?"
>
> "No doubt such a reader might exist. He might say 'I accept as true the Hypothetical Proposition that, if A and B be true, Z must be true; but, I don't accept A and B as true.' Such a reader would do wisely in abandoning Euclid, and taking to football."
>
> "And might there not also be some reader who would say 'I accept A and B as true, but I don't accept the Hypothetical'?"
>
> "Certainly there might. He, also, had better take to football."
>
> […]
>
> "I'm to force you to accept Z, am I?" Achilles said musingly. "And your present position is that you accept A and B, but you don't accept the Hypothetical—"
>
> "Let's call it C," said the Tortoise.
>
> —but you don't accept
>
> (C) If A and B are true, Z must be true.
>
> "That is my present position," said the Tortoise. […] Achilles then writes A, B, C, and Z into his note-book, and:
>
> "You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
>
> "And why must I?"
>
> "Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
>
> "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
>
> "You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."
>
> "Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it
>
> (D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true. […]
>
> "Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"
>
> "Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!" Achilles triumphantly replied. "Logic would tell you 'You can't help yourself. Now that you've accepted A and B and C and D, you must accept Z!' So you've no choice, you see."
>
> "Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down," said the Tortoise. "So enter it in your book, please. We will call it
>
> (E) If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true.
>
> Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z."

After that the Tortoise remarks that we now have quite a new series, with no end in sight.

My questions, in roughly the order they've become blocking for me:

1. At the transition to (D), what exactly is Achilles's mistake? I keep cycling among three candidates — he has treated an inference rule as though it were another premise; he has treated the Tortoise's refusal as a gap in his beliefs rather than a failure of a disposition; he has simply conceded the point by answering "why must I?" with a further proposition — and I can't tell whether these are three descriptions of one error or three distinct errors, one of which is the real one. Relatedly: if the regress has to stop anywhere, what distinguishes C from D? C looks like a premise too.

2. Achilles's task is to force the Tortoise *logically*. Is the dialogue about validity, or about the normativity of logical consequence for what a person ought to believe? And what principle is doing the work in "Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z"? As far as I can tell that principle — something like *you are only obliged to accept a conclusion if you accept some proposition that licenses the move* — is never stated in the text, only enacted at each step. If that's right, how much of the paradox's force depends on an assumption Carroll never puts in anyone's mouth?

3. The two readers. Why is the second (accepts A and B, rejects the hypothetical) the philosophically interesting one and the first (accepts the hypothetical, rejects A and B) uninteresting? And what work is the football remark doing — is it Carroll signalling that the Tortoise is already outside the game, or a joke he then declines to cash, leaving Achilles to argue with him seriously for another page?

4. My own working hypothesis, which I would like pressure-tested rather than endorsed: the dialogue is not a sceptical puzzle at all but a reductio of a picture of inference on which inferring is nothing over and above coming to believe one further proposition. On that reading the Tortoise isn't a reasoner who has gone wrong but someone who has withdrawn from the practice of inferring while continuing to make noises inside it, and Achilles's error is to keep negotiating with him instead of refusing the terms. Is this a live reading, a familiar one, a comforting dodge, or just wrong? What does it cost me — in particular, does it leave the dialogue with anything to teach, or reduce it to a five-minute point?

5. I also want to press the dialogue onto a case from my own field, and I need to know whether that's legitimate. Suppose a system reliably produces Z whenever it is given A and B, but cannot state, endorse, or even represent C. Has it inferred? Carroll's text seems to give me a way to say it has, since the Tortoise's demand is precisely for yet another representation — but it also seems to license the opposite verdict, since nothing in the system's economy corresponds to what Achilles says is required. Tell me whether the analogy survives, whether it quietly equivocates between two senses of "accept," or whether the dialogue actively warns against it.

Two constraints on your answer. First, please be explicit about the difference between what is settled among people who work on this text and its descendants and what remains genuinely contested: where you give me the standard line, mark it as the standard line rather than as the truth, and don't present any single resolution — including mine in (4) — as though the question had been closed. Second, don't give me a reading list or names of papers; I can't chase references this month and I'd rather have your reasoning. If you think I've misread the passage somewhere, say so first, even if that makes the rest of my questions dissolve.
