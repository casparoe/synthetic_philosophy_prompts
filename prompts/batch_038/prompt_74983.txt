I am not an academic and I have no formal training in any of this, so I would like you to treat what follows as an amateur's system rather than as a summary of a literature I have read. For eleven years I have coordinated a volunteer ground-search group in a mountainous county — roughly sixty active members, mostly tradespeople, retirees, and a few EMTs — and we regularly have to decide things like which drainage to commit people to, whether to keep a sector open overnight, or whether to request a helicopter. Around year four I noticed that we kept making the same kind of mistake: the first two or three teams would go north, and every team after them would go north too, and by the end of the day forty people had independently concluded that north was right, when in fact only two people had ever had a reason to think so. Since then I have read what I could find on herding, on cascades, and on when groups are smarter than their members, and I have written up a set of house rules that I now want to put into our handbook. Before I do, I want someone to tell me whether the thing actually holds together, because I suspect parts of it are in conflict and I cannot tell which parts.

Here is what I hold. (1) When a member of my group goes along with what everyone else is already doing, I do not think that person has made an error; if forty people have committed to the creek drainage, ignoring your own hunch is often the correct read of the evidence, and I reject the popular framing that herding is a bias to be drilled out of individuals. (2) At the same time I think the group decision that results is genuinely bad — not merely unlucky — and I think it is entirely proper to be furious about the outcome while declining to blame any participant in it. (3) I hold that a crowd outperforms its best member only when the contributions are independent, so a cascade is the same crowd with the independence removed; I am comfortable saying the same sixty people can be smarter than their most experienced member and dumber than their median member, depending on how the question was run. (4) My remedy is a designated dissenter: before we commit to a sector, one member is assigned the job of arguing against the emerging consensus, and we do not move until that case has been made out loud. (5) That dissent must be anonymous, because I have watched people swallow objections the moment they realized the incident commander could see who was objecting; I want the anonymity strong enough that we cannot tell who wrote it. (6) Dissent must also be expensive — I call this my cost-of-noise rule: objecting has to cost the objector something real, such as volunteering to walk the sector they are proposing or taking the overnight shift, because objections that are free to produce are worth nothing. (7) In a true emergency — a storm front moving in, a member injured on the slope — the most experienced person present takes over and issues orders immediately, with no round of private estimates and no dissent phase. (8) I believe the deepest damage in a cascade is informational: once everyone has deferred, the private reasons that would have settled the question are gone permanently, and no amount of later discussion reconstructs them. (9) Yet I also believe cascades are fragile, and that one credible public objection can reverse forty people in the space of a few minutes — I have seen it happen. And (10) I believe we ought to train members to withstand social pressure and to think for themselves, which is a standing item on our annual training day.

I would like an audit rather than an essay on the topic. Go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only look like they conflict and dissolve once you state them precisely, and rank the real ones by severity instead of presenting them all as equally troublesome. For each genuine conflict, say exactly what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me in practice. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage. Two things I cannot resolve on my own. First, on a search last spring a member who had fished the creek in question as a boy sat silent through the whole planning round and said afterwards that the creek could be walked end to end in twenty minutes; we switched to the ridge within five minutes and found the missing man there the same afternoon. My rules praise him for speaking and simultaneously tell me he was rational to stay quiet for as long as he did, and I do not know how to hold both. Second, when dissent is anonymous, I cannot tell whether an objection came from the member I assigned to that role or from someone with actual information about the terrain, and my own cost-of-noise rule tells me those two objections are worth completely different amounts — but rule (5) makes them indistinguishable by design. Please do not assume I know which of my commitments I would rather abandon; I do not.
