I teach a third-year seminar called something like "Ethics at the Limits" — 14 students, one 90-minute block, no break, and I've got a whiteboard and a handfull of index cards, no projector. This week we hit populations that are infinite in size and I want them to arrive at the reading having already felt the problem instead of reading about it cold. They've all done intro consequentialism, they know the repugnant conclusion and mostly find it annoying, and three of them took a first course in analysis last year while the rest haven't seen a series since high school. That spread matters: whatever you design can't lean on convergence tests or sigma notation or any notation at all. It has to be arithmetic they can do with a pencil on paper in a couple of minutes.

What I want them to run into by themselves is this. You put two worlds in front of them, each with infinitely many people, each person's wellbeing written down as a plain number, and you ask whether switching from one to the other is an improvement. One group confidently computes that it is, another computes that it isn't, and both have done the arithmetic correctly, from the very same two cards. Then separately I want each student to make a small individual choice that touches only finitely many lives, realise their choice changes nothing about any total anyone could actually write down, and then realise that the class's choices taken together change everything. If that second half is too much for 90 minutes, say so and tell me what a 50-minute version has to give up. So: the rules, the world-cards themselves with the real numbers written out (don't describe them, write them so I can print them), a stake that isn't tokens — last year I tried tokens and it turned into a game about hoarding tokens and nobody thought about ethics for one second, so I want the payoff to be something else entirely — a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the point it was producing, with the names of things coming out only at the very end.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm specifically worried about the analysis kids shouting the answer in minute five, about a group that refuses on principle to compare infinite worlds at all and then just sits there, and about the room concluding this is a maths trick with no ethical content — that last one would be a real failure for me, because its supposed to land as "add up the wellbeing" not being a well-defined instruction once the population is infinite, not as "series are weird." And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce. I know 14 people with cards isn't an infinite population, but tell me which specific feature of the problem is going to be missing and what I'll have to supply by hand in the last ten minutes.
