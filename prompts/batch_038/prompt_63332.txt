Just got out of a two hour argument with a friend (phil of science, entity realist, does work on scientific imaging) and in three weeks I'm presenting basically the view I was defending to our department's grad colloquium, so I need to know what he and his friends are going to do to me.

My view, roughly: epistemic structural realism, but with a twist I think makes it less exposed than the standard Worrall version. I say the only thing we're epistemically entitled to believe about unobservables is the invariance content of a theory — what stays fixed across every empirically adequate reformulation of it — and I explicitly deny that what we believe is a mathematical structure. For me structures are just an indexing device for the invariances; there are still objects that the invariances are invariances of, but any claim about what those objects are like apart from their role in the invariance scheme isn't false or unknown, it isn't a claim. So I'm not doing Ramsey-sentence structural realism and I'm not doing ontic structural realism, I want to say I'm in a third place. Motivation is the usual story: Fresnel's equations survived the abandonment of the ether, so what got retained is what was ever worth believing.

Audience: ~25 people. Two or three committed entity/causal realists who think you can literally watch tracks form in a cloud chamber and that manipulability hands you causal access to the relata — one of them works on interventionist causation and will definitely ask what an intervention on an invariance would even be. One constructive empiricist who thinks I've either rebranded his view or built an unstable halfway house (he's fond of the van Fraassen charge that structural realism smuggles in a priori principles about what could be known). One category-theory-leaning person who'll think my notion of invariance is way to weak — isomorphism-invariance doesn't pin anything down, they'll say, and I owe them an account of what happens when you permute the relata inside a single model. Plus a pile of grad students, one of whom has read Newman's objection and will deploy it triumphantly. Nobody in the room does history of physics and nobody does quantum foundations, so please don't build the prep around entanglement or the OSR-from-QM moves, that isn't the fight I'm going to get.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of me. Give me these sections in this order:

1. **The room** (~150 words): what this audience's default assumptions are, what makes them impatient vs. receptive, and specifically where my invariance twist reads as clever and where it reads as evasion.

2. **Concede these first** (~100 words): the two or three things I should hand over in my first five minutes so the entity realists don't spend the whole hour on them.

3. **Objections, worst first** (the main section, seven or eight of them): ordered by how much damage each does to *my* version specifically, not to structural realism generically. For each one: how it'll actually be phrased in that room, using their words and their examples — cloud chambers, interventions, cardinality, permutation — not a textbook formulation; a reply I can deliver in about 90 seconds; one line saying where that reply is soft; and a damage score out of 10 with a sentence on why.

4. **The one that has to land** (~150 words): the single reply that, if it fails, takes the whole position down with it, and how to make it as hard to miss as possible.

5. **The question I'm praying nobody asks** (~100 words): the thing I genuinely have no answer to becuase I've never thought about it, plus the least humiliating way to stall or reframe live.

6. **Is my third place a real place?** (~200 words): your honest verdict on whether the invariance-content move actually escapes Newman's objection and the instability charge, or whether I've just put a new name on one of them.

Be blunt with me. If a reply works on grad students but bounces off the interventionist, say so explicitly.
