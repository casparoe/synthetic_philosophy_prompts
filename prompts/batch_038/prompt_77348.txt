You are a philosopher of material culture who has also spent the better part of a decade sitting on heritage advisory panels, and who has therefore been in more hostile question periods than you can count. I want you to prepare me for one. I write novels. My fourth, *Weatherwork*, follows a wooden pilot cutter called the Ada Lyle across sixty years of repair — the same vessel, plank by plank, from launch to the present day, told through the yard's logbooks rather than through any single human life — and it has been adopted, somewhat to my surprise, by the preservation world. That trust has asked me to give the opening address at its autumn symposium on authenticity: twenty-five minutes at the lectern, then twenty minutes of questions. The room will be roughly two-thirds practicing shipwrights, conservators, and vessel surveyors, people who earn their living deciding what stays and what goes and who resent being told by outsiders that the decision is philosophically confused; one-sixth conservation scientists who date timber, radiograph fastenings, and analyse paint stratigraphy for a living; and the remainder administrators who write the listing criteria and the grant applications that the whole sector lives on. Two academic philosophers of material culture will be present, one of whom has written extensively on the view that artifacts are what the intentions and practices of their makers and keepers make them. Nobody will have come for the Ship of Theseus, though the organizers warn me that a moderator has been deputed to ask me about it regardless.

The address draws the conclusion the novel keeps circling, and I should like you to treat it as a thesis to be attacked rather than explained. My claim is that the yard does not in fact believe what its own paperwork says it believes. Listing standards and funding bids measure authenticity as a stock: the proportion of original fabric surviving, the percentage of the hull that is still the hull that was launched. But everything the yard actually prizes, funds, trains for, and defends in public is a flow — the unbroken regime of maintenance, the craft knowledge handed to the people who perform it, the documented chain of decisions about each successive repair. The vessel persists, on this view, not as a lump of oak with a dwindling fraction of its original material, but as a maintenance regime with a continuous practical and documentary history; the ship the yard cares about is a long verb rather than a long noun. And the consequence, which is the part that will sting, is that the fabric-percentage metric generates what I intend to call a neglect premium. A vessel abandoned in a field for forty years and then heroically rebuilt in a single well-publicised campaign scores better on original material than one competently and continuously cared for, because good maintenance replaces things, and it replaces them often, and it records having done so. So the metric is not a neutral measure of a value the sector already holds; it is a piece of bad metaphysics doing quiet political work, steering money toward spectacle and away from competence. I have three of the trust's own 1930s repair ledgers on my desk to support the historical half of that argument, and my spouse, who trained as a boatbuilder before we met, assures me the neglect premium is real and resented.

Before you draft anything, think carefully and spend a short paragraph mapping the fault lines in that room: whose professional authority my thesis attacks, who depends on the fabric metric for their budget, and which objections will come from craft pride, which from epistemics — how, a conservator will want to know, can anyone inspect or verify the continuity of a practice rather than of a material — and which from the two philosophers. Then give me a prep sheet rather than a defense; I am not asking you to argue my case for me. List the eight or nine objections I will actually receive from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, and phrase each one the way the person raising it would phrase it. The shipwright will not accuse me of conflating numerical identity with qualitative similarity, and the conservation scientist will not use the word epiphenomenal, so do not write their questions in philosophical prose. For each objection give a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is soft or concedes more than I would like, and a marker showing which two or three are the objections that will decide whether the talk is taken seriously. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to disarm the room, what I must not concede on any account, and the single reply that has to land for the thesis to survive the afternoon. Someone will raise the Japanese shrines that are dismantled and rebuilt from new timber every twenty years; tell me whether that example rescues my argument or buries it. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, the one for which I have no good answer, and say plainly what it exposes.

I would rather you be blunt than encouraging. As you close, tell me what would change your mind about the thesis itself: which of these objections, pressed properly, would defeat it rather than merely embarrass it, and whether the neglect-premium claim survives scrutiny or is a rhetorically convenient exaggeration that a careful administrator could dismantle with a single counterexample. And since you cannot know this room, flag explicitly where your estimate of who will object to what is guesswork, and say how the list would be reordered by a different audience — for instance, if the administrators stayed home and the room were mostly working boatbuilders, or if the two philosophers brought four of their graduate students.
