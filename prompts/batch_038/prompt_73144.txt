For about two years now I've been having a long email arguement with a friend from grad school about epiphenomenalism — we were supposed to co-author something, gave up on that, and now we just keep writing at each other — and neither of us has budged, which is starting to feel suspicious, like we aren't actually disagreeing about the same thing. Let me lay both sides out as fairly as I can. The view: conscious experiences are caused by physical brain processes but cause nothing in return; nomological danglers. My friend accepts roughly that. The objection we keep circling is the one about phenomenal judgment. If the experience itself is causally inert, then the causal story behind my typing "this experience is vivid and reddish" is already complete without ever mentioning the experience. So it looks like a coincidence that my reports line up with what I'm actually feeling, and worse, they'd come out word-for-word identical in a world where my qualia were absent or systematically shuffled, which makes it hard to see what such beliefs could be about or how they could count as knowledge. His reply has two parts. First: the experience leaves a neural trace, and when I later form the judgment the trace gets reactivated, so there's a causal chain running from experience to report through physical intermediaries — indirect causation, which he thinks is all that reference or knowledge ever required. Second: phenomenal concepts aren't descriptive or informational, they pick the experience out directly, by acquaintance, in a way that doesn't need the experience to shove anything around.

My side, roughly: the trace only carries what the neural event encoded, and whether that encoding lines up with the quale type is precisely what's in dispute — the alignment is being supplied by the psychophysical laws, not by the experience, so at best we have lawful covariance dressed up as a causal explanation. And the acquaintance move either begs the question, since an acquaintance that does no causal work can't plausibly be doing the explanatory work either, or it quietly relocates the fight to a theory of mental content. The thing I keep reaching for is the counterfactual: invert or delete the qualia, hold the physics fixed, and the judgment is unchanged, so on any ordinary notion of tracking these beliefs aren't sensitive to what they're supposedly about. His answer is that I'm smuggling in a causal/informational account of content and then triumphantly reporting that his view fails it, and that acquaintance-style reference is exactly the account on which the counterfactual test doesn't get a vote. My answer to that is that he's smuggling in an account of content that's only available if the concept is already hooked up to its object, and hooking-up is what we're arguing about. Round and round, no movement, both of us convinced the other is cheating.

So diagnose it. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working hypothesis — treat it as a hypothesis and push on it — is that almost none of the work is being done by claims about causation, and that we're really fighting an unacknowledged battle over what fixes the content of a phenomenal concept: covariance/information, direct acquaintance, or inferential role, with the causal-inertness business functioning as a proxy. A second guess is that we're applying different tests to the same view, his about actual causal history and mine about what would have happened under a change, and that no single argument can win once the criteria differ. A third and darker guess is that the objection isn't special to epiphenomenalism at all: if the real problem is "how can we know about something that makes no causal difference to us," the same problem should bite for mathematical objects, moral facts, maybe even other minds, and the reason this debate has stalled is that it's a general unsolved problem wearing a philosophy-of-mind costume. Tell me which of those is closest, or whether the right diagnosis is something else — a merely verbal dispute about what "causally efficacious" is supposed to cover (does law-mediated covariance count?), two different questions being fought under one heading (could epiphenomenalism be true vs. could an epiphenomenalist's beliefs be knowledge), or a sign the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Argue from what each side actually says in the setup above; please don't give me a tour of positions on consciousness, I know the tour already. And be concrete about progress: is there anything that would count — a distinction that breaks the proxy fight, a worked-out account of phenomenal content whose terms both sides could accept, a case that forces one of the two tests to give way, some formal result linking content and causation, an empirical finding about how reports of experience are generated — or should I expect us to be having this identical exchange in ten years? If it's the latter, say whether that's a failure of the arguers or a feature of the question, and what a third party should do with the thread. No reading list, I can't chase sources right now.
