And here is the student's answer, copied out of the script (I've tidied the spelling, not the argument):

**Answer.** Aumann's result says that if two agents begin from the same prior probability distribution over the states of the world, update by conditionalising on their own private information, and it then becomes common knowledge between them what each of them believes about some event A, their posterior probabilities for A must be equal. They cannot agree to disagree. Each of them may privately hold a different view, and each may even know that the other holds a different view, but that difference cannot be out in the open in the sense of common knowledge.

The two assumptions doing the work are the common prior and the common knowledge of the posteriors. The proof goes through the agents' information partitions. Private information determines, for each agent, a partition of the state space into the cells they cannot tell apart, and their posterior is constant across the cells of their own partition. The event that agent 1's posterior for A equals q1 is a union of cells of agent 2's partition, since agent 1 knows their own posterior. Where it is common knowledge that the posteriors are q1 and q2, the relevant event is a union of cells of the meet, the finest common coarsening of the two partitions, and on each such cell both agents' posteriors must equal the prior probability of A conditional on that cell. So q1 = q2.

Much is made of the phrase "common knowledge of rationality", but rationality is not an assumption of the theorem at all: what the theorem needs is Bayesian updating from a shared prior and a shared awareness of what the other now believes. That is why the popular slogan is misleading. Two perfectly rational agents with different private evidence will rationally reach different posteriors, and there is nothing incoherent about each of them being aware that the other has reached a different one. What the theorem rules out is only the further step in which that awareness itself becomes fully transparent.

I would push the last point further. Common knowledge is treated as a demanding idealisation, but the idealisation costs nothing. Suppose the agents' posteriors are mutual knowledge to order n: each knows them, each knows the other knows them, and so on up to n levels. The argument above proceeds level by level, so agreement is forced at every finite stage, and letting n grow as large as we please delivers the same conclusion. Common knowledge is simply a shorthand for mutual knowledge to arbitrarily high finite order, and the theorem therefore does not depend on any fixed-point property. Since no finite amount of mutual knowledge permits disagreement, nothing changes in the limit, and worries about common knowledge being an unrealistic assumption are beside the point.

The common prior assumption is likewise a convenience rather than a substantive commitment. Agents who look as though they have different priors differ only in what they take themselves to know about the situation they are in, and that difference can be represented as further private information about one another's types. Once it is written into the state space, the two share a prior again and the theorem applies unchanged. So the assumption costs the result no generality, and the disagreements we actually observe are traceable to differences in evidence or to lapses from Bayesian updating, never to genuinely different starting points.
