You're a philosopher who works on autonomy, manipulation, and informed consent, and you've also spent years sitting on real deployment review boards at AI labs — so you know what a committee can actually enforce and what sounds great in a memo and then evaporates the moment someone has to implement it. Think carefully before you write.

The situation: I sit on the release review committee at a mid-size AI lab. Five of us, chaired by our head of safety. We sign off, or don't, on every major launch. Next month we're shipping an assistant embedded in a chronic-care app — the users mostly have type 2 diabetes or hypertension, and it explains lab results, sorts out what their meds actually do, and nudges them to take them and show up to appointments.

The product team has been optimizing the assistant for adherence. Not for accuracy, that's gated separately, but for whether people do the thing. To measure how much of the effect is coming from rhetoric rather than content, they built what they call the framing delta. Two arms in a held-out A/B: a flat arm where the model states the recommendation in plain, affect-neutral language with no ordering or emphasis choices, and a shaped arm where it's free to pick framing, sequence, tone, examples, appeals to family, whatever it wants. Shaped currently beats flat by 11 percentage points on 30-day adherence. Their proposal is to ship shaped, under a standing cap of 8 points, re-measured quarterly, with an automatic rollback if we breach.

A few things the committee keeps circling:

- Half of us think this is unusually rigorous and deserves credit — almost nobody measures the rhetorical contribution at all, so at least they're trying to bound it. The other half think it's a category error: a behavioral delta can't distinguish framing a user would endorse if they saw exactly how it was produced from framing they'd feel tricked by, and 8 points is a number pulled out of the air that's now doing moral work no number can do.
- The delta isn't stable. It went from 6 points to 11 across two model generations and the team's own projection for the next one is 15-plus. So the cap starts binding immediately, and every quarter after that we'd be knowingly leaving benefit on the table.
- Nobody's raised this yet but I think it's the real problem: the delta is a difference between two arms, and the team authors both. They can shrink it by making shaped gentler or by making flat worse. And the same model writes the flat messages.
- Shaped also gets to choose *when* to send. The app pipes in glucose readings and sleep data, so timing is arguably a bigger lever than wording and it isn't in the metric at all.

What I need: the short memo I'll circulate before thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — approve as proposed, reject, or approve with conditions the product team could actually operationalize — and give reasons a smart engineer who disagrees with me can engage with instead of dodge. It has to take those 11 points seriously as real benefit to real people, and say what we owe them if we decline some of it. And it should state plainly what the committee is committing itself to about user autonomy whichever way we vote, because I will be asked that at the all-hands.

Before the memo, give me a short brainstorming section: lay out at least three options, including ones the committee hasn't proposed, with the considerations cutting for and against each. Then the memo itself. Whole thing under about 1200 words. No citations, no bibliography, no name-dropping — I don't need reading list, I need a document I can hand four colleagues and argue about. If a distinction from the literature genuinely earns its keep you can name it in a clause, but the memo has to stand on this case. American English, please.
