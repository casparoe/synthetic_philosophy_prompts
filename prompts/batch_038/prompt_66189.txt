I am a software engineer who works on distributed systems. I have no formal philosophy training, but I have read enough of the peer-disagreement literature to recognize the landmarks by name: conciliationism and its Equal Weight View, the steadfast alternatives (both the right-reasons flavor and the total-evidence flavor), the claim that a recognized peer's dissent functions as higher-order evidence, the uniqueness thesis, the spinelessness objection to conciliationism, and the conciliator's response that genuine peers are rare and that the question of who counts as a peer is itself disputed. Right now I hold all of this as labels I can repeat but cannot operate with. I would like it converted into the framework I actually reason in, and I would like an honest account of what is lost in the conversion.

Here is the system, stated as concretely as I can. A fleet of N workers all consume the same replicated input log; each independently computes a point estimate of some quantity plus a self-reported confidence, and the fleet must publish a single reconciled value. I have implemented and measured two reconciliation policies. Under the first, a worker that learns another worker's estimate and confidence revises to a confidence-weighted midpoint with that worker, treating the other's report as fully as informative as its own derivation; under the second, a worker ignores peer reports entirely unless it has in-band evidence of a fault in the reporting worker, such as a missed heartbeat, a checksum mismatch, or a divergence from a reference value it can independently verify. The observed behavior mirrors what I take to be the philosophical dialectic: the first policy converges quickly when the workers genuinely see different inputs, but when re-evaluation is triggered by messages rather than by new log entries, the published value flaps and sometimes fails to settle at all, and workers with better inputs get dragged toward workers with worse ones; the second policy is stable and settles immediately, but occasionally a worker that is silently degrading keeps its estimate in the published value indefinitely, because nothing in-band ever reveals that it is the faulty one.

Three further details matter, because they are places where I suspect my vocabulary may be quietly doing philosophical work. First, we operationalize a peer by what I will call the symmetric-fault assumption: absent evidence, any worker is a priori as likely as any other to be the faulty one, and no worker can inspect itself against ground truth. Second, our design documents distinguish measurement disagreement (workers were partitioned and really did see different inputs) from processing disagreement (workers saw identical inputs and disagree, so the disagreement is about the function applied to the data); we treat these differently, and it seems to me that most of the philosophy I have read is about the second kind while several of the conciliatory arguments sound like the first. Third, the midpoint rule is only definable because our state space is convex: estimates are real numbers. When the fleet has to agree on a discrete choice instead, such as which shard to promote, splitting the difference is not even well-formed and we fall back on voting, quorums, and leader election.

With that setup, I would like the following:

1. Restate the conciliationism/steadfastness debate in this vocabulary as faithfully as you can, using the apparatus I already command — agreement and validity as separate properties, failure detectors, crash versus Byzantine faults, quorums, partitions, gossip and convergence, auditability of a node against a replayable log, convex versus discrete state spaces. Where a philosophical notion has no term in my apparatus, invent one and define it explicitly rather than borrowing a word that carries my framework's assumptions with it.

2. Go through the debate claim by claim — the Equal Weight prescription, the requirement that a peer's dissent be treated as independent evidence about one's own reliability, the higher-order-evidence characterization, the uniqueness thesis, the spinelessness objection, the rareness-of-peers reply, the right-reasons and total-evidence responses — and for each give one of three verdicts: translates cleanly; translates only with a specified distortion (say precisely what has to be bent); or has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

3. Attend especially to three spots I expect to be residuary. Whether anything in my framework corresponds to the validity condition's connection to truth, given that a consensus protocol can converge unanimously on a wrong value and still be correct by its own lights. Whether there is any counterpart to a separate metadata channel for evidence about reliability rather than about the quantity itself, or whether in my framework reliability information is always eventually cashed out in observations. And whether the auditability asymmetry runs the way I have assumed: my workers can be checked against a replayable log after the fact, and I have been assuming that in the epistemic case there is no such replay — but perhaps the shared evidence base is exactly a replayable log, in which case tell me what the difference in replayability does to the argument.

4. Then give me the residue report proper. For what fails to translate, take a position: is the failure a limitation of the distributed-systems vocabulary, which can express safety properties but not the notion of a consideration that makes a belief likely to be true, or is it a sign that the philosophical disagreement is thinner than its practitioners suppose — that conciliators and steadfast theorists are largely just assuming different fault models and different failure-detector strengths, and that once those assumptions are stated explicitly there is no further substantive dispute?

5. Keep the translation honest. Do not import a protocol assumption I have not earned — a bound on the number of faulty nodes, partial synchrony, an external oracle, guaranteed auditability, a convex state space — and then present the resulting conclusion as a translation of a philosophical claim. Every time you need such an assumption to make the mapping work, flag it separately and say which side of the debate it favors.

6. Finally, tell me whether the translation pays for itself in my own domain: does casting the debate this way predict anything about the flapping under the first policy or the silent degradation under the second that I would not have seen otherwise, and is there a third policy that the philosophical literature suggests and that I have not tried?
