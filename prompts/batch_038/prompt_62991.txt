I serve on the grants committee of a community foundation in a post-industrial city of roughly 85,000, and I have to circulate a decision memo before our September meeting. Eight years ago a local man who made his money in industrial fasteners died and left the foundation about $5.4 million along with a single handwritten page of instruction, which his daughter has now read aloud to the board twice: the money is to be spent "helping this town learn how to live, not merely how to make a living," and the mechanism is left entirely to our discretion. I keep being pulled back to Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic prospects of his grandchildren — the one quoted in every automation essay ever written, in which a fifteen-hour week figures as the expected end point — because the donor appears to be asking us to fund the thing Keynes thought would be genuinely difficult, while nearly everyone around our table is proposing to fund the thing he thought was easy. I should add that I have read the rationalist blogs since before they were respectable and I can usually spot motivated cognition in myself, which is why I want to disclose that I own a banjo and a woodlot and that this is probably not irrelevant to my enthusiasm for the third proposal below.

Staff have narrowed the field to three, each costed at about $4 million over five years with the balance held in reserve. The first subsidizes a four-day week for two years at eight participating local employers: hours down a fifth, pay held level, evaluated on retention and a well-being survey. The second is an unconditional annual grant of $2,500 to roughly 700 residents below a household-income threshold, marketed in the staff paper as buying back one's Saturdays, with no strings attached; a pilot survey we commissioned suggests most recipients would spend it retiring a second job or a gig shift rather than not working. The third funds what the program officer calls an arts-of-life network: paid apprenticeships and open-ended small grants in gardening, repair, music, cooking, local history, and conversation, explicitly non-vocational, with no deliverable required of anyone who takes part. The board is split three ways. One faction holds that only the first two respect recipients as agents of their own lives, since the third presumes to know what living well looks like and will end up, as these things do, funding hobby classes for the already comfortable. Another holds that the first two simply hand people more means — more money, more unclaimed hours — which on Keynes's own diagnosis is not the binding constraint at all, and that a donor who wrote the sentence above would be appalled to see it spent on wages. The third faction, which includes our two members with labor backgrounds, notes that something like 40 percent of our working-age clients hold more than one job, that median household income here is under $46,000, and that the entire framing is condescending to people whose difficulty is not that they lack the art of life but that they lack the money to practice any art at all. One of them recalled that Keynes himself thought the wrench would fall hardest on people with no roots and no accumulated habits of leisure to fall back on, and asked whether we are proposing to convert a working-class town into the rentier class's hobby farm, at its own expense.

Please draft the memo I circulate: a decision document with an actionable bottom line, not a survey, and no bibliography or footnotes, since three of the seven members will not read past the second page. It should come down to a recommendation — fund one, sequence two, or reject the set and specify precisely what to send back to staff — together with a defensible timeline and a stated condition under which we would abandon it. I want it to engage Keynes's argument in substance rather than by name-checking: the distinction he draws between absolute and relative needs and his claim that the relative ones are insatiable, his diagnosis of the habit of always pushing the meaning of one's life into the future and postponing enjoyment, and his insistence that the transition rather than the scarcity is the hard part. It should say plainly whether any of that actually licenses the third proposal, or whether I am using a dead economist to launder my own taste in hobbies. Two difficulties I would especially like pressed on. First, I cannot find an objective function for this decision that does not collapse into Goodhart's law the moment staff see it: hours worked, self-reported satisfaction, take-up rate, and number of completed projects each reward the wrong behavior somewhere, and I would rather the memo name that problem and propose something workable than paper over it with a balanced scorecard. Second, I do not have an answer to the revealed-preference objection buried in the second paragraph, which strikes me as the strongest thing that will be said in that room; at present I have nothing better than an appeal to preference formation, which sounds like a way of saying that people want the wrong things and we know better. Treat the labor faction's objection at length and honestly. If the honest conclusion is that we should not fund the arts-of-life program, say so and say why; if the objection is better answered by redesign than by retreat, show me the redesign — who gets chosen, who pays, and what would have to be true for it not to be the paternalism it looks like.
