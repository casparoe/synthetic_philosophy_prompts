Retired analytical chemist, spending my mornings on probability and philosophy of science for fun now. There's a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a word for.

Two people, both honest as far as I can tell, both doing their updating correctly, look at the *same* piece of evidence and end up further apart than they started — each of them citing that very study as support for their own side. Not cherry-picking, not filtering: same data, same day, both walk away more sure of the opposite conclusion. So "confirmation bias" feels wrong to me, and "echo chamber" too, since neither is controlling what the other sees.

I wondered whether it was just a starting-point problem, so I did a toy calculation — two hypotheses, one yes/no measurement, repeated. With the same likelihoods but different priors, the two agents drifted apart for a while and then closed the gap again as I fed in more measurements; the log-odds gap stayed pinned at a constant the whole time. I could only get a permanent, widening split when I let the two agents use *different* likelihoods — different ideas about what the instrument would read if each hypothesis were true.

Is there an established name for that specific thing? I gather there's a theorem to the effect that rational agents who started from the same place can't agree to disagree, and I want to know whether my case is a recognised loophole in that theorem or has its own literature, and how it differs from the psychology version where people really do weight identical evidence unevenly, and from the talk about whole frameworks being untranslatable. Don't just hand me a label — I want the edges: what has to be true for it to happen, what makes it stop, and which borderline cases don't count.

Also, tell me how confident you are in what you say and point at the one bit you're least sure about.
