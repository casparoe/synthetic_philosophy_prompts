I am preparing a reconstruction for a seminar on Hume's monetary writings — the essays on money and the balance of trade from the *Political Discourses* of 1752, together with the later essay on the jealousy of trade — and the argument I have assembled turns out to prove far more than I intended. Its conclusion is that Hume's own theory destroys his own policy advice, or that his policy advice destroys his theory. I cannot accept that, but I cannot find the step to refuse. The premises below are my own paraphrases of Hume's position, and the reasoning between them is entirely mine.

(1) Money is not itself any part of a nation's wealth; it is the instrument by which commodities are exchanged, and it has value only in that role. Real wealth consists in the stock of labour and of consumable and exchangeable goods.

(2) Because the quantity of money is not part of wealth, that quantity adjusts itself to the level of a country's industry and consumption. Where money is plentiful relative to goods, prices rise, foreign demand falls, and money flows out; where it is scarce, prices fall and money flows in. Once adjustment is complete, any quantity of money is equally serviceable to a country.

(3) Therefore a state cannot augment a nation's real wealth by augmenting its stock of money. This is the anti-mercantilist conclusion Hume wants.

(4) Hume nonetheless advises the magistrate to keep the quantity of money increasing if he possibly can. His reason is that an increase operates while it is happening. New money enters at particular points — with the merchant who receives it, the manufacturer who employs the newly paid hands, the farmer who sells to them — and passes from hand to hand by degrees; only after a time does it lift the prices of labour and commodities throughout the country. During that interval the labourer and the tradesman find their receipts rising before their costs do, and their diligence is quickened.

(5) The whole of the benefit described in (4) lies inside the interval between the arrival of the money and the completion of the adjustment. When the adjustment is complete, (2) and (3) reassert themselves and nothing has been gained.

(6) An interval persists only so long as the agents inside it fail to anticipate its end. Merchants, farmers, and labourers who expected the rise would advance their own prices at once, and the interval would close.

(7) So the beneficial effect of an increase in money depends on its being unanticipated, and a state can secure it only by a policy whose operation is not publicly understood. An increase announced as policy, and expected as such, ceases to work.

(8) But a recommendation to magistrates is a public doctrine. Hume prints it, in an essay, for the very men who would act on it and for the merchants who would trade against them.

(9) Hence the policy of keeping money increasing is self-defeating: publishing the theory removes the mechanism on which the policy depends.

(10) The only escape is to deny (5) and hold that the increase keeps working after prices have adjusted. But that is to say that money has real effects on industry at every quantity, not merely in transition, which is to deny (1) through (3): money would be more than an instrument of exchange, and the anti-mercantilist conclusion would be gone.

(11) Therefore either Hume's monetary policy is self-defeating or his monetary theory is false.

Two things trouble me beyond the conclusion itself. First, the reasoning at (7) generalises destructively: on that step, any published argument to the effect that expansion has real effects would abolish the effects it describes, so the same argument would refute in advance every case for announced stimulus, in Hume's century or in ours. That is a large price to pay for a premise I have not questioned. Second, I would rather surrender (9) than (3), and yet I do not see how to do it without giving up (6) — and (6) looks to me like the most secure step in the whole chain, since it says nothing more than that expectations matter.

So: which step should I reject, why did it look unimpeachable from where I was standing, and what does rejecting it cost elsewhere — in particular, what must a Humean give up about the mechanism in (2), or about the standing of (3), if the rejection is made? If you think instead that the argument is sound and that I should simply accept (11), tell me which horn is the softer one and what is left of Hume's advice to the magistrate. And if your diagnosis of the flaw is meant to be general, show that it also disposes of the generalisation about announced stimulus, rather than merely relocating it.

One further difficulty I have kept out of the numbered steps, in case it changes your verdict: Hume also treats paper credit as an artifice by which a country may substitute a cheaper medium for gold and silver and send the precious metals abroad. If the magistrate follows that advice too, the increase he produces is not an increase in the total means of exchange, and the interval in (4) seems never to open. Whether this is an independent problem for Hume, or the same problem under another description, is something I would like your view on once the main diagnosis is settled.
