I've spent a decade going back and forth on the word *collapse*, mostly in comment threads, and I want to know whether my usage adds up to anything. Here are the cases where my judgment feels settled, and the ones where it doesn't.

Eastern Mediterranean, around 1200 BCE: the Mycenaean palaces burned, the palace accounting apparatus and the script that went with it vanished, settlements thinned, and literacy went away for centuries — collapse. The Hittite kingdom disappeared, leaving only small successor states out on its former borders — collapse. Egypt, caught in the same storm, kept its pharaoh, its temples, and its scribes and got poorer: decline, emphatically not collapse. Three verdicts, one event, and I can't see the line I'm drawing.

The Classic Maya southern lowlands: dated monuments stop, the dynasties end, the great cities empty — yet the north went on building afterward, and Maya people and Mayan languages are alive today. I refuse to call that a collapse; I want to call it relocation and political reorganization.

Rapa Nui: I no longer believe the story I grew up on. My current view is that the islanders fed a stable population on stone-mulch gardens right up to European contact, and that the palms were destroyed largely by the rats that came with the first settlers rather than by reckless clearing. On that view there was no pre-contact collapse at all, which is the verdict I want — and yet something resists. A people who could no longer quarry and move their own statues were not the people they had been, and I don't want a principle that makes that difference invisible.

Cahokia: the mound center dispersed into ordinary villages with no obvious die-off. I want to call that not a collapse but an exit — people concluding that the political order wasn't worth submitting to and walking away. That is uncomfortably close to my description of the Mycenaean case.

Norse Greenland: the Norse settlements ended and the Inuit did not, and my instinct says the Norse failed where the Inuit succeeded. Except that the isotope evidence from Norse skeletons suggests the late settlers were getting most of their protein from the sea — the very thing they were supposedly too stubborn to do — and the Inuit way of life was itself ended a few centuries later by other pressures.

The western Roman Empire: politically over, and yet the language, the church, the law, and the cities carried straight through — more cultural continuity by any count than in my Maya case, where I say there was no collapse. Still, I call this one a collapse, largely because the physical record looks impoverished: coarser pottery, tile roofs giving way to thatch, smaller cattle. I hold both verdicts and I know they don't fit.

And the case I keep returning to: the Soviet Union. The state dissolved, output fell by something like a third, and male life expectancy in Russia dropped by years over the following decade — while the cities, the factories, the languages, and nearly all the people persisted, with most residents of the successor states reporting they are better off now. I want to call that a collapse, and a serious one. I also want to say the Maya case wasn't. I have no account of the difference.

My rough principle, offered for demolition: a society collapses when it can no longer afford the complexity it needs to solve its problems, and the simplification that follows is not one its members chose. I know the "not chosen" clause is doing a lot of work, mostly to protect Cahokia.

What I want from you:

State the principle — or the two or three rival principles — precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen, and tie each clause to the case that forces it. Then tell me which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and which one I should surrender rather than complicate the principle further; I would rather lose a verdict than end up with a rule that carries an exception for every case. Rule explicitly on the Soviet Union, and on two cases I haven't mentioned that you think would be most diagnostic.

Address a suspicion I can't shake: that *collapse* isn't one question but three — what happened to the population, what happened to the political order, and what happened to the material conditions of ordinary life — and that my inconsistency comes from switching among them without noticing. If that's right, say which question I should keep asking and which I should retire, and say what the subject of the predicate ought to be. States collapse, or settlement systems, or populations, or ways of life?

One more thing, and please don't soften it. I suspect some of my verdicts are set by politics rather than by evidence — I resist *collapse* for the Maya partly because living Maya people reasonably object to being described as collapsed, and I embrace it for the Soviet Union partly because I want the twentieth century's experiments to have failed. Tell me where that kind of motive is doing legitimate work in the concept and where it is merely contaminating my judgments, and whether a principle that can't be separated from the sympathies of the person applying it is worth having at all.
