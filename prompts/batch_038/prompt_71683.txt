I have no academic background in any of this; I arrived at it sideways, through writing software and reading around the subject by myself. What follows is a view I have come to hold about induction, and I should like it examined rather than explained back to me.

The view has two halves. The first is normative: the universal prior over computable hypotheses is not merely one respectable way of setting inductive weights among many, but the correct one — so that a learner whose degrees of belief fail to converge, in the limit of experience, on those of a Solomonoff inductor has not made a different but defensible choice; it has made a mistake. The second is diagnostic: whenever such a learner and the universal prior diverge in practice, the divergence is always traceable to one of two things — the finite computational resources available to the learner, or a mis-specified description of the data stream — and never to the prior itself being wrong about the world.

What would count against this? I should like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I should like to know which of them a committed proponent would themselves concede and which they would dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding — together with an assessment of whether the concessions are cheap ones. Please engage directly with four worries of my own:

First, machine-relativity. Two universal priors dominate one another up to a multiplicative constant, but nothing tells me the constant is small, and every observation I shall ever have is finite. Is the invariance result a genuine answer to this, or does it bite only at a limit no actual inquirer reaches?

Second, the hypothesis class. Consider a case I have been turning over: a stream fixed throughout by an uncomputable fact — say, determined by the answers to halting questions about machines whose indices I have been handed but never permitted to run. No hypothesis I can entertain is true of that stream, yet from the inside it presents only as a model that never settles down. Would such a world refute my view, or is my view simply silent about it — and if silent, is that a limit on the view or a retreat built into it?

Third, the fit with scientific practice. The simplicity that working scientists reward looks like symmetry, a small number of adjustable quantities, and the unified treatment of disparate phenomena, none of which is evidently the same property as having a short description in a fixed language; and a relabelling trick of the grue variety can make an ugly hypothesis short in some language. Does my view absorb this, or is it embarrassed by it?

Fourth, whether the normative half is substantive at all, or is the domination lemma restated with a moral attached to it.

Then tell me what this view predicts that its rivals do not — set against, for instance, a family of inductive methods with tunable smoothness, or an approach that treats domain-specific bias as the essence of good inference rather than as a deviation from an ideal — and give me something I could in principle check. Finally, address the insulation worry head-on: the appeal to the limit and to finite compute appears able to explain away any failure whatsoever. Is the view therefore unfalsifiable, and if it is, is that a defect in an account of this kind or is demanding falsifiability of it a category mistake? I want a straight verdict, not a defence of either side. Where I can, distinguish the defeaters that would show the view false from those that would show it merely idle — unobjectionable, but doing no explanatory work.
