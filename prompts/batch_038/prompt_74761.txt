I recently took a volunteer seat on the ecological review panel of a regional land trust in the upper Midwest, and I'm preparing for my third meeting. Two years ago the trust amended its mission language to say that the welfare of individual wild animals counts alongside biodiversity, which sounds like a small change until you try to apply it. The panel has issued seven written determinations since then, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule we're actually running. We endorsed converting a chronically eutrophic farm pond — one that lost most of its fish to summer oxygen crashes — back into a wet meadow, with the memo noting approvingly that the meadow would support far fewer animals but that those animals would fare better. We endorsed a contraceptive baiting program for an overabundant deer herd, explicitly in preference to a proposed sharpshooting cull. We declined to endorse draining a beaver impoundment that would have eliminated a very large midge and mosquito population and the associated forest dieback, on the grounds that it would also destroy amphibian and mink habitat. We endorsed a prescribed spring burn of a remnant prairie while acknowledging in writing that it would kill large numbers of insects and small mammals outright. We opposed permanent floodlighting along a recreational trail corridor, citing moth mortality and disruption to nesting songbirds. We declined to take any position at all on releasing a biocontrol agent against an invasive defoliating moth, saying the population-level welfare consequences were unknowable. And we endorsed, narrowly and with conditions, the removal of a non-native predatory fish from a lake, specifying that the operation use netting and electrofishing rather than piscicide. I came to this panel from a career in stormwater engineering, so the hydrology is comfortable and the ethics is not; the chair has now asked me to draft the reasoning section of our next determination, which means whatever I recommend effectively becomes our stated principle.

Next Thursday we vote on a proposal that doesn't sit obviously inside any of those grooves. A developer wants to build a distribution facility on 300 acres of currently unprotected seasonal wetland, and as mitigation offers to deed the entire parcel — plus 200 acres of adjacent upland — to the trust in perpetuity, hydrologically regraded so that the marsh drains and is restored to native tallgrass and oak savanna. The consultant's own figures, which I don't think are inflated, project roughly a ninety percent reduction in aquatic invertebrate and amphibian abundance on the parcel, a modest increase in nesting birds and small mammals per acre, and a smaller total number of vertebrate individuals. Unlike the farm pond, this marsh has no anoxic die-offs and no documented fish kills; the seasonal-wetland type is regionally uncommon but not listed or otherwise regulated. The realistic counterfactual, if we refuse the deal, is not that the marsh stays as it is: the parcel is zoned for drainage and row-crop conversion, and the trust would end up holding nothing.

What should I argue for if I want the panel to be consistent with its own record? Which of the seven determinations is the closest analog, and what is actually doing the work in that analogy — the wet-to-dry conversion itself, the developer-driven character of the harm, the mitigation trade, or the comparison to the agricultural counterfactual? My suspicion is that our precedents are jointly compatible with at least three different underlying principles: something like a net-welfare view on which fewer animals is better so long as average welfare does not fall; a harm-reduction view that cares about the intensity and duration of suffering among animals who already exist and is largely indifferent to headcount; and an ecological-integrity view that treats habitat type and trophic function as primary and welfare as a side constraint. There may be a fourth, in which vertebrates are weighted far more heavily than invertebrates and the huge insect numbers barely register. Please tell me what each of those predicts about this proposal, which one best explains the full pattern of our seven determinations — including the awkward ones, such as why we endorsed a burn that kills many animals outright but refused to touch the biocontrol question, and why we preferred contraception to sharpshooting — and then land somewhere with a recommendation and your reasons for it. If the record genuinely underdetermines the vote, say so plainly and tell me what principle we would be committing ourselves to by voting yes and what we would be committing ourselves to by voting no. And name the single further question — the kind I could put to the developer's wetland consultant or answer from our own files before Thursday — that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles.
