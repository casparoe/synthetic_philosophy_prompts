In our master's module on scalable oversight, our convenor has repeatedly defended the AI safety via debate proposal by likening it to an adversarial trial. On his account, two trained models correspond to zealous advocates for opposing parties; the bounded human judge corresponds to a lay tribunal with no specialist knowledge of its own; and the alternating exchange of claims and rebuttals corresponds to examination and cross-examination. He then deploys the comparison to defuse the objection from obfuscated arguments. His reasoning runs as follows: in court, the party who lies must construct and sustain a coherent false edifice, any part of which may be pulled apart under cross-examination, whereas the party who tells the truth need only gesture at the record; so a fact-finder who cannot herself verify highly technical evidence may still be steered towards the truth by the contest between the advocates. By the same token, he argues, a judge who cannot check a fifty-thousand-word argument for a buried flaw will nonetheless be led to the truth by the opposing model, since exposing a flaw is easier than concealing one. The conclusion he draws is that obfuscation is a technical nuisance rather than a structural limit, and that no oversight mechanism beyond the debate itself is required. In seminar last week he reinforced the point after an exercise in which we judged two models debating whether a short program contains an exploitable flaw: several of us voted for the more fluent advocate and were wrong, and he treated our error as a reminder that the judge must be given the right procedural setting rather than better instincts.

I am unpersuaded, and what I want is a rigorous audit of the comparison, not a survey of the debate literature. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what in the courtroom plays the role of the advocates, the judge, the rules of admissibility, the burden of proof, discovery, sanctions for dishonesty, expert witnesses, and appeal — and for each say whether the mapping holds exactly, approximately, or fails altogether in the AI case. Then identify the disanalogies and sort them into those that bear on the inference my convenor is making and those that are merely decorative. I am particularly interested in whether the courtroom's truth-tracking depends on features the debate set-up lacks: compulsory powers to obtain evidence independently of the advocates, penalties that make lying costly, the fact-finder's freedom to defer, reserve judgement, or appoint an expert of her own choosing, an essentially unbounded budget of time and money, and a shared stock of lay common sense about the kinds of facts typically in dispute. Please also test the reverse worry, that the analogy once taken seriously cuts the other way: legal systems plainly track resources rather than truth when one party is vastly better funded than the other, or when the expert evidence is contested and unverifiable by the tribunal — which is a fair description of the situation the obfuscation objection posits, especially where the obfuscating model is stronger than its opponent. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, whether any better analogy is available for a situation in which verifying a claim is far harder than producing a convincing one. The candidates I have come up with are refereeing or peer review, a used-car market in which sellers know more than buyers, and the relation between finding a proof and checking it; say which of these, if any, carries the relevant structure, and what each of them in turn conceals.
