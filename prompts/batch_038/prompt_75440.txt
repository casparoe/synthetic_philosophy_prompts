For roughly three years a small group of us has been running a persistent Nomic — Peter Suber's game, in which amending the rules is itself a move, first published in Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas column — as an asynchronous forum game, using a lightly modified descendant of Suber's original ruleset, including his distinction between immutable rules, which must be transmuted before they can be altered at all, and mutable ones. We are now at somewhere around one hundred and eighty live rules and two dynasty resets. More interesting than the rule count is that our recurring disputes never actually resolve. I am now fairly confident the players are not disagreeing about the rules; they are running different background models of what a rulebook's authority consists in, and the surface argument about any particular proposal is downstream of that. Two of us are lawyers, one insists the entire question is ill-posed, and I suspect I have been arguing past all of them for years. For the next meetup I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts people by the model they actually hold.

Please write a decision tree of eight branch questions, each posed as a concrete proposal situation in our game with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Every question must be answerable by someone who has played for a year but has never read legal philosophy, and none of them may simply ask which view the reader finds attractive — the whole point is that our players would attach different labels to themselves than their answers imply. The situations I want used, in roughly this order: (1) a proposal amending the amendment rule itself to forbid any further amendment of any rule, carried by the required majority; (2) a single proposal that both transmutes an immutable rule and then repeals it, versus the same two changes brought as separate proposals a turn apart, and whether packaging matters; (3) a rule providing that one player's voting weight may not be reduced without that player's own consent — an analogue of the Article V proviso that no state, without its consent, shall be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate — and whether such a clause binds from inside the game or only because players keep voluntarily honoring it; (4) an amendment that passes and conflicts with a rule nobody repealed, in a system with no consistency requirement: does the later text prevail, is the amendment void, or do both stand until someone moves to reconcile them; (5) a proposal declaring that an amendment carried three weeks ago was void from the outset and retroactively adjusting scores; (6) a proposal stripping the named arbiter of validity questions of their authority, after that arbiter has ruled the very proposal invalid; (7) a player's objection that the whole rulebook is void because the founding vote was taken when there were yet no rules to authorize it; (8) a proposal to dissolve the game outright, and whether a successful dissolution is an exercise of the rules or an exit from them.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first lay out the axes your questions are probing — something along the lines of whether a rule can bind its own future amendment, whether validity is a matter of pedigree or of expected acceptance, whether the system tolerates live contradictions, and whether the authority of the rules bottoms out inside the rules or in something presupposed from outside — and only then locate positions within it. The leaves should include at minimum: a continuing view on which the present majority can always do whatever it likes and entrenchment clauses are merely persuasive text; a self-embracing view on which a rule can genuinely bind later amendments, permanently if it says so; an acceptance view on which validity just is a prediction about what the players will keep treating as authoritative; a hierarchical or basic-norm view on which the rules derive their force from a presupposed top-level norm that cannot be amended, only abandoned; a reform view that takes the paradoxes to be real defects of drafting practice and prescribes explicit tiers and explicit conflict rules; and a deflationary view on which there is no deep question, only bookkeeping once a tie-break protocol is fixed. Where the logical space permits a combination nobody in our group occupies, say so and diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your eight questions cannot separate; I suspect the continuing and deflationary views coincide on every in-game case and diverge only on the meta-question, and I want to know what further question would tell them apart. Please do not include a reading list, bibliography, or citations, and do not invent article titles or dates: if a leaf corresponds to a position with a standard name in jurisprudence or in the literature on self-amendment, name it in passing and move on. Roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, with the questions and scoring key in a format I could hand out and score with a pencil.
