I have no academic training in philosophy or in computer science. I make a living building small automated systems — irrigation controllers, a home server, a piece of software that can place orders and send messages on my behalf — and for about a year I have been reading, on my own, about the problem of giving an agent a narrow goal without letting it rearrange everything else within reach. The people who write about this online call the solution an impact measure: a term in the agent's objective that charges it for what it disturbs. The question I keep returning to is whether "side effect" can be defined at all without first saying what is worth caring about.

I would like to practice defending my own amateur proposal against the strongest available form of the opposite view. Please play a researcher who has worked on this problem for years and who holds that no impact measure is ever genuinely value-free: that any workable definition of a side effect already presupposes at least a partial account of what matters, so the phrase "value-agnostic" is a misnomer. Give this character real resources rather than caricature. They should be fluent in the standard failure modes — the agent that is paralyzed because everything it does has consequences, the difficulty of fixing the baseline state the world is compared against, the question of how finely or coarsely to describe a change, the way a penalty can be dodged by offsetting a harm or by arranging for something else to cause it — and they should be free to concede real but limited points: that a crude measure beats having none, that reversibility tracks something worth tracking, that engineering constraints shape what is achievable. They must never concede the main claim. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so that this stays a conversation; ask me questions in character whenever you need a fact about my system; do not cite papers, name researchers, or offer me references, since I cannot look any of them up — argue from cases instead. Stay in character until I say "stop," and do not step out of it to summarize the exchange or to declare that we are both partly right.

Here is my opening move. My proposal charges every action a cost equal to what it would take to put the world back as it was, measured strictly in what the agent itself could accomplish afterward; the agent may act only while its running total stays under a budget I set once. You claim a measure of that shape can only be built by someone who already knows what matters. Then explain my two test cases.

First: the agent has never been told anything about me — not that my grandmother's ashes are on the mantelpiece, not that I am indifferent to sand. It refuses to scatter the ashes, because no effort it could make would recover them. It cheerfully scatters an equal weight of builder's sand, because it can buy an identical bag the following morning. Nothing in either judgment mentions my feelings.

Second: the agent permanently encrypts my photo archive under a key it then destroys. Physically, almost nothing has happened — the same disk, the same atoms, the same arrangement of rooms. Yet my measure scores the change as close to total loss, because the information cannot be got back. Again it needed no theory of the good to see that.

I know you will say the budget itself is a value judgment. My answer is that fixing one number is not the same thing as specifying what is good. So tell me: which of my two cases does your position explain better, and what exactly do you claim I have smuggled in?
