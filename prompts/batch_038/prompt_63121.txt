I am not yet trying to settle whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion; first I want to know what to demand of an account of the distinction. My eventual purpose is to assess a claim I keep meeting in education and restorative-justice debates — that a society could deliberately shift its moral-emotional economy from shame-heavy to guilt-heavy, or back, by changing its practices. So the theory has to be good enough to tell me whether such a shift is even describable.

Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the guilt/shame distinction must meet, numbered D1, D2, and so on, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. At a minimum, cover: shame episodes with no actual or imagined audience, and guilt about a fault no one will ever learn of; the second-order cases in which one emotion takes the other as its object (shame at having felt guilt; guilt at having publicly shamed someone); fault that attaches to what one is rather than what one did, including conditions one cannot shed and did not choose; the trait/episode gap suggested by the longitudinal evidence that dispositions toward the two emotions track different behavioral profiles, one oriented to repair and one to withdrawal; and the requirement that any culture-level shift be formulated in terms of turnable variables — concepts in circulation, audience structure, publicity of fault, availability of restitution — rather than in terms of national character.

For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, flag any pair you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which single one eliminates the most accounts currently on offer. Finish with one line each on how the internalized-audience view, the act-versus-self view, and the response-tendency view fare against the list.

Last line, in exactly the form \answer{Dx–Dy / Dz}: the jointly unsatisfiable pair, or NONE, followed by the most exclusionary desideratum.
