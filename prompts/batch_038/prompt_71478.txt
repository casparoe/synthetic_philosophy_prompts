And here's the student's answer, unedited apart from the spelling:

"Dennett and Kinsbourne distinguish two ways of explaining reports like the ones you get in color phi. On an Orwellian revision, the subject really does have the experience as it first seems to be, and the memory of it gets rewritten afterwards, in the light of what happens next — like the records being altered in Nineteen Eighty-Four. On a Stalinesque revision, the material is edited before it ever reaches consciousness, so the subject never has the unrevised experience at all: what gets shown is already the final cut, like the show trials where the verdict was decided before anyone spoke. The two are supposed to be empirically indistinguishable, since a subject who is having an experience and then misremembering it can't be told apart, from the inside or the outside, from a subject who never had it.

In color phi two dots of different colours are flashed in quick succession a few degrees apart, and subjects report a single dot travelling across and changing colour partway along the path. The problem is that at the moment when the colour change would have to be experienced, the second dot has not been presented yet, so nothing in the system at that moment carries the information that the colour is going to change. A faithful real-time playback of the sequence therefore cannot produce the report. So the report must be composed after the second dot arrives, with the colour change written back into the middle of the path. That is the Stalinesque option, and it is the one the data favours.

The Cartesian theatre objection seems to me to be aimed mainly at the Stalinesque account, because that is the one that needs a definite moment at which the finished reel is shown to the subject. The Orwellian account does not have that problem: on it the experience happens when it happens, at the natural time, and the revision is downstream in memory, so there is no place where content has to be assembled and presented all at once. That is why I think the multiple drafts model is best read as a generalisation of the Orwellian side of the distinction, with lots of little revisions happening to memories instead of one big one.

The same distinction does some work on backward masking. If a target is followed quickly enough by a mask, the target becomes unreportable; the Orwellian reading is that it was seen and then overwritten, the Stalinesque reading is that its content was never completed. Since there is no observable difference between the two readings there either, I take it that Dennett and Kinsbourne conclude we cannot settle which is right. The honest position is agnosticism about when the colour change was experienced: there is a determinate fact of the matter, it is just not accessible either from the report or from the neurophysiology."
