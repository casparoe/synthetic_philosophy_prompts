The question was:

"Explain how hyperbolic discounting is supposed to account for akrasia. Does it vindicate or undermine Aristotle's claim that the akratic agent acts against her own better judgement?"

The student's answer:

Hyperbolic discounting says that we discount the future more steeply than exponential models assume, so the value of a later reward falls off fast as it gets pushed away. Because the discount function is hyperbolic rather than exponential, the ranking of a sooner-smaller reward against a later-larger one can flip depending on how close we are: from a distance the agent prefers the later-larger reward, but when the sooner-smaller one is right in front of her the preference reverses. That's the structural account of akrasia — the agent isn't confused about what's best, she's just time-inconsistent, so she can sincerely hold a standing preference and still act against it when the moment comes. The obvious response is precommitment, Ulysses and the mast style.

As for Aristotle: he denied that anyone can act against what they think best, holding that what looks like akrasia is really just ignorance of the good, and the empirical model confirms his point from the other direction. More generally, once you can explain the reversal descriptively the akratic act stops looking irrational — at the moment of choice she's doing exactly what her current preferences tell her to do, which is all rationality requires.
