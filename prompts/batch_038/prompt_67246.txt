I should say at the outset that I am a software engineer and have never studied philosophy formally, so please bear with me if some of what follows is muddled. Two things collided over the Christmas break. The first was a long article about the birth of Louise Brown at Oldham General Hospital in July 1978 — the first child conceived in vitro — and about the strange, decades-long road that led to it. The second was a paper a friend of mine, who is doing an MA in ethics, sent me after I complained that most of the moral reasoning I encounter at work consists of guessing at consequences and then dressing the guess up as an estimate: James Lenman's "Consequentialism and Cluelessness", in Philosophy & Public Affairs. I read it twice and found the argument compelling but oddly bloodless, and I have become convinced that the way to test it is to run it against a real decision taken by real people who knew perfectly well that they were operating blind. The Oldham birth looks to me like an unusually good candidate, and I should like a careful analysis of that episode through the lens of the cluelessness objection, running in both directions.

Here are the facts as I understand them; please correct me where I have gone wrong, but do not let the analysis turn on any detail I may have misremembered. Robert Edwards at Cambridge and Patrick Steptoe, a gynaecologist from Oldham, had been working towards human in vitro fertilisation since the late 1960s, together with the embryologist Jean Purdy, whose contribution went largely unacknowledged for years. The Medical Research Council refused to fund the clinical work, there was public hostility, and there was legal action. In November 1977 an embryo was transferred to Lesley Brown, who had blocked fallopian tubes and had been trying to conceive for nine years, and the couple were not told, so far as I can gather, that no previous attempt had produced a baby. Louise Brown was delivered by caesarean section just before midnight on 25 July 1978. Edwards, Steptoe and Purdy then set up the clinic at Bourn Hall, which became the template for a worldwide industry; Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in 2010, to the audible displeasure of the Vatican, having earlier had to field remarks from senior churchmen about women being turned into baby factories. It is the cascade afterwards that interests me: the committee of inquiry in the mid-1980s and the fourteen-day limit it recommended, the statute and the regulator that followed at the end of that decade, the injection of single sperm into eggs, the testing of embryos before transfer, the commercial freezing of eggs, the clinics now advertising on the London Underground, the approval of mitochondrial donation techniques in 2015, and — further out still — the arguments about editing the human germline, of which nobody in Oldham in 1977 could have had the faintest concept. Several million children have now been born this way. My impression is that almost none of the later items were knowable in advance even in principle, as opposed to merely unknown in fact.

What I want is the mapping done properly before any verdict is pronounced. Who exactly is the agent under the consequentialist description — Edwards, Steptoe, the two of them jointly, Purdy, the Browns themselves, the hospital, or the Medical Research Council, whose decision not to fund being as much a decision as any of the others? What were the live options at each decision point (attempt the transfer in 1977, or wait for a successful animal model or a prior live birth; publicise the birth or keep quiet; found a clinic or hand the technique to others), and what would the calculation have looked like using only the information genuinely available in 1977 rather than what we now know? Then bring the objection to bear: on Lenman's account, does the case show that the consequentialist calculation was empty at the time, or only that it was extremely difficult? At this point I want to press two distinctions of my own, which I do not believe come from the literature and which may be confused; tell me plainly if they are. The first is between cluelessness that better inquiry would have cured and cluelessness that no amount of inquiry in 1977 could have cured, because the facts to be discovered did not yet exist: the health of the first IVF babies seems to belong to the first kind, the global fertility market and germline editing to the second. The second is borrowed from the way we do risk work, where a register separates what you priced, what you knew you could not price, and what never occurred to anybody to write down; my suspicion is that the objection only bites on the last of these, and only on its incurable subset. If that is right, is the objection still worth making, or has it been narrowed until it is harmless — since a consequentialist can simply reply that one ought to act on the evidence one can obtain and treat the unknowable long run as a wash? Related to this: does the fact that the outcome turned out magnificently vindicate the decision, or is that pure moral luck, and how would the view handle a decision in 1977 identical in every respect except that it produced a badly harmed child? I am also curious whether what Edwards and Steptoe said in their own defence — the identifiable couple in front of them and the suffering they could relieve — is the retreat Lenman recommends, a permission the theory can accommodate, or evidence that the theory is being aimed at people who were never attempting to do what it says they should. Where the framework has nothing to say about the episode, I would much rather you said so than manufactured a verdict. One last point about form: I should like the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no numbered lists and no tables, and with no reading list or citations, since I cannot chase them up and would rather have your argument than your bibliography.
