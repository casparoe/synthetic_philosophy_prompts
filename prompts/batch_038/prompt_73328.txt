For the past year I have been working as the standards editor at a mid-sized regional daily, which means two things land on my desk every week: the corrections column, and the copy for the fact-checking box we run in partnership with a larger outlet whose Truth-O-Meter assigns one of six labels to a politician's statement — True, Mostly True, Half True, Mostly False, False, and Pants on Fire, the last of which is reserved not merely for statements that fail but for failures that are, in the outlet's own framing, egregious or absurd. Alongside that we follow the near-universal newsroom rule that we check statements of fact and never opinions; a speaker's value judgment is simply declared out of scope before anyone asks whether it is true. I mention all of this because I came to the job with a graduate seminar on theories of truth behind me, and I keep failing to make the practice hang together philosophically, while my colleagues insist we are not in the business of metaphysics at all.

The thing that will not let go of me is that the newsroom seems to operate with three different and possibly incompatible notions of truth at once. The fact-check scale treats truth as graded, something a single claim can have more or less of, and its top label appears to smuggle in a fact about the speaker's state of mind — that they knew, or ought to have known, that the claim was absurd — so that the rating is not purely semantic at all. The corrections column, by contrast, is resolutely binary: a sentence either misstated something or it did not, and the correction names the misstated item and replaces it. And then there is the clarification, which most large newsrooms distinguish from a correction precisely on the grounds that nothing in the piece was inaccurate, but the phrasing invited a false inference, or left out something that would have changed how a reader took it. So we publicly hold that a statement can be wholly true and yet journalistically defective, that a statement can be half true, and that a statement can be false-and-culpable rather than merely false. Meanwhile the fact/opinion filter assumes some sentences are truth-apt and others are not, which is itself a substantive claim about language that nobody here would think of defending.

What I want is a reconstruction of the theory of truth this practice actually presupposes, as far as it has one. Take the deflationary family seriously as the candidate: redundancy-style views, disquotationalism, minimalism on which the whole content of the truth predicate is captured by the equivalence schema, and prosentential accounts. Can a deflationist make sense of a truth predicate that comes in degrees, or must Half True be reanalysed as something other than a degree of truth — as a claim about how much of a compound assertion survives checking, or about how much relevant information was omitted, or about the reader's likely inference rather than the sentence's standing? Is the practice committed to something like correspondence, or would it be equally licensed by several rival accounts, in which case I want to know exactly which features of the practice do the discriminating and which are underdetermined. Please distinguish sharply between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me where its parts genuinely contradict one another rather than merely looking odd side by side. Finally: if our newsroom adopted an avowedly deflationary account as official doctrine, what would have to change on the page — would the scale collapse, would Pants on Fire have to be renamed or split in two, would clarifications become unintelligible or become the only category left? I am not after a reading list or citations; give me the argument. And where you can, mark the difference between what is settled among people who work on truth and what is live and contested, and do not present a deflationist or an anti-deflationist line as the consensus if it is not.
