I am seventeen and, for the past year, I have run a peer accountability program at my high school. Twenty-six students take part: each writes one academic or personal goal on a card at the start of a six-week cycle, meets me for a ten-minute check-in every Thursday, and forfeits a ten-dollar deposit if they miss two consecutive check-ins or abandon the goal without my agreeing that the abandonment was legitimate. A faculty advisor signs off only when I propose enforcing a forfeiture. Beyond that, I have no authority to compel anyone to do anything except run the program and exclude people from it.

The hard part is not tracking compliance. It is deciding, every Thursday, whether a member who has stopped doing the thing has actually changed their mind about whether it was worth doing, or has simply run out of motivation in a way they would not endorse if I asked them in June. My current practice is to accept whatever explanation sounds sincere, and I have come to think this is nearly worthless: the explanations shift from week to week in a pattern I can describe but cannot name, and I suspect I have been letting people off for wanting something now rather than for having learned something new.

Please write the decision procedure I should run at each check-in, built out of whatever the philosophy of weakness of will and the behavioral economics of time-inconsistent preference actually offer that is usable. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in legitimate revision and where it terminates in discounting, and where it stops and hands the case to a person because the question is one of value rather than fact. It has to be runnable in ten minutes by someone with no training, and it has to work through conversation alone, since I cannot observe my members between Thursdays. I do not want a reading list or a survey of positions; I want the machinery.

Be explicit about which distinctions cannot be dropped in the simplification — in particular, whether a change of judgment and a change of motivation can be told apart from the inside, by asking, at all — and about which of my existing rules the procedure will have to break. I expect at least one of them to be the forfeiture, and I would rather be told that plainly than have the procedure quietly accommodate it.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a member in week three of a six-week pledge to run at six every morning, who tells me the goal was really her mother's and that she has now realized this. Second, a member who has replaced his goal four times in eleven weeks, each substitution two or three days before a check-in, each time for reasons that sound good on their own. Third, me: I spent the summer building software to automate our club's record-keeping, got roughly forty percent of the way through, have not touched it since August, and have been telling myself a version of the explanation in the first case.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I already suspect that it cannot distinguish a goal that is genuinely no longer worth pursuing from one that has merely become expensive in the moment; that it cannot tell me whether the person who wrote the card in September has any authority over the person sitting across from me in November, rather than assuming it; and that it has no business screening for conditions that belong to a physician rather than to a student. I want to know where else it fails, how often, in which direction the errors fall, and what I should do on those Thursdays instead.
