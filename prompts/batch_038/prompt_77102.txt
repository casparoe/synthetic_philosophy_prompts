I'm drafting the framing chapter for a collection on infinity, and I've spent about a page saying where I stand on supertasks so the other contributors know what they're arguing with. My co-editor read it, went "hmm", and wouldn't say what he'd found. So: does this package hang together, or is there something in it I'm blind to? As it currently stands —

1. On Thomson's lamp I'm with Benacerraf. Flicking the thing on and off through a halving sequence for two minutes simply doesn't settle whether it's on or off at the end, and that's not a contradiction, it's an underdetermined bit of fiction. Thomson's argument for impossibility doesn't go through.

2. But I don't want to stop there, because I do think the lamp story describes something impossible. My reason is a determinacy principle: whatever state a thing is in at a moment has to be fixed by what occured before it. Nothing gets to arrive at a limit moment unmade, as it were. So the gap in 1 isn't a merely verbal shortfall — no genuine possible world could contain it, which is why the story doesn't describe one.

3. The tennis-ball case (ten in, one out, at each halving stage, run to the limit) I say the vase is empty at the end, because every ball that went in was labelled and eventually taken back out. I'm unmoved by the objection that the number inside is growing without bound, so it had better be infinite at the end.

4. Zeno's runner was never a paradox. Get the reals and Achilles's position at the finish is just the limit of the sequence of his positions; the geometric series converges; done. Anyone who thinks there's leftover metaphysical work there is confusing mathematics with metaphysics.

5. Plain vanilla analysis, to be clear. No hyperreals, no infinitesimals as genuine quantities, no nonstandard models doing quiet work in the background.

6. The Grim Reaper style cases I do take seriously. Reaper n is set to strike at 1/2^n minutes past noon, but only if the victim is still alive; so no single reaper can ever get its blow in, and yet the poor chap is dead at any time after noon. I read that as evidence for causal finitism — no actually infinite stack of causally operative things — and I'm half-tempted to add no beginningless past on top.

7. And the big moral I want to defend is Thomson's after all: you can't complete a task that calls for infinitely many successive acts. That's definately the thesis; the lamp is one exhibit among several.

8. Methodologically, I won't count a paradox as solved if solving it needs exotic kit — extra set theory, nonstandard models, that sort of thing. If it only dissolves once you've bought something heavy, I don't count it dissolved.

Audit it, please. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do on a quick read? I'd like the real ones ranked, worst first — tell me about the fatal one rather than hand me six mild quibbles — and for each, what would have to be dropped or reworded and what I'd lose by it. I'm not wedded to any single item, but equally I don't want to be told to bin the lot if a small edit would see it right.
