I help run a tiny podcast about how machines try to figure out what people want — a couple hundred listeners, nobody on the team has a degree in anything relevant, we just read alot and argue in a group chat. Next week I'm interviewing a researcher who works on inverse reinforcement learning, the setup where you watch somebody's choices and try to back out the reward function they're apparently maximizing. I've listened to her talks twice and I think I can state her position, tho what I actually want are questions that put the position under pressure instead of letting her give the talk a third time.

Here's her view as I understand it — and please correct me in your answer if I've got it wrong, because half my questions depend on this being right:

She holds that observed behavior is essentially the only honest evidence we have about what a person values, so anything a person *says* they want gets treated as just more behavior to be explained rather than as data that can override the inference. And when behavior doesn't look like competent reward-maximizing — someone keeps picking the thing they say they regret — her explanation is planning noise plus a limited ability to see ahead, not a sign the inferred reward was wrong. Her go-to line when someone pushes on this is some version of "well then that just isn't the preference they think it is." To me that sounds like a net that catches every possible counterexample, and that's the thing I want to go at.

Give me eight to ten questions. For each one:

- the question itself, phrased so a listener with no technical background can follow it, but don't water down the content — I'd much rather explain a hard term live on air than ask something vague
- one line on the answer she's most likely to give
- a follow-up keyed to that specific answer, not a generic "but why do you say that?"

Make sure at least one question lands on each of these, since they're the ones I keep circling back to:

1. When someone doesn't get the outcome they were going for, you can't tell from outside whether they didn't want it enough or they just had a false belief about how to get it — they wanted the coffee perfectly well, they thought the beans were in the other cupboard. Ask her how more data is supposed to break that tie, given that a mistaken belief and a low-weight reward produce very similar traces.
2. There's a knob in these models for how noisy or random the choice rule is, and as far as I can tell you can trade reward magnitude against that knob: a big reward pursued sloppily looks exactly like a small reward pursued carefully. Ask her what's supposed to pin down which of the two we're seeing.
3. If the reward gets inferred as basically fixed across a long stretch of behavior, what happens to people whose options were narrowed early — someone who stopped applying for things a decade ago. Do we end up reading resignation as contentment, and does she consider that a fixable flaw in the method or a limitation she's at peace with?
4. Once you've recovered what people actually pursue, a good chunk of it is stuff nobody would want a system helping them with — spite, addiction, keeping up appearances in front of strangers. Ask her whether recovering it is even the goal, and where in her story an "is" is supposed to become an "ought."
5. Close on the hardest one: a question that makes her say what observation, if any, would get her to give up this model of human preference. If her answer turns out to be that nothing would, I want the follow-up that leaves her sitting in that admission rather than moving on.

Skip anything in the shape of "what got you interested in this work" — I have eleven minutes of airtime and I don't want three of them gone on it.

Make the very last line of your answer exactly: Verdict: Q[number] — [the reason it's the question most likely to force a real concession, ten words or fewer]
