I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a public ideas festival has booked me for thirty-five minutes with slides in front of roughly a hundred non-specialists — mostly retirees, some undergraduates, no background in philosophy or vision science. The listing they published promises the big claim: Why You Never See What's There. What I actually want to teach is the narrower, live question underneath it — whether what we believe, expect, or want can get inside perceptual experience itself, as distinct from steering attention, changing what we say we saw, or changing what we later remember. My session runs immediately after a retired detective's talk on eyewitness error, so a good part of the room will arrive already convinced that seeing is unreliable, and will take that conviction to be the same thesis I'm about to present.

Plan the session for me; don't script it and don't argue the debate at me. I need five things.

First, the dependency order: what has to be in place before the question is even intelligible to this audience. I assume that includes getting them to separate what someone experiences from what they report about the experience, and I want to know where that move belongs and what collapses if I make it too early.

Second, the one case to carry everything. My shortlist is an image in which two patches printed at exactly the same luminance look like different shades; the mid-century card experiments in which people shown a spade printed in red named an ordinary black spade, or a heart; and the value-laden size-and-steepness research from my own beat, which I covered at the time and which has since been picked apart. Tell me which one to build on, what that case must have and must lack to do the work, and what I lose by dropping the other two.

Third, the misconceptions and exactly where in the sequence to head each off — mine so far: that "top-down" is just another name for penetration; that attention counts as a counterexample; that ordinary size and brightness illusions settle the matter; that the dress which split the internet settles it; and that if penetration is real, no eyewitness can be trusted. Flag which of these arrive with the audience and which my own example will manufacture.

Fourth, one question to put to the room near the end whose answers would show me they have the experience-versus-judgment distinction rather than just the pictures.

Fifth, what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level — I expect the apparatus for ruling out response bias and demand characteristics — and what to say to the room about having cut it.
