THE QUESTION AS SET: Explain the argument from illusion, and say how the disjunctivist about perception responds to it. Then say whether the disjunctivist owes any further account of hallucination, and if so what shape that account can take. Finally, take this case: Nadia's visual cortex is stimulated by a neuroscientist so that she has an experience as of a yellow umbrella on her desk, while there is no umbrella anywhere near her. Afterwards she reports that her experience was exactly like the one she would have had if a yellow umbrella had been sitting there. Must the disjunctivist concede that Nadia's experience and the umbrella-owner's share a mental state? Why or why not? (Week 5 covers this; the two extracts on the reading list are Hinton and McDowell, but you don't need to know them by heart.)

THE CANDIDATE'S SCRIPT: The argument from illusion starts from cases like a straight stick half-submerged in water, which looks bent even though it isn't. In such a case something is present to the subject which really has the property of bentness, but no physical object in the vicinity is bent. So what is directly present to the subject can't be the stick itself; it has to be something of a different sort, a sense-datum, which genuinely has the property the experience presents. The real trouble for the direct realist is that there's no principled place to stop: a sufficiently clever illusion is undetectable from the inside, so the sense-datum analysis has to be generalised to every case of perception. We never perceive the stick, only the sense-data it causes.

Disjunctivism refuses that generalisation. Saying "either I see the stick or I don't" doesn't license the inference that there is one kind of mental state, a having-of-an-appearance-as-of-bentness, common to both sides of the disjunction. On the disjunctive account the veridical situation and the non-veridical one are two fundamentally different kinds of thing which happen to be told apart only from the outside, and all they share is that the same description fits them. So the disjunctivist, like the intentionalist, holds that the content of the experience does the explanatory work: in the good case the experience is about the actual stick and its straightness, in the bad case it is about a stick that isn't there. Since contents can fail to be satisfied, there's no pressure to posit sense-data at all, and the argument from illusion is blocked right at its first step. That's also why McDowell can say the subject's warrant in the good case is factive: she's in a position to know the stick is straight, and nothing of that sort is available to someone in the bad case.

On the case, the stimulation produces an experience which is not of a kind with the umbrella-owner's, so no, the disjunctivist doesn't have to concede a shared mental state. In fact she has to resist Nadia's report. Nadia says her experience was exactly like the one she'd have had with a real umbrella, but she can't be right about that: she's never had both experiences side by side to compare, and if the two states really were alike in the respect that matters they would be states of the same kind, which is precisely what the disjunctivist denies. So the report should be read as Nadia being disposed to say the same thing in both situations, not as evidence that the situations have a phenomenal property in common.

What's left is hallucination, and here the argument from illusion does the disjunctivist no damage whatever, because the illusion cases turn out to be hallucination cases in disguise: a stick that looks bent is a case in which nothing is presented to the subject at all in the way she takes it to be. So the two can be handled together, and the only extra thing owed is an explanation of why hallucinations seem to have a positive character when they in fact have none. Since the view rejects the common factor, it rejects that the bad case has a character of its own, and that is the whole answer to the second part.
