been chewing on this since a LessWrong comment thread derailed into political obligation and I still can't tell if it's a good argument or a trap I built for myself. It starts as the old Hume move against tacit consent — the poor man who can't leave — and then escalates into a general refutation of voluntarism, which is the part that scares me:

(1) An obligation binds you only if you could have declined it without giving up something you were already entitled to.
(2) Declining what the state provides — courts, roads, defense — means leaving, and leaving costs you your home, your family, your livelihood.
(3) Those are things you're already entitled to, so nobody can decline the state's benefits at acceptable cost.
(4) So the state's benefits generate no obligation to obey. Fine so far, that's just philosophical anarchism.
(5) But run the identical reasoning on any scheme you didn't opt into. Your parents fed and housed you; declining that as a child would have cost you everything you were entitled to.
(6) So on (1) there are no filial obligations either. Same goes for the well somebody dug before you were born, the union your grandfather joined, the shared wifi in the building.
(7) An argument that dissolves gratitude to your parents has proved too much, and the only premise that could be doing the damage is (1).
(8) So (1) is false: some obligations bind us even when declining them isn't a live option.
(9) Which means the anarchist can't get off the ground on voluntarism, and we're pushed back toward something like fair play — the Nozick-with-a-public-address-system problem notwithstanding.

I'd much rather keep (4) than (8); I'm not ready to hand over political obligation. But something clearly slips between (6) and (8) and I can't name it. Which step do you reject, why did it look innocent when I wrote it down, and what does each of the available rejections cost? Include the option of just biting the bullet on (6), if that's cheaper than it sounds.
