I sit as the public member — the only one without a clinical background — on the disciplinary panel of my state's Board of Examiners for Marriage and Family Therapists, and next Wednesday we hear a matter that has split the seven of us three ways. The licensee, whom I'll call M.R., has practiced here for eleven years with no prior complaint. What triggered this one was a feature story in our local paper: it emerged that M.R. writes and self-publishes erotic fiction under a pen name, all of it involving lawful adult scenarios, and that M.R. lives in an openly non-monogamous household with two consenting adult partners and had discussed that arrangement on a panel at the public library. A parents' association filed the complaint under two provisions of our rules: one requiring licensees to be of good moral character, and one prohibiting conduct that impairs or tends to impair public confidence in the profession. Nothing in the file alleges a boundary violation, a dual relationship, deception of a client, or any clinical error. One complainant, however, states that her adult daughter terminated therapy with M.R. the week the story ran. Three members want dismissal outright; two want a formal reprimand plus a consent order requiring M.R. to cease publishing under the pen name and to stop appearing at public events in a way that identifies the practice with the household arrangement; the chair, who abstains from voting but sets the agenda, has floated a middle course I find unsatisfying — dismiss the complaint on the ground that the court of public opinion has already imposed enough, given the lost referrals, the picketers outside the office, and the exposure of M.R.'s family to local notoriety, so the Board need not add anything. I've been reading Bentham on this question, mainly the essay he wrote in the mid-1780s on sexual offences and left unpublished in his lifetime, together with the earlier treatise where he lays out the principle of utility, the ascetic principle, the four sanctions, and the cases in which punishment is groundless because it is inefficacious, unnecessary, mis-seated, or unprofitable. I want the memo to work in that idiom rather than in the language of rights and dignity that dominates our hearings, because the idiom is what makes the disagreement arguable: it forces anyone invoking public confidence to name the assignable pains they are tracking.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the panel before Wednesday. It should reach a concrete disposition — dismiss, reprimand, conditions, or something else — and it should say what the Board should do, in what order, and on what timetable. Three things I need it to handle carefully. First, the chair's middle course: my worry is that declining to sanction because the moral sanction has already operated is not leniency at all but an endorsement of a punishment nobody calibrated, one that falls on the discovered rather than on the harmful, on the conspicuous rather than on the culpable, and that is indifferent to whether any mischief occurred. If that is right, the memo should say so plainly and explain what follows for our disposition; if it is wrong, and there is a defensible Benthamite sense in which an institution may stand aside once social disapproval has done the work, the memo should say what that sense is and what its limits would be. Second, the strongest case against M.R.'s position, which two members make and which I do not think is a mask for disgust: this profession works only because clients disclose intimate things, and disclosure depends on a standing presumption of discretion and shared norms, so a licensee who has become locally notorious for their private sexual arrangements may genuinely impair the instrument through which the work is done, and the Board's duty runs to clients and not to the licensee's reputation. The single reported termination is evidence on that side, and the memo should take it seriously rather than explaining it away — while also saying what would count as evidence of the general claim, what the Board would have to know that it does not know, and whether a sanction would enlarge the very publicity that caused the harm. Third, the clause itself: the panel cannot rewrite the rules, but we can recommend, and the memo should include a short recommendation to the full Board about what a Benthamite would substitute for a bare public-confidence standard — a nexus requirement, perhaps, tying any disqualification to an identifiable interference with the licensee's function — together with the strongest objection to changing the standard now rather than letting it fall into disuse.

Write it as a document the panel could act on, roughly a thousand words, with the disposition stated at the top and the reasoning beneath it. Assume the other members have never read Bentham: gloss each technical term in a clause as you use it, and do not build the argument on any quotation or on any claim about what Bentham wrote that you cannot state from memory of his framework — I would rather have the reasoning reconstructed in his terms than attributed to him in detail. If you think my own instinct is wrong somewhere, say so in the memo rather than accommodating it; I would rather hand my colleagues a dissent than a confirmation.
