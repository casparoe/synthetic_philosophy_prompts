I'm putting together a seminar session on the EU's democratic deficit and I want a case analysis of autumn 2011 rather than the general argument. Facts I'm taking as given, so you don't have to: in August 2011 Trichet and Draghi sent the Italian government a confidential letter, later leaked to the Italian press, pressing for privatisations, labour-market reform and a balanced-budget rule while the ECB resumed buying Italian paper; at the Cannes summit at the end of October Papandreou announced a referendum on the second Greek bailout and was talked out of it within days; that November he was replaced by Papademos, a former ECB vice-president, and Berlusconi by Monti, a former Competition commissioner who'd been made a life senator the week before.

Map the episode onto the accounts people actually use -- input vs output legitimacy, Majone's regulatory state, the no-demos thesis, Weiler's defence of the status quo -- and say which one bites and which one just doesn't apply here. Then the part I actually care about: the pressure came from national executives (Merkozy) and from a central bank, not from the Commission or the Parliament, so the actors doing the pushing are themselves electorally accountable -- just not to Italians or Greeks. Does the democratic-deficit concept have room for that, or is it a configuration it was never built to describe? And does the case suggest the concept was designed to criticize rule-making by unelected agencies rather than the formation and removal of governments -- meaning it goes silent here instead of being vindicated? Be honest about where the theory has nothing to say about, e.g., the referendum being withdrawn under threat rather than banned.
