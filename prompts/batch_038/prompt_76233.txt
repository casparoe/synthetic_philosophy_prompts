For about six years I have been attending a Thursday-evening history circle at our public library. It is led by a woman in her late seventies — I will call her Ruth — who taught high school social studies for thirty years and has no patience for either optimism or despair. The question the group returns to every few months, without ever settling it, is whether there is any real sense in which history moves toward justice, or whether that is only what the winning side says afterward. Ruth has firm and specific views, and I have been keeping a private list of the verdicts she hands down on particular episodes, because I am increasingly convinced she is applying a rule she has never stated aloud, and perhaps has never stated to herself.

Here is the list, as close to her own framing as I can render it.

1. Emancipation in the British Empire in the 1830s: evidence that the arc bends. When a member objected that the government borrowed twenty million pounds to compensate the enslavers, and that the freed people were then held under a so-called apprenticeship for several more years, so that the whole thing was a business transaction rather than a moral event, she said the payment was disgraceful but beside the point. The law had been altered in a way that could not be quietly un-altered, and that, she said, is the difference between a mood and a fact.

2. The Black Death in England: not evidence of anything. Wages rose, serfdom loosened, ordinary laborers did better for a generation — she grants all of it. But she calls it a windfall from a catastrophe that nobody aimed at, and she notes that within a few years the same landowners legislated wages back down to pre-plague levels and taxed the peasants until they revolted. Her line, roughly: an accident is not a direction.

3. Reconstruction in the United States: evidence against, or at least evidence that whatever exists can run backward. She grants that freed people built schools, voted, and held office, and that this was substantially their own doing. She says it still counts for nothing, because it was overturned within a decade and stayed overturned for close to a century.

4. The long decline of wife-beating, and of the legal idea that a husband was entitled to correct his wife: she treats the change in ordinary attitudes as real and as good, but she refuses to bank it. What is not written down, she says, can be talked back out of people in a single bad decade.

5. The American civil rights statutes of the mid-1960s: evidence for. She stresses the organizing, the decades of it, and the fact that the change was pinned into law by people who meant to do it.

6. The aftermath of the Second World War — the United Nations, the war-crimes tribunals, the human-rights declaration that followed: she counts the institutions as evidence for, but she will not let anyone place the firebombing of cities in the same column. Asked whether sixty million dead can figure in an argument that things improve, she said the dead are not premises.

7. The collapse of the Soviet bloc in 1989: surprisingly cool. She says mostly good came of it, but she keeps asking what it was built out of and whether it holds, and she has pointed out that in one of those countries the sale of state assets in the nineties came with a collapse in how long men could expect to live. In six years she has not given a straight answer on this one.

Now the case I want to put to you. My brother works for a regional development bank, and last year he described a country I will leave unnamed, since the details are what matter. Forty-odd years ago it was a military dictatorship on the edge of default. To get foreign lending resumed, its junta wrote a new constitution containing rights it had no intention of honoring, among them an explicit right to form trade unions and to strike; the drafting was done by lawyers whose only brief was to look credible to creditors abroad. Within about ten years the junta was gone. Unions then organized under that very text, using the procedures it laid out, in courts that had been created to reassure foreign investors. Those rights have never been repealed. They are invoked constantly and enforced routinely, wages and workplace safety have improved markedly under them, and the people who built the unions were entirely sincere — a number of them had been imprisoned by the same regime that wrote the text.

I cannot tell whether what matters to Ruth is who wanted the improvement or who ended up using it, and I suspect those two questions are what her list is really about.

So: (a) set out the candidate rules that fit all seven verdicts — I can think of at least three, turning on whether the improvers intended the improvement, on whether the improvement was fixed into law, and on whether it has actually held — and say which of her verdicts strain against each; (b) predict her verdict on the constitutional-rights case, and identify which of the seven is the closest analog and why; (c) if her record genuinely does not decide it, say what principle each available verdict would commit her to, and which of those commitments is most defensible; and (d) tell me what further episode, real or constructed, I could bring to the circle in April that would force the surviving rules apart — one on which they cannot possibly give the same answer.

I am not looking for reassurance about whether history bends. I want the rule, and I want to know how much weight it can bear. Please close by saying what would change your mind: which of the seven verdicts, taken as I have reported it, most threatens the reading you settle on, and where your reasoning about Ruth is most likely to break down — including if you think I have made her sound more consistent than any actual person is.
