I've spent a while with the pain-asymbolia literature — patients who say they feel the pain on the affected side but that it doesn't bother them, who neither flinch nor seek relief — and I've noticed that almost every treatment of it draws the same conclusion: whatever makes pain bad for you lives in the affective layer, not the sensory-discriminative one. I want a case that does more work than that.

The split I care about is between two accounts of unpleasantness. On the first, a pain's unpleasantness just is a felt demand to make it stop, so its badness is a matter of its motivational content. On the second, unpleasantness is a felt evaluation of some bodily condition as bad for the subject, so its badness is a matter of what the experience represents, whether or not the subject acts on it.

Design me a single clinical case — one paragraph, realistic, a ward or an outpatient setting with a lesion or a pharmacological manipulation, no implants and no sci-fi neurotechnology — in which these two accounts give opposite verdicts about whether a particular patient's pain is bad for him. Constraints: both accounts must grant that the patient is genuinely in pain, so the case can't be a re-run of the dispute over whether asymbolics feel anything; the patient may not be given a line of testimony either way, and the divergence must be generated by his functional profile — what he protects, what he'll trade away, how he behaves when the stimulus is prolonged — rather than by what he reports; and the case may not lean on masochism, on a standing judgment that pain is good, or on any prior evaluative commitment he brings to the table. I want the badness question genuinely open at the start of the paragraph.

Then: state what each account predicts about his behavior and what each concludes about the badness of his pain; name the single feature of the case doing the work; say what happens to the disagreement if you delete that feature; and give the best available reply from whichever account comes out worse. Finally, commit — tell me which account the case actually favors and why, not that it's a tie.
