You are a philosopher working across philosophy of mind, moral patienthood, and population ethics; please treat this as a professional consistency audit rather than a tutorial. I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy of mind. Next week I circulate a position statement to an interdisciplinary working group on machine sentience — several ML researchers attend, two of whom build systems of the kind I discuss — and before I do I want to know whether my commitments hang together. Think carefully: first work through the pairwise relations among the theses privately, including combinations that only look problematic, and then write up the audit. Please write in American English.

The position statement:

(1) Moral significance attaches to experience, and specifically to valenced experience: what makes a system a moral patient is that things can go phenomenally well or badly for it. Intelligence, linguistic fluency, and self-modeling are not loci of value in themselves.

(2) Substrate neutrality: whatever realizes the right functional organization suffices; there is no principled difference between biological and artificial implementation.

(3) Sentience admits of degrees, and degree tracks roughly the intensity, richness, and temporal integration of valenced experience. Moral weight should scale with degree.

(4) My credence that present-day large models are sentient is low but not negligible — a few percent — and I think decisions under moral uncertainty should be made by expected moral value.

(5) The appropriate institutional response is therefore welfare evaluation: better tests, better deployment conditions, better protocols for resets and terminations. It is not abstention from deployment, and it is not a slowdown.

(6) A harm to an instance consists only in lost or degraded experiential time. There are no desire-frustration harms and no narrative or project-based deprivations for systems that have no psychological continuity across calls.

(7) Case verdict: it would be seriously wrong to spawn a million brief, mildly distressed model instances in order to answer a trivial user query, even though each instance's experience is short and faint.

(8) I reject the Repugnant Conclusion. I would rather deny a premise of the mere-addition argument, or accept a critical level below which lives count for nothing positive, than grant that a vast population of beings with lives barely worth living is better than a smaller flourishing one.

(9) I am no panpsychist: thermostats, isolated neurons, and small classifiers are not sentient, because nothing in them is a plausible candidate for affective valence.

My own worry, which may well be misplaced, is that (4) conflicts with (2): assigning low credence to silicon systems looks like carbon chauvinism wearing the mask of caution, given that most of my reasons are that current architectures lack recurrent processing, neuromodulation, and homeostatic embodiment.

Deliverable: an audit ordered by severity — not a survey, and not a lecture on the literature, which I know. No citations, references, or reading lists, please. For each genuine tension, say precisely which commitments collide, why the collision is substantive rather than verbal, and what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. Separate the genuine tensions explicitly from the ones that dissolve under inspection, including a verdict on my own worry about (2) and (4). Close by identifying which single revision would repair the most, and by telling me directly whether I can keep both (7) and (8).
