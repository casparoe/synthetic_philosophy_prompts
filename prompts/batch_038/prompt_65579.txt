For the past year I have served on the allocation committee of a community clinic that distributes a fixed number of specialist referral slots each month. When I joined, I was the member most confident that the arithmetic settled things: more health produced per slot, case closed. In the eleventh month, the arithmetic settled a case in a way I still have not been able to defend to the woman in the room on whom it fell — not because the decision was unfair by any procedure I can name, and not because it was wrong all things considered, but because she was the one who paid, and nothing that the rest of us gained was ever going to come back to her. What I could not say at that table is, I now think, exactly the thing my students have been told about but never made to feel: the objection that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the distinction between persons, whose canonical statement is Rawls's sentence in A Theory of Justice (1971) that utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons."

I teach an evening ethics seminar: sixteen students, eighty minutes, once a week, movable chairs, whiteboard, index cards, no reliable technology. They have met consequentialism and can recite its standard objections as slogans; nobody has read anything in depth on this one. I would like an activity in which they run into the objection rather than hear it described. Please design it to the following specification, and tell me where my specification is wrong.

The shape I have in mind has four rounds, and I want you to keep the architecture but supply all the engineering:

1. A purely intrapersonal round. Each student individually manages their own token stream across three periods and faces a smaller-sooner loss against a larger-later gain. Nearly all will accept it. They then write, in one sentence, the rule they used. The rule must be theirs, in their words, posted where everyone can see it.
2. An interpersonal round that is numerically identical in aggregate. The same magnitudes, redistributed so that the whole cost lands on three identifiable students and the gains on everyone else. The class decides as a committee whether to apply the rule they themselves just wrote — and some of the students who wrote it will be voting to apply it to others.
3. An anonymity round. Same numbers, but no one is told who bore the cost; only that the total rose. This should let us see whether what moved them in round two was the separateness of the persons or merely the identifiability of the losers.
4. A consent-and-compensation round, if the clock allows. Advance consent solicited before the round, plus a transfer that actually arrives in the loser's hand before the round ends. This should test whether the utilitarian's best reply dissolves the objection.

Two constraints on the token economy: no money and no grades may be at stake, and yet the losses must be felt — I want you to name the device you are using to achieve that and to explain why it works on adults at nine in the evening. The aggregate totals in rounds one and two must be exactly equal, and the arithmetic must be simple enough to do on a card.

I want participants to come away having noticed three things for themselves: that their own considered rule, applied without distortion, produces an outcome they cannot endorse at the table; that the shift in their verdicts between rounds one and two cannot be explained as unfairness, because nothing about the procedure changed; and that even where the maximizing choice is the right one, something is left uncancelled for the person who bore the cost. The third is the one I most want them to arrive at unassisted, because it is the one I could not articulate at the clinic.

Please also build into the debrief a way of handling two students I can predict: the consistent maximizer who genuinely takes the round-two deal and says so without flinching, and the student who says the intrapersonal round is just as objectionable, because their future self is not really them. I want both dissents to strengthen the session rather than derail it. The name of the objection and the Rawls sentence should appear only in the last few minutes.

Please answer in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

- **Summary of the activity** (~150 words): what happens and what it is for.
- **Materials and setup** (~150 words): a list I can assemble in an afternoon, including how the room is arranged and what is pre-written before students arrive.
- **The four rounds** (~800 words): full playable rules, the exact numbers on the cards, the token scheme, and how you reset between rounds without giving away the parallel.
- **Minute-by-minute timeline** (~200 words).
- **Participant handout** (~250 words): the text exactly as I should print it, giving away nothing.
- **The debrief** (~650 words): an ordered sequence of questions, each one tied to a specific moment that will have occurred, with the naming of the objection and the three takeaways placed where they belong, and including what to say if round three does not produce the contrast I am hoping for.
- **What will go wrong** (~400 words): the three or four predictable failures on a first run, why each happens, and what I should do in the moment — including the risk that students who lose tokens in round two end the evening actually annoyed with me.
- **What the activity cannot show** (~300 words): which feature of the objection no game of this kind can reproduce and I will have to supply in words. I suspect it has something to do with the fact that tokens are transferable and compensable in a way that harms to a person are not, so that the very currency of the game assumes the fungibility the objection denies; tell me whether that is right and what else is missing.
- **Variants** (~200 words): a version for thirty students, a version for fifty minutes, and a version that works with no tokens at all.
