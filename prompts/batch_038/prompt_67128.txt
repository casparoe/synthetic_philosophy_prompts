so I've been reading Bentham's unpublished essay on "offences against one's self" — the pederasty one from around 1785 that he never dared print — and I've basically adopted his line, and a friend in my jurisprudence group says my position is a mess. I want an actual audit of my own view, not a summary of Bentham and not a history lesson. Here's what I hold:

1. Straight hedonistic utilitarianism. Pleasure and pain are the only things good or bad in themselves. Everything else — rights, dignity, desert, duty — is either shorthand for facts about pleasure and pain or it's nothing at all. I'm with him that natural-rights talk is empty rhetoric.
2. Where all the parties consent and are competent, there's no mischief, so punishment in those cases is groundless in his technical sense: there's nothing for it to prevent. Criminalising consenting adult sexual conduct does more harm than the conduct ever did.
3. I don't buy paternalism either. If two adults are making each other worse off by their own lights, that's still their business, and I want that to follow from my utilitarianism rather than being bolted onto it.
4. But I do count the pain that disgusted third parties feel. Popular disapproval is one of his four sanctions, i.e. a genuine source of pain, and I'm not going to pretend those people feel nothing. Their distress goes into the calculus alongside everything else.
5. At the same time I think disgust-driven disapproval deserves no weight of its own. It's a hangover from the ascetic principle, produced by bad upbringing and association of ideas, and the right response is to educate it away rather than legislate around it.
6. Some desires are corrupt even where acting on them yields net pleasure — certain deceitful or manipulative ones, say — but I want to hold that this is only because of what they do to a person's character and their future capacity for enjoyment.
7. I'm also a psychological hedonist: everyone ultimately acts for their own pleasure. And I still think people can and ought to act for the general happiness, which I take to be no contradiction of point 1.
8. Numbers matter — extent is one of the seven circumstances — but I also think a small group's intense pleasures can outweigh a huge crowd's mild disgust.

Something in there has to give and I dont know what. Go through the pairs and tell me which conflicts are real and which ones dissolve once you state them precisely; for the real ones rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, say exactly what has to be dropped or weakened to repair each, and what the repair costs me. Then tell me which single revision buys the most consistency overall. Be especially careful about 4 and 5 — I suspect there's a reading on which they're compatible and a reading on which they aren't, and I want to know which one I'm actually committed to given 1 and 2.

Don't hedge the whole thing into mush: commit to a ranking. But do flag, separately, which parts of your answer are you reasoning about the structure of the view and which parts lean on claims about what Bentham himself held, since I care much more about the first and I can't check the second right now.
