Went to a talk last week where the speaker built his whole argument around Bongard problems — you know, those grids of twelve little panels, six on the left and six on the right, all abstract line drawings, and you have to work out the rule that sorts them into the two groups. His pitch: solving one is basically doing science in miniature. The twelve pictures are your observations, you propose a hypothesis about what separates the two sides, you test it against the panels you havent accounted for yet, it gets falsified, you revise, and eventually one description survives. From there he made two claims. First, that this is why humans are so absurdly good at these puzzles and machines are so bad — we run the scientific method unconsciously, it's the same cognitive machinery, just pointed at twelve squares instead of at nature. Second, that therefore the fix for AI is to build a system that does proper hypothesis formation and testing over a space of candidate descriptions, and that today's vision models fail at Bongard problems because they can't do science, not because they can't see.

I left half-convinced and half-itchy and I can't tell which half is right. Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the mapping laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the observations, the hypothesis, the experiment, the falsification, the theory choice, and whatever else in the scientific process corresponds to something in sitting there squinting at twelve panels. Then the places it breaks, sorted into the ones that actually threaten his two conclusions and the ones that are just surface-level differences nobody should care about.

A few spots I already suspect are load-bearing, tell me if I'm wrong: a Bongard problem has an author who already knows the answer, so is solving it really an investigation of the world, or is it closer to working out what someone meant — a communicative act where the success word isnt "true" but "what they were going for"? And the data is fixed: you can't run a new experiment or collect more examples, you just get twelve pictures, whereas science's whole point is that you can go and look again. And his solutions don't generalize anywhere — the rule for problem 42 tells you nothing about problem 43, which seems unlike a scientific theory in a way that might matter for claim one. And finally, is the bottleneck in these puzzles really hypothesis testing? My own experience is that I can check a proposed rule in about two seconds, and the hard part is that the predicate I need wasnt in any list I was searching — it might be something like "every one of these is a drawing of a drawing" or "all these shapes could be cut from paper without the pieces falling apart." If that's right, what does it do to claim two, which is all about building a good search space?

End with a verdict: does the science analogy survive, is it doing more work than it can bear, and is there a better analogy for what a person is actually doing on a Bongard problem — or is there not one, and the honest answer is that the puzzle just doesn't resemble any single familiar activity? No need to point me at sources, I just want the reasoning argued through.
