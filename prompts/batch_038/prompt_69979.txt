Three weeks from now I present at a medical school's neuroethics seminar. My thesis is that Dennett's brain-transplant scenario — the brain sustained in a vat, kept in radio contact with its own emptied, radio-driven body — has been over-read. The intuition that the person goes with the brain, I will argue, tracks where the capacities that identify someone to their intimates are located: face, voice, gait, the shared stock of stories. It is not evidence about persistence. And Dennett's own report of feeling himself to be wherever the vat is comes after he has been told the arrangement, which makes it a verbal inference rather than an indexical experience. I close with a variant I predict will flip reported intuitions: the vat brain driving a body fitted with the original face. The room is mostly clinicians — a surgeon who performs facial transplantation and has treated patients distressed about whose face they are wearing, a pediatric neurologist with a large hemispherectomy caseload, an intensivist who makes brain-death determinations — plus two philosophers, one an animalist and one who has taught the case for a decade and knows every standard reply. Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in two minutes, what I should concede before it is asked, and where the reply is softest. Mark the one objection that takes the whole argument down if it lands, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
