I am a second-year doctoral student in history, working on how the quarrel over German responsibility for the First World War was transformed by Fritz Fischer's 1961 book on German war aims, and last term my reading group — roughly half historians, half people from the philosophy department — got dragged into an argument I have not managed to shake. It started when one of the philosophers remarked that the entire controversy, from the war-guilt article of the Treaty of Versailles through Fischer and the historians who answered him, is philosophically naive, and that a very short argument shows why. I have tried to reconstruct his case as charitably as I can:

(1) To hold a state responsible for a war is to hold it responsible for what the war did — the deaths, the destruction, the political catastrophe that followed in its wake.
(2) A state can be responsible for an outcome only if a reasonable government in its position would have recognised that its actions were likely to bring that outcome about; short of that we are in the territory of luck rather than culpability, and luck is no ground for blame.
(3) Every government that decided for war in July 1914 deliberated on the assumption of either a localised Austro-Serbian affair or a short continental war settled in a matter of weeks; a four-year war of attrition killing millions entered nobody's calculations.
(4) So no government in 1914 acted as one that recognised the likelihood of the outcome for which we now wish to hold it responsible.
(5) Hence no state is responsible for the First World War, and this holds whatever the archives establish about Berlin's intentions in July, about the December 1912 meeting whose significance is still disputed, about the preventive-war calculation built on Russian rearmament, or about the war-aims memorandum drawn up for the Chancellor's office in September 1914.
(6) The expectation of a short war is not a peculiarity of 1914 but a standing feature of the record: governments that take countries into war overwhelmingly expect the fighting to be brief.
(7) So no state has ever been responsible for any war, and the apparatus of assigning war guilt — treaty clauses, commissions, historiographical controversies — rests on a conceptual mistake.
(8) Yet some wars plainly have parties more to blame than others, and a conclusion that erases that difference cannot be accepted.

He is happy to run this as a reductio against (8) rather than as a reductio against anything earlier: he thinks the chain is valid and every premise true, so the practice is what has to go, and the historians in my seminar spent fifty years arguing about nothing. I keep wanting to answer him by disputing (3) — several of us pointed out that the short-war expectation is itself partly a legend assembled after the fact, and that contemporaries were well aware that a general European war might be long and ruinous — but he says this misses the target, since even if some ministers foresaw a long war, the argument survives for the states whose ministers did not, and (6) would need only weakening, not abandoning. So I do not think historical correction is the reply I am after, and I would rather not be told that the question is empirical.

What I want from you is a diagnosis. Where, if anywhere, is the chain actually broken: which step fails to follow, or which step trades on a description of the outcome that shifts as the argument proceeds? And if you think it is valid throughout, which premise should I surrender, and what does surrendering it commit me to — including what it commits me to as a historian, since I will resist any diagnosis that makes the archival questions irrelevant by fiat. If I am pushed to choose I would rather keep (2) than keep (8), because dropping (2) seems to make states strictly answerable for everything that happens to follow from their acts, condemning the prudent and the defensive alongside the reckless; but I want the price of each option stated rather than assumed. I would particularly like to know whether (1) is as innocent as it looks, since it is the premise I reach for when I try to resist him and the one I am least able to say anything definite about.
