I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on concepts and analogy, and I want a critical reading of Jorge Luis Borges's short story "Funes the Memorious" set against the Copycat research program on fluid concepts. Both sides of the pairing should be treated as substantive, and I would rather have an honest assessment of where the pairing strains than a tidy illustration. To make sure nothing depends on your recall of either source, I will stipulate the relevant material for both.

The story, in brief: a narrator bearing the author's name revisits Fray Bentos and finds Ireneo Funes, a young man paralysed by a fall from a horse who has acquired total recall and, with it, total perception — he registers the shape of the clouds at every moment, together with the muscular and thermal sensations accompanying each instant, and claims an intuition of a horse's mane or a shifting flame as immediate as our intuition of a triangle. He passes the time reconstructing an entire past day, an exercise that occupies another entire day, and inventing a system of enumeration in which every number receives its own arbitrary name; the narrator points out that this is precisely the opposite of a system of enumeration. Funes cannot sleep, because he must attend to every crevice and moulding of the houses around him. The narrator states flatly that he was incapable of Platonic ideas, of generalities, of abstractions, and that his world was one of intolerably uncountable details. He dies at nineteen of congestion of the lungs.

The technical side, also in brief. The Copycat project, developed by Hofstadter, Melanie Mitchell, and their collaborators, works in a deliberately impoverished microdomain of letter strings and analogy problems of the form "abc is to abd as ijk is to what?" The architecture has three components: a slipnet of standing concepts with associative links whose effective weights shift with activation; a workspace in which temporary structures are built over the strings at hand; and a coderack of small processes, codelets, that run in parallel and probabilistically, some building structures in the workspace bottom-up, others selected top-down according to the current state of the slipnet. Fluidity arises because activation spreads and, crucially, decays: as one concept's activation falls, neighbouring concepts become available, so that a concept built early in processing can slip into a related one later. The standard examples are chosen to force such slips. Given abc → abd, the string xyz invites either xyd, treating "successor of the rightmost letter" literally, or wyd, which requires "rightmost" to slip to "leftmost" and "successor" to "predecessor" once the solver notices that xyz runs in the opposite direction from abc; and the string mrrjjj invites mrrjjjj only if "letter" can slip to "group of same letters" and "successor" to "group one larger." The program's broader thesis is that analogy-making is the core of cognition and that categorisation is high-level perception accomplished by this same slippery machinery.

With both sides on the table, I want the reading to answer the following, in this order:

1. Which architectural defect does Funes actually have? There are at least two candidate diagnoses, and the story may support only one. On the first, he lacks a slipnet altogether: nothing in his head is a standing, shareable concept, so there is nothing to slip. On the second, he has concepts but they cannot deactivate — activation never decays, so no neighbouring concept ever becomes competitive and slippage is structurally impossible even though the machinery is present. These are different claims with different consequences for what the story is about. Argue for one, or explain why the story genuinely underdetermines the choice, and say what turns on it.

2. Is the narrator's thesis — that to think is to forget differences, to generalise, to abstract — the same thesis the Copycat program embodies, or only a cousin of it? The program's fluidity is purchased by decay and by stochastic competition, which sounds like forgetting; but the slipnet's concepts are permanent, and what decays is activation, not content. Does Borges conflate the loss of particularity with the loss of salience, and is that conflation a defect in the story or a genuine insight the architecture only half-expresses?

3. Consider a variant I will stipulate rather than defend: a Copycat-like system whose workspace is unbounded and lossless, so every structure ever built persists at full strength, and whose slipnet nodes are barred from being shared across positions or strings, so that each node encodes one particular token in one particular place. Call this the Funes architecture. Would such a system, faced with abc → abd and xyz, return xyd, wyd, or something else entirely? I want a concrete account of what it does at the level of codelets and activation, and then a judgement: is the Funes architecture a coherent member of this family of models, or does specifying it reveal that Borges's character is not a possible cognitive system at all but a limit concept?

4. Attend to the enumeration system, which I take to be the story's most philosophically loaded device. Every number named uniquely is exactly the slipnet's failure mode run in reverse: a general concept is what allows the same node to be recruited across indefinitely many particulars, and Funes's naming scheme buys expressiveness at the cost of any such recruitment. Does the story know that it is describing a failure of analogy rather than of arithmetic, and does the day-long reconstruction of a day function as the same point at the level of memory?

5. Where does the reading break? State plainly what the story is not doing: it is not a theory of concepts, its protagonist's condition is presented as a pathology of perception and recall rather than of analogy, the narrator's epistemology is ironic and self-undermining in ways that resist being read as argument, and nothing in the story corresponds to the stochastic, parallel, urgency-driven search that does the real work in the program. I want you to identify the strongest objection to my pairing the story with this research program at all, and to say whether it survives that objection.

6. Close by saying what the story contributes to the debate about concept formation that the technical literature, as far as you can tell, does not articulate — for instance, whether it advances the thought that abstraction is a cost paid in the currency of the particular, or that conceptual fluidity is not a positive power but a managed consequence of lossiness, or something else you find better.

Please do not summarise either the story or the architecture beyond what is needed to make the argument; I have given you both. Do not supply a bibliography, a list of further reading, or attributions to secondary commentators, and do not quote either text at length — I want your analysis, not your recall. Aim for something in the range of fifteen hundred to two thousand words, organised under the six headings above, written at a level suitable for handing to graduate students who have read the story but know nothing about Copycat.
