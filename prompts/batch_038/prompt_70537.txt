I direct the Technical Review Office of a city of roughly 400,000. Since last spring every capital-project specification and every information-technology procurement above $250,000 passes through my office before it reaches the purchasing agent — about twenty a month. The office was created after a council committee read Langdon Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and decided it wanted to know, before concrete is poured, whether a specification is quietly answering a question the council has never voted on. My reviewers are civil engineers, planners, and procurement analysts. None has studied political theory and none will read it. Each screen gets forty-five minutes and one page.

What we have is an argument, not a procedure. One faction of my staff would flag any specification with distributional consequences, which flags nineteen items in twenty and has earned us a reputation as a nuisance. The other faction would flag only what someone intended to be political, which flags almost nothing, because procurement files rarely record intent and because the consequential cases are usually the unintended ones. I need a procedure whose operative criterion is neither of those: something a reviewer can apply from the documents already in the file plus at most one call to the project manager.

Please write it, subject to the following requirements.

- Ordered questions, cheapest and most decisive first, with explicit stopping rules: where the procedure terminates early with a finding of ordinary, and what the default verdict is when the reviewer runs out of time or the file is silent.
- A fixed classification of at most four labels, including at least the two Winner separates — an arrangement that settles an issue of power within a particular community by foreclosing what would otherwise have remained an open question, and a system whose normal operation appears to require, rather than merely permit, a particular form of organization: centralized, hierarchical, expert-managed. State what to record when both apply, and what distinguishes requiring from merely encouraging.
- Three house tests, made precise and then stress-tested. The ten-year test: could the council choose otherwise in a decade without tearing the thing out? The silence rule: is the specification quiet about a question the artifact will answer anyway? The forced-choice audit: when a vendor says a requirement is dictated by physics, a building code, a standard, or interoperability, what evidence in the file distinguishes a genuinely forced choice from a preference wearing a constraint's clothing? For each test, give the threshold, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the characteristic misfire.
- Dispositions rather than verdicts. A flag cannot kill a project; my office classifies and refers. Specify which finding leads to a redesign and alternatives analysis, which to a plain-language disclosure note attached to the procurement, and which to referring the matter upward as an open question of values that the specification is currently deciding on the council's behalf. Mark the steps where a reviewer's own politics will leak in, and say which of those the city attorney — who signs every flag — cannot absorb.
- An honest accounting of the translation. Which distinctions from the theory you preserved, which you dropped to make it usable, and why dropping them is safe in this setting. Which classes of case the procedure structurally cannot see, including slow cumulative reshaping of a city by something like the automobile, which no single specification carries. And whether Feenberg's idea of a technical code — that a specification encodes the interests and vocabulary of whoever drafted it — belongs in a procedure that will be run by people who later write specifications themselves, or must be excluded as untestable.

Then run the procedure on three items from my current docket.

1. A parks specification, drafted after a merchants' petition, requiring a fixed center armrest on all benches in two downtown plazas at a spacing that makes lying down impossible. The file records the petition and states the purpose as deterring overnight occupancy. We already think this one is political; I want to know whether your procedure reaches that result by a route that would still work if the file said nothing about intent.

2. A water-utility specification for advanced metering with remotely actuated service valves, replacing a disconnection process that currently requires written notice and a court order. Nothing in the file mentions disconnection policy; the stated justification is billing accuracy and leak detection. The meters ride a fifteen-year single-vendor data-platform contract, and the shutoff logic will live in the vendor's system rather than ours.

3. An adaptive traffic-signal procurement requiring continuous vehicle and pedestrian detection at every intersection in the central corridor under a ten-year single-vendor analytics contract, justified by emissions reductions and by a federal safety grant whose deadline falls in nine weeks. The mayor's office announced it at a press conference last month and my deputy expects the screen to clear it. I would like it to. Tell me plainly whether it does; if it does not, identify which finding does the damage and draft the two-paragraph referral note to a council that has already been told the project is a done deal.

Where a case turns on a fact my description omits, name the fact, say whom the reviewer would call for it, and give the outcome under both possible answers.
