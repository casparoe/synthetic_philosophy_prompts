hey, could you write me a piece of satire for me? i want a fake internal corporate memo — the kind a compliance officer sends round a mid-sized company — announcing that as of Q3 all employee communication must be strictly implicature-free. roughly 700-800 words, formatted like a real memo: header block (to/from/date/subject line/policy reference number), numbered clauses, a couple of indented worked examples with compliance annotations, and one appendix.

the deadpan is the whole thing — nobody inside the memo knows they're funny. the target is the discourse of communication-compliance mandates (plain-language policies, approved-phrasing lists, tone policing), not any real person or company; the invented compliance officer is fair game.

what i want the humour to come from is the policy being built on the pragmatics being exactly right, and then failing at it anyway:

- the four maxims restated as HR clauses with bureaucratic sub-numbering, so that e.g. clause 1(a) becomes a rule about giving as much information as the situation calls for and a rule against oversharing in the all-hands channel
- an approved-phrasing table where the compliant rewordings still obviously carry the exact implicature they were meant to remove, and the annotations mark them cleared without anyone noticing
- at least one case where the memo writes something like "some managers may be affected" and then, in a footnote, panics about what the reader will infer from the word some
- a lexical blacklist of words that smuggle meaning in as a matter of convention rather than context — the but / even / therefore / yet class — which has to keep getting longer across revisions, because rewording doesn't detach the implicature
- a very serious distinction between flouting, violating, opting out of, and merely infringing a clause, with disciplinary consequences attached to each (flouting is a fireable offence, infringing gets you sent to training)
- a passage where Legal tries to cancel the implicatures the memo's own examples are generating by explicitly denying them, and thereby makes them louder
- the appendix: a reccomendation letter the compliance officer wrote for a departing employee, which observes every single clause perfectly and reads as utterly savage

the payoff i'm after — but don't let anybody say it out loud and don't wink at the reader — is that implicature isn't an optional extra you can switch off with better drafting, it's what happens automatically the moment you assume the other party is cooperating. someone who does pragmatics should read it and think the theory has been handled correctly and the satire is fair; someone who's never heard of conversational implicature should still just find it funny as a workplace document.

no citations, no name-dropping the guy, no verbatim quotations from any real text — invent all the examples yourself. and no joke should depend on the reader spotting a philosophy reference: it has to work entirely from inside the memo's own world.
