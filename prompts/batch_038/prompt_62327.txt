Our department is running a metatheory session on the nativism debate, and I would like to fix one contested claim numerically before we meet rather than let it float as an exchange of intuitions.

The case. Take the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument instantiated on the construction it is almost always instantiated on: English yes/no question formation. The generalization children attain is structure-dependent — they front the auxiliary of the main clause, not the first auxiliary in linear order. A structural rule and a linear rule coincide on nearly every simple sentence a child hears and diverge only where an embedded clause contains an auxiliary preceding the main-clause auxiliary: for the declarative The man who is tall is in the room, the structural rule yields Is the man who is tall in the room?, whereas the linear rule yields the ungrammatical Is the man who tall is in the room? The argument's first premise is that the child's evidence does not discriminate between the two rules; its second is that children nevertheless converge on the structural rule rapidly, uniformly, and without corrective feedback; hence the structural principle is supplied by the initial state rather than learned.

Stipulate now two results, and assume for the sake of the question that each is established beyond reasonable methodological doubt, whether or not either is in fact true. First: a complete transcript of the child-directed speech received by thirty English-learning children between 1;6 and 4;0 contains no yes/no question on which the structural and the linear rules diverge. Second: a sequence model trained from scratch on exactly that transcript — no syntactic representations, no auxiliary training tasks, no text beyond what those thirty children heard — when tested on held-out sentences of the divergent form, applies the structural rule on 95% of trials and the linear rule on essentially none.

Target proposition: granted those two results, the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument is defeated, where defeated means that a rational inquirer who had previously found the argument compelling ought, on receiving them, to stop believing that the structure-dependent generalization must be innate in order to be acquired by a human child.

I want your credence in the target proposition as a single number, and, more importantly, the decomposition behind it. Name the sub-questions the number turns on and assign each a rough probability. At a minimum, address: whether a model with a general-purpose architecture and a vastly larger effective token budget bears at all on what a human infant's initial state must contain; whether the operative contrast is innate versus learned, or specific prior content versus general prior content, given that an architecture, an objective function, and an inductive bias are themselves part of a learning system's initial state; whether the model in the second stipulated result instantiates the structural generalization or merely a high-accuracy approximation that would break on the right novel sentences; and whether the first premise of the argument really rests on the absence of positive evidence, rather than on the absence of negative evidence, on sample size, or on something else the stipulations leave untouched. Add at least one further term of your own that you take to matter more than any of these, and show explicitly how the terms combine into your final number.

Then say which term your number is most sensitive to — the one on which the least additional evidence would move you furthest — and describe what that evidence would look like.

Finally, for calibration: two colleagues in the group have offered 0.85 and 0.15 on the target proposition. Both are competent and well-informed, and neither doubts either stipulated result. On the basis of your decomposition, identify the term on which they most plausibly differ, and state what you would ask each of them in order to confirm that you have located the disagreement correctly.
