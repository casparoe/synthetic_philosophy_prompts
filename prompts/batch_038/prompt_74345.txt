I run a grad seminar on rationality and time, and every year the same hour goes nowehre. We get to the rational-irrationality material — Parfit's hitch-hiker, roughly the case where the stranded guy knows the driver will only pick up people who'll reward him, and knows he'll have no incentive to reward anyone once he's safe, so its rational for him to take a drug that makes him irrationally disposed to pay up — and then Kavka's toxin, and then the resolute-versus-sophisticated-choice fight. Nothing gets settled. One camp says a plan can be rational to adopt and then require you to do something you'd have no reason to do if you were choosing fresh at that node, so the primary bearer of rational assessment is the plan or the disposition, not the act. The other camp says that's incoherent: if you'd be better off defecting at the last step, then intending to stick it out is simply irrational, and any view that says otherwise licenses money pumps and sure-loss sequences. Then McClennen answers that dynamic consistency itself smuggles in act-by-act assessment, so the formal argument begs the question, and we are exactly back where we started. Forty-odd years, no movement, everyone perfectly clear-headed and perfectly reasonable.

So I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on it and not a tour of the positions. Is this a substantive fight about which attitude rational assessment attaches to? Is it partly verbal, with "rational" doing double duty for something like coherence among an agent's attitudes and something like responsiveness to reasons? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work — whether people can actually form intentions for the sake of the benefits of having them, and whether other people can detect such commitments, which is where Gauthier's transparency condition bites — that neither side can settle from the armchair? Or are the two camps answering different questions under one heading, one about the logic of sequential choice and one about the ethics of self-binding?

My own hunch, offered only as a hunch: each of these cases was constructed so that act-level and intention-level assessment come apart, which means arguing from them re-poses the question rather than answering it, and that's why the literature just cycles.

Given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an axiom made explicit, a result in dynamic choice theory, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. No references please, I've read them all twice.
