I'm in my third year of a PhD, and I've been asked to circulate a short statement of where I've landed on the exclusion argument before a departmental reading group whose stated purpose is to spend the hour trying to find the crack in it. The problem is that I assembled these pieces at different times, some of them under the influence of one literature and some under the influence of another, and I no longer trust my sense of whether they were bought on compatible assumptions. I'd like an audit rather than a defence, and I'd like it to be blunt. Here is the package:

(1) Physicalism: every particular is physical, and every instance of a mental property is realized by, and metaphysically necessitated by, some instance of a physical property. There are no mental substances and no further ingredients in the world over and above the physical ones.
(2) Causal closure of the physical: every physical event that has a cause has, at its time, a sufficient physical cause.
(3) Mental causation in the robust sense I actually care about: my wanting a glass of water caused me to walk into the kitchen, and it caused it in virtue of being a wanting, not merely as an inert accompaniment to the neural event that did the work. I am not an epiphenomenalist and I don't want to be manoeuvred into being one by a sequence of individually plausible steps.
(4) I reject the exclusion principle. Mental and physical causes are not rivals competing for a fixed budget of causal work, because realization is not a relation of independence between two separate causes; there is one process here picked out at two levels, so the overdetermination worry never gets off the ground.
(5) My account of causation is interventionist: c is a cause of e just in case some intervention on c would change e. I want to be strict about "intervention," though — an intervention on a candidate cause must be able to change that property while leaving its realizing base untouched, and if no such intervention is metaphysically available, the property is not a cause. I hold onto this strictness because I think it is what stops the account from becoming a licence to call anything a cause.
(6) I also accept a proportionality constraint: among properties that make a difference to an effect, the one proportional to that effect is the genuine cause, and properties that are too specific, or too general, relative to the effect are not. Applied to the case at hand, the neural realizer is too specific and the mental property is proportional.
(7) I'm a pluralist about levels of causal relevance: I don't think there is a principled fact about which level is the causally relevant one, and I'm happy to say the mental and the neural are each genuinely causal in their own right.
(8) Two case verdicts I want to keep. First, if my wanting water had been realized by some quite different neural pattern — one shared with an octopus, or instantiated in a silicon system with the same functional organization — I would still have walked into the kitchen; I read this as showing that the realizer is not what does the causal work. Second, I'm an externalist about content: what makes my state a wanting-water rather than something else depends partly on my environment and history, and I think Oscar and Twin Oscar have different desires.

So: which of these pull against each other, and how hard? Please go through the pairs you think matter and rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of worries, since I'd rather fix the worst one than tidy up all of them gently. For each genuine tension, tell me what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package — and then tell me which single change would clean up the most, and whether it leaves (3) intact. Equally, please flag the pairs that only look like tensions and say why they dissolve, because I don't want to spend the reading group defending myself against a problem I don't have. I'd like you to commit to your ranking rather than hedging it into mush: if you think one of these tensions is fatal to the package rather than merely awkward, say it is fatal and say which commitment dies. And if you think something in my formulation isn't a recognizable thesis at all — if, for instance, what I've written as (5) or (2) is really a conflation of two distinct notions, or if one of my case verdicts in (8) is simply unavailable to someone who holds (1) — say that plainly and tell me what the recognizable version would be, instead of dutifully auditing my wording. If you think the exercise can't be done well as posed, because the commitments are too loosely specified to support a ranking of severities, I'd much rather hear that and hear what further specification you'd need.
