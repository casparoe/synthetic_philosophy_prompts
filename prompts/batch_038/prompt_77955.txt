I've been a driving instructor for about fifteen years now and lately I've got properly stuck on a question I don't have the words for, so I'm going to dump it on you. It's about what the whole licensing system here in the UK quietly assumes about the difference between knowing stuff and being able to do stuff, because the more I look at it the less consistent it seems.

Here's the setup, which you probably know but I'll spell out since the details are the point. To get your licence you pass two totally different tests. First the theory test, which is itself two parts: multiple choice questions on the Highway Code, and then a hazard perception section where they show you video clips from a driver's point of view and you click the mouse when you see something starting to develop into a hazard — you're scored on how early you click, not on whether you can say what the hazard was. Then the practical: about forty minutes in my car with an examiner and a marking sheet, faults graded driving/serious/dangerous, up to fifteen minors allowed but one serious and you're failed. If you take your practical in an automatic, your licence says automatic only unless you pass the practical again in a manual — and when you do upgrade, they don't make you retake the theory test, only the driving one. And once you've passed at seventeen, nobody ever tests you again barring a medical or a ban.

What I can't work out is what single picture of knowledge would license all of that, because the pieces seem to be pointing in different directions:

- The multiple choice part only makes sense if having the right facts about driving does something — if it's necessary, or at least decent evidence you'll be safe. But I teach people who got 90-something percent on the theory and absolutely cannot find the bite point on a hill. Not nerves. They have no idea what to do with their feet and no amount of reminding them of the facts helps. So the system acts like the facts matter while everyone who does this for a living knows they don't get you there.
- Then the hazard clips. That one isn't testing what you believe, it's testing how fast you see, scored to fractions of a second. That looks like they've admitted the important thing isn't the believing part — it's a skill test wearing the costume of a written exam. So why keep both, and what does the combination assume?
- The marking sheet assumes competent driving is a sum of separately scoreable bits — mirrors, positioning, junctions, response to signals, and so on. But I have students who perform every one of those visibly, head on a swivel, textbook everything, and are still dangerous, because the bits are all present and whatever turns them into judgement isn't. And I've had the opposite: quiet kids who couldn't name one rule who drive beautifully first time.
- The automatic-only restriction assumes the skill is somehow bolted to the machine and doesn't generalise. But they don't re-test your theory knowledge when you upgrade, which assumes the facts transfer across machines fine. And both of those sit alongside the assumption that the skill you demonstrated once, at seventeen, in a hatchback, with a stranger in the passenger seat, is the skill you'll still have at sixty.

Then there's me. I've done this so long I can't give you the sequence for a parallel park any more — if you made me write the steps out I'd produce something I know is wrong. And yet I can watch a learner do one and tell you exactly what went wrong and at what moment. My account of my own skill looks like a story I invent afterwards. Students generally only get it once they stop listening to the story and feel the car. Except that I do teach, mostly by talking, and most of them pass, so I can't be right that the talking is useless. Someone once told me there's a big argument in philosophy about whether a skill is just a species of factual knowledge or something genuinely different, and I didn't follow a word of it, so please bring whatever of that is relevant rather than making me guess.

What I'd like from you:

Reconstruct the theory of knowledge and skill this whole apparatus is committed to — not the one the DVSA would print in a leaflet, the one its actual design requires. Go piece by piece and say which rival picture of skill each piece rules out, and be strict about separating what the system genuinely cannot function without from what merely happens to fit it comfortably. My suspicion is that a lot of it is history and administrative convenience dressed up as a theory of the mind, so tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where two opposite views of what a skill is would license the exact same tests, and so where the design proves nothing.

Then do the counterfactuals. What would licensing look like if the opposite of its implicit view were true — if a skill really were just a big enough pile of the right facts that anyone who had them could deploy them? And what would it look like if skills were wholly non-verbal, so that no amount of explaining could ever transfer one — would you have to scrap the theory test and fire every instructor, and if not, what would survive and why? Finally, tell me what to make of my own case: instantly able to spot a bad park, unable to describe a good one. Is that evidence for one side, or is it exactly what both sides would predict, in which case its not evidence of anything and I should stop thinking about it on the way home.
