I spent thirty-four years in a state water quality lab, mostly chromatography, and retired three winters ago, and like a lot of retired scientists I found I had no idea what to do with my mornings. So I started reading — first philosophy of science, which felt like looking at my own field from the outside, and then, this year, political philosophy, which does not. Rousseau, a book on populism that was recommended to me, bits of Mill. I'm slow at it and I take everything far too literally, which my reading group has learned to tolerate.

The trouble is that the reading landed right on top of something practical. Our borough has a volunteer panel — five of us, no pay, no staff — and I somehow ended up chairing it. Under a local ordinance, any group that collects enough signatures can get a question put to the whole borough, but the panel has to approve the wording that goes on the ballot and in the mailing, and specifically we rule on whether the group gets to describe its proposal as what the residents want. That last part is the whole job in practice. We get maybe one or two of these a month, more in an election year, and they arrive with cover letters saying the people have spoken, the silent majority is behind this, the community is united, nobody I know supports that. One group last spring turned in about three thousand signatures and it later emerged that a large share were collected at a shopping center two towns over. Another group has a polling firm and a very professional-looking 61 percent. We have two hours per case, no one on the panel has any statistics beyond high school, we have to post a one-page written justification for every ruling, the borough lawyer signs off on legal sufficiency only and never on the framing, and we've now twice been accused in the local paper of being undemocratic ourselves, which stings. So I want a decision procedure I can put in front of four volunteers and actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which question the claim dies outright, and at which points the procedure has to stop and hand the thing back to a human because it's a question of values and not of fact. I want it grounded in whatever the political philosophy here actually says — the difference between an aggregate of everyone's preferences and something more like a common interest, the problem of who counts as the people in the first place, whether a majority that isn't asked anything has a will at all, what a petition or a rally can and can't show that a vote or a poll can — but translated, so it survives contact with people who have never read any of it. Tell me which of those distinctions absolutely have to survive the simplification and which I can safely drop, and be straight with me about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes.

Two more things. First, after a bad month I invented my own little rule, and I'd like you to tell me whether it's sound or whether I've dragged something across from the lab that doesn't belong: a claim only passes if the people making it would accept the identical test applied to the claim their opponents are making. I got that from blinding and pre-registration, from insisting before the run that you'll publish whatever comes out, and I suspect it's a category mistake to apply it to politics. Second, run the procedure on three cases: the bus lane petition with the shopping-center signatures and its cover letter about taking the streets back; the developer-backed housing proposal with its clean poll and its phrase about the community being behind it; and — this is the one I'm braced for — the river restoration panel I set up myself last year, twenty-eight residents picked by lottery who met six weekends and came out unanimous, and whose report opens by stating that this is what the valley wants. If the procedure says I can't let my own report use that line, I want to hear exactly why, and I want to hear what the procedure can never settle for us, however carefully we write it.
