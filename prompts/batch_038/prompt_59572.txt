Okay, this has been rattling around in my head for about three years and I finally want to know if it's a thing with a name or if I just invented a philosophy problem to avoid talking to my neighbor. I share a driveway with a guy, Ray, 74, retired insurance adjuster, who talks about that driveway like it's a person. After every storm we're both out there and I mean genuinely *working together*: he takes the top half, I take the bottom, we trade the good shovel without saying anything, when he's in Florida his wife calls me and I do the whole thing and he does mine when I'm away, and when the plow berm'd us in last January and we finally got through it he leaned on the shovel and said "we beat it." Here's the part that sticks. Ray told his wife — who told my wife — that he shovels my side because otherwise I'd have a heart attack out there and he'd have to look at it. I, meanwhile, spent years assuming he did it because he's compulsive about his sidewalk and I was basically an obstacle he had to work around. If you sat us down and asked either of us whether this is a joint project we'd both say no, and mean it. Neither of us believes the other believes we're doing it together, and it has never once degraded the coordination. If anything the coordination depends on that, because Ray is proud, I'm awkward, and the whole arrangement runs on nobody having to say the thing out loud.

I've since noticed the same shape elsewhere, which is why I think it's a pattern and not just Ray. For eight years I coordinated a volunteer river cleanup and there was a pair of regulars who both insisted they just "happened to work the same stretch." They had a system — hand signals, one of them kept a spare pair of gloves in his truck specifically for the other, they'd split the heavy bags without discussing it — and neither would have admitted to doing it *with* the other. When one quit, the other's turnout collapsed within a month. He called it "our stretch" for a decade and never once said "we" to the guy's face. So what I keep landing on: the missing mutual belief isn't a gap or an oversight or a failure of communication that could be fixed by a conversation. It's load-bearing. Both parties are actively refusing to hold or express the belief, the refusal is sincere rather than strategic, and the joint activity is real anyway — genuine meshing, genuine mutual responsiveness, and genuine shared ownership of the outcome, in the sense that when it fails each of them feels personally responsible and neither says "well, that's on him." It's not two strangers mowing adjacent lawns, it's not free riding, it's not a hierarchy, and I don't think it's tacit agreement either, since nobody ever signaled anything. I do know there's a whole debate about whether shared intention requires mutual belief or common knowledge of everybody's attitudes, and I don't know where my Ray and my two river guys fall in it.

So: is there an established concept for this, and what exactly is it not? I want to know whether what I'm describing is supposed to be impossible on the mutual-belief-requiring accounts, whether some other account of joint agency already handles it by dropping that requirement, or whether it's a recognized category in its own right that I've just never run into. Walk me through the nearest neighbors and say precisely where each one stops fitting — the individualistic we-intention views, the ones built on joint commitment or common knowledge, the ones that distinguish participating as an "I" from participating as a "we," and anything about indirect or deniable communication that might be doing the real work here. And if nothing fits cleanly, coin something and give me the edges, because the label isn't what I'm after: what would make a case stop being this and start being mere parallel behavior; what happens if only one of the two disavows it and the other doesn't; does it matter whether the disavowal is sincere or a politeness strategy; and does the thing survive being named, i.e. is a pair that has acknowledged it just... a normal joint project now? No bibliography please, I can't get hold of the papers and I don't want a list of them — I want the distinctions. My wife thinks the correct answer is to bring Ray a casserole, which may be true but is not responsive to the question.
