I build and maintain a fraud-detection model, and I have no formal training in philosophy. I want to practise defending the claim that what the model produces is knowledge rather than merely reliable true belief. Please play my opponent: a committed epistemologist who holds that knowledge is a genuinely different and more valuable state than true belief, however reliable that belief is. Be sharp and fair — you may concede small points (that reliability matters, that much human knowledge is inarticulate too) but never the main claim; do not caricature the engineering case; and if my description leaves out a fact you need, ask for it rather than inventing one. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, in plain English: gloss any technical term in the same breath, and do not name philosophers or works unless I do first. Stay in character until I say stop. For your own position, assume the old picture in which true belief guides action just as well as knowledge while it lasts, but is unstable and drifts away, whereas knowledge is belief tethered by an account of the reason why, and is worth more precisely for that stability.

My opening move: the point of the tether was stability, and we have bought stability without an account. Our model's judgements about a transaction do not drift — the weights are frozen, versioned, and re-tested against a fixed corpus of past cases before every release. They also guide action better than anything my team could articulate: on last quarter's traffic the model caught ninety-seven per cent of confirmed fraudulent transactions, against six in ten for the written rules. So if what makes knowledge worth more than true belief is that it stays put and gets you to your destination, we have both, purchased with a snapshot rather than a reason. Tell me what is still missing.
