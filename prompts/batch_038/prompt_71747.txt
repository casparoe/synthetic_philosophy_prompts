ok so — i'm teaching the last session of a philosophy of mind seminar, and it's on Dennett's multiple drafts model and the whole time-and-the-observer mess, and instead of assigning yet another paper i want to hand people a short story. So: act as someone who does both halves of this — a working philosopher of mind who also writes literary fiction that isn't embarrassing, and who knows the phi-illusion debate cold. Think carefully before you start. Brainstorm out loud first, maybe five or eight lines at the top: which paradigm you're building on, and which narrative device will actually carry the argument rather than just decorating it. Then write.

1200–1800 words. The spine should be a color-phi style case: two little lights a few degrees apart in an otherwise dark room, one green one red, flashed in succession with the right interval, so the subject reports seeing a single dot travel across the gap and change colour somewhere in the middle. The trouble the story has to dramatize is that the dot can't have changed colour midway, because midway the second light hasn't fired yet — the information isn't in the head. So either the brain sits on it and delivers the whole trajectory after the fact, fully reconciled before it ever becomes anybody's experience (the Stalinesque option: the subject's report is honest about what they had), or the subject genuinely saw green all the way across and the record gets edited a fraction of a second later to say the colour changed midway (the Orwellian option: the report is a rewrite). And the punchline the story has to land, not state, is that the two camps agree about every neuron, every keypress, every confidence rating, every detail of how it feels from the inside — they disagree only about whether the spot where the wrong content shows up counts as pre-experiential or post-experiential. There's no experiment and no amount of introspection that could settle it, because settling it would require a publication moment, and that's exactly what's being denied.

Structure it as two interleaved drafts, call them A and B. Same lab, same subject, same session, same dialogue, same observable record — the only difference is which of the two options is true, and that difference has to show up purely in what the interior feels like while it's happening. Then somewhere in the last third, let the labelling start to fail: passages stop being attributable to either draft, and the two collapse into one unmarked run of text. Someone who knows the argument should be able to say which draft is which in the first few hundred words, and should feel the collapse as the conclusion of the argument arriving in the form itself, not as a typographic trick.

Ground rules: no name-dropping inside the fiction — Dennett, the Cartesian theater, multiple drafts, Orwellian, Stalinesque all belong in your afterword, not the story. Keep the science honest; you can fold in a cutaneous-rabbit moment (taps at wrist and elbow and shoulder coming out as evenly spaced taps along skin that was never touched) or a flash-lag detail if it earns its place, but don't invent psychophysics. And above all don't let it resolve into a reveal — no "it was Stalinesque all along," no "the memory really did get rewritten." The point is that the question comes apart.

Then don't hedge on the way out. Add an afterword of about 200 words where you commit: which device you chose, why it's the right one for this argument specifically, and — candidly — where the story takes liberties. If you've leaned on a contested reading of the multiple drafts model (is it a claim about timing? a denial that there's any fact about when an experience exists? an eliminativist move about the theater?), say so and say why you leaned that way. And if the no-fact-of-the-matter punchline is one a critic of Dennett's would reject — e.g. someone who thinks postdiction effects like the flash-lag really do show experience being constructed backwards in time, or someone who thinks the whole dispute is a verbal one about the word conscious — flag that and tell me which side you'd bet on. Definite choices, definite reasons, no both-sides shrugging at the end.
