You are an experienced teacher of early modern philosophy who has run many undergraduate sections on Hume. Next week I have a fifty-minute discussion section in a second-year course on the philosophy of death; the students have read Hume on causation and on miracles, and they will arrive expecting the contemporary debate — terminal illness, physician-assisted dying, depression, personal autonomy. My aim is a single idea: that Hume's posthumous essay on suicide is a reductio of the natural-law prohibition rather than a positive defence of a right to die, and that its force depends on granting, for the sake of argument, the providential framework it is then turned against.

Plan the section; do not explain the argument to me. Begin by sketching the dependency structure — what has to be grasped before what — and then give me: the order the components must come in, including whether the three-heads structure (duties to God, to one's neighbour, to oneself) can be laid out before they see why Hume concedes the premise about divine governance, and whether his analogy about diverting a river should precede or follow the point that the objection proves too much; the one case or scenario that should carry the whole session, saying what it must have and what it must lack, and why Hume's own illustrations will or will not serve; the misconceptions they predictably bring or form — I expect at minimum "Hume is running a cost-benefit calculation on one's usefulness to society," "this is just the miracles argument again," and "Hume is saying anyone may end their life whenever they feel like it" — with the exact point in the sequence at which to head each one off; one closing question whose answers would separate the students who have grasped the structure from those who have merely registered that Hume is provocative; and what you would cut at this level, and announce that you are cutting it.
