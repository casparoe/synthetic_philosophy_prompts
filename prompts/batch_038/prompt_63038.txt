I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-size B2B infrastructure company, and for two years I've chaired our quarterly engineering prioritization committee: six engineers plus me, one three-hour session, roughly fifty candidate projects — platform migrations, reliability investments, long-standing bugs, customer-facing features — competing for about fifteen funded slots. Our process is pairwise. Each candidate gets written up on four dimensions (customer impact, effort, risk of not doing it, strategic fit), the committee compares options two at a time against a rubric we drafted ourselves, and we assemble a ranked list from those pairwise results. Two quarters ago we hit a three-cycle in the middle of the list, broke it with a chair's tie-break, and nobody complained. Last quarter we hit two more cycles, plus a case where the final list said a project we funded was beaten by three projects we cut, each of which was in turn beaten by the funded one. Our current remedies are either the chair's tie-break or collapsing the four dimension scores into a single weighted number, and I can no longer tell whether either is defensible or whether they just hide the problem. The stakes are not enormous but they are real: the output decides what six teams work on for a quarter, the losing teams see the ranking as arbitrary, and I have to hand the list plus a one-page rationale to a VP who signs off without reading the write-ups.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my committee can run in the room, not background. Three parts. First, a short diagnosis: an explicit taxonomy of the kinds of thing a cycle in a ranking like ours can be, which keeps apart at least the case where the group's aggregated comparisons are cyclic even though every member's own are transitive, from the case where an individual's all-things-considered comparisons are themselves cyclic, from the case where the cycle is an artifact of noisy or inconsistent estimates on our four dimensions. I suspect we have been treating these as one problem, and that this is most of why our fixes don't hold. Second, the procedure: numbered steps in the order we should run them, each step stating the question the committee asks, what we concretely do to answer it, what counts as a sufficient answer, what happens on failure (jump to another step, or stop with a no-ranking), and — explicitly marked — the points where the step hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is about what we value rather than about facts, plus which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Third, worked applications to three cases: (a) our funding cutoff, where the difference between the fifteenth and sixteenth candidates is smaller than the noise in our own estimates yet one is funded and one is not, and the procedure has to say what we owe the team whose project got cut, and whether 'we cannot rank these two' is an admissible output or a cop-out; (b) an accumulation case, where twenty small reliability fixes each lose a pairwise comparison to the same large migration, but doing all twenty looks clearly better than doing the migration; (c) at least one case where the procedure produces an answer I will not like — I expect it will either require us to throw out the rubric or tell me to cut a project I have been championing for three quarters. Do not soften that one.

A colleague pointed me at some philosophy here and I want you to use it rather than ignore it, but I have no formal background and I am not asking for a survey, a reading list, or citations. The claims I've been given: that the relation all-things-considered-better-than is not transitive in some cases, argued from spectra in which each adjacent pair differs negligibly while the endpoints differ enormously (Temkin, and Rachels after him); the opposing claim that those spectrum arguments rest on a mistake and that the appearance of a cycle comes from letting the relevant factors shift between steps; and Quinn's self-torturer, where each individual step looks like a straight improvement and the chain ends somewhere no one would choose. Tell me which of these, if any, licenses a departure from transitivity in a committee ranking projects, and translate whatever survives into steps an engineer can follow without knowing the argument. If your conclusion is that none of them applies and that a cycle in our setting is always a defect, say so plainly and say what the defect consists in, rather than assuming transitivity silently. Close with a short section on where the procedure will most often misfire in practice, which of its signals are unreliable, and what would make it worse.

Please format this so I can drop it into our internal wiki and a spreadsheet. Give the diagnosis as prose with a compact list. Give the procedure as a JSON array of step objects, one per step, with exactly these fields: `step_id` (integer), `question` (the question the committee asks at this step), `how_to_test` (concretely what we do in the room, under forty words), `sufficient_answer` (what counts as passing), `on_failure` (either `stop` or the `step_id` to jump to), `handoff` (`null` if the step is mechanical, otherwise the deciding role — `chair`, `committee vote`, or `vp` — plus one sentence on why that question is about values rather than facts), `minutes` (integer estimate for the committee), and `judgment_disguised_as_procedure` (boolean). The JSON must parse: no comments, no trailing commas, no prose inside the array. Then give the three worked cases as a Markdown table with exactly these columns: case | kind of cycle (from your taxonomy) | first step that decides it | outcome the procedure produces | admissible output (`rank`, `refuse to rank`, or `hand to human`) | who decides | why I will not like it.
