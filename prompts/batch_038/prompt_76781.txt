The answer I need graded:

"The debate is usually dated to Mises's 1920 article, which reached most English-speaking readers in the volume of translations Hayek edited in the mid-1930s. Mises's argument is fundamentally an information argument. In a market, prices are the only device that carries information about relative scarcities and about the countless local, time-and-place circumstances that determine what something costs to produce, and that information exists only in the heads of the people who happen to have it. A central authority cannot collect it, since it is dispersed and much of it is tacit. Hayek later developed the same argument in much more detail, so people sometimes treat the knowledge problem as Hayek's contribution, but the core of it is already in Mises. If the planners cannot know the relevant data, they cannot compare alternative uses of steel, coal, land and machinery in any common unit, and so they cannot calculate at all.

Lange's reply is that the planners do not need to know the data in advance. In his model firms are state-owned but still run by managers, who are given two rules: minimize the average cost of producing whatever output they end up producing, and choose the level of output at which marginal cost equals the price of the product. A Central Planning Board posts prices for producers' goods. Managers treat those prices as parameters — data to react to, not numbers to bargain over — and the board revises them by trial and error, raising a price where there is excess demand and lowering it where there is a glut. Lange notes that in principle the equilibrium conditions could be written down as a system of simultaneous equations (Barone had already shown this), but that solving millions of equations would be hopeless in practice, so the board should imitate Walras's auctioneer and grope toward equilibrium by successive approximations instead. Consumer goods are still bought and sold for money wages, consumers still choose freely, and workers still choose their occupations; what is abolished is only the market for capital goods. Lange adds that socialism could even outperform capitalism, since the board can take external effects into account and avoid the waste of slumps and mass unemployment.

So Lange answers Mises head on. Because the board learns about shortages and surpluses from what actually happens when it posts a price, it converges on the same prices a competitive market would have produced, and the planners never needed to possess the dispersed knowledge in the first place. Mises himself later allowed that a socialist state could make use of prices observed in foreign markets, which is effectively a concession. Once you grant the model, whatever objections remain are about incentives, about bureaucracy, and about dictatorship — not about calculation. That is why the debate was generally regarded as settled in Lange's favour from the late 1930s until the collapse of the Soviet bloc revived it."
