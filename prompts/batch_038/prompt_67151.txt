I'm revising a dissertation chapter on Hobsbawm and Ranger's invented traditions and Anderson's account of print capitalism, and one of my readers objected with an analogy I haven't been able to shake. Nations, he writes, are like adults revising their own life stories: since autobiographical memory is reconstructive rather than archival, no one's personal past survives untouched by the needs of the present, so a national tradition assembled in the 1870s out of older material and then claimed as immemorial is doing exactly what any identity does. On this reading the charge of inauthenticity mistakes the genre — invented traditions are not forgeries posing as antique but ordinary narrative construction, and faulting them for being invented is like faulting a memoirist for having a point of view.

Audit the analogy for me. First the correspondence table: what plays the revising author, the earlier draft, the reconstructive process, the audience, and what counts as a successful outcome. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten his conclusion from the merely surface ones. I especially want to know what the analogy imports without earning it — a single agent who does the revising, a standard of coherence or health by which revisions are judged, and the absence of anyone who can be wronged by getting the story wrong. Does the conclusion that authenticity is simply the wrong test survive those imports? If not, tell me what would be a better analogy for invented tradition, and what it would license instead.
