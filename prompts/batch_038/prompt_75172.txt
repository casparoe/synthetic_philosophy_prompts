An essay in a general-audience technology magazine argues that the rule-following paradox has direct consequences for large language models. Here is the central passage:

"Wittgenstein proved that no rule can determine its own applications. Consider '+': everything I have ever done is equally consistent with the rule 'quus', which agrees with addition for all the small numbers we have actually encountered and returns 5 for every pair thereafter — a cousin of Goodman's 'grue', wearing a different hat. So there is no fact of the matter about what I meant by '+'. Wittgenstein's way out was to locate meaning in the dispositions of a community: I mean addition because that is how we are all disposed to go on, and because we share a form of life — a common culture and a common set of beliefs — our agreement settles what counts as correct. The upshot is a kind of eliminativism about meaning: strictly speaking, no one means anything by their words, and no answer can genuinely violate a rule, since any performance whatever can be redescribed as consistent with it. The same reasoning disposes of the worry about language models. If meaning is nothing over and beyond agreement in a community's responses, then a system trained on those responses has thereby been trained on meaning; it cannot fail to mean what we mean, because there is no further fact for it to fail to match. It also explains why a solitary speaker could never follow a rule: with no one to check against, whatever seems right to him is right, which amounts to saying that nothing is."
