Background. Our reading group has two members who have been arguing about the absent-minded driver problem for the better part of a year and have stopped listening to each other entirely. The setup, so you don't have to guess at it: a driver leaves work and there are two junctions on his route home. To get home he must go straight on at the first junction and take the exit at the second. Exiting at the first pays him 1, exiting at the second pays 4, and driving past the second pays 0. Once he's on the road he cannot tell the junctions apart — no memory of whether hes already passed one — so whatever he does at a junction he will do at any junction he reaches. Working it through, the best plan available to him before he sets off is to exit with probability 5/8 at whatever junction he finds himself at, which yields 25/16 in expectation. All the trouble starts when he's actually sitting at a junction.

Camp A, who I'll call the planners, say the only genuine decision problem is the one the driver faces in his kitchen before he leaves. Once he's at a junction he hasn't learned anything he didn't already expect to learn, so conditionalising on the fact that he's at a junction is illegitimate, and the interim calculation that seems to tell him to exit is just double-counting a single decision twice. On their view the rational driver follows the plan and the so-called paradox dissolves.

Camp B, the localists, say that's a dodge: the only place a decision ever happens is at a node, a plan is at best a prediction about what he'll do later and has no normative grip on him once he's there, and if he is genuinely uncertain which junction he's at then he ought to reason from that uncertainty like any other agent. They also insist the planners' numbers only come out tidy because they were rigged to, and that time-consistency is definately non-negotiable for any decent decision theory.

I want the document the two of them should have produced together and never did. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of the claims both camps can sign. Include claims about the arithmetic itself — the 5/8 and the 25/16, what the interim calculation actually gives, and whether the prior at a junction should be plan-dependent or plan-independent — as well as claims about what the case is supposed to demonstrate and about what would count as a real dynamic inconsistency as opposed to a merely apparent one.

2. A disagreements table for whatever survives: the precise residual disagreements, each worded so that its own side would happily sign its own row. No strawmen, and nothing so vague that it could be signed by anybody.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them — and for each crux a concrete case, calculation or argument that both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance of the result.

4. Your own verdict on which parts of the dispute are purely verbal, and what stipulation would be needed to make each of those parts non-verbal.

Here's a test case I've invented for them, and I want it in the document with both predictions recorded: same driver, but three junctions instead of two. Exiting at the first pays 1, at the second pays 4, at the third pays 9, and driving past the third pays 0. Again he can't distinguish them and does the same thing at any junction he reaches. What does each camp say his optimal pre-commitment is, what does each say he should do once he's at a junction, and does each camp predict those two answers will come apart?
