Last night my friend and I got into it for about three hours about promises and I still haven't come down, so I want you to settle it in a form I can actually use at work.

I'm the clinical ethicist on call for a hospice group — four inpatient houses plus a home-visit program — and I get pulled into two or three cases a month that turn on a promise somebody made to a dying person. Staff promise things at 3am. Patients extract promises constantly: that they won't be sent back to the hospital, that the dog will go to a specific nephew, that the ashes will end up at the lake, that somebody will come back tomorrow. Then the patient dies, and I'm left with a nurse or a chaplain who feels bound by something and a family that either wants it enforced or wants it void, and I have no framework, just vibes and a lot of "it depends on the circumstances."

My friend's position — he's a lawyer, not a philosopher, but he's not stupid — is that a promise only binds if the person you made it to can let you out of it. Release is the whole engine. So a promise to someone who's dead, or permanently unconscious, or who will never find out you broke it, isnt a promise at all, it's an intention delivered in a solemn tone. My counter is that the obligation gets created in the moment the promise is taken up, and the promisee dying doesn't delete it — if anything it hardens, because there's nobody left to renegotiate with. He said that's exactly backwards, and also that it's the kind of thing people say at funerals, not a moral fact. Then we slid into whether the real wrong is the promise or just the dashed expectation: he thinks a nurse who says "I promise I'll sit with you tonight" and doesn't has done the same wrong she'd have done by saying "I'll sit with you tonight" and not doing it, because what matters is the belief you planted in somebody and then let die with them. I think that collapses something important, but I couldn't say what without just repeating Scanlon-ish and Hume-ish gestures at each other for an hour.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can run on a claimed promise. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the tiers it should sort utterances into — an actual promise, an assurance, a statement of intent, a hope, a polite bedside formula that means nothing — because half my caseload is people who can't tell those apart. It needs an explicit step for uptake: what has to happen on the other end for a promise to exist, including when the other end has advanced dementia, is sedated, or is nine years old. It needs to say where the release power goes when the promisee dies or loses capacity — does it lapse, does it pass to the family, to co-promisors, to nobody — and whether two siblings who promised jointly can release each other. Tell me where the procedure stops at "no obligation here" and where it hands the decision to the family or to me because the question is one of values rather than facts, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. Constraints: a social worker or chaplain should be able to run it in fifteen minutes with no philosophy background, no legal advice in it, I'm the one who signs off on anything contested, and I'd like a short bedside script for what staff should say *instead* of the word "promise," since "just don't promise anything" is not going to survive contact with a dying person asking you to.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A patient made a night nurse promise his ashes would be scattered at the cabin lake. No witnesses, no note, he died four days later, the estate is closed up, and she has since moved 900 miles away. Her supervisor wants to tell her she's off the hook. Is she?
2. Two adult children promised their dying father, jointly, in front of him, that their mother would stay in her own home and never go into a facility. Eighteen months later the mother has fallen twice and wants a place with help; the son says the promise binds his sister and that he won't release her; the mother herself says she's happy to go.
3. The one where I expect the answer I won't like: a chaplain told a patient with advanced dementia — who asked the same question eleven times in an hour and retained almost none of it — "I'll be back tomorrow, I promise." He got rotated off that house and wasn't back. I've been assuming nothing was owed there, because there was no uptake, and I want the procedure to tell me straight whether that's right or whether the wrong lives somewhere else entirely. Don't soften it if I'm wrong.

Finish with what the procedure can't handle — the categories of case I should just take myself rather than run through a checklist — and where it's most likely to give a confident answer that's wrong.
