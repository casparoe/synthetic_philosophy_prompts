For the better part of a decade I have been defending a reading of Carroll's Tortoise, and I am now preparing to commit it to print. Before I do, I want a rigorous map of its exposure — not a defense of it, and not a survey of the secondary literature. Please take the adversarial role seriously: where you think one of my claims is untenable, say so and say why.

The reading, which I call practice-first, consists of five claims.

1. The dialogue is not a puzzle about the justification of deduction but a reductio of the assumption that grasping a rule of inference could consist in assenting to propositions. Achilles's error is supposing that every component of an inference's warrant must itself be capable of functioning as a further premise.

2. The positive moral is that inference is a transition one performs rather than a further thing one believes. To grasp modus ponens just is to be disposed, under suitable conditions, to pass from *A* and *if A then B* to *B*. Nothing else could constitute that grasp.

3. Consequently the Tortoise's predicament is not epistemic. It is not short of evidence, and it is not irrational; it is incompetent — rule-blind in the manner of someone tone-deaf. To describe the Tortoise as logically defective is, on my view, a category error of the same family as faulting a fish for failing to climb.

4. The regress generalizes. It is not peculiar to deduction: induction, practical deliberation, and the following of constitutive norms — the norms of a game, of assertion, of a social practice — all involve transitions that cannot be exhaustively captured in further propositions. Deduction is only the limiting case in which the point is most vivid, because deductive rules are austere enough that no further premise could plausibly do the work.

5. As a corollary, the strategies that try to vindicate deduction by argument — the rule-circular strategy Boghossian defends, which reasons in a modus-ponens-governed way to the reliability of modus ponens, and the entitlement-style strategy associated with Wright, on which we are non-evidentially entitled to trust our inferential faculties — are not vicious but off-target. The regress does not show that deduction cannot be justified; it shows that framing the matter as one of justification is not a coherent project.

What would count against this? I want the following, in roughly this order.

A ranked inventory of defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward. For each, state precisely what would have to be true — which argument, formal result, conceptual analysis, or empirical finding — and why it belongs at that rank rather than a different one.

Take seriously the possibility that claim 2 is simply false, and that a wholly propositional grasp of a rule is available. Proof theory converts at least some rules into conditionals, and distinguishes rules that are derivable from rules that are merely admissible. Does that machinery show rules to be propositional after all, or does it presuppose the very transitions at issue? I want the honest verdict.

Then consider a variant of my own construction, aimed at claims 2 and 3 jointly. Suppose we build a system with no general competence of the sort I describe — it cannot state any rule, and there is no disposition to pass from an arbitrary pair of suitable premises to a conclusion — and yet it reliably produces the conclusions, having been drilled on a very large but finite stock of instances and generalizing by pattern-matching across them. It survives every test Achilles can devise. Is this rule-grasp without the disposition, or does it fail as a counterexample because the dispositions are present and merely implemented oddly? Whichever way you come down, tell me which of the five claims absorbs the damage, and how much of the rest of the view goes with it.

Claim 3 is where I expect the heaviest fire, so be adversarial. If the Tortoise is merely incompetent, what normative work remains for logic? We criticize people for bad inferences, and criticism seems to presuppose that the criticized agent is answerable and could have done otherwise. Does claim 3 purchase immunity from the objection that logic cannot be normative at the price of rendering logic unable to fault anyone? If there is a version of claim 3 that escapes this, say what it costs elsewhere.

On claim 4, consider the deflationary alternative: if the regress runs equally for induction and practical norms, perhaps it establishes nothing about the nature of rule-following and only the banal point that no finite set of premises entails a conclusion about what is to be done next — a point about the content of conclusions rather than about rules. Is there a discriminating case, one where the regress bites for deduction but not in virtue of the general point? If not, say so.

On claim 5, weigh the following: a reading which entails that a large and sophisticated literature has been addressing a non-question may thereby be indicting itself rather than that literature. Is claim 5 a bold explanation, or a symptom of a reading that has dissolved the puzzle instead of solving it? Also identify any version of the rule-circular strategy my reading cannot dismiss — for instance, one addressed explicitly to the reliability of a rule rather than to a subject's warrant for it.

On empirical exposure. My thesis is conceptual, yet it makes contact with findings: dissociations in which patients retain the ability to state a rule while losing the ability to apply it, or the converse; work on how reasoners behave when an inference conflicts with what they already believe; developmental evidence about children who apply rules they cannot articulate. Tell me which of these could bear on claims 2 and 3, which are merely suggestive, and which are irrelevant because my view is not a psychological hypothesis. Be firm about where the empirical hook is illusory.

Finally, and most importantly: has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? A dispositionalist account of rule-grasp has a standing escape — any non-dispositional candidate proposed for the grasp is redescribed as a disposition in disguise. Show me exactly where each of the five claims is genuinely exposed to refutation and where it is sheltered by its own vocabulary, and give me your judgment on whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind. I will not accept the general reassurance that conceptual theses are not falsifiable as empirical ones are and that this is therefore unobjectionable; I want to know whether this particular view risks anything at all.

Close with what the view explains that its rivals do not — the rule-circular reading, a quietist reading on which the dialogue dissolves without any theory of inference, and the reading on which the puzzle is genuinely about justification and admits of no solution — and, if there is no such discriminating consideration, say so plainly. Then give me the single objection I should most fear, and your verdict on whether practice-first survives.

No bibliography, please, and no hedged non-committal summary at the end.
