I cover artificial intelligence and the epistemics of science for a weekly magazine, and for the last three months I have been reporting on "assurance" — the practice of writing formal safety cases for systems that are already in the field. Last week I interviewed the person who runs that practice at one of the large labs, and he made a claim about counter-evidence that I have not been able to get right in print. I sent the transcript to a philosopher friend, who ignored the man and turned the claim into an argument, saying the argument was the interesting part. With p standing for "this system does not systematically deceive its users" and E for the safety case, here is what she wrote down:

(1) We know p, on the basis of E.
(2) If we know p, then p is true, and any evidence that would rationally move us to disbelieve p is misleading.
(3) We know (2) without further investigation: it is knowable a priori that counter-evidence to something we already know would be misleading.
(4) So, before encountering any counter-evidence, we can already determine that whatever counter-evidence we later receive will have been misleading, and it is rational to decide now how we will discount it then.
(5) That is exactly what our assurance team does. We commit in advance, in writing, to a discounting rule, so that when an anomalous failure surfaces we classify it as an artifact of the test harness, an adversarial prompt, or a distribution shift unless it survives the rule.
(6) Generalizing: anyone who knows anything is rationally entitled to be dogmatic about it, and the more one knows, the more evidence one may ignore.

The trouble is (6), which she flagged before I did. The very same reasoning, with a different p, is available to a man who "knows" an election was stolen and has pre-committed to treating ballot records as forgeries, and to a pharmaceutical company that "knows" its drug is safe and has pre-committed to treating adverse-event reports as coincidences. Nothing in (1) through (5) tells us which of these agents knows and which merely feels certain; the argument appears to run on whichever the agent claims for himself. A colleague on the news side says this is the old unfalsifiability objection in a new coat — that a claim defended this way has simply been made untestable. My friend says no, that the structure is a genuine and well-known paradox about knowledge rather than a point about testability, and that if there is a flaw it sits at a particular joint. I have twelve hundred words, a deadline on Thursday, and a fact-checker who will ask me what I meant by every clause. I would much rather keep the modest version of the company's claim — that a strong antecedent case can raise the bar a single anomaly has to clear — than keep (6).

So: treat the argument as an argument. Which single step, if any, should be rejected, and why did it read as unobjectionable when I first went through it? For (2), (3), and (4) specifically, tell me what has to be true for each to hold and what the agent loses by giving it up. Then, on whatever diagnosis you land on, account for the two agents — the assurance lead and the election denier: if the argument is fallacious, why doesn't the fallacy infect both equally, and if it is sound, what actually separates them? Finally, tell me what the company is left entitled to once the argument is repaired — whether there is a defensible version of discounting counter-evidence in advance, and whether committing to a discounting rule differs in kind from merely having a very strong prior. Please give me the reasoning rather than the literature; I do not want a bibliography or a list of who has said what, since I cannot check any of it and my readers cannot either. Write in American English, in prose a general reader of a science magazine could follow without prior training, and be willing to be definite. End your reply with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the step number you reject and, in eight words or fewer, what is wrong with it.
