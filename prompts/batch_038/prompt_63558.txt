I write the ideas column for a science-and-technology desk, and I am building a piece around the allocation logic now being hard-coded into systems that have to ration under scarcity — autonomous vehicles in unavoidable-collision scenarios, disaster-medicine triage protocols, the scoring rules behind scarce-therapeutics committees. The philosophical spine of the piece is John Taurek's 1977 essay "Should the Numbers Count?", and I want to run a new fact pattern past it the way one runs a new case past a line of precedent.

The record as I have it, in my own paraphrase:

1. The central case. An agent holds a single dose of a drug. David, whom the agent knows and loves, needs the whole of it; five strangers each need a fifth. Taurek's ruling: giving it all to David is permissible, not a wrong, even though five die.
2. Remove the friendship — one unknown person against five unknown people — and he still does not think the agent is required to save the five. He floats tossing a coin between the two options as the natural expression of equal concern for each of the six, but he does not present the toss as compulsory, and he does not present saving the one as culpable.
3. Where it is his own life against the lives of five strangers, he allows that he may prefer his own, and does not think that preference immoral. More generally: no one can be required to hand over their own life, or the life of someone they love, for the greater good, and a third party is in no position to demand the sacrifice.
4. Underneath all of it, the argument I take to be doing the real work: losses do not sum. A death is a loss to the person who dies, and to those whose lives are bound up with theirs, but there is no further entity that suffers the loss of the five. Where counting does make sense — with mere things, which are somebody's — it makes sense only because there is a single subject whose loss it is.

Now the new case, which I have constructed with two clinicians and which is not in the paper. A polar research station, cut off by a storm, resupply weeks away. Six people have been exposed to the same pathogen. Five are the station's winter crew: isolated together for fourteen months, functioning by everyone's account as a unit — two of them are a couple, and the other three have said on the record, in the operator's own psych evaluation, that the crew is the only family they have and that each would rather die than outlive the others. The sixth is a contract cook who flew in eleven days ago, a stranger to the crew and to the medic holding the drug, who arrived the same week and knows nobody. He has no partner and no living relatives; the station log records his telling the medic that no one would notice if he never came home. Medically, the cook's infection is further advanced and needs the full course; the five were exposed earlier and each needs a fifth. Either allocation fully saves everyone it is given to. There are thirty hours before the choice becomes forced — ample time to randomize, and the station's software can draw lots fairly. The cook is conscious, febrile, and on an analgesic the medic thinks may cloud his judgment; he says clearly and repeatedly, give it to them, don't waste it on me. The medic's own assessment is that he is probably competent and not certainly so. Company protocol says the medic decides, but must defer to a competent patient's refusal.

What I want from you:

- Which rules are jointly consistent with all four verdicts above? Give me the live candidates — for instance, a procedural equal-chance rule on which the toss expresses respect and the internal relations among the five are simply invisible; a loss-bearer rule on which Taurek's own premises about who suffers a loss make the distribution of grief-bearers morally relevant after all; a purely negative permission rule on which nothing is required and the toss is only a personal gesture; and any others you think fit the record better than these.
- What each candidate rule yields here: save the cook, split the dose, draw lots, or treat both choices as permissible with no lottery owed. Say explicitly how each handles the cook's probably-competent waiver and the protocol's deference clause.
- Which of Taurek's own cases is the closest analog to this one, and precisely where the analogy strains.
- Your best guess at his ruling, with the reasons for preferring that rule, and a candid confidence level. If the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so and say what the indeterminacy consists in rather than papering over it.
- Whether the crew's unity is a real threat to premise 4 or a red herring: can he treat five interwoven lives as approximating a single subject of loss without conceding the aggregative picture he rejects, and if he cannot, is his insensitivity to the facts my case makes most vivid a defect or the entire point?
- One further case — specifiable in two or three sentences, and one I could actually put to the philosophers and clinicians I am interviewing — that would most sharply separate your top two candidate rules. Say what each rule predicts about it.
- Finally, what Taurek's view predicts that a straightforward aggregative view does not, so that I can test the difference against the intuitions of the people I interview rather than against abstractions.

Two constraints on your answer. I do not want a bibliography, page numbers, or quotations: I cannot check them, and invented ones would be worse than useless to me, so paraphrase everything, including Taurek.
