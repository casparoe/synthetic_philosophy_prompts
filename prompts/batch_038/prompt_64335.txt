I have settled on a particular reading of Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial cult, and I want it put under pressure rather than summarized back to me. The reading — call it the strong concessionary reading — makes four connected claims.

First, in Guide III:32 Maimonides argues that God did not command Israel to abandon sacrifice, because a people cannot abruptly give up the form of worship to which custom has bound them; the familiar apparatus of temple, altar, offering, and incense was retained but redirected exclusively to God, so that idolatry would be effaced without demanding an impossible psychological leap. Second, it follows that these commandments are willed not for their own sake but for the sake of a further end: nothing in the act of slaughtering and burning an animal pleases God, and the entire religious value generated is a function of displacing idolatrous worship and habituating the community to obedience and reverence. Third, the particulars — the species permitted, the procedures, the fixed seasons — are consequently indifferent in themselves, except where they carry anti-idolatrous content. Fourth, none of this touches their legal standing: Maimonides expects the messianic king to restore the sacrifices, as he says in Hilkhot Melakhim 11. The resulting category is an institution that is permanently binding and nowhere desirable — provisional in rationale, perpetual in force.

What would count against this? I want a map of the reading's exposure, not a defense of it and not a general survey of ta'amei ha-mitzvot. Please do the following.

1. Distinguish defeaters that are textual and internal to Maimonides' corpus from those that are conceptual, turning on whether the category itself is coherent. Rank everything you produce from fatal, through serious, to merely awkward, and say why each sits where it does.

2. Address these pressure points individually, stating for each whether it is a genuine defeater, a defeater only against a weaker version of my claim, or no threat at all:
   - The Mishneh Torah's entire book of Temple service, legislated in exhaustive and evidently reverent detail, together with the daily liturgical petition for the restoration of the offerings that Maimonides retains in his laws of prayer. What does it take to legislate and to pray for a merely concessive institution?
   - The ninth of his thirteen principles, on the permanence of the Torah: nothing is to be added to it or subtracted from it. Does "commanded but not willed" amount to subtraction in principle, and does he have the resources to deny that?
   - Guide III:26 supplies reasons for the particulars themselves — the species commanded for sacrifice are precisely those venerated by the Egyptians and by the Sabian sects. Does giving a rationale for the details contradict my claim that the details are indifferent, or is instrumental specification fully compatible with indifference as to content?
   - The asymmetry drawn in III:32 between sacrifice, confined to one place and one tribe, and prayer, permitted to anyone anywhere at any time. I treat this as supporting evidence. What would have to be true for it instead to show that sacrifice keeps a positive standing of its own, merely a contained one?
   - Nahmanides' attack in his Torah commentary on Leviticus 1:9 — that on this account the table of the Lord is defiled, that offerings are being reduced to a polemic against fools, and that the service answers a need on high. Which parts of that objection actually engage my claim rather than a weaker relative of it? And does Maimonides' God — incorporeal, impassible, whose will is not distinct from His wisdom — have any purchase from which to answer?
   - Sacrifices offered and received favorably before Sinai, by Noah and by the patriarchs, on the one hand, and the prophetic texts that deny the offerings were what God commanded at the exodus, on the other. Which way does each of these cut, and can the same datum be used by both sides?
   - The metaphysical worry: if God wills nothing except in accordance with wisdom, can there be a commanded act that God does not will for its own sake? Is my category available inside Maimonides' account of divine will, or does it require a distinction he explicitly refuses?

3. Tell me what the strong concessionary reading predicts that its rivals do not, and which textual signatures would discriminate among it, the weaker view on which the cult has real but subordinate value as training of the imagination toward God, and the Nahmanidean theurgical view. How each of the three handles the recurrent biblical formula describing the offering as a pleasing odor to the Lord seems to me the sharpest test; say whether you agree, and what the others would be.

4. Report which of these defeaters a committed concession-reader would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss, and be explicit about the standard escape routes: that the Guide is deliberately esoteric, that the halakhic writings are exoteric and pitched at a different audience, that Maimonides elsewhere freely reinterprets aggadic material. Then assess honestly whether, once those routes are open, my position risks anything at all — and whether an interpretive thesis that can absorb every counterexample is thereby worthless, or merely difficult to dislodge in the normal way.

A note on what would move me: considerations drawn from inside Maimonides' own framework will weigh far more with me than objections that depend on accepting Nahmanides' metaphysics of the commandments. Say where you think my stated weighting is itself a way of insulating the view.

Do not give me a bibliography or a list of scholarship; reason from the primary material you can recall. Where your memory of a passage's exact wording is uncertain, paraphrase and flag the uncertainty rather than reconstructing a quotation, and do not invent chapter numbers.

Finally, close by saying what would change your mind about your own assessment: which single consideration, if it came out the other way than you have assumed, would most alter your ranking, and at which point in the above your reasoning is most likely to have failed.
