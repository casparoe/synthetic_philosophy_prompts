I've been trying to settle, for myself and not for a class, whether I actually believe in completed infinities or am just saying I do. My method has been to write down concrete cases and record my gut verdict, then look for the pattern. Here's the list as it stands.

1. Hilbert's hotel, full, one new arrival, everybody shifts up a room: completely fine. Also fine when an infinite coachload turns up and everyone moves to room 2n. No resistance at all.
2. A genuinely fair lottery on the natural numbers, every number equally likely: impossible. Not impractical — impossible, couldn't exist even in principle.
3. A dart thrown at the unit interval that lands on some particular real, with each individual real having probability zero: fine, I accept that without blinking.
4. Zeno: Achilles really does cross infinitely many intervals, they're all there, all actual, and he finishes. Fine.
5. Thomson's lamp, toggled at a half minute, three-quarters, seven-eighths, and so on: I don't think it's impossible to wire up, but I think there's simply no fact about whether it's on at the end of the minute, and I hate that.
6. A ball that rolls halfway to the wall, then halfway again, forever, arriving at t=1: fine, and its position at t=1 is the wall.
7. An infinite future of days: fine. An infinite past of days, all of them elapsed to get me to today: impossible. I feel this one very strongly and I know full well it's the same sequence run backwards.
8. A hotel with one room per natural number where a crew demolishes exactly one room per day forever: at no moment were they all gone, and nothing ever removed more than one, and yet at the end there are none, and I want to say that's incoherent.
9. The vase: add ten balls and remove the ball numbered the same as the step, forever — vase empty at the end, count grew without bound. Or add ten and remove the highest-numbered one you just added — infinitely many left. I accept both, and I think the difference is about *which* balls, not *how many*.
10. Set of all the naturals: exists. Set of all sets: doesn't. The ordinals: not a set. Never felt any pull to revise that.
11. Case 8 is case 9's first version with rooms instead of balls, and I say something has gone badly wrong in one of them and not the other. I can't defend that split and I know it.
12. A hotel whose rooms run 1, 2, 3, ... and then one more room at the very end, call it room ω. Full. A guest arrives. The obvious move — everyone shifts up one — doesn't work, there's nowhere to put the ω guest. But you can do it a different way: leave ω alone, send n to 2n, new guy takes room 1. I think that works, and I find it more disturbing than the plain hotel, which shouldn't be the case since it's strictly easier.

The principle I keep reaching for, crudely, is that it's never the *number* of things that's the problem, it's whether something has to *get through* them one after another. Achilles gets through his intervals because they're nested inside a finite stretch with a limit at the end; the counter in an infinite past never gets through anything. I honestly don't know if that's a principle or a bumper sticker, and I already suspect it breaks on 2 and 8, where nothing traverses anything and stuff just sits there, and where I nonetheless have a verdict. My roommate's take is that I've just decided I dislike infinities that face left, which is funny but not obviously wrong.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, precise enough that I could apply it to a case nobody's thought of yet — not a slogan. For each clause, tell me which numbered verdict above it's there to save, because I want to see the machinery. Then run it on the case I actually go back and forth on: an infinitely old universe where, once per day for all of past eternity, one new room gets built and one new guest moves in, so that today the hotel is full with countably many rooms and countably many guests. That looks like Hilbert's hotel stretched out through time, which by 1 is fine, and it also looks like reaching infinity by successive addition, which by 7 is impossible, and I can't hold both readings at once. Also apply it to the ω hotel in 12, and tell me whether it says anything about a hotel whose rooms have order type ω+ω, or one room per countable ordinal — I have no intuition there at all and I suspect that's a sign my intuitions are keyed to something other than size. Then be blunt about which of my verdicts have to go. I'd rather sacrifice 6 or 9 than 7, but tell me what each concession costs and whether 11 is really the pair you'd attack first. And if the best principle on offer turns out to imply that 5 and 6 are the same kind of case and my split between them is a prejudice, say so — and say what would have to be true about physical quantities, or about which of them are real, for that to come out right. Finally, invent a case or two I haven't listed that would discriminate between the candidates you end up with, and tell me what each principle predicts about it.
