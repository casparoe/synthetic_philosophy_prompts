Last night I sat through nearly three hours of argument between two people in my epistemology reading group, and I did almost none of the talking. Our topic for the past month has been higher-order evidence — evidence not about the world but about the trustworthiness of your own cognitive position — and the connected question of whether a rational agent can be epistemically akratic, that is, whether you can rationally believe P while believing that believing P is not permitted by your evidence. Dana, a fourth-year working on testimony, and Marcus, a second-year who came over from statistics, went at it until roughly one in the morning. I have transcribed the case and their exchanges from my notes as faithfully as I can manage, and I have numbered the moves below. I found myself pulled in different directions at different points, sometimes within a single sentence, which is exactly why I want a verdict move by move rather than a balanced summary.

The case both of them were reacting to is this, and I will state it once because much of the dispute turns on how it is described. A researcher has pulled an all-nighter running a long statistical analysis of a clinical dataset and concludes that the treatment has a real effect. The next morning a careful colleague tells her three things: she was running on four hours of sleep; three corrections have appeared in her published work over the past two years and all three involved sign errors in her code; and she cannot now reconstruct the intermediate steps of last night's analysis from memory. Nothing about the data or the model has changed — she retains precisely the first-order evidence she had when she formed the belief. Should her confidence in the conclusion come down?

1. **Dana:** Yes, and the reason is that there is no principled way to treat facts about one's own cognitive condition as epistemically inert. If a colleague she trusted had reached the same conclusion after an all-nighter, and she had been told the same three things about that colleague, she would discount the conclusion. The only difference in her own case is that the colleague is her, and treating that as epistemically significant is simply claiming an exemption for oneself.
2. **Marcus:** He agrees that something must change, but denies that it has to be the first-order belief. These are two different claims: that the treatment works is a claim about the world; that he is not currently in a position to assess his own reasoning is a claim about his cognitive position. He can hold both, and he thinks holding both is the correct response — even though he concedes it is, by the letter of the enkratic principle, an incoherent state. His view is that the principle is what has to go.
3. **Dana:** Two tests show it is not a genuine state. First, the avowal test: asked to say it out loud, Marcus would have to say that the treatment works and that he ought not believe that it works, and that is a sentence no one can coherently assert. Second, and more importantly, the behavioral test: he would not submit the paper without flagging the analysis for replication, he would not tell his students the result, and he would not bet on it at ordinary stakes. That is not the profile of someone who believes P; it is the profile of someone with middling confidence in P, and the "level-split" is just lowered credence wearing a costume.
4. **Marcus:** He distinguishes readings of the principle. Read narrowly — if you believe that believing P is impermissible, then you must not believe P — it is false, because it can be made to conflict with the requirement to follow your evidence, and ought implies can. Read widely — you may not both believe P and believe that believing P is impermissible — it is satisfiable, but only by abandoning one of the two beliefs, and it says nothing about which one to abandon. Dana needs the narrow reading, and the narrow reading is precisely the one that cannot be a genuine requirement, since in cases of misleading higher-order evidence no doxastic state satisfies it.
5. **Dana:** Ought-implies-can does not get him what he wants. If the conflict is unavoidable because one piece of his evidence is misleading, then his failure to be coherent is excused rather than correct, and blameless irrationality is still irrational; he is running together being reasonable with being right. She adds that his view has a corrosive consequence: it lets anyone insulate a belief by acquiring, or manufacturing, higher-order doubt about his own reliability in that domain, so the sincerely self-distrustful dogmatist gets a free pass that the honest inquirer does not.
6. **Marcus:** He offers an asymmetry of errors. If he lowers his confidence and the analysis turns out to have been sound, he has destroyed something he earned at real cost and cannot reconstruct, since he no longer remembers the steps and re-running costs a week. If he keeps the belief and the analysis turns out to have been flawed, he is protected by the hedges he has already built in — the replication note, the colleague re-running the script. So keeping the belief while accepting the higher-order verdict is the risk-managing response, and it is not ad hoc, because higher-order evidence about one's own impairment is a guess made from the outside, whereas the analysis is something one actually did.
7. **Dana:** This converts an evidential question into a pragmatic one. If the reason to retain the belief is that losing it is expensive, the belief is being kept for its usefulness rather than its truth, and Marcus has left the debate about evidence altogether. Worse, the argument generalizes: on that reasoning one should retain every belief whose loss would be costly to re-earn, so the more effort one has sunk into a belief, the harder it becomes to dislodge, which inverts how evidence ought to operate. She also denies his closing premise: that higher-order evidence is systematically noisy while first-order assessment is not is an article of faith, given that the case is stipulated to involve someone who cannot reconstruct her own reasoning.

At that point they agreed on exactly one thing — that the case had been badly designed — and neither would characterize the other's position fairly enough to satisfy the other, so it ended there.
