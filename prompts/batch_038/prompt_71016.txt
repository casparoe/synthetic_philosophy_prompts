So I'm giving a 40-minute talk at a sleep-and-consciousness research conference this spring, and the room is not going to be friendly. It's mostly empirical people: dream-lab PIs who run overnight awakening protocols and code dream content, one guy who does lucid-dream signaling studies out of a sleep lab, a couple of people doing neuroimaging of sleep stages, a clinician who treats nightmare disorder in PTSD patients, and about three philosophers of psychology who are all higher-order-theory types. Nobody in there is going to be on my side, and what they care about is whether a physiological measure predicts a report and whether their own paradigm survives my argument.

My thesis is the strong one: dreaming is not an experience. Not "we forget most of our experiences," not "some dreams are experiences and some aren't" — the Malcolm-style line that there is no ongoing inner show that the morning report describes, that the dream is authored rather than witnessed, plus the Dennett point about why there's nobody home during it anyway. My version has a twist I need defended, because it's where I'm most exposed: I'm not a skeptic about the data. I take the high recall rate from REM awakenings at face value and explain it as a high *disposition* to confabulate at the wake boundary. And I run a two-stage story — (1) during the night there's fragmentary affect and imagery with no narrative unity and no subject to whom it appears, and (2) binding into a story happens in the transition to waking. I want to say stage 1 isn't experience either, it's just subpersonal material, so I'm not sneaking experience back in through the fragments.

Give me my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight-ish questions I'll actually get from *this* audience — not the ones another analytic philosopher would ask me — ranked by how much damage each would do to the talk. Phrase each one the way that specific person would phrase it: the dream-lab PI who has 30 years of REM/NREM report-rate data, the lucid-dreaming guy who has subjects signaling with pre-agreed eye movements from inside verified REM, the nightmare clinician whose patients are visibly suffering and who runs imagery rehearsal therapy on the dreams themselves, the higher-order theorist who will say my "fragments without a subject" is either an experience with a weird shape or nothing at all. Then give me roughly 90 seconds of answer for each, and a blunt note on where my answer is soft or where I'm just restating the thesis back at them.

Also tell me what to concede in the first ten minutes so I don't come across as a crank, which single reply has to land or the whole position collapses, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks — with something to say if it comes anyway. And give me a line for climbing out of the lucid-dreaming rabbit hole once I'm ten minutes deep in it.

Oh, and there's a methodological one coming about unfalsifiability — that I explain the recall data away by assuming there was nothing there to recall, so nothing could count against me. Put that where it belongs in the ranking, because I think it's more dangerous than I'm admitting.

Write it in American English please, and no reading lists or citations — I know the literature, I just need the arguments and the tactics.
