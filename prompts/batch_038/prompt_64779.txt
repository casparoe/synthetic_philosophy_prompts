Three weeks out from a departmental workshop talk and I'm starting to sweat the Q&A more than the paper.

The room: about fifteen people. Mostly environmental historians, two historians of medicine and empire, one economic historian who does climate econometrics and thinks entirely in instrumental variables, a few grad students who've read to much Latour, and my chair, a social historian of famine who is deeply allergic to what she calls geography-as-protagonist. Nobody in the room is a philosopher, which is both a relief and a problem.

The hill I've picked. I want to argue that a non-human cause earns the word "actor" in historical explanation under exactly one condition: when it answers back — when what the cause does next is a function of what people did about it. A pathogen that evolves resistance after we deploy a drug answers back; a pest that shifts to a new host crop once we've wiped out the first answers back; a volcano that takes its top off and chills the stratosphere for two years does not, however enormous the consequences, because it never registers our response. So actorhood on my account isn't about magnitude of effect, isn't about unpredictability, isn't about who's to blame, and isn't about the cause having anything like intentions. It's about the causal arrow running both ways. I'm calling it the reciprocity criterion, and my claim is that this is precisely what stops the whole enterprise from sliding back into environmental determinism: determinism is what you get when the environment sets the terms and people only ever react, and the answers-back test is a test for whether that's actually the shape of the case.

Rough argument in the paper: (1) historians already grant something like agency to germs and weeds and withhold it from rocks and ash, but they can't articulate why, and the practice is currently incoherent; (2) the available criteria — magnitude, unpredictability, moral salience, Latour's flat ontology of actants — all either over- or under-include badly; (3) reciprocity draws the line roughly where the practice already is and explains why the line feels right once you see it; (4) it isn't idle — it tells you what's wrong with "it was really just a drought" framings of famines, and it turns the history of antibiotic use from a chronicle of human error into a chronicle of a two-sided negotiation.
