Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about something I have never been able to name, and I would like a considered opinion better informed than either of ours.

Here is the configuration I keep running into. In a political transition, a perpetrator applies for amnesty. The condition of receiving it is that he describe what he did completely and truthfully, in public and under oath, in front of the people he harmed: the names, who gave the order, where the remains were buried. A commission tests his account against the evidence, finds it complete, and certifies it. The state then declares that in law he bears no liability, criminal or civil, for the acts he has just described. One institution therefore produces, in the same proceeding and at the same moment, an official finding that a thing happened and an official determination that it shall carry no legal consequences. The best-evidenced account of the crime in the entire country is the perpetrator's own certified sworn narrative, and that narrative is simultaneously the reason no court may act on it. Afterwards the victim's family possesses an authoritative public record of exactly what was done to them, issued by the state, which entitles them to nothing.

I have a domestic near-case that feels related but not identical. A witness compelled to testify under a grant of immunity must tell the whole truth, and his own truthful admission cannot be turned against him; there too the state purchases a certified true account of a crime it has agreed not to pursue. My instinct says the two differ in kind — the immunity arrangement seems to disable one particular piece of evidence, whereas the case above seems to unmake the liability for the act itself, no matter what the evidence shows — but I cannot tell whether that instinct tracks anything real or is merely my attachment to the puzzle.

My friend's line is that I have manufactured a mystery out of an ordinary procedural bargain: amnesty with a disclosure condition, nothing more, and whatever strangeness I keep pointing at is a legal fiction of the usual sort. That last move is what I most want to press on. A legal fiction, as I understand it, directs us to treat something false as true. What I am describing runs the other way: it establishes at considerable cost, and with genuine evidentiary care, that something is true, and then voids its consequences. I do not think one word can cover both operations, and I do not think "fiction" is the right word for the second.

So my question is whether there is an established concept or a literature for this thing — a state certifying a proposition as true and rendering it legally inert, where the certification is the very price of the nullification. I am not looking for a bare label; the label is the least important part. What I want is the carving. Tell me what the phenomenon actually is, in terms precise enough that I could recognize a new instance of it in the wild. Tell me which of the neighboring ideas fail to cover it — blanket amnesty granted in silence, indemnity, pardon after conviction, sealing or expungement of a record, compelled testimony, officially sponsored forgetting, and the several varieties of denial — and for each, exactly where the overlap ends. If your view is that one of them does cover it after all, say so and show me what my description is adding beyond that concept. And if nothing established fits, say that plainly and coin a term: give a definition with conditions sharp enough to settle the boundary cases I care about. Does an amnesty granted without any disclosure count? Does a commission report whose named perpetrators are subsequently prosecuted count? Does a conviction whose record is later sealed count, given that the order of operations is reversed there? What would be an unambiguous non-instance?

Please close with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing one short phrase: the established term you judge closest, or the term you coin if you conclude that none exists.
