I am a junior at Harrow Bend High, and this year I have been seated on the Lattimer County flood centennial committee, which is responsible for the text of seven aluminum wayside panels along a walking trail that opens next spring. Panel six stands where the north dike used to be, and the draft has fallen to me because I am the only member who has spent serious time both in the county records room and in the kitchens of the families from Sinker Bottom, the settlement on the low ground that the water took. This is not a class assignment; the panels are really being cast. The two sets of accounts do not agree, and the committee has reached the point where it would rather I produce something than hear any further discussion of why they disagree.

Here is the shape of it. In March of 1926 the river came up, and the town of Harrow Bend did not flood while Sinker Bottom did. The county engineer's report, the entry in the town council's minute book for that week, and the account in the weekly paper all describe a decision to open the north dike so that the water would go into the bottomland rather than down Main Street. The paper gives the vote as four to two and names the men. But the families in the Bottoms have always said that nobody voted anything: that men working for the railroad cut the dike with dynamite somewhere around three in the morning without warning anyone, and that the story of a council vote was composed afterward, partly so the council could be thanked for saving the town and partly so the county could be approached for money. What complicates my position is that neither account comes out clean. The minute book's entry for that meeting is a single line saying that the north dike matter was taken up, with no tally and no names; the four-to-two vote appears only in the paper, whose editor was the town clerk's brother-in-law. The relief ledger lists eleven households paid, while the census sheet from 1920 counts thirty-one households in the Bottoms, which the elders say is precisely the point, since a ledger can only pay people who hold a deed and most families there had never been permitted to hold one. On the other side, the remembered story slips as well: my grandmother's cousins place the night on the Sunday before Easter, the paper places it on a Tuesday, and the two branches of the family disagree about which dike was opened at all.

The committee is split three ways and I owe them a draft in nineteen days, because the foundry needs six weeks. The historical society president, whose grandfather sat on that council, calls the dynamite account a grudge with a hundred years on it and wants panel six to leave it out entirely. My fellow student says we should print both versions and let readers weigh them. The county historian, who is more careful than the others and has told me plainly that the minute book's silence is not nothing, objects that a panel is not a courtroom and that we cannot place a claim the records will not carry onto public land under the county seal. My grandmother, for her part, has told me she does not want her mother's account on a sign with a question mark hanging off the end of it, because being labeled a family story amounts to being dismissed. I want to know what the panel should actually say, given that I have one hundred fifty words and a piece of metal that will stand in the rain for thirty years. But underneath that is a question I do not know how to put properly: when the account a community has carried in its families for four generations contradicts the account the paper trail supports, which of the two is supposed to give way, and on what grounds? Everyone at the meeting spoke as though the documents were simply what happened and the memories were simply what people feel, and I could tell that this was wrong without being able to say how. Please give me the reasoning as well as the recommendation, and tell me honestly if the right move is to hand this draft back to the committee instead of writing it.
