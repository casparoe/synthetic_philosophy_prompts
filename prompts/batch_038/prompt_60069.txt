Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Reproduction isn't built into the concept of selection. What matters is heritable variation in persistence-and-proliferation, so differential sorting of entities that lack a bottlenecked life cycle or a germ line still counts as selection — which is why I'm comfortable calling species-level sorting "selection."
2. The Price equation partition is substantive rather than a trivial identity: splitting change into between- and within-components tells you something real about the population.
3. But which partition you write down is descriptive. The contextual-analysis and neighbour-model formulations are intertranslatable, and the same covariance can be rewritten as either a group effect or a relatedness effect, so the formalism doesn't decied whether group selection is happening.
4. There's still a fact of the matter about the level at which selection acts, and it's a fact about causal process. I want to resist the old line that group vs. kin selection is a level of description rather than a level of causation.
5. MLS1 and MLS2 are different animals. Only MLS2, where groups are themselves reproducers with heritable group-level characters, can yield group-level adaptations; trait-group structure alone gets you a statistical group effect at most.
6. For explaining adaptations I'm basically a gene's-eye-view person. r in Hamilton's rule should be read as a regression coefficient, and inclusive-fitness maximization only goes through under further conditions like additive gene action.
7. I also think species are individuals in the Ghiselin–Hull sense, and that this is what makes them candidates for being units.
