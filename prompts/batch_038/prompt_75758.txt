**Question 3 (25 marks)**

When Huizi comes to offer condolences on the death of Zhuangzi's wife, he finds him drumming on a basin and singing. In explanation, Zhuangzi retraces the stages that led to her being alive at all, and compares the sequence to the running of the four seasons.

(a) Set out the reasoning Zhuangzi offers there, and say what work the comparison with the seasons is doing in it. [10 marks]

(b) Later in the same part of the text, a skull tells Zhuangzi that death is more pleasant than the happiness of a king facing south. Does Zhuangzi hold that death is a good for the one who dies, or only that it is not an evil? Justify your answer by reference to at least one further passage. [10 marks]

(c) Could someone who rejected Zhuangzi's account of the Dao nonetheless adopt the position you have described? [5 marks]

Classification: 70 per cent and above, first; 60–69, upper second; 50–59, lower second; 40–49, third; below 40, fail.

**Candidate's answer**

(a) The episode is the clearest statement of Zhuangzi's attitude to death in the whole text, and its first move is easy to miss. He does not deny that he has lost anything: he says that when his wife first died he was as distressed as anyone else. What altered his response was reflection on her origins. Before she was born there was no life; before there was life there was no form; before there was form there was no vital breath; and out of the undifferentiated blur there came breath, breath changed and there was form, form changed and there was life, and now a further change has brought death. The comparison with the seasons does two things at once. It presents death as one phase in a cycle that continues rather than as a termination, so that resenting it is like resenting winter for following autumn; and it presents the phases as equal in standing, so that none of them is the point of the others. That is why he says he stopped: to go on wailing while she lies at rest in the vast chamber would show that he had not understood what is appointed.

The same structure governs the passage in the chapter containing Cook Ding's carving of the ox, where the mourner who comes to Laozi's corpse cries out three times and leaves, and explains that the old people weeping as though for a son of their own are escaping from heaven and turning their backs on what was given them, whereas one who is content to dwell in the season and at ease in the position is beyond the reach of sorrow and joy. The argument, in both places, is about the mourner rather than about the dead.

Beneath it lies a stronger claim, which Zhuangzi shares with Epicurus: death cannot be an evil for the person who dies, because once it has arrived there is no subject left for it to be bad for. The rehearsal of the stages before birth is Zhuangzi's version of the symmetry argument, since the state before we were born was nothing to us and we do not grieve it, and the state we enter at death cannot therefore be an evil either.

(b) Zhuangzi does not stop at the negative claim. The skull tells him that in death there is no ruler above and no subject below, no business of the four seasons, and that one takes heaven and earth as one's spring and autumn; even the happiness of a king facing south could not surpass it. He is said not to believe it, and offers to have the skull's body and family restored; the skull recoils at the prospect of giving up a king's happiness for human toil. The episode belongs to the outer chapters, which are often taken to be the work of followers rather than of Zhuangzi himself, and that may be part of why the report is framed as a dream he doubts. The questions in the long discussion of equalising things point the same way: how do we know that delighting in life is not a delusion, and how do we know that the dead do not regret having longed to live? So does the butterfly dream and the "transformation of things" ("物化") that closes it. My conclusion is that Zhuangzi regards death as not merely neutral but positively good for the one who dies.

He also thinks that something is carried through. The Cook Ding chapter ends with the image of the firewood being used up while the fire is passed on, and we do not know its end; and the chapter on the true man describes a progression of forgetting — the world, then things, then life itself — which ends in entering what is neither dying nor living. The self is not annihilated; it takes on a new shape. This is close to the doctrine of rebirth that Buddhism later developed, minus the machinery of karma. The two strands of the consolation are thus independent of one another, and the position survives whichever one prefers: if nothing survives, death is still not an evil, because there is nobody for it to be bad for; and if something survives, death is a good, because the transformation continues.

(c) It follows that the position does not depend on Zhuangzi's account of the Dao. All it requires is that death is a phase in a continuing process and that there is no subject to be harmed, and neither of these needs the cosmology of breath or the idea of a spontaneous ordering of things. Anyone, including someone with no metaphysical commitments whatever, could hold it. It follows, too, that the view cannot condemn killing: if death is not an evil for the one who dies, then killing cannot wrong its victim, and the position leaves us with no protection against being killed. That is a serious difficulty, and one Zhuangzi does not appear to notice.
