I am the technical assurance lead for a national deep geological repository programme. Roughly six times a year a modelling team brings my group a package of assumptions — groundwater flow, radionuclide transport, canister corrosion, buffer chemistry, biosphere conversion — and we have to decide, assumption by assumption, whether the package is fit to be built into a safety case. The decisions are effectively irreversible: once the facility is closed, the engineered barriers cannot be revisited, and the compliance horizon is of the order of a hundred thousand years. We are therefore in the business of committing enormous sums to scientific models over periods in which, historically, the theories underpinning such models have been replaced.

Our current in-house rule is a crude version of a pessimistic argument about science: if an assumption depends on a theoretical posit of a kind that has been eliminated before — the way caloric or phlogiston was — reject it; if it depends only on quantities that have survived theory change by being refined rather than dropped, accept it. It is unusable. Applied literally it rejects nearly everything, because almost every quantitative model in our packages rests on posits no engineer could point at in a laboratory. Applied loosely it is a mood, and it lets the reviewer's prior sympathy for or against a package decide the outcome while wearing the costume of a principle.

Some years ago, someone at a workshop described a distinction that seemed to cut the knot better, in roughly these terms: within a single theory, some posits were load-bearing for the predictions the theory actually got right, while others were merely part of the picture its authors painted around them; only the second group is genuinely exposed when the theory is overthrown. I do not know how much of that survives contact with the serious literature, and I do not want a literature review.

What I want is a decision procedure my reviewers can run on one assumption drawn from one package. Give the questions to ask, in order; state for each what counts as a sufficient answer and what does not; say where the procedure terminates in a rejection, where it terminates in acceptance with conditions, and where it stops and hands the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of risk appetite or of values rather than of fact; and specify how the outcome should be recorded so that a successor can audit the reasoning a decade later. It has to be runnable in ninety minutes per assumption by a hydrogeologist or a materials engineer with no training in philosophy. Our chief scientist signs off, and she will not sign anything whose rationale she could not restate to a regulator in two sentences, so every step needs a plain formulation — the technical vocabulary belongs inside the procedure, not in its output.

Please mark explicitly which steps are judgement calls dressed as procedure. I would rather see them labelled than see a false sense of mechanical resolution.

Tell me also what the procedure cannot do. I expect that at least one of its limits concerns theories nobody has yet thought of: the possibility that the right description of some part of our system has not occurred to anyone, which no screen on existing posits can detect. I want to know whether that limit is fatal to the enterprise or merely changes how the output should be used. Relatedly, I have heard it argued that the pessimistic case I am implicitly relying on draws on a biased sample, since failed theories are catalogued as failures while the ones that worked simply become invisible as science. Decide whether the procedure should be adjusted for that, and if so how, rather than relegating it to a footnote.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: Darcy flow through a fractured crystalline rock represented as an equivalent porous continuum, calibrated against tracer tests over metres and years and deployed over kilometres and millennia. Second: an Arrhenius-type rate law for the anaerobic corrosion of the copper canister shell, fitted to laboratory data at elevated temperatures over months and extrapolated to ambient conditions over geological time, where two competent groups disagree about whether the reaction proceeds at all under the relevant conditions. Third: a biosphere model converting a release rate into a dose to a future person using present-day diet, occupancy and land use — here the assumption is not theoretical in any interesting sense, but the procedure should still deliver a verdict, and if it cannot, that tells us something about its shape. At least one of the three must come out in a way the programme will not like. If the honest result is that our most heavily validated and most regulator-approved package carries the greatest exposure, say so and show why the procedure says it.

No citations, no reading list, no named authorities. If you are unsure who said what, describe the idea without attaching a name to it; we will not check, and a confident misattribution is worse than none.

Finally, I would rather be told now than after we have trained forty reviewers on the thing. If you think the request rests on a confusion — if, for example, the question a repository actually faces is not whether our theories are true but whether they remain adequate under extrapolation beyond their calibration range, so that the philosophical machinery I have invoked is doing no work at all — say so plainly and tell me what the procedure ought to be built on instead. Do not produce a compliant document merely to be agreeable.
