Last winter I helped write the pest-management policy for a regional land trust that leases about sixty acres of vegetable and small-fruit ground to seven tenant growers. I'm not an academic — I worked in insurance claims for twenty years and came to farming late — but I ended up drafting most of the document, and now that it is in force I keep finding clauses I cannot square with one another. The policy has four load-bearing pieces. First, every tenant must keep a written integrated pest management plan: weekly scouting, positive identification of the insect causing the damage, and a numeric action threshold below which no insecticide may be applied at all. Second, that threshold is defined in money — the level of crop injury at which the value of the loss prevented exceeds the cost of the treatment — and it is set crop by crop, usually as a percentage of leaf area eaten or a count of insects per plant. Third, only inputs on the national organic permitted list may be used: plant- and microbe-derived insecticides, copper and sulfur compounds, and a short list of allowed synthetics, with most conventional synthetic insecticides ruled out regardless of rate or timing. Fourth, we require a no-spray window while the crop is in bloom, plus a pollinator planting on at least five percent of each tenant's acreage. Alongside all of this we actively encourage the purchase and release of beneficial insects — lacewings, lady beetles, tiny parasitic wasps — and count that as a preferred control rather than a last resort.

What I want is a reconstruction of the view of value this document presupposes, because the parts seem to answer to different views and I cannot tell whether there is a single theory underneath or a stack of them that never got reconciled. The dollar threshold appears to treat the insects as nothing at all: the calculus runs over crop value and spray cost, and the deaths the spray causes enter only as a line item in that cost, not as harms. The permitted-input clause looks like it runs on a completely different currency — where a substance came from and how it was made, rather than how many insects it kills or how badly — and I have heard it argued that some of the broad-spectrum botanical and microbial products we require are, per unit of crop protected, harder on non-target insects than the synthetic products we forbid, which would mean the clause can select for more insect death while presenting itself as the more conscientious option. The bloom window and the pollinator planting protect one ecological guild, and in practice largely one managed species, at one moment in its life cycle; the same tank mix applied two weeks later is fully compliant. And the beneficial-insect clause treats living animals as equipment, buying them by the thousand and releasing them into conditions where most will starve or be eaten in turn. For each of the four pieces, tell me what it strictly requires and what it merely happens to fit: which would be licensed by an instrumentalist view on which insects matter only through their effects on human interests, which by a view on which naturalness or ecological role carries independent weight, which by a welfarist view, and where the practice is genuinely underdetermined because several theories predict the same clause.

Then I would like you to take seriously a substitution I have been turning over, and say whether it could actually bear weight. Instead of a threshold set in dollars, set it in welfare: spray when the expected harm prevented — to the plants, to the grower's livelihood, to the insects that will otherwise be eaten alive or parasitized from the inside, and to whatever else is genuinely at stake — exceeds the expected harm inflicted by the spray, with both sides discounted by how confident we should reasonably be that the insects involved are sentient at all. Three things about this worry me, and I want them addressed rather than set aside: it puts harms to the pest insects on the opposite side of the ledger from where the current policy puts them, since being slowly eaten is presumably worse for them than being killed outright; it makes the right answer depend on a probability of sentience nobody can measure, and one that may differ between a caterpillar and a beetle in ways that would flip decisions at the margin; and it may in the end reproduce almost exactly the decisions the dollar threshold already produces, in which case the change is expressive rather than practical. Finally, tell me what the document would have to look like if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which insects matter for their own sake — which clauses would go, which would survive intact, what scouting and record-keeping would have to start measuring that it currently ignores, and whether a policy of that kind could still be one that seven small farms can afford to sign.
