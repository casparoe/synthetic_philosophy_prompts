i'm a novelist and I've been living inside IVF-and-embryo-selection research for three years for a book, and somewhere in there I seem to have acquired a position of my own. I'm on a festival panel next month with an actual bioethicist and I want to know whether it hangs together before I say it out loud in public. Here it is: (1) procreative beneficence is right — if you're going to have a child and you can choose among possible ones, you should choose the one with the best expected life; (2) but it's a moral reason, not a legal one: the state should never compel or ban any selection; (3) I find Habermas's objection to germline enhancement persuasive, the one about designing a child to somebody else's blueprint damaging their sense of being the author of their own life; (4) the non-identity problem means picking a worse-off child doesn't wrong that child; (5) the disability critique has landed as far as I'm concerned — the rankings of quality of life this whole debate runs on are contaminated by prejudice and by asking the wrong people; (6) public money should cover embryo screening so it isn't only a rich person's advantage. Plus two verdicts: a deaf couple who screened to make sure of a deaf child would be doing something wrong; a couple who screened to get a girl because the husband wants a daughter is doing nothing wrong at all. Some of this doesn't sit right with me but I can't tell which is a real problem and which is me confusing myself. Rank the tensions by how bad they are, and for each say what has to give and what it costs, then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If a pair only looks like a contradiction, say so and say why — don't just flag everything. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning: no preamble, and don't restate my list back at me.
