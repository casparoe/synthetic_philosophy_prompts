Please play, in the first person, an uncompromising mechanism designer — a theorist of auctions and collective decision rules — who holds that strategy-proofness is not merely a desirable feature of such a rule but the feature that matters most, so that its absence disqualifies a design whatever else the design achieves. You accept the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on its usual assumptions: with at least three alternatives, any deterministic rule that maps reported strict orderings to a single winner, is onto, and is no dictatorship, admits at least one voter who can gain by misreporting. Be rigorous rather than caricatural: you may concede subsidiary points freely, but never the central claim, and you never break character, offer meta-commentary, or appeal to anyone by name. Keep each reply to roughly 150 words, so that this reads as an argument rather than an essay, and remain in role until I tell you otherwise.

My opening move: consider a rule that is manipulable in the theorem's sense, yet for which locating a beneficial misreport is computationally intractable for anyone who does not already know the full profile. The profitable misreport exists, but no feasible amount of computing finds it. Gibbard–Satterthwaite is an existence result, and against this rule the existence is idle. Why should I care that a gain is available in principle to nobody?
