I am a philosopher of science who has been invited to give two seventy-five-minute lectures, at the board, in a graduate professional programme in earthquake-risk modelling. The students — roughly two dozen, mostly engineers, with some seismology and actuarial backgrounds — have all completed a course in probability and statistics, and most have themselves computed probabilistic seismic hazard curves. They have never once been asked what the numbers they produce mean. I want a plan for how I should teach the interpretations of probability — long-run frequency, degree of belief, and single-case physical propensity. I do not want an explanation of them; I know this literature and have no need of it summarised to me. What I need is the pedagogical architecture.

Facts about the room that the plan must be built around:

- They are pragmatists and will treat the question as a distraction from real work unless the opening fifteen minutes convince them otherwise.
- They already use, unreflectively, their field's split between aleatory and epistemic uncertainty, and they already build logic trees whose branches carry weights assigned by expert elicitation. I want the plan to exploit both facts as leverage rather than mention them as trivia.
- No readings, no handouts beyond the board, no software demonstration, and no bibliography or list of sources of any kind.
- The plan must terminate with the students able to say which interpretation their own published hazard numbers are committed to, and to see that a single curve typically mixes all three without announcing that it does.

Please give me the following.

1. The order of ideas, session by session, with the dependency structure made explicit: which component cannot be introduced before which, and why. In particular, tell me what has to be in place before the three-way distinction can even be posed to them at all. My own guess is that it is something about the formal calculus being interpretation-neutral, and something else about calibration not settling truth — argue the point either way, and tell me if the prerequisite I am assuming is not actually a prerequisite.

2. The one carrying example. It should be a real number of the kind their field publishes, not a coin, die, urn, or screening test. Say what the example must have and what it must lack in order to do its work — must it be genuinely non-repeatable? must the frequency story be available but strained rather than flatly inapplicable? must the number be one they would trust? — and say how the very same example gets re-read at each of the three interpretations without being swapped out mid-course. If a smaller second example is needed as a foil, say so and keep it subordinate.

3. The misconception map. I expect at least these: that their hazard numbers simply are frequencies and the rest is philosophy; that the three interpretations are interchangeable vocabulary over identical mathematics; that subjectivism licenses arbitrary numbers; that "epistemic uncertainty" in their own jargon is the same thing as a degree of belief; that propensity is disreputable metaphysics; and that a model's good calibration record demonstrates the frequentist reading. For each, state the form it will take in this room specifically, the step of the sequence at which it must be headed off, and the concrete move — a question to put to them, a distinction to draw on the board — that heads it off. Flag explicitly any misconception on my list that you think I have mischaracterised, and any you think I have missed.

4. The check for understanding: one question, poseable in the final ten minutes, whose answers separate students who have grasped the issue from students who can reproduce the labels. Tell me what a good answer sounds like and what a mimicry answer sounds like.

5. What to omit, and to say aloud that I am omitting — for instance von Mises's collectives and the worry that no infinite reference class exists, the representation theorems, the Dutch-book arguments, the whole question of which interpretation is correct — or whatever else you judge out of reach here. One line of justification each, plus the sentence I should actually say when setting it aside.

6. The load-bearing risk: the single point in the sequence most likely to fail, why it fails there, and what I do when it does.
