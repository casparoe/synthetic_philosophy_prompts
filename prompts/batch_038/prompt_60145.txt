I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of blame, and this term I'm co-teaching a seminar with the English department on moral psychology in Jacobean drama. My session is Shakespeare's *Measure for Measure*, which I keep gravitating toward as a case study in standing to blame — the title, on the standard account, comes from the Sermon on the Mount, where the passage giving us the mote and the beam tells the would-be corrector to cast the beam out of his own eye first, "and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye." That conditional *then* is what interests me, and I suspect the play turns on whether it can be satisfied. For orientation, since not all my students will know the plot: Duke Vincentio withdraws from Vienna and leaves his deputy Angelo to revive a long-dormant statute against fornication; Angelo sentences Claudio to death for having slept with Juliet, to whom he is betrothed but not yet formally married; Claudio's sister Isabella, a postulant, begs for his life; Angelo offers the trade of Claudio's life for Isabella's body; the Duke, who has been walking the city disguised as a friar, hears all of it and arranges the bed trick with Mariana, whom Angelo jilted when her dowry was lost at sea; when Angelo nevertheless orders the execution, the head of a prisoner who has died in the gaol is sent instead; and on the Duke's return Mariana and Isabella accuse Angelo publicly, the Duke sentences him to death, the two women plead, and he is pardoned — after which the Duke proposes marriage to Isabella, who says nothing at all.

The obvious reading — Angelo as textbook hypocrite, the play as a dramatic gloss on that passage — is where my session has stalled, because several features of the play seem to me to cut against the accounts of standing I know. First, Angelo is not self-deceived: his soliloquies are almost cruelly lucid about his own corruption, which makes him an awkward case for any account that locates the defect in insincerity, in self-blindness, or in a failure to endorse the norm one invokes. Second, the play keeps letting true judgment come from those with least standing to render it — the brothel keeper, the pimp, and the bumbling constable voice the soundest diagnosis of a city in which nobody's sexual life is clean; and Lucio, whose running slander of the Duke to the Duke's own disguised face is uncomfortably close to accurate, is whipped and married at the end for it while the Duke's far larger deceptions go uncensured. That is the distinction between a blaming's being unfair and its being unfitting, which my literature often blurs, staged rather than argued. Third, and most troubling, the judge is himself compromised: the Duke knows of Angelo's proposition for weeks, engineers a deception to expose it, and lets the sentence run far enough that a severed head has to be delivered before he intervenes. Fourth, the closing movement is dominated by self-blame rather than blame — Angelo, having imposed death on another man for this very offence, asks for his own execution as his deserts, and the Duke overrides the request and pardons him anyway, over his objection; and the pardon itself is extracted from the two women Angelo wronged or tried to, at the Duke's instruction. Whether a magistrate conscripting victims' pleas in the service of his own clemency is forgiveness, pardon, or something else is the question my students will press, and I don't have an answer.

So here is what I'd like: a reading of the play through the standing-to-blame literature that treats both the drama and the philosophy as serious, and that begins by taking a position on my own tentative thesis rather than summarizing it. The thesis is that *Measure for Measure* is not about the loss of standing but about its scarcity — that Shakespeare stages a city in which no one is entitled to condemn anyone, and resolves the impasse by installing a figure with near-absolute power who can pardon without needing standing at all, so that the much-maligned artificiality of the finale is the argument rather than a failure of it. Argue for or against that. Then tell me which account of what hypocrisy does to blame the play's dramaturgy actually supports and which it embarrasses: the unfairness account, on which the hypocrite exempts himself from a demand he presses on others; the second-personal account, on which he lacks the authority to address a demand as an equal; the insincerity or lack-of-commitment account; the complicity account; and the grandstanding account, on which the vice lies in using moral talk for status rather than in inconsistency as such. Say which scenes you would put in front of the class and why, including whether the Duke's early prison encounter with the pregnant Juliet does any work the main plot doesn't, and whether Isabella's final silence is philosophically load-bearing or merely a staging accident the critics have over-fed. And be candid where the reading resists: this is a comedy with a theological architecture and a set of farcical underplots that no moral-philosophical apparatus will absorb, and I would rather you say what the play does that argument alone cannot than see it flattened into an illustration of a thesis. Paraphrase rather than quote the text — we'll have the play in front of us — and please don't give me references or further reading; what I need is your judgment, defended.
