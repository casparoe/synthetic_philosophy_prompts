I'm six weeks into reporting a piece about people who deliberately sign up for experiences they expect will change their minds — my own case is a six-month embed with a community whose central claim I currently put at maybe 0.05. A philosopher I interviewed for the story walked me through the following, and I can't find the exit.

(1) Reflection: if I'm certain that at some later time I'll have credence r in p, then my credence in p now, conditional on that supposition, should also be r.
(2) The force of (1) is the Dutch book: if my present and future credences come apart that way, someone who knows both can sell me bets at each end that I'll accept at both ends and that lose me money come what may.
(3) I'm certain that in six months, having lived with these people, I'll be at 0.8 on their central claim — not because I expect to encounter evidence I'd now count as evidence, but because I know how embeds go, which is the whole point of the assigment.
(4) So by (1) I should be at 0.8 now.
(5) Which means the six months can't teach me anything: whenever I'm certain how I'll end up, I should already be there. And the same goes for any foreseeable change of mind — a conversion, a drug, a head injury, exhaustion, a propagandist I know I wont be able to resist.
(6) By parity it goes for other people too. My editor has read the same literature and can predict my 0.8 without going anywhere near them, so she should be at 0.8 as well, and so should any reader who can predict that about her.
(7) That's absurd. It makes a predictable opinion indistinguishable from a well-founded one, and it means I should now believe whatever I expect to be caused into believing by processes I currently regard as junk.
(8) So (1) is false, and the Dutch book argument behind it must be broken somewhere.
