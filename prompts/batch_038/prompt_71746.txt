I'm putting together a handout for a reading group that mixes historians with moral psychologists — this term's theme is explaining atrocity, and we open with the Browning/Goldhagen fight over Reserve Police Battalion 101 (middle-aged Hamburg reservists, a commander who lets anyone opt out of the first shooting action, only a handful who take him up on it). What I want is a decision tree, not an essay: a reader starts from "the facts of the case are settled and gruesome" and ends up at a named position about what's doing the explanatory work.

Every branch node has to be a question someone could answer without knowing where it leads — questions about explanation, not labels: would this same man have shot in a differently structured situation? does a stable trait buy you anything the situation doesn't already give you? does the antisemitism earn its place in the account, or is it a post-hoc decoration? does removing the peer pressure remove the killing? Leaves should include Browning's situational account, Goldhagen's ideology-as-cognitive-model account, the situationist attack on character traits (Doris/Harman flavor) and the virtue-ethics answer to it, something Arendtian about thoughtlessness, and at least one leaf you just call "unnamed."

Then tell me which answer-combinations your nodes make available that nobody actually occupies, and for each say whether it's incoherent, merely unappealing, or just underexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate — I'd rather see that admitted than papered over. No reading list or bibliography, the tree is the deliverable.
