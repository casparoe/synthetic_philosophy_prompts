I've got a 16-person honors seminar that reads GEB together, one 75-minute meeting a week, and we're on the chapter about the location of meaning — the part where Hofstadter says any message has three layers: the frame message (the sign that there's something here worth decoding at all), the outer message (the actual pattern presented), and the inner message (what the sender meant by it). Half of them skimmed it. I dont want to lecture them about it, I want them to have to build messages and crack other people's and run into the layers that way.

Constraints on materials: index cards and markers only, no printing budget, no phones except as a timer, and no prizes — I want the score to be something they care about for its own sake rather than for candy. And on the cards themselves: no letters, no digits, no English words. Shapes, marks, dots, whatever they invent. They have to bootstrap the conventions from nothing.

What I want them to come away having felt, roughly in this order:
1. that a pattern with no frame is just marks, and that they can't help trying to decode it anyway;
2. that the instant someone hands them the key, the meaning feels like it was sitting in the card the whole time, and they can't get back to not-seeing it;
3. that whether a given card "has a meaning in it" turns out to track something about the decoders as much as about the card;
4. some version of the worry that the key is itself just another pattern that needs a key.

Two things I'd really like built in if it doesn't make the game unrunnable. First, some of the cards in circulation should be deliberate noise — genuinely meaningless — and students should have to commit to whether a card is decodable before they find out, so that finding meaning in the noise costs them something. Second, at least one person should end up inside a frame they don't know they're inside. I'm okay with a bit of deception but I need you to tell me exactly what I'm allowed to say out loud, what has to stay in my head, and what has to be handed to someone on a card, because if I hint at it early the whole thing collapses.

So give me: the roles and the rules; the scoring (what earns points, what loses them, and how a round resolves); a minute-by-minute timeline that genuinely fits 75 minutes with 16 people, including where I lose time; and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments of play onto each of the three layers and onto the is-the-meaning-in-the-message-or-in-the-decoder question — I want "at 22 minutes, when Priya did X, that was the frame layer doing Y," not a general summary. Then an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, each with the fix.

Last thing: tell me straight which part of Hofstadter's picture this game cannot reproduce and that I'm going to have to supply by just talking. My guess is the record-and-record-player case, where the decoder is a machine physically realizing an isomorphism and there's no interpretation happening at all — but tell me whether that's really the biggest gap or whether there's something worse I'm not seeing.
