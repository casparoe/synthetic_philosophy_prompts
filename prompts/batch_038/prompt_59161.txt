I have spent eleven years as a respiratory therapist, and in the spring of 2020 I sat on the small group at my hospital that had to turn the state's crisis standards of care into something a night shift could actually follow. We were not philosophers, and I do not claim we did it well. Since then I have been reading, slowly and on my own, about the argument over what it is that egalitarians want to equalize — wellbeing, resources, or capabilities — and I have become convinced that our scoring sheet was, without anyone realizing it, an answer to that question. I would like you to test the conviction.

The facts, as I recall them and as they were widely reported at the time: the frameworks that circulated scored patients on likelihood of surviving to discharge and, in several versions, on years of life expected to be saved; some included conditions — severe cognitive impairment, certain neurodegenerative diagnoses, in some drafts intellectual disability — that either excluded patients outright or cost them enough points to exclude them in practice; several added a life-cycle element giving weight to patients who had not yet been through the stages of a life; and allocation ran in cycles, with reassessment after a fixed number of days. Disability-rights organizations objected within weeks, arguing that the instruments were not measuring survival but measuring the worth of disabled lives, and that quality-of-life estimates made by healthy people run systematically lower than the estimates disabled people give of their own lives. Complaints went to federal civil-rights authorities, and in more than one jurisdiction categorical exclusions were struck in favor of individualized assessment. The survival-based core of the scoring largely survived the revisions.

Four things, please.

First, map the episode onto each of the three answers, and be exact about what each takes as the thing to be compared. For each: what would the metric tell our committee to rank on, what would it actually produce for two patients and one ventilator, and where is it silent rather than merely hard to apply? I especially want your judgment on whether the capability approach has anything to say at all about dividing one indivisible good between two claimants, given that it was built as a space for comparing social arrangements and for assessing what people are free to do, not as a rationing rule. If you think it has nothing to say, say plainly that asking it to is a category error rather than a limitation.

Second, take the resource view seriously rather than as a strawman. Run the objection through Dworkin's hypothetical insurance market: what would bidders insure against — a lower quality of life, or the material and social cost of a life that needs more support in order to go equally well? Does the finding about self-rated quality of life damage that metric as much as it damages a welfare metric, or is it the one framework built to absorb the finding?

Third, tell me whether the objections actually filed were capability objections. My suspicion, which I cannot defend, is that they were closer to a complaint about what the criteria express about a class of people's standing — the relational line, about who counts as an equal in an institution's eyes — and that this is why capability vocabulary never seemed to gain traction in the rooms I was in. If you agree, does that mean the episode was never really about equality of what at all?

Fourth, turn the analysis around. Which metric anticipated the shape of the objection, which was embarrassed by it, which had nothing to say? And what does it tell us that the revisions changed procedure and expression while leaving the survival scoring in place — that the dispute was about the metric, or that it was never about the metric?

I am not after a survey or a bibliography, and I would rather you be blunt than balanced. Finish by telling me what would change your mind: which of the four parts you hold most loosely, what consideration or piece of evidence would move you to the opposite verdict, and where you think you are most likely to have gone wrong in reconstructing a theory from my description of it.
