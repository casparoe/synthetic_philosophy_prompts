I'm pitching a three-month independent project to a formal-epistemology research group and need a proposal of roughly 1,300 words. My half-formed idea: "updateless" in updateless decision theory is being made to carry two distinct theses at once — a claim about which distribution to compute expectations from (the prior, conditionalized only on the agent's own act), and a claim about how to design a decision procedure so that it performs well when the world contains predictors of it. Once those are separated, a third question becomes visible and, as far as I can tell, unanswered in general: given an agent's total information at a choice point, which parts of it may the agent update on, and which parts must it remain updateless about? In practice this is settled case by case, by checking whether a candidate policy beats its rivals in a toy problem, and the resulting pattern looks ad hoc to me. As I read the literature, updatelessness is applied to ordinary empirical evidence in predictor problems but set aside for self-locating evidence in cases involving copies or amnesia, and I have not seen a stated principle governing the switch. The project would try to derive a criterion for partitioning information into updatable and non-updatable from the decision-theoretic purpose the partition serves, rather than reverse-engineering it from the verdicts we already want, and would test it against several problem types: predictors that simulated an earlier version of the agent, agents whose present evidence is an effect of their own earlier choice, self-locating cases, and cases where the agent receives information about its own decision procedure. If you think my reading of those cases is wrong, say so briefly and build the proposal around the sharpest version of the question that survives.
