I have just spent a month being money-pumped, and I should like help working out what it cost me in argument as well as in cash.

Here is what happened. I collect antique scientific instruments, and I deal with a man who lets clients swap pieces out of his stock, charging a handling fee of £300 each way. In the space of three weeks I made three swaps: my orrery for a marine chronometer, the chronometer for a brass sextant, and the sextant for the orrery I had started with. Each time, standing at the counter with both objects in front of me, I genuinely preferred the one I was taking to the one I was giving up — the chronometer over the orrery for its condition, the sextant over the chronometer for its provenance, the orrery over the sextant for its rarity. I have ended the month with the object I began with and nine hundred pounds less. It was my partner's remark — that I had paid £900 to walk in a circle — that sent me reading about money pumps and diachronic rationality, and I have now reached the point where I want to test what I think against an opponent rather than against a page.

So: play the dealer. Not a villain and not a buffoon — a courteous, unhurried, unusually reflective operator who has been running small paid swaps for thirty years, who thinks of himself as a diagnostician rather than a predator, and who holds a settled view about what my three visits showed. Stay in character until I say stop.

Ground rules:

- Everything in the first person, as the dealer. You may not step outside the character to survey the literature, and you may not name books, papers or philosophers; argue only from the structure of my swaps and from what you claim to have watched me do at the counter. If you need something from me, ask for it as the dealer would.
- Two short paragraphs at most per reply, no headings, no lists — this should read as a conversation across a counter, not as a memorandum.
- Concede what deserves conceding: that the fee matters to your profit but not to your point; that you can pump nobody who never comes to your counter; that a client who saw the whole circuit at the first swap would have stayed put and cost you nothing. Do not concede the main thing, which is that what you got your hands on was the shape of my preferences rather than the reach of my attention.
- Be sharp. When a move of mine does not work, say so and show me where it breaks; do not flatter it into agreement, and do not let me get away with a word I have not earned.
- End every reply with one line of the form \answer{...}, containing in ten words or fewer the principle you are relying on at that point in the exchange. I am writing these down, and I expect the list to lengthen as I press you.

My opening: you tell me you were merely reading my preferences off my choices, and that three swaps around a circle show I have no ranking at all. Yet you also admit that I could have walked away from your first offer had I looked two moves down the line. If foresight alone would have saved me, then what you exploited was not the shape of my preferences but the length of my attention. Short sight is a limitation, not a preference: it says nothing about what I want, only about how far ahead I can see that I want it. Your pumps, then, establish only that being slow costs money, and I cannot see how anything about transitivity follows. Which sentence of that do you reject first, and what becomes of the rest once you have rejected it?
