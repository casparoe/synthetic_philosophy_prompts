I am in the third week of a seminar on public memory, and I have become convinced that the dispute we keep circling is not the dispute the syllabus says it is. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the case.

The setup: our running case is a mid-nineteenth-century civic benefactor whose name is on a public library and on four cross-streets, and who endowed a scholarship with eligibility restrictions we would now regard as indefensible. He is not a monster by the standards of the archive; the historical record on him is settled and everyone in the room accepts it. Two camps have formed and have not moved in three weeks.

Camp one holds that commemoration is a speech act performed at a time by a community, that its content is fixed at dedication, that the present generation inherits the monument along with the building and the endowment, and that unilateral removal is a censorship of the past by the present. They also press the slope: on what non-arbitrary stopping point do we keep the library and lose the street?

Camp two holds that commemoration is not a record but an endorsement; that the archives already preserve the fact that the man existed; that the only thing a statue or a street name adds to the record is a present-tense claim that this person is worth honouring; and that retention is therefore a continuing speech act for which the current council is responsible. On this view neutrality is not on the menu.

The striking feature is that both camps grant that honouring requires some endorsement by someone now, both grant that the destruction of evidence is bad, and both grant the same facts about the man. So I do not think this is a factual dispute, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about purity that neither side can argue for. My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I want it tested rather than flattered — is that the disagreement is a displaced one about what fixes the content of a standing public utterance: whether a monument's message is set by the dedicators' intentions, by the conventions of the genre, or by how it is actually received by the people walking past it today. On that reading camp one is doing interpretation-by-intention and camp two is doing interpretation-by-uptake, and neither side has noticed that it is importing a theory of meaning into what it presents as a moral claim.

A second, related suspicion: a statue and a street name may not be the same kind of object. A street name is spoken and written thousands of times a day by people with no interest in the honouree, embedded inside an address they need for other purposes; a statue can be walked past, ignored, or covered in scaffolding. If uptake is what settles the message, the two cases should diverge — and part of our stalemate may be that we argue about them together and so average out our intuitions.

So: is the interpretation-theoretic diagnosis correct, or is there a genuine moral disagreement underneath that my diagnosis merely restates in different vocabulary? If it is correct, what would count as progress — a distinction, a thought experiment that separates the two camps cleanly, an institutional arrangement that dissolves the question, a change of question — or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard? If it is not correct, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is and whether anything could move it.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
