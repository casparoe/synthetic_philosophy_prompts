I am setting the final examination for a third-year and first-year graduate philosophy of science course, and I would like you to write one of its three essay questions, together with everything an examiner needs in order to mark it consistently. The paper runs three hours; each question is worth 30 marks.

The topic is the long-running dispute over whether philosophy is of any use to science — the exchange in which senior physicists complain that philosophy has contributed nothing to physics, while philosophers reply that physics cannot settle its own interpretive problems without it. I do not want a question that a student can answer by narrating that feud, by itemising philosophers who happened to be great scientists, or by reciting a general defence of the humanities. I want a question that forces a genuine argument.

Please build the question around two distinctions that the standard treatments of this dispute run together.

The first distinction separates two claims about philosophy's relation to science. Call them the incubator thesis — that philosophy contributes by generating problems, distinctions and frameworks that eventually harden into autonomous empirical disciplines — and the collaborator thesis — that philosophy contributes to scientific practice that is already up and running, here and now, by working on problems internal to mature fields. Much of the public dispute consists of physicists attacking the second claim and philosophers answering the first.

The second distinction sorts contributions into three grades: (i) conceptual hygiene, meaning the removal of ambiguity, equivocation and category confusion; (ii) hypothesis generation, meaning the proposal of claims that could in principle be tested and that no working scientist had proposed; (iii) adjudication between empirically equivalent rivals, meaning the giving of reasons to prefer one theory over another when the two agree on every observable consequence. The question should require students to assign at least one concrete case to a grade and then to say whether the grades are genuinely distinct or whether (i) collapses into (ii), or (iii) collapses into (i).

The crux I want the question to turn on is a piece of reasoning I will call the graduation objection: if the incubator thesis is true, then every success removes the material philosophy was working on, so a strong track record of past usefulness is evidence of present irrelevance. Students who simply defend philosophy by appealing to its historical successes thereby strengthen the objection they were trying to answer. And a second trap runs in the opposite direction: the most readily available defence of contemporary philosophy is to insist that its contribution is clarificatory, of grade (i) only, which concedes that it produces no content and so again plays into the objection. An excellent answer must notice that these two defences pull against one another and must say which, if either, survives the pressure. A merely well-read answer will deploy both without seeing the tension.

Please deliver the following.

1. The question exactly as it would appear on the paper: no more than about 150 words of set-up, then sub-parts (a) and (b) with mark allocations summing to 30. State explicitly on the paper that an unanalysed list of historical cases cannot earn more than a fixed low number of marks.

2. A model answer of 900 to 1000 words that reaches a definite conclusion rather than surveying positions, and that handles the mutual-undercutting problem described above.

3. A marking scheme that assigns the 30 marks across at least six named moves, states what a first-class answer does that a good-but-not-excellent one does not, and draws the A/B boundary in terms of specific content rather than tone, fluency or effort.

4. Four characteristic errors, each with a sentence on why it looks persuasive to a student and exactly which marks it should forfeit.

Constraints: the question must not require knowledge of any particular book or article, and must not presuppose physics beyond a first-year course; no citations are expected of students, so nothing should hinge on a source they would have to recall. The difficulty must come from the crux and the distinctions, not from obscurity, and the question must not take the form of a discuss or compare-and-contrast prompt.
