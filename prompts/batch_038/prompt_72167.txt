I am helping to adjudicate a proposal that has come before our department's graduate admissions and research-group governance committee, and I would like a careful philosophical assessment of it rather than a survey of Nietzsche scholarship.

The proposal, which I will call somatic credibility discounting, runs as follows. When a member of a research group advances a substantive claim — that a certain social policy will predictably fail, that a certain metaphysical position is untenable, that a certain historical narrative is tendentious — other members are permitted, in deciding how much weight to give the claim, to take account of publicly observable features of the claimant's bodily regimen: sleep schedule, stimulant and alcohol use, regularity and composition of meals, exposure to daylight, amount of walking. The stated rationale is that a disordered regimen produces characteristic distortions of judgment, among them pessimism masquerading as rigor, grandiosity masquerading as insight, and resentment masquerading as critique; a group that ignores this is naive about its own evidence base. The proposal explicitly disclaims using regimen evidence to bar anyone from speaking, and confines it to the internal weighting of testimony.

Please describe the most important implications of Nietzsche's views on diet and health for this proposal. I have in mind above all the material in Ecce Homo (written in 1888, published posthumously in 1908), where the choice of food, of place, of climate, and of recreation is treated not as a private lifestyle matter but as a question of the highest self-interest and of one's capacity for self-formation; his recurring claim that moral and metaphysical valuations are symptoms, a sign-language of bodily states; and his treatment of asceticism and of decadence as physiological conditions rather than as freely adopted positions.

In particular, please address:

1. Whether Nietzsche's physiological manner of reading convictions licenses the inference the committee wants to draw — whether a disordered regimen tells us anything about the truth or falsity of a claim, as against something about its type, its value, or what it expresses in the person holding it — and whether the proposal therefore imports a genetic fallacy that Nietzsche himself would, or would not, have minded.
2. What his emphasis on what he calls the small things (nutrition, locality, climate, recreation) implies about how much diagnostic weight regimen evidence can carry when the regimen is only partly observable and the claimant's medical and personal history is unknown.
3. The reflexive difficulty: the evaluator applying somatic credibility discounting is himself a body with a regimen, and on Nietzsche's own account his confidence in his diagnostic acuity may itself be a symptom. Explain what this does to the proposal — whether it is fatal, merely limiting, or something Nietzsche would have welcomed as a further test of the evaluator.
4. Whether the proposal can be held apart from ressentiment, given that it empowers a group to grade individuals by their vitality, or whether an instrument of this kind predictably ends up in the hands of the weaker members and is turned against the exceptional ones.
5. Which of these implications is, in your judgment, most decisive for the committee, and what the proposal would have to become in order to survive it.

Please do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not attempt verbatim quotation; I cannot check them and do not want invented ones. Ground the answer instead in specific Nietzschean commitments, and mark explicitly wherever a point is a natural extension of his views rather than something he actually held. Where the proposal is merely Nietzschean-sounding rather than genuinely Nietzschean, say so directly.
