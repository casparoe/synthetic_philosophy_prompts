I sit on the coverage-policy committee of a large health plan, and I have been asked to draft the conceptual background for a review of our surgical coverage rules. What I need is an account of what those rules presuppose about disease, not a defense of them.

Here is the practice. Our policies divide procedures into cosmetic and reconstructive-or-medically-necessary, and only the second category is reimbursed. Moving a procedure into the second category requires the claim to be recast in a particular vocabulary. A breast reduction is denied when the request is framed as relief from distress about appearance; the same operation is authorized when the file shows documented neck, back and shoulder pain, grooving from bra straps, a period of failed conservative management (physical therapy, supportive garments, analgesics), and a planned resection above a minimum number of grams per breast, sometimes scaled to body size. Rhinoplasty is denied as cosmetic and authorized as functional when a deviated septum impairs breathing. Correction of prominent ears is, in a good many policies, denied to an adult and authorized for a young child on the ground of anticipated psychological harm. Bariatric surgery requires a body-mass-index threshold, at least one weight-related comorbidity, and a documented history of failed dietary attempts. Treatment of male-pattern hair loss is excluded although it is distressing, visible, and pharmacologically treatable; treatment of acne is covered. Infertility treatment is capped or excluded by many plans even though it involves the failure of a bodily function. And we authorize statins for people who feel entirely well and function entirely well, on the strength of a number. The whole apparatus is administered by committees that write policy bulletins, set numeric cut-offs, and occasionally revise them by consensus rather than by discovering anything.

Take these details as given; where plans differ, treat the variation as part of the data.

What I want from you:

(a) Reconstruct the theory of disease and health the gatekeeping rule presupposes. Does it require that disease be a statistically atypical departure from the functional organization of some reference class, such that felt harm is never sufficient on its own? Or does it presuppose something more normative — that a condition counts when it defeats goals a person is socially expected to be able to pursue? Or a hybrid on which both a functional failure and a harm judgment are necessary? Say which features of the practice force the reading and which are merely consistent with it, and then say which single account best explains the pattern as a whole.

(b) Separate the metaphysics from the administration. How much of this pattern would be generated by budget limits, auditability, moral hazard, and the need for rules fixed in advance, without any theory of function at all? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what is merely expedient under it.

(c) Consider the reference-class structure. Our thresholds are indexed by age, sex, and body size, and the ear case treats the same anatomy differently in a child and in an adult. Does that commit us to a statistical notion of normality, and to which reference classes? Or is the child/adult asymmetry evidence of a different commitment — and if so, is it consistent with the rest of the rules, or does the practice hold two incompatible conceptions at once?

(d) Tell me what would have to change if the gate were replaced by an openly normativist one, on which a condition qualifies because it makes the patient's life go worse. What becomes of the cosmetic/reconstructive line? What would the claimant have to establish, what would the committee have to weigh, what would we lose by way of a stopping point, and what new machinery — a measure of harm, a ranking, a cap — would the practice need to remain administrable? Say briefly what would have to be dropped if we went the other way and purged the rules of everything not tied to a demonstrable functional lesion.

Finally, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: the points at which two or three rival accounts of disease would license exactly the same rules. That is the part I most need for the background section.
