Retired four years ago after 35 years in a plant physiology lab — mostly grafting, source-sink relations, clonal stuff. Since then I've been reading philosophy of biology pretty seriously and I've gotten stuck on the units of selection debate, and what I want from you isn't a summary of it, it's a diagnosis of why it hasn't moved.

Here's the situation as I understand it, correct me where I'm wrong. One side — Williams, Dawkins, and most of the working biologists I still talk to — says the only things that qualify as units of selection are replicators, and genes are the only replicators with the copying fidelity to accumulate complicated design. Groups on this view have heredity that's far too leaky: groups bud off new groups that don't resemble them reliably, migration swamps the between-group variance, and within-group selection wins on timescales because individual generations are shorter. The other side — Sober and Wilson and the multilevel people — says that's just a redescription. The Price equation is exact and can be partitioned at whatever level you like, you can always write down a group-level term, so whether there's group selection is a question about where the causal action is, not about which bookkeeping is prettier. And they're right that the gene's-eye account of any given case is always available, which is exactly their complaint: available, and empty.

And yet nobody converts anybody. The same case studies get cited by both camps — social insects, myxobacteria, the volvocine algae, Dictyostelium, ant colonies — same numbers, same phylogenies, and each side reads them as obviously deciding the matter in its favour. Worse, the two formalisms people actually use (the partition approach versus the contextual-analysis kind of approach) can apparently disagree about whether there is group selection in a given dataset, and then you get a separate argument about which formalism is right, which looks to me like a different argument wearing the same coat.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and I'd genuinely like it attacked: I don't think there's a factual disagreement here, and I don't think its a purely verbal one either. I think the word "unit" is being made to do at least three seperate jobs — what gets copied, what interacts with the environment and actually dies, and what the adaptation is for, i.e. who benefits — and the two camps have quietly elected different jobs as the one that matters. The argument about which job matters isn't a biological question at all, it's a question about what an explanation of design has to bottom out in. If that's right, the impasse isn't a failure of evidence or of cleverness, it's two answers to two questions sharing a name.

The second half of the hypothesis is the bit I actually want your view on, because it comes out of my own field and I don't think it's in the debate in the form I'd want. Graft chimeras and clonal colonies are cases where the adaptive phenotype belongs to a pair or a patch rather than to a genome. A scion on a rootstock: the drought tolerance, the fruit load, whatever you care to measure, is a property of the combination, and neither genome alone has it, and the combination is never copied — it gets made fresh every season by a person with a knife. Aspen groves, coral colonies, a mycorrhizal network, all the same shape of problem: real variance in a trait that lives above the genome, but with no honest heredity, because the "offspring" group is either not produced by reproduction at all or is produced with almost no resemblance to the parent. Both sides seem to exclude these by construction. The gene camp says no fidelity therefore not a unit; the multilevel camp says no group reproduction therefore not the interesting case, MLS1 at best. Which makes me suspect that what's really holding the debate still isn't the disagreement over facts but the shared agreement about which cases count as data — an agreement neither side argues for, it's just baked into the models both of them use.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, and if so about which word — unit, selection, adaptation? Two different questions under one heading? A hidden formal question about which partition is the right one? A hidden empirical question about group-level heritability that somebody could in principle go and measure? Or bedrock intuitions about explanation that neither side can give reasons for? Tell me which of these it is, or which combination, and argue for it from what the two sides actually say to each other. I've read the usual suspects, so no survey and no reading list please — I want the diagnosis.

Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress? A new distinction? A case one side can't redescribe without losing something? A theorem that pins down exactly when the two formalisms come apart and shows that the region where they do is empty, or isn't? Or a change of question — and if so, into what question, phrased how? I'd like an honest answer about whether progress should be expected at all. And one specific thing: when people say both descriptions are correct and the only issue is which is more useful, is that a substantive claim that could turn out to be false, or is it a way of conceding the debate is unresolvable while looking like you resolved it? What could make one description better than the other if they always get the numbers right?
