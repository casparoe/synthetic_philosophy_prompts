In three weeks' time I have to stand up at my father's eighty-fourth birthday lunch and give a toast, and I would rather have it written properly than trust myself to improvise. The room will hold about thirty-five people: my brother and his family, my own, my father's ninety-one-year-old sister, the two carers who come in most mornings, and half a dozen former colleagues from the firm where he spent forty years as a structural engineer, mostly designing for load cases that would never occur but had to be provided for. My father will be present and will follow much of what is said. He has dementia with Lewy bodies, diagnosed three years ago, which means his attention comes and goes rather than sliding evenly downhill; on a good afternoon he can carry a conversation about a bridge he inspected in 1978 and lose the thread of a sentence addressed to him now. Twenty-two years ago, after watching his own wife spend eleven months on a feeding tube, he had a solicitor draw up what the family calls his living will — a written advance decision refusing artificial nutrition and refusing antibiotics for any chest infection, in the event of a cognitive decline he would not be able to reverse — and he named me as the person to see that it was carried out.

My brother believes that document should now be set aside, and his argument is not sentimental. It is that the man who signed it is not the man by the window: that a person who no longer remembers signing, who enjoys his food, who is affectionate and often content, cannot be bound by the instructions of a frightened sixty-two-year-old writing against a horror he has since been spared; that honouring the paper is loyalty to a document rather than to Dad; and that if the same question were put to him now he would not give the same answer. I do not think my brother is simply wrong. Since the diagnosis I have read enough around the subject to recognise that the dispute turns on questions nobody in that room would identify as questions at all: whether an advance decision is a standing instruction from a person who persists, or the last testimony of someone who has gone; whether there is any real difference between a sailor who wanted to be tied to the mast and a man who now wants nothing much in either direction, so that the wish being honoured belongs to nobody present; whether the interests that survive in him — comfort, sociability, the taste of his tea — are the interests the document was drawn up to protect; and whether a promise extracted from a man by witnessing his wife's death can bind a man who has forgotten both.

Please write the toast: spoken length five to six minutes, so roughly 700–850 words, in sentences that can be said aloud without running out of breath. The following constraints are meant to do structural work, not to decorate: it must not function as a eulogy for a living man, and nothing in it may imply that he is already absent, or that the version of him we are honouring is somewhere else; it must not name the living will, read from it, or allude to it in terms he would have to ask about afterwards, since he will be listening; it must not promise my brother that it will be set aside, and must not promise my father that it will be followed to the letter, because I do not yet know which is true; it must nevertheless let my brother hear that his objection has been understood rather than outvoted, and let my father hear that he is the subject of the occasion and not its antecedent; it may use his engineers' idiom — limit states, provisions for conditions the designer will not live to see — but must not turn it into an allegory so obvious that it insults the men who actually did the work; and it must not assert anything I believe to be false, including anything that requires pretending that the person who signed the document and the person who will hear the toast are unproblematically one person, or that they are unproblematically two. If a line would be apt and you have to cut it to keep a constraint, cut it and record it below rather than smuggling it in. The toast should leave the room able to sit down to lunch together without having settled anything.

After the toast, give me a JSON object in British English (spelling and idiom throughout, in the toast and in the JSON values alike) with exactly these fields: `constraint_audit`, an array of at least five objects each with `constraint` (the constraint in your own words), `carried_by` (the exact phrase or sentence from the toast that carries it) and `deliberately_unsaid` (the true thing that was not said at that point, and why saying it would have broken the occasion); `the_two_positions`, an array of two objects, one for my brother's view and one for the view that the living will still speaks, each with `position` (one sentence), `granted` (what the toast concedes to it) and `withheld` (what it refuses to concede); `unspeakable`, an array of at least four objects with `statement` (a claim that would be perfectly appropriate in a seminar on the subject) and `why_impossible_here` (one sentence); and `bottom_line`, a single sentence saying whether the toast sides with the document, with my brother, or with neither, and by what means it manages that.
