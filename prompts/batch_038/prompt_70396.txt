I'm writing a seminar paper on Kierkegaard's hatred of the press and I've decided to build it as an extrapolation problem instead of a survey, so I've been collecting his actual verdicts on concrete cases and trying to work out what rule generates them. The trouble is I can't get the rule to come out unique. Here's my list:

1. The daily newspaper and its readership — condemns. His line in the Two Ages review is that the public isn't a thing at all, it's an abstraction the press alone makes possible; a crowd in the street is at least concrete, a public is nothing.
2. The Gyllembourg novel that the review is actually about — he spends a whole book praising it, and its author published anonymously, signing herself only as the author of an earlier successful novel. He never treats that anonymity as a defect and never asks her to step forward. So anonymity by itself obviously isn't the sin.
3. His own pseudonyms — defended at length, and later he used a daily newspaper to print a confession telling readers not to credit him with everything issued under those names. He policed his own anonymity in the very medium he calls an abstraction.
4. The Corsair — he went and provoked it on purpose, asked to be flayed by it, then got mocked by shopboys and schoolkids for years and loathed it, and then in the Point of View he says it was about the most important thing that ever happened to him, governance that taught him what the crowd really is.
5. Talkativeness, in the discourse where the lily and the bird are held up for their silence — the fault isn't saying false things, it's saying anything you haven't appropriated; chatter is a third category next to action and creation.
6. Associations, committees, petitions, popular meetings, anything numerical — condemns; a decision a group takes is a decision nobody took.
7. Leveling — a genuine religious achievement when a single individual does it alone before God, a catastrophe when the crowd does it. Same activity, opposite verdict, depending on who's doing it.
8. Øjeblikket in 1855 — he writes, prints and has numbers of a pamphlet hawked in the street against the bishop and the state church, in his own name, and insists this is not the press, because one existing individual stands behind every line and is willing to suffer for it. He also wrote the newspaper advertisements for his own books and ran parts of the attack through Fædrelandet and Flyveposten.

As far as I've found he says nothing about the telegraph or about news getting faster, so I don't want to lean on that.

Now the case I actually want to test, which I invented to sit exactly between his verdicts. A man — one man, no institution, no editorial board — publishes a weekly sheet. Every issue is signed with his name and his address. He has maybe four thousand subscribers who have never met each other and never will. He refuses to print readers' letters, refuses to call any meeting of them, refuses to state his circulation, and says he'd go on printing for one reader; all each issue asks is that the reader decide something alone that week, act on it, and not discuss it. He knows perfectly well that most of them read it and then talk about it at their clubs. He has to know the print run, obviously, he just won't say it out loud.

So what does he say about this sheet? I want (a) the candidate rules that fit all eight verdicts, including ones I haven't thought of — I've got "its about whether a single existing individual stands behind the words" and "its about the abstraction the medium creates no matter what anyone intends," but I doubt those are the only two; (b) which of those rules diverge on my sheet, and what each predicts; (c) your best guess at his verdict, with the reason for it and how confident you are; (d) one further case, described concretely, that would separate the rules my sheet can't separate. And if the eight verdicts simply aren't jointly consistent, tell me which one he'd have to give up, because that changes what anyone can predict from the rest.
