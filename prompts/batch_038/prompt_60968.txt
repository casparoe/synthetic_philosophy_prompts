I'm a novelist, and this happened at a residency in the mountains where I spent April failing to finish a book I no longer believe in. Two other residents got into it at dinner and I mostly kept my mouth shut, which I now regret, because I want to know who was right. The subject was Tolstoy's What Is Art? — the late one where he decides that most of what his culture calls great art isn't art at all — and the object was a novel by a fourth resident, Ilse, called The Salt Almanac, which came out the previous year to enormous reviews and a prize. It's 600 pages, second person, sections unnumbered, no character ever named, about a hydrologist who spends thirty years gauging a river that then gets dammed and buries the village she grew up in; the footnotes periodically contradict the main text. Bea (a poet who teaches) and Callum (nonfiction, long pieces about water rights, so he'd read it for the river) argued about whether Tolstoy's test can even be applied to a book like that. The complicating facts, which both of them had: nobody at the residency could tell me they'd enjoyed it, two people said it had genuinely wrecked them, one of those two is Ilse's wife, and Ilse says she wrote it over eleven months in a state she describes as the closest she's ever come to panic.

The moves, roughly in order:

1. Callum: the book isn't bad art, it's not art. Nothing in it infects you; you come out of it feeling clever rather than joined to Ilse or to anyone else who's read it, and Tolstoy has an exact name for that — counterfeit art, propped up by critics and committees who need it to be good. He quoted him to the effect that real art gets a person "without exercising effort and without altering his standpoint," and said The Salt Almanac is 600 pages of altered standpoint.
2. Bea: then the criterion condemns its own author. Tolstoy demoted most of his own fiction — including, I think, the big novels every shelf in this building reveres — and pointed at simple folk art instead. A test you can only pass by throwing out your own masterpieces isn't a test, its late-life asceticism wearing a theory's clothes.
3. Callum: backwards. The self-conviction is the one thing in the book's favor: he paid the cost, he wasn't tailoring a criterion to flatter what he already liked. A rule that bites its maker is doing something.
4. Bea: something, but not what he claims. Infectiousness is measured by nothing except whether people report being moved, and people are moved by jingles, by recruitment videos, by precisely the sentimentality he despises. So either infection is the whole criterion and kitsch is the best art going, or he needs the second half — feelings that unite people, appraised by the religious perception of the age — in which case infection is only a gate and the real work is being done by morality. It's an ethics book that mislabels itself.
5. Callum: he never hides that. The two halves run together: form tells you whether the transmission happened, content tells you whether the feeling was worth transmitting. He says outright that degree of infectiousness is "the sole measure of excellence in art" apart from subject matter, so the two parts arent competing for the same job.
6. Bea: then the cart-driver goes out the window. He wants the criterion available to ordinary people — that's the whole anti-elitist force of it, folk art as the model — but if whether a work is good depends on whether its feeling matches the religious perception of the age, you need a theory of what that perception is, which is theology, which the cart-driver doesn't have. Either the test is available to everyone and collapses into whatever moves people, or it isn't and the anti-elitism dies. He gets to keep one.
7. Callum: fine, keep neither, the sociology still stands. He was the first to say plainly that a big share of what a culture certifies as great is great because a professional class needs it to be — prizes, chairs, reviews, the whole apparatus — and a book nobody here can describe enjoying that everybody praises is Exhibit A.
8. Bea: that's a different book, and it doesn't detach. His claim about the professional class is that it prefers bad art, which presupposes he already knows what good art is; you can't rescue the sociology by cutting it loose from the criterion it was derived from.

Can you take those eight moves one at a time and rate each — clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right — with a sentence on why, and then say who came out ahead and on which specific point? Two things I especially want. First: is Bea's dilemma in 4 and 6 actually a dilemma, or does Tolstoy have a reply available that neither of them used? Second: what do you do about the fact that the book looks high on sincerity (if Ilse is honest about those eleven months) and high on individuality but has almost no clarity, when he says sincerity is the condition that includes the other two and pushes the artist toward clear expression — does that combination mean she wasn't sincere after all, or does it break the ordering of his three conditions? And finally, since I have to sit next to Ilse at dinner for three more weeks: is there anything she could do to the manuscript — not to win prizes, just to pass his test — that wouldn't make it a worse book on any other account?
