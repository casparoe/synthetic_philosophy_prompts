I write novels, and next month I'm giving a talk in a philosophy department's "art and mind" series on self-deception, built out of my last book — the narrator spends 300 pages failing to acknowledge that he was the one who tipped off the police about his brother. I wrote it so the narration never reports a suppressed belief: there's no split self, no buried knowledge, just a man re-describing the same few evenings in ways that get steadily more convenient, each retelling a small act of revision, like an editor cutting a manuscript she claims not to have touched. My thesis for the talk is that the philosophy of self-deception has the wrong object — it isn't a belief-state acquired against the evidence, it's an ongoing narrative act — and that once you see it as authorship rather than evidence-management, the classic puzzle (how can he both know and not know?) dissolves, because knowing gets distributed across hundreds of small drafting gestures instead of landing at one moment of decision.

The room will be maybe 25 analytic philosophers of mind and action, several of whom work on self-deception directly and hold pretty deflationary motivated-belief views, plus three grad students in aesthetics and one visiting psychoanalyst who I'm told has no patience for the analytic vocabulary at all.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get in that room, roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would phrase them rather than how I would. For each one — how much damage it does, a reply I can deliver in about 90 seconds, and where that reply is soft. Also tell me what I should just concede up front, and flag the single question I'm praying nobody asks.
