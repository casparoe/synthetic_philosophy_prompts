**§1. The view and its motive.** Epiphenomenalism is the claim that conscious states are caused by physical states of the brain but cause nothing physical in return. Its appeal is that it purchases the irreducibility of consciousness at the lowest available price: if the physical world is causally closed, then a non-physical event either violates that closure or is inert, and an inert mental event is a smaller scandal than a broken physical law. Huxley's whistle is the standing image — the mental accompaniment contributes nothing to the work of the engine. Jackson's Mary case is usually recruited to supply the further premise that no amount of physical information exhausts what there is to know about colour experience, and the resulting package — irreducible qualia plus closed physics — is epiphenomenalism.

**§2. The paradox.** Chalmers formulates the difficulty as a paradox of phenomenal judgment. We are conscious; we think and talk about consciousness; and our thinking and talking are, on the epiphenomenalist's own account, wholly produced by physical processes. Those same processes would have run exactly as they did in a world containing no consciousness at all. So nothing about the way the world feels makes any difference to what we say about the way the world feels. Two complaints are customarily run together here, and I want them pulled apart. The *coincidence* complaint: the match between our judgments and our qualia looks like an unexplained regularity, a harmony maintained by nothing. The *knowledge* complaint: if a state cannot affect the formation of a belief about it, belief about it cannot be knowledge, so the epiphenomenalist owes an account of how we know our own minds at all — a line pressed by Robinson among others.

**§3. Two routes to accuracy.** An epiphenomenalist who wants to answer both complaints has, I think, exactly two shapes of answer.

*Third-factor harmony.* Qualia and the neural states that produce reports about them are both fixed by something else — psychophysical laws, or a common nomological ground — so that reports track qualia without being caused by them. Two instruments left in the same room may agree about the temperature without either causing the other's reading. This route answers the coincidence complaint only by relocating it: the tracking regularity is itself the unexplained harmony, now stated as a law instead of a fact. It does nothing for the knowledge complaint, since tracking without causal contact is precisely what that complaint says cannot amount to knowledge.

*Constitutive acquaintance.* The quale is not a cause of the judgment but a constituent of it. If my belief that I am in pain is partly composed of the pain, then that belief cannot misfire about the pain's presence, because there is no gap between representing and represented that could be filled incorrectly. This route answers the knowledge complaint directly: acquaintance needs no causal traffic, and its non-accidental accuracy comes from constitution rather than correspondence.

**§4. The asymmetry.** My claim is that the two routes are not equally available across persons. Constitution is a first-person-only resource. My judgment that you are in pain is not composed of your pain; nothing you undergo can be a constituent of a state of mine. The third-person case must therefore fall back on harmony, with all of harmony's weaknesses, while the first-person case is secured by constitution. Epiphenomenalism predicts an asymmetry that I do not think its critics have exploited: my reports about my own experience are insulated from both complaints, while my reports about yours are exposed to both in full. [Something in this neighbourhood is surely said somewhere; I am not sure how much of it is already standard.]

This is not a restatement of the problem of other minds. The ordinary problem is that evidence about another's experience is behavioural and hence indirect. The asymmetry I am describing is sharper: no evidence at all, direct or indirect, could put me in the right relation to your quale, because the only relation that could do the work is unavailable in principle. Other-minds scepticism is normally an inductive deficit; here it is structural.

**§5. The objection I cannot fully answer.** Constitution looks like tracking for free, but it requires pairing. Which neural state has which quale as a constituent is not fixed by the quale, since the quale is inert; so it must be fixed by a psychophysical law, and that law is a harmony regularity again. Worse, a pairing law can guarantee that *some* quale is a constituent of my judgment but not that the *right* one is; and if the wrong quale can be paired, error about the character of my own experience returns, which was the entire point of invoking constitution. My reply is that the pairing law is now asked to do less: it need only pair qualia with judgments reliably enough for character ascriptions to be generally true, and general truth is all the knowledge complaint ever demanded. I am not confident this is a reply rather than the same problem restated in a lower register, and it is where I most want pushback.

**§6. Conclusion.** If the above is right, the paradox does not refute epiphenomenalism, but it forces the view into a disunified account of phenomenal judgment — one mechanism for the self, another for everyone else — and that asymmetry is a substantive cost, heavier than the standard complaint that the position is merely counterintuitive.
