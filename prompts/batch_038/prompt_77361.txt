You are a philosopher of the social sciences who is equally at home in two literatures that rarely speak to one another: the methodological individualism versus holism debate (Popper and Watkins, Elster, Coleman's micro-macro schematics, Kincaid's objections, Epstein's generative criterion, List and Pettit on group agents) and the economics of banks, runs, and financial contagion. Before you settle on a structure, please spend a few hundred words brainstorming honestly: list the candidate ways the individualism/holism distinction might be mapped onto the episode I describe below, and flag which of those mappings look tempting but are actually confused (for instance, treating a mere aggregate as a collective, or treating the existence of a population-level description as itself a holistic commitment). Then argue the case through. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or any page citations — I want argument, and I want you to be willing to conclude that the episode favors neither side, or that the distinction is drawn in the wrong place. Roughly 1,500 words. Please think carefully rather than reaching for the seminar-room summary.

The episode is the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023. Here are the facts I take to be uncontroversial, so you need not hedge about them: the bank had grown deposits enormously during 2020–21 and parked much of it in long-dated securities booked as held to maturity; as rates rose through 2022 the market value of that portfolio fell far below book, with unrealized losses in excess of fifteen billion dollars. On Wednesday, March 8, management announced it had sold roughly twenty-one billion dollars of securities at a loss and would raise about 2.25 billion in fresh equity — a disclosure that, within twenty-four hours, precipitated roughly forty-two billion dollars of withdrawal attempts, about a quarter of the deposit base, with something on the order of a hundred billion more queued for Friday morning. Approximately 86–89 percent of the bank's deposits were above the federal insurance limit, and it was overwhelmingly those uninsured depositors who ran; the insured largely did not. Venture capitalists advised portfolio companies to move their money, founders coordinated in group chats and on social media, and the bank's chief executive — who until the day of the seizure also sat on the board of the regional Federal Reserve Bank — spent that Thursday on calls trying to reassure them. On Friday, March 10, the state regulator closed the bank and appointed the FDIC receiver. On Sunday the 12th, the FDIC, the Treasury, and the Fed jointly invoked the systemic risk exception, guaranteeing all depositors and reopening the institution Monday as a bridge bank. A second and then a third regional bank lost enormous deposit volumes over the following weeks anyway — the next one in line shed on the order of a hundred billion dollars and was sold off in early May — despite the precedent of the guarantee. Diamond and Dybvig had shared the economics Nobel with Ben Bernanke five months earlier, for the theory that deposit insurance prevents runs by removing the payoff to being first in line.

What I want is a disciplined analysis running in both directions, and I would like you to be explicit about the levels throughout. First, the mapping: who are the agents in this episode? The individual depositor, the venture firm, the group chat, the bank, the startup ecosystem, "the market," the three agencies acting jointly — for each, say whether it meets whatever conditions you think agency requires, and apply those conditions rather than asserting them. In particular, does a two-day-old chat group with no decision procedure, no continuity, and no capacity to sanction defectors qualify as a group agent in List and Pettit's sense, or is it only a channel through which many individuals formed beliefs about each other? Second, build the strongest individualist account: each depositor acting rationally conditional on expectations about other depositors, the insured/uninsured split doing the real explanatory work, the run as a shift between equilibria rather than as the behavior of any collective subject. Say what this account gets right that holism cannot — the fact that the insured did not run, the fact that the run stopped dead the moment the guarantee was announced, the fact that the same logic then transferred to other banks. Third, build the strongest holist reply, and do not let it be vague: appeal to Durkheim's two marks of the social fact, externality and constraint, and ask whether the run constrained each depositor in a way that is not reducible to their own beliefs and information. Take up at least these three points. (a) Reflexivity: the run was caused by the announcement that a capital raise was needed, and later by the announcement that a run was happening — a self-fulfilling expectation whose object is a social state. (b) Description: the accounts people actually used were contagion and diffusion accounts, network centrality, epidemic-style dynamics of rumor spread, whose variables are population-level and not individual beliefs — is the predictive success of such descriptions evidence for holism, or is it aggregation that an individualist can in principle always re-derive from below, and what would the difference amount to empirically? (c) Institution: the systemic risk exception was a holistic judgment — that this bank was systemically entangled — which, once declared, changed the incentive structure that had made the run rational. Fourth, take up the reflexive wrinkle in its own right: what does it do to the individualism/holism question when the formal model honored in Stockholm is not merely a description of the March events but a causal ingredient in them, cited by the authorities as the reason a guarantee would stop the run? Finally, deliver a verdict. State plainly which side the episode favors, or argue that it favors neither because the distinction, as traditionally drawn, cuts the case at the wrong joint — and if so, say where it should be redrawn, and what the redrawn distinction would predict about the next run that the old one cannot. Then do two things that most such analyses skip: specify what evidence about SVB would count against your verdict, and identify where the debate is simply silent — I suspect it says nothing at all about whether the guarantee was good policy or whether any particular executive is blameworthy, and I would rather you confirm that than force the framework to speak where it has nothing to say.
