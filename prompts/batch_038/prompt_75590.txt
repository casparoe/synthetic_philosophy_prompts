Just got back from a four-hour arguement with my flatmate Sam (he does physics, I do philosophy) that started because someone in my metaphysics seminar said the whole natural kinds debate is just the old Plato butcher image wearing a lab coat, and Sam agreed way too hard. His view is that every scientific category is a human filing system, and that the gap between electron and race is a difference of degree in how useful the filing is, not a difference in kind — he thought that pun was hilarious, I did not. I said no, some categories track genuinely projectible clusters out there and some are just labels we stapled onto a continuum, and around 2am we did the classic thing and invented a scoring rubric in his notes app to settle it, which obviously didn't settle it, so now I want a third opinion from something less invested.

The rubric, for what it's worth. Score each category 0–5 on three axes: P = projectibility, i.e. if you learn some non-obvious thing about a handful of members, how much should you expect it to carry over to the rest, including when you poke and intervene on them rather than just look; B = boundary, 5 if membership is settled by the facts and 0 if you can't decide who's in without a threshold, a stipulation, or an actual vote; T = interest-neutrality, 5 if the category earns its keep whatever anyone wants it for, 0 if it only exists relative to a purpose. Composite = (2P + B + T) / 4, ties broken toward higher P. The categories: electron; chemical element (gold, uranium, the slots in the periodic table); biological species; planet; mineral species; human races; major depressive disorder as a psychiatric diagnostic category; gene (and be explicit about which sense of gene you're scoring, since I think that one splits); protein fold; money; and noun, or part of speech generally. Sort them into S/A/B/C tiers off the composite, one or two sentences each on why it lands where it does, and then flag the one or two where you think informed people would disagree most and say which way you'd bet.

Sam also has an extra rule I think is bogus, and I want you to referee it: any category whose extension has ever been pinned down by a committee vote gets an automatic B = 0 and can't be placed above the bottom tier, which sends chemical element (IUPAC), planet (the 2006 Pluto business) and mineral species (whatever the international body is that signs off on new ones) straight to C. I said that conflates two separate things — a vote can fix where the label line goes while the clustering underneath is still objective, and in at least some of those cases there's a real discontinuity doing the work — and he said if it takes a vote then by definition there was no discontinuity to find. So: which of his three cases his rule genuinely bites on, which are category mistakes, and whether there's any way to restate his principle so it isn't obviously wrong but still does some work against me. Also tell me where the rubric itself is bad, because I suspect it is — is there an axis we should have included, and which of the eleven would come out differently under a better one? Don't give me sources or paper titles or names, I litterally just want your numbers, your tiers, and your reasoning. I'm reading it out to him tomorrow and he will nitpick every score, so make them defensible.
