I have been reading around the literature on regimes whose claim to obedience rests on what they produce rather than on how they were chosen, and I keep running into a pattern that I cannot find named anywhere. I would like to know whether it has one.

Here is the pattern. A government's claim is anchored, more or less explicitly, to a specified deliverable — rising incomes, say, or housing, or a growth rate. The deliverable stops arriving, or its measurement becomes disputed, or a shock knocks it out entirely. Then a different deliverable is put in its place: safety, cleanliness, competence, the elimination of absolute poverty, technological self-reliance, national standing, order. The substitution is never announced as a substitution. Nobody says the original test has been failed and a new one adopted; the new item is simply added to the running account and treated as continuous with the old one. Supporters credit it, and — this is the part that interests me most — critics often ratify it too, accepting the replacement deliverable as though it discharged the same burden of proof that the first one had been carrying. The result is that the original claim is never actually tested. It is neither vindicated nor falsified; it is quietly retired while remaining nominally in force.

Three instances from my reading. A city-state whose ruling party had anchored its claim in rising incomes and mass home ownership found, after an election in which its vote share fell sharply and immigration became a salient grievance, that the public vocabulary had shifted toward being safe, clean, harmonious, and well run — and commentators who had been predicting the collapse of the bargain instead described it as being honored in a different currency. A large one-party state, when its headline growth rate came down from double digits, moved its emphasis onto poverty elimination and national restoration; many outside observers who doubted the growth figure nonetheless accepted the poverty claim as though the two were answers to the same question. And in a third case, after a financial shock removed the growth claim from the table altogether, what the state delivered was order — queues that did not form, banks that did not fail, streets that stayed quiet — and that was entered on the credit side of the same ledger.

One further feature that makes me think this is not just ordinary political communication: the substitute is usually something the same instruments produce. The capacities that failed at growth — information management, mobilization, surveillance, administrative coercion — are precisely the capacities that succeed at order and at spectacle. So failing the first metric builds the means of supplying the second, and the failure is not merely survived but partly converted into an asset.

I want to be clear about what I am not describing. It is not the everyday point of moving the goalposts inside an argument, because there is no single arguer here and no moment of retreat; the shift is distributed across the government, its supporters, and its critics. It is not the familiar distinction between support that depends on particular deliveries and support that survives them, since what I am describing happens entirely within the first kind. It is not the trivial observation that governments deliver many goods and may be judged on any of them. And it is not simply propaganda, because the substitute deliverable is often real, and often something the population genuinely values.

So: is there an established concept for this, in political theory or in the empirical literature on authoritarian persistence? If there is, give me the term and tell me how closely it fits the description above, including where my version overstates or distorts it. If there are near neighbors instead, walk me through each one and say precisely what the described pattern adds or subtracts. And if there is no established concept, coin one — but I care much less about the label than about the carving. What I want from you is: a definition with real edges; the conditions under which this happens rather than a government simply being re-evaluated on a different good; a way to tell, in real time and not only in hindsight, whether a substitution is a legitimate reweighing of public goods or the failure mode I have described; and an account of what it would take to hold any government to a fixed criterion in advance, and whether that is even possible for a claim of this kind.
