For thirty-eight years I directed an isotope-ratio mass-spectrometry facility in a department of geochemistry. I retired four years ago, and since then I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time since I was an undergraduate. For two terms I have been auditing a graduate seminar in classical Indian epistemology at our university. It is run by a scholar I shall call Dr. R. The seminar works through the standing disputes about the pramāṇas — how many sources of warranted cognition there are, and whether the additional ones posited by some schools collapse into the others — but what I have found most instructive is her practice of ruling on cases. I have kept a notebook of those rulings. She has never stated a general criterion; when pressed, she says the inventory is something one argues about case by case, and that anyone who states the criterion first has usually begged the question. So the notebook is all I have to go on.

Her rulings, in the order I took them down:

1. Seeing a copper vessel on a shelf in good light: a source in its own right, and the paradigm against which she tests the others. She insists that perception requires actual contact between faculty and object, must be free of error, and must be determinate rather than a mere registration.
2. Smoke on the far ridge, fire in the hollow below: a source in its own right. She rebuked a student who said that in such cases we simply see the fire; nothing is in contact with any faculty of his.
3. A trustworthy traveller's report that the pass is snowed shut: a source in its own right. I suggested this is inference from the traveller's past reliability. She rejected the suggestion, on the ground that if it were, nobody could ever learn anything from anybody.
4. The child who is told by a forester what a wild ox is like, and who later, in the forest, sees one and knows it for what it is: not a source in its own right. She decomposes the cognition into the forester's word, retention, and recognition. Several of the texts we read treat this as a distinct source; she knows that and is unmoved.
5. The stout man who is never seen to eat during the day: not a source in its own right. That he eats at night is inference, whatever the difference in felt mental gesture.
6. The bare shelf where the vessel is not: we perceive the absence. She refuses to posit a separate source for non-apprehension.
7. Memory of any of the above: not a source at all, since it delivers nothing that was not already delivered.
8. A statement in a standard textbook, which nobody in the room could check and for which the class had no independent means of assessment: she declined to file this under 3, remarking that the warrant belonged to the practice that maintains the textbook rather than to any of us, and that she was unwilling to call it testimony without qualification.
9. An experienced clinician's immediate and unarticulated reading of a patient, in which no premise could be stated: perception, not inference.

My own case, which I have not yet had the nerve to put to her. A bone sample is run on the instrument. The operator sees a trace and a number on a screen; the number is a ratio of stable isotopes. From that number she reports, of the sample itself, that its carbon came from a marine rather than a terrestrial diet. I set aside the second step — that is ordinary inference and she has already ruled on inference. What interests me is the prior cognition: that the ratio in this sample has this value. Its features, which I think matter:

- Nobody has ever seen the molecule, and the deliverable is a numeral produced by software, not an image.
- The instrument was calibrated against certified reference materials traceable to a national standards laboratory. Part of the warrant therefore consists in somebody else's word about something none of us has checked.
- The instrument drifts. We recalibrated weekly; its reliability was a property of a maintained practice, not of an object sitting on a bench.
- Two reductions occur to me, and each seems strained. The first treats the instrument as a prosthesis extending the contact of the faculty with the object; but the contact requirement and the requirement that perception be non-verbal both look awkward when what arrives is a number from a firmware routine. The second treats the peak as a mark of the ratio, licensed by a generalization established in calibration; but that generalization is known to us only through the certification, and it is statistical, so the inference would be borrowing its warrant from ruling 3.

What I should like from you:

(a) Set out the candidate general rules — the decision procedures, so to speak — that are jointly consistent with all nine rulings. I have three in mind: a rule on which the classical inventory is closed and any newcomer must be shown to be one of its members or a compound of them; a rule that individuates sources by kind of truth-conducive process, on which the classical list is a summary of the main human process-types but not a limit in principle; and a rule on which the unit of assessment is the practice rather than the individual cognizer. I expect there are others, and I would rather you found them than flattered mine. Say for each rule which rulings constrain it, and which of the nine, if any, it sits uneasily with.

(b) For each candidate rule, state what it predicts Dr. R. will say about my case, and — where the prediction is that the cognition is reducible — reducible to what, and how the reduction is meant to run given the calibration and the drift.

(c) Commit to a single best prediction, with your reasons. I am not asking you to be even-handed; I am asking for the verdict I would back with a bottle of wine at the next seminar. If you think the notebook genuinely underdetermines it, say how far it underdetermines it and which reading is nevertheless the better bet.

(d) Tell me what to bring next. Which single case, put to her, would best separate the surviving rules? If no single case does it, give me the shortest set that would, and say which pair of rules each member of the set separates. I am thinking of variants — an uncertified home-built device giving a true reading that its owner cannot explain; a simulation of the sample that never touches anything; a colleague's off-the-cuff estimate from a spectrum, made before the software has finished — but I do not know whether any of these is sharp enough, and I would rather you designed the experiment than chose among mine.

One constraint on your answer: please do not give me a survey of the schools, a reading list, or attributions to particular texts and authors. I have the books, and I know that guessing at them is worse than saying nothing. What I want is the reasoning about the rules and the case. And if you think I have misdescribed my case — that the cognition I have isolated is not one cognition but two, or that the features I have flagged are the wrong ones — say so first, and then answer the question as you have amended it.
