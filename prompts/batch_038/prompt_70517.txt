I have served for three years on the examinations board of a university whose undergraduate degrees are classified by weighted average against fixed boundaries: 40 to pass, 50 for a lower second, 60 for an upper second, 70 for a first. Alongside those boundaries our regulations run what they call the borderline procedure. If a candidate's final average falls within two marks below a boundary, the board does not simply apply the number; it examines the distribution behind the average and may award the higher class provided a majority of the candidate's credits are marked at that higher level. A student finishing on 58.6 therefore leaves with either an upper second or a lower second depending on a profile review, while a student on 57.9 is classified mechanically and nobody looks at the profile at all. Last month the board spent two and a half hours on a single case of that kind, and afterwards a colleague remarked that the band buys us mercy rather than truth — which is the sentence I have not been able to stop thinking about. I am drafting a paper for next year's senate review of the regulations, and I would like to know, before I defend or amend them, what the procedure assumes about the marks it operates on.

Other parts of our practice seem to pull the opposite way. At module level there is no band whatsoever: an essay marked 61 and an essay marked 59 are treated by the scheme as belonging to different classes, and external moderators adjust only for gross leniency or severity, never for the two-mark region where all of us would admit the difference is noise. The same board that agonises over 58.6 will certify a departmental prize to whichever candidate has the higher average even when the gap is a tenth of a mark, an interval far finer than any of us believes our marking can resolve and one we would not defend as a real difference if asked about it directly. And the band itself has a sharp outer edge: the two-mark threshold is every bit as stipulated as the classification boundary it exists to soften, so that the candidate at 57.9 is excluded from mercy by a line we could just as well have drawn at three marks. A philosopher friend tells me there are rival accounts of predicates like "belongs to the upper second class" — some hold the boundary is sharp but unknowable, some hold that borderline cases are genuinely neither in nor out, some hold the boundary is ours to fix by stipulation with any of a range of choices equally defensible — and insists the differences are not merely verbal. I would like to know which of these, if any, our regulation is committed to.

So please reconstruct the account of classification that the borderline procedure presupposes, keeping careful track of the distinction between what the procedure strictly cannot operate without and what merely sits comfortably alongside it; say whether the sharp outer edge of the band reintroduces exactly the problem the band was meant to absorb, and whether that is a fatal incoherence or only a commitment the regulation does not advertise; say whether the three parts of the practice — module-level cutoffs, the borderline band, and the prize rule — are committed to different accounts, and whether one board can coherently hold all three; and tell me what would concretely have to change if we adopted one of the rival accounts wholeheartedly, for instance the view that across a range of averages there is no fact of the matter which class a candidate belongs to: what happens to the mechanism, to the transcript, to the wording of the regulations, and to the appeals process. I want a committed answer rather than a survey: open with your verdict in two or three sentences, then argue for it, and tell me plainly which of the three practices you think should be reformed and why. No references or reading list needed, and please keep the whole response under 800 words.
