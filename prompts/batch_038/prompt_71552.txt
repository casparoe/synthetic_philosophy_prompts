I have no academic training in philosophy — I have been a home inspector for twenty-two years — but a question that grew out of my work has occupied me for some years, and I have arrived at a position I would like tested with a number attached to it rather than a survey of opinions.

The question is this: can two people have exactly the same evidence for the same claim, and yet one of them knows it while the other does not, purely because more turns on being right for one of them? The example people always use has the same shape as one I meet at work. Suppose my observations about a roof are identical in two situations — same shingles, same attic, same age, same wear. In the first, a buyer is deciding whether to spend forty dollars on a second opinion before authorizing a four-hundred-dollar repair. In the second, a young couple is about to commit everything they have saved on the strength of my assurance that the roof is sound. I find it very hard to say that what I know is the same in the two situations, even though nothing in my evidence has changed.

Here is the mechanism I worked out for myself, and it is the part I most want examined. On my view, stakes do not raise the amount of evidence required; they lengthen the list of specific ways you could be wrong that you must actually have checked and eliminated. Low stakes, three entries on the list. High stakes, thirty. And because a person can only test so many things in the time available, the list has a natural stopping point: past some level of stakes it ceases to grow, and the verdict stops being "you need to check more" and becomes "nobody is in a position to know this at all." That ceiling is not skepticism about the world. It is a fact about what checking costs.

What I would like from you:

1. A number: your credence that pairs of cases of this kind genuinely exist — same evidence, different knowledge, the difference explained by stakes alone. I am not asking for a hedge about how it depends on one's theory of knowledge; I want a figure and the steps that produce it.

2. A decomposition, with a rough probability and a sentence of justification for each term, and then an account of how they combine. The sub-questions I expect to matter are whether knowledge is governed by a threshold fixed independently of the agent's practical situation; whether the principle that one should act on a proposition only if one knows it is true, since that seems to be the engine of the whole argument; whether the pull of my high-stakes case might be about something other than knowledge — whether it is appropriate for me to say "I know," or how confident I should feel — rather than about knowledge itself; and whether my mechanism is more than a redescription.

3. On that last point specifically: if having to rule out thirty alternatives instead of three is always equivalent to demanding more evidence, does my account collapse into the standard one? Or can alternatives be counted in a way that does not track evidential strength? Please give this its own probability rather than folding it into a general discussion.

4. A second number: your credence that a lengthening list with a ceiling is the correct explanation of the phenomenon, conditional on the phenomenon in (1) being real; and a separate line on whether the ceiling itself is coherent, or whether "the list stops growing" smuggles in a claim about human capacities that has nothing to do with knowledge.

5. Sensitivity: which sub-question your overall figure moves most on, and what argument or case would move it. For calibration, I sit at about 0.75 on (1) and 0.5 on (4). If your numbers are far from mine, tell me which sub-question is carrying most of that gap.

Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a live minority position to me as though it were the consensus; where a term in your decomposition rests on a contested premise, say so rather than resolving it silently in your favor. I would also like the standard label for each position you name, but no names of philosophers and no titles of works — I cannot check them, and I would rather have none than have guesses.
