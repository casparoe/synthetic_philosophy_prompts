Ok, context first. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training past one intro elective I dropped, and my roommate is a 4th-year philosophy PhD student. We got into it last week over an actual thing that happened at work and he won't let it go, and honestly I think he might be right, which is annoying.

At work we publish a public API spec for a payments product, and it has a clause saying the total SHALL be the sum of the line items, rounded to the nearest cent. Our reference implementation and a partner's SDK both pass the conformance suite, all 57 vectors, and then a customer cart came through where the exact answer was $125.005 — we returned $125.00, they returned $125.01, and both teams filed bugs against each other claiming the other was non-conformant. So we wrote an errata to clarify it. The errata used the words "round", "nearest" and "tie", which kicked off a second dispute about what the errata meant, then a clarifying note on the errata, and by round three the errata chain was longer than the spec itself. In the end the working group voted 7-4 to declare our behavior conformant and everybody went home. My roommate heard all this and said it's the quus thing from Kripke — he says our function might as well be "plus" for everything under 57 and something else past that, and no amount of extra text fixes it because the extra text is itself text needing a reading, and that all that actually happened at the end is the community agreed, and agreement is all "correct" ever meant here. I don't follow every step of his argument but the shape of it is obviously true, because I lived it over like six weeks.

So here's what I want: a parody "blameless postmortem" doc about the incident, the kind we'd post to an internal wiki, roughly 700-800 words. Use the real headers — Summary, Impact, Timeline (UTC), Root Cause, Contributing Factors, What Went Well, Action Items, Appendix — and keep the register completely deadpan corporate. Nobody in the document should realize anything deep is happening; no winking, no asides. Invent the company, the spec name, the service, the people. The jokes have to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from tech humor: the Root Cause section should hunt for a root cause and structurally fail to find one, because every candidate cause gets restated in words that themselves need interpreting, and the section should end by defering to a definition that defers to another definition. What Went Well should note that all 57 test vectors agreed on both sides and that this somehow turned out not to help. Action Items should include "add more test vectors" with a named owner and a due date, sitting next to a quiet, unbothered admission that no finite number of them settles anything. The resolution gets recorded as a vote, in the same flat tone as a rollback. In the Appendix put the disputed clause text and one errata (all invented), plus a single quoted dissenting comment from the architecture review board member who voted no — he should read like he's just griping about process, but on a second look he's stating the skeptical solution without ever naming it. Don't explain any of it anywhere and don't tack on a moral or a "what we learned about meaning" bow at the end. Also: no real quotes from Wittgenstein or Kripke and no citations, everything in the doc should be made up. Goal is that someone who's read the rule-following stuff laughs, and someone on my team just says it's uncomfortably accurate.
