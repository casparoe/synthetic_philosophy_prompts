Two months ago a community foundation offered to fund a documentary about the street I grew up on, on the condition that it change how the rest of the city sees the neighborhood. I accepted, and I have been arguing with myself ever since, because a filmmaker friend told me afterward that I had simply agreed to make a promotional video with better cameras. When I tried to defend myself I reached, without deciding to, for Du Bois's 1926 case for art as propaganda — and then discovered I could not state his case in a form I actually believed. The draft below is my attempt to do so. It is intended for a general-interest magazine, roughly 1,200 words when finished.

**"The Purity Default" (draft)**

The standard reading of that essay — Du Bois subordinates art to politics, his opponents defend art's autonomy — assumes a neutral position is available. I do not think he assumed it, and I do not think it is true of my street. The field is already occupied: before I shoot a frame there exists a settled and widely believed picture of what the neighborhood is. A work that declines to contest that picture does not leave the picture untouched. It leaves the picture standing, plus one more artifact that might have contested it and did not. On this reading his purist critics are not wrong that his art is propaganda; they are wrong that he had a choice.

That reframes beauty. In the standard telling, his insistence that the work be beautiful as well as true is a concession to aesthetics, a sop to keep the piece from reading as a pamphlet. On my reading it runs the other way: beauty is a functional requirement of the propaganda. A work that is true but repellent is consumed by people who already agree with it and changes no occupied field. It has to be good enough to travel into hostile attention and stay there. Effectiveness, not purity, is what beauty is doing inside his criteria.

This is where the draft falls over, and where I most want to be told it falls over. If beauty is instrumental to persuasion, the criterion inverts whenever persuasion calls for something else — comfort, flattery of the funder, an ugliness legible as urgency. And in my own case the foundation's audience already agrees with the foundation; nobody needs turning. So my film does not have to be beautiful, it has to be watchable, and it is Du Bois's own logic that licenses the substitution. I have converted his standard into a grant application.

A second problem, possibly the deeper one. "The field is already occupied" requires a mechanism. The absence of a counter-picture does not reliably produce one; often it produces nothing at all, no belief in either direction. If unmade works serve the status quo only where the status quo has already supplied an image to occupy the space, the claim is much narrower than I want it to be — and my film, about a street most of the city holds no image of whatsoever, may be precisely the case where it does not apply.
