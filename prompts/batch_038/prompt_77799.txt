Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend who works in numerical weather prediction, and the argument has left me with the bones of what I want my students to confront. I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar — mostly third- and fourth-year undergraduates, with a handful of first-year graduates — and I would like to turn our impasse into the essay question on the final. What I do not want is another batch of papers in which students recite the definition of sensitive dependence and then announce either that chaos has refuted Laplace's demon or that the demon survives because it is omniscient, as though one of those two sentences settled the matter.

Her argument ran roughly as follows. The atmosphere is chaotic: small errors in the initial state grow exponentially, with an e-folding time on the order of days, so the useful prediction horizon grows only as the logarithm of the ratio of the tolerated error to the initial error — halving your observational uncertainty buys you one additional e-folding time and nothing more. But no physical system carries infinitely precise information about its own state; every measurement has a floor, and on her view there may be a floor in principle, since specifying an exact point in a classical state space takes infinitely many digits. So the exact initial condition that determinism needs is not merely unknown but unavailable, and not merely unavailable but not there to be had. Hence, she concluded, the future state of the atmosphere is not fixed by anything the world actually contains, and the honest description of it is stochastic. Determinism, for chaotic systems, is not so much false as empty.

I pressed three objections. First, the demon is stipulated to know the state exactly and does not measure anything, so exponential sensitivity costs it nothing. Second, her reasoning proves too much: it would render a frictionless pendulum and an ordinary coin toss equally indeterministic. Third, she slides from "not predictable by finite beings" to "not determined," and those are different claims. She conceded the first two and said the third begged the question against her, because the slide is licensed by the information-finitude premise: if no state in nature is more precise than finitely many digits, then "the exact state" is not a feature of the world for a unique-continuation thesis to latch onto. For my part, I conceded that chaos destroys pointwise predictability without obviously touching statistical predictability — seasonal climatological averages remain stable even where individual trajectories do not — which made her conclusion look simultaneously too strong and too weak.

Please write this up as a closed-book final-exam essay question in two parts, to be answered in 75 minutes and worth 25 marks in total, together with a model answer of roughly 900 words and a marking scheme detailed enough that a teaching assistant who did not sit in the seminar can apply it consistently.

The stem should be self-contained: state her argument as a numbered set of premises leading to a conclusion, supply the logarithmic horizon relation as given data rather than as something to be recalled, and gloss any technical term — e-folding time, initial-condition error, statistical versus pointwise prediction — in a clause or two, since my students have had no dynamical-systems course. Part (a), worth about 8 marks, should require students to identify precisely which inference in the argument fails as stated and to say what distinction has to be drawn before the argument can even be assessed. Part (b), worth the remainder, should ask them to evaluate the argument once it has been repaired in the way she intends — is determinism "empty" for chaotic systems? — and a strong answer should at minimum engage with what determinism actually asserts, whether the empirical indistinguishability of deterministic and stochastic models of a chaotic system is fatal to the thesis or merely a limitation on testing it, what the survival of stable long-run statistics does to the case, and whether "nature contains no exact real-valued states" is a metaphysical claim or a claim about measurement dressed up as one. If a student raises the issue that numerically integrated trajectories are contaminated pseudo-orbits, and that shadowing-type results place such an orbit near some true orbit without any guarantee that it is near the one actually taken, that is worth credit but must not be required of anyone.

In the marking scheme, list the must-hit moves with the marks attached to each, and separately name the mistakes that should cost marks, with the deduction each warrants — in particular, treating the demon's omniscience as though it covered only the laws and not the initial conditions, invoking quantum mechanics as though it resolved a question posed at the classical level, and declaring determinism unfalsifiable and therefore meaningless without saying what determinism would have to imply in order to be falsifiable. Then give me one or two sentences on which single move most separates an A answer from a B answer, and which mistake is most likely to survive in an otherwise strong paper. Please do not build the question or the model answer around citations, attributions, or a reading list; my students will not have read the specialist literature and I want to grade their reasoning, not their recall.
