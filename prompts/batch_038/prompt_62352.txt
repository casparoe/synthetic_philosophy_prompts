Thirty-eight years in a materials lab, retired two years ago, and now I read philosophy all day, which my wife find hilarious. I've built an argument that I think is sound, but several people have told me it proves far too much and I can't see where.

The spectrum. A1 is two minutes of the worst pain you can imagine. A1000 is a year of a faint background ache that never quite goes away but never stops you doing anything. Between them I put 998 intermediate options, each a shade less intense and a shade longer than the one before.

(1) A is better than B iff someone who understands both would choose A over B.
(2) Given any adjacent pair, they choose the later one — the relief is worth the extra time. I can make the shade as small as I like by adding rungs, and that's the whole point of using so many of them.
(3) So A2 is better than A1, A3 better than A2, and so on down the line.
(4) Given only A1 and A1000, they choose A1. Two minutes over a year.
(5) So A1 is better than A1000.
(6) But (3) plus transitivity gives the opposite of (5). Since (3) and (5) are both just what people choose, it's transitivity that has to go.

Two things bother me. First, the recipe looks completely general — (1) is the same operational thinking I used for every instrument I ever calibrated. Run it on height, a thousand men a tenth of a millimetre apart, and it seems I could prove 'taller than' isn't transitive either, which is arithmetic nonsense. The stock reply is that height is one number and badness isn't. But isn't that just assuming there's a single quantity running along my rungs — its exactly what my argument denies?

Second, and worse: I also ran a version with only three options, no spectrum at all, differing in severity, in duration, and in how much treatment they'd need. My judges went round in a circle: A over B, B over C, C over A. If three options can do that on their own, what was the continuum ever doing in my argument?

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I'm forced to choose I'd rather keep the data and give up transitivity — I spent my life trusting measurements. Tell me the price I'd be paying, and whether the three-option circle and the thousand-rung spectrum are the same problem or two different ones.
