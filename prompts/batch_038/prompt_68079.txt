I'm working through something for a writing group and I need an actual argued position, not a survey of the terrain. Here's the setup and the claim I want you to defend.

Start with the standard worry about biopics and historical novels: they invent things about real people who are dead and can't object, and the wrong is usually framed as a harm to the person portrayed. But there's a timing problem sitting right in the middle of that. If harm needs a subject who's around to be made worse off, then the dead aren't harmed by anything we do after they're gone, so harm-based accounts have nothing to latch onto. And yet our intuitions here are wildly graded in a way that harm-talk struggles with. Nobody blinks at a novelist supplying an invented interior life for a minor eighteenth-century diarist, but a film that invents a motive for someone who died a decade ago feels like a genuine transgression, and the feeling decays smoothly over time, tracking something like how many living people actually knew the person.

So: argue that the duty not to invent inner lives for the dead is not owed to the dead at all. It's owed to the living — to people who knew them, to descendants, and to audiences in their standing as people entitled not to be used as raw narrative material — and the time-decay gradient is the best evidence for that, because it's exactly what a third-party account predicts and exactly what a harm-to-the-deceased account can't explain.

Argue the sub-claim too, and I think it's the more interesting one: the fabrication that does the actual wronging is the invention of private mental states — thoughts, desires, motives, things nobody could have witnessed — rather than the invention of outward acts, even though invented acts look more like flat-out lying and get policed much harder. I've been calling the line between them witnessability. An invented act is at least in principle observable, and so in principle the kind of thing the person could have been asked about or could have authorized; an invented thought is not, which is why it can't be squared with any story about consent, even a hypothetical one. Give me the best argument for thinking witnessability is what matters morally here rather than, say, falsifiability or reputational damage.

Then handle the two objections that seem strongest to me. First: a duty owed to nobody in particular looks like a strange thing to posit just to save the phenomenon, and you'd be better off saying our intuitions are confused. Second, and this is the one I really want engaged rather than waved at: living-third-party accounts can't explain why we think it's wrong to invent the inner life of someone who left no descendants, no one who remembers them, and no community with a stake in the record — a person whose fabrication harms literally no one identifiable. If your account says that case isn't wrong, say so and defend it; if it says it is wrong, tell me to whom the duty runs.
