"Nash's celebrated bargaining solution is governed by a technical axiom with an uncomfortable consequence: a party whose prospects genuinely improve — because a better agreement has become available to it, even one that will never actually be struck — may find that the recommended division does not budge at all. Kalai and Smorodinsky repaired this in the mid-1970s by dropping that axiom in favour of a monotonicity requirement, and the resulting rule is far easier to explain. You start from the breakdown point, what each party walks away with if no agreement is reached, and you identify each party's best conceivable outcome, the most it could possibly extract from any deal the two of them might strike. Those two best-case figures form a target point. Draw the straight line from the breakdown point to that target and take the first place where it meets the set of efficient agreements; that is the fair division. Two virtues follow at once. Because the rule consults nothing but the endpoints, no agreement that the parties would never actually settle on can shift the recommendation, and neither can the units you happen to measure value in: you may record each party's stake in dollars, in arbitrary utility points, or even as ranks from best to worst, and the same split emerges. The deeper point is that each party ends up realizing the same fraction of its best case, which is why the solution so often looks like a straight split down the middle — the two sides come away equally well off."
