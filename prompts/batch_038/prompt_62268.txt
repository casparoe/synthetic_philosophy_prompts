So I've been handed the job of writing the guidance on how our county emergency-management office is supposed to express uncertainty in its recommendation memos — after the storm-surge call last fall went sideways there's a whole review of "how analysts communicate confidence," and I'm the one drafting it. I've been reading around imprecise probabilities (sets of credence functions, dilation, the Ellsberg urn, maximin-flavored decision rules) and sketched an essay arguing for a particular way of framing it. A colleague who does straight-up Bayesian forecasting tore into the middle of it and I can't tell if he's right. Can you read the draft and tell me where it's actually *wrong*, not just where it's unclear?

Draft:

When an analyst writes that a levee overtops with probability 0.2, she is usually reporting the midpoint of a range she'd be willing to defend, and everyone downstream treats the midpoint as the finding. The standard fix is to publish a confidence interval. But an interval is still a claim about one precise probability out there — it says the number is somewhere in [0.1, 0.3] — and it invites readers to average it back into a point. What the evidence actually supports isn't ignorance about a sharp value; it's that no sharp value is on the table at all. That's the imprecise-credence picture, and I think it's the right one for this kind of memo.

Two objections get raised. Inertia: with a wide enough set of priors, maximin or Γ-maximin-style rules refuse to rank the available actions in plenty of cases where we obviously have to choose, so an imprecise state seems practically useless. Dilation: for some sets of priors, learning the outcome of a test *widens* your interval for the hypothesis no matter what you learn, which sounds absurd and gets cited as evidence the whole framework is self-undermining.

I think both objections smuggle in the same assumption: that the quality of a credal state is measured by its width, because the point of the state is to be an accurate map. Measure it instead by what I'll call its decisional reach — the range of payoff structures (options, stakes, utilities) over which that state still picks out a unique admissible action. A precise credence has maximal reach, but buys it with content the evidence doesn't supply: it commits you to bets you'd refuse. A very wide state has near-zero reach, and that's not a bug, it's a reading. Inertia isn't a paradox about the framework; it's a measurement of this state relative to this decision. So the question a memo should answer isn't "is 0.2 more accurate than a range?" but "does the range still decide the question the memo was written to decide?"

Two payoffs. First, this changes what we publish: the range, plus an explicit statement of the decision it's meant to serve, plus a flag when incoming data would dilate rather than sharpen — which is a genuinely useful warning, not a reductio. Second, it explains the Ellsberg intuition without calling people irrational. The preference for the known urn isn't ambiguity aversion as a bias; it's a report that the subject's state has reach over the known-urn bet and not over the ambiguous one. The bet is deficient, not the state.

And on dilation: the paradoxical cases require that you be offered a wager conditional on evidence you already know is uninformative about the hypothesis. If it's irrelevant, widening is the correct response. The feeling of absurdity comes from assuming any data whatsoever should sharpen you.

My colleague's pushback, roughly: reach is just a loss function wearing a hat — I've moved the stakes inside the credal state, so I no longer have imprecise *belief*, I have belief-plus-stakes, which is ordinary Bayesian decision theory with the numbers hidden somewhere less visible. On Ellsberg he says I'm relabeling a known bias as a virtue. And he says my dilation move — nobody actually offers you those bets — dodges the point, because the framework is supposed to be about belief, not wagering.

So: is the reach notion coherent, or does it collapse into expected utility the way he says? Is the Ellsberg paragraph defensible, and if so against what — a specific argument that ambiguity aversion is a bias? Is "the bets aren't offered" a real answer on dilation or a dodge? Tell me the one paragraph I should cut, and give me a concrete decision case where my framing gives obviously bad advice. Oh, and flag any place I've mis-stated the dilation or Γ-maximin material, since thier version of these things matters more than my phrasing does.
