**The examination question (30 marks; candidates were advised to write 900–1,100 words)**

*Question 4.* In *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946), Ruth Benedict distinguishes guilt cultures from shame cultures, holding that shame depends on an audience while guilt does not.

(a) Explain the distinction and say how Benedict uses it to characterise Japan. [10]

(b) Contemporary emotion psychology reports that shame-proneness predicts withdrawal, hostility, and the disowning of responsibility, whereas guilt-proneness predicts apology and repair. Does this finding tell against Benedict's framework? [10]

(c) Suppose a society became convinced that it would do better relying less on shame and more on guilt. Could it act on that conviction? [10]

*Marks were to be awarded as follows: 27–30 first, 24–26 high second, 20–23 low second, 17–19 third, 14–16 pass, below 14 fail. Candidates were told that no sources or quotations were required and would not be rewarded.*

**The candidate's script**

*(a)* Benedict's distinction sorts cultures by the location of the sanction on wrongdoing. In a shame culture the sanction is exposure: what makes a lapse intolerable is that it is seen, reported, and counted against you, and the relief arrives when it stops being seen. In a guilt culture the sanction travels with the person: the wrongdoer stands condemned whether or not anyone ever finds out, and confession, atonement, or punishment can restore him with no audience present at all. Benedict assigns Japan to the first type and the United States to the second, and she is careful to add that the audience in a shame culture need not be present in the flesh, since a man can be ashamed in an empty room while carrying other people's eyes in his head. Two facts about the book's manufacture should be recorded. Benedict wrote it for the U.S. Office of War Information during the war without ever having been to Japan, working from documents, films, and interviews with Japanese-Americans and with prisoners of war; and Japanese scholars criticised it from the moment it appeared in translation, on the ground that the two categories quietly install the West as the reference case and every other society as a departure from it. Neither qualification touches the distinction itself, which is a claim about moral psychology rather than a claim about Japan.

Given that shame is a response to being seen while guilt is a response to one's own verdict, however, the two categories cannot really be alternatives sitting on the same plane. Shame is what morality looks like before its standards have been taken in, guilt what it looks like once they have; the passage from one to the other is the passage from an immature to a mature moral outlook. Benedict's arrangement is accordingly developmental, with cultures climbing from shame towards guilt, and that is what made the typology serviceable to the occupation authorities: a shame culture is not a permanent condition but a stage, and stages can be hurried along.

*(b)* The emotion-psychology programme of recent decades, particularly the work associated with Tangney and her colleagues, does not sort cultures but sorts individuals by proneness, and its results point consistently in one direction. Shame-prone people withdraw, brood, turn hostile, and externalise blame for what they did; guilt-prone people confess, apologise, and set about repairing. The proposed explanation is that shame delivers a global verdict — I am bad — while guilt delivers a local one — I did a bad thing — and only the local verdict leaves the agent anything to do next. This tells against Benedict's framework in two distinct ways.

First, the psychology supplies the normative ranking that the framework was criticised for lacking. If shame reliably corrodes and guilt reliably repairs, then to describe guilt as the mature form of the same capacity is not to flatter one's own culture but to report a finding, and the Japanese objection to the typology loses much of its force.

Second, and more damaging, the discovery that shame is frequently private — felt in an empty room, with no one there to be exposed to — does not so much refine Benedict's distinction as dissolve it. Her shame was defined by publicity; the sanction was other people's eyes. A shame that no eye registers is either guilt under another label or a straightforward counterexample, and in either case the audience criterion cannot carry the weight the typology puts on it. Benedict cannot have this both ways: either shame requires an audience, in which case the psychologists' private shame refutes her, or it does not, in which case her two cultures are distinguished by nothing at all.

*(c)* Two readings of the question need to be separated before it can be answered. The first asks whether a population could become more guilt-prone and less shame-prone, person by person. The second asks whether the sanctions a society actually applies could shift from exposure towards internalised conviction. These come apart. A legislature could abolish shaming penalties — the pillory, public execution, the branding of offenders, the publishing of names — without making anyone a degree more guilt-prone; and a childrearing regime could produce unusually guilt-prone adults inside a legal system that still trades heavily on disgrace. Only the second reading describes something a society might decide to do, and the first does not look like the object of a decision at all.

Even on the second reading the answer is no, though the reason deserves care. Emotions are not actions: nobody can resolve to feel guilt in the way he can resolve to raise his arm, and a society is not an agent with a psyche, since what is loosely called its shame is a distribution of proneness across millions of people, none of whom chose theirs. A society can of course alter its punishments, its schooling, its novels and its films; but altering these is not choosing an emotional economy, it is rearranging conditions that happen to produce one, over generations, with no moment at which anyone decides the outcome and no assurance about what will emerge. There is a further difficulty, and it is the most interesting thing about the question. A reform of this kind must be motivated from inside the order it would replace, and the motives available inside a shame-governed order are shame-based: to get people to abandon shame you would have to make them ashamed of being shame-governed, which spends the very currency it is trying to abolish. Finally, shame is extremely cheap social control. It requires no police, no courts, no records, and no budget; it runs on talk and on the fact that people cannot bear to be talked about. A society that dismantled it would have to substitute something far more expensive, and that is a standing reason for the change not to occur, one that no amount of conviction about the merits of guilt would remove. So the society described in the question could not do what it is supposed to want — though it might fail to notice that it had tried.
