I've talked myself into a fairly strong version of Neurath's line about picture languages and now I want somebody to try and break it.

The position: pictorial signs can't add up to a language, and every system that looks like one is a hybrid that borrows its logic from text. Neurath basically conceded the first half himself — he built Isotype with Arntz and Marie Reidemeister, insisted you show a bigger quantity by repeating the same-sized figure rather than by enlarging it, made the central step a "transformation" from source material to chart, and then refused to call the result a language, settling for something like a language-like technique that always has to ride along with words. (Amusingly, the book laying the method out was titled International Picture Language.) I want to push past him and say why he was right. My reason isn't vagueness or ambiguity — plenty of words are vague. It's that a picture can't mark an operation with no visible correlate: negation, quantification, embedding, the difference between using a sign and mentioning it. So whenever a pictorial system does manage those, it does it by adding marks that aren't pictures of anything — a slash through a cigarette, a bar over a Blissymbol, a little cone meaning "this one's a noun" — and at that point it's grown an alphabet. On this view Blissymbolics failing to become anyone's first language wasn't bad luck, it was structural, and the same goes for any future universal-icons project including the recurring claim that emoji are on their way to being a language.

So what would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and take the hard cases seriously rather than listing objections I could generate myself. Specifically: Peirce's existential graphs, where negation is just enclosure by a closed curve and conjunction is just juxtaposition, and the fact that people have built sound and complete systems out of Venn- and Euler-style diagram notations — is a cut just another "mark that isn't a picture of anything," or does my move there amount to relabeling the problem? The worry that the slash argument proves too much, since spoken words are arbitrary noises too: what's the principled difference between a slash and the word not? Whether Goodman's account of pictures as dense, replete, non-notational systems actually helps me or undercuts me, given that density is a fact about syntax and not about what a system can express. Sign languages and homesign, which suggest language shows up in any modality once there's a community and a need — does that turn my structural story into a just-so story? And comics and kids' drawings, which seem to get across negation, sequence, counterfactuals and thought-versus-speech with no alphabet in sight.

Also tell me what my view predicts that the obvious rival — pictures aren't structurally limited, just much harder to learn and standardize — predicts differently, and how you'd separate the two with evidence I could actually get at. And give me an honest verdict on insulation: I've defined picture so that anything with real grammar counts as non-pictorial, which starts to look like the thesis can't fail. Is that fatal for a claim of this kind or a legitimate stipulation? Which of these would a committed Neurathian grant counts against him and which would he wave off? I dont want a reading list, just the arguments.
