I spent thirty-eight years running stock assessments and long-term monitoring programmes for marine ecosystems, and in the four years since retiring I have been auditing philosophy seminars to make up for what I neglected. Next month our local university's public lecture series has asked me to moderate the question-and-answer session, and I find I am out of my depth in precisely the way I expected to be: I can read the argument, but I cannot tell which of my objections are already handled in the literature and which are not. So I would like your help composing the questions rather than improvising them.

The speaker defends a position in the neighbourhood of David Rieff's *In Praise of Forgetting* — that organized historical memory, far from inoculating a society against a repetition of its past crimes, tends instead to supply the raw material for the next round of grievance — but he pushes the thesis considerably further, and it is his version that I must question. Let me set out what I take his view to be, so that the questions engage the position rather than a caricature of it:

(i) public commemoration — monuments, anniversaries, days of remembrance, mandatory curricular recitation — ought to be progressively abandoned for wrongs more than roughly two generations old;
(ii) a society's commemorative attention is a fixed budget, so attention spent on the dead is attention withdrawn from the living;
(iii) the target is ritual, not knowledge: he wants us to keep the archive and drop the ceremony;
(iv) the dead have no interests and are therefore owed nothing, which means any duty to remember must be an impersonal one about the kind of community we wish to be;
(v) the dispute is empirical rather than moral, and the record favours forgetting, his preferred illustrations being the Spanish settlement after Franco and the way memory of victimization was mobilized in the former Yugoslavia.

Please draft a sequence of ten to twelve questions I could actually put to him from the podium, arranged in the order I should ask them. At least one question must bear on each of the following, and each must come with the answer I should expect from him and a follow-up keyed to that specific answer rather than to a general theme:

- Whether the archive/ritual distinction in (iii) can survive his own economics: an archive is maintained only because someone keeps finding it worth maintaining, and it is commemorative sentiment that funds the finding. If the ceremony goes, what carries the record through the third and fourth generation?
- The evidential asymmetry hiding in (v). His theory and his opponents' theory both predict quiet when they work and horror when they fail, so the successful cases look identical from the outside. Press him for a mechanism and for what observation he would accept as counting against him — and let him give the reply a scientist would find least embarrassing before you follow up on it.
- The point that struck me hardest from my own working life. In fisheries we call it a shifting baseline: each generation takes the depleted state it first encountered as normal, so a series of small forgettings compounds into a decline no one experiences as loss. Ask him whether his view can accommodate a harm that is invisible precisely because the standard against which it would be measured has been forgotten — and if he says the monitoring record is exactly what his archival clause preserves, ask him why anyone would keep funding the monitoring once the ceremonial reasons for it are gone.
- Whether a society is the kind of thing that can forget actively rather than merely fail to retain. Margalit's distinction, as I understand it, seems designed for persons, and the whole notion of blameworthy forgetting appears to depend on it. Ask him who the agent is in his prescription and what the policy would consist in, given that the removal of a monument is itself a conspicuous act of attention.
- Whether (i) and (iv) are symmetrical. If the dead are owed nothing, then the resentment that remembering one's own crimes provokes in their descendants is just as impersonal a harm as the resentment that remembering victimization provokes. Where does his asymmetry come from, and does his statute of limitations run at the same speed for both?

Two further requests, and I would rather you met them than hedged around them. First, do not hand me a menu of twenty-five possibilities and leave me to choose: give me the sequence you would actually run, and state in a sentence or two why that order works on an audience that has just heard a confident and popular talk. Second, commit yourself on the merits. Tell me plainly which of (i) through (v) you think is genuinely untenable as stated, which is merely uncomfortable, and which survives my best questions; if the position holds up, say so and say why, and do not soften the verdict into a summary of the debate. Finally, if in your judgment I have mis-stated the view — if, for instance, no serious defender of it holds (iv) in the blunt form I have given it — correct my summary before writing the questions, and write them against the corrected version.
