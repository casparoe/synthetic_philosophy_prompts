I spent forty years as a working theorist — field theory, and in my later years some numerical relativity — and have come to philosophy only since retirement. I have been reading on the hole argument, and I have arrived at a position that I would like tested rather than endorsed.

My position is that the hole argument, as Earman and Norton set it out in 1987, is not a discovery about spacetime at all but a restatement of something already built into the practice of general relativity: that diffeomorphism-related solutions are alternative descriptions of one physical situation, in the same way that gauge-related configurations are in a Yang–Mills theory. On my reading, the argument manufactures its much-discussed indeterminism only by first granting the points of the bare manifold an identity independent of the fields defined upon them — a grant that Einstein's point-coincidence reply already refused, and that no working relativist would ever make. Hold instead that a spacetime point is individuated by the field values there, or by nothing, and the two models in the hole agree physically; the indeterminism evaporates. So the argument, I maintain, exposes a metaphysical temptation rather than a defect in the theory, and its headline conclusion — abandon manifold substantivalism — is available to anyone who simply declines the temptation at the outset.

I want to know what would count against this. Please rank your answer from defeaters that would be fatal to the position down to those that are merely awkward, and for each say what the world, or the mathematics, would have to look like. In particular I would like you to press on the following, though not only on these:

- Whether my analogy with Yang–Mills redundancy is as close as I assume. A gauge group acting on fields over a fixed background arena seems importantly unlike one whose action drags the arena along with it. Does that difference carry weight I am dismissing too cheaply?
- Whether "diffeomorphism invariance implies redundancy" can be read off the formalism at all, given the old objection that any theory whatsoever can be dressed up in generally covariant form. If the claim really requires background independence rather than covariance of presentation, is my deflation secretly leaning on a substantive thesis about the theory's content — and if so, which one?
- The vacuum case. The point-coincidence reply appeals to coincidences; but in a hole containing no matter there are none, and the only field available to individuate points is the metric, which is the very object in dispute. Does my "individuated by the fields" move become circular precisely where the argument bites hardest?
- Theories in which the same construction is not a redundancy — a material reference frame such as pressureless dust coupled to the metric, unimodular gravity, or shape dynamics. If in some such theory diffeomorphism-related pairs are genuinely distinguishable, does that reduce my view to a theory-relative claim, and thereby weaken its use as a dissolution of the argument in general relativity proper?
- The initial value formulation, where the maximal development of data on a spacelike slice is unique only up to diffeomorphism. Is that "up to" a mathematical footnote, or is it the exact locus of the dispute? And would a demonstration that the quotient structure is badly behaved — fixed points of isometries, non-Hausdorff behaviour, obstructions of the Gribov type — count against me, or can a deflationist absorb it?
- The difficulty of writing down diffeomorphism-invariant observables, and the frozen formalism of the canonical theory. If the redundancy were as benign as I claim, why is the space of genuine observables so hard to characterize? Is that evidence against my reading, or evidence about quantization rather than about the classical metaphysics?

I would also like the two questions I find hardest to answer for myself. First: which of the above would the opposing camps themselves concede as telling against my position — the sophisticated substantivalists, the structuralists who treat the hole argument as motivation for their view, the metric-field theorists, and those who would dissolve spacetime into the behaviour of matter fields — and which would they wave away as beside the point? Please be honest about where a camp's response is principled and where it is evasive.

Second: does my position risk anything at all? If I say that no difference registerable by no possible observation is a physical difference, have I adopted a principle that renders my view compatible with every mathematical outcome, including the ones that look like outright counterexamples? If it has been insulated that way, tell me plainly whether that is a defensible methodological stance for a physical theory or a sign that I am no longer asserting anything about the world — and whether the same charge could be turned on the substantivalist side.

For calibration: what moves me is a precise demonstration rather than an intuition pump. A diffeomorphism-related pair that differs in some invariantly definable quantity would be decisive; a theorem showing that the quotient programme cannot be carried through in the relevant sense would be nearly so; a worked comparison with a specific theory in which the same formal move is not a redundancy would be very persuasive. Appeals to what our concept of a point ought to be, and arguments that turn on the bare possibility of haecceitistic difference between indiscernible worlds, I confess leave me cold — though if you think that coldness is itself the flaw in my position, I want that argued rather than assumed.
