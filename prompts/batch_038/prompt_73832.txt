A colleague in my AI-policy reading group has a formal argument for a fast intelligence explosion, and it seems to prove far too much. He insists on one idealization, which I am granting for the sake of argument: there are no non-cognitive bottlenecks — compute is free, training runs are instantaneous, data is unlimited, and the only input to progress is researcher cognition.

(1) Suppose a system S performs the cognitive work of AI research at least as well as a competent human researcher.
(2) Then S can be set to that work, yielding a successor S′ that performs it better than S does.
(3) Because the same work is now being done better, the interval from S′ to its own successor S″ is shorter than the interval from S to S′.
(4) So the process iterates: each generation is better than the last and arrives sooner than the last.
(5) Hence the intervals between successive generations shrink at least geometrically.
(6) A geometric series with ratio less than one converges to a finite sum.
(7) Therefore the entire remaining path from S to arbitrarily capable systems is traversed in a finite, and indeed short, time.

The trouble is that the argument nowhere uses anything specific to frontier AI. Run it instead on fields whose tools have for decades helped design better tools of the same kind — chip-design automation, compilers, interactive theorem provers — and it predicts explosions that plainly have not occurred, even though those fields face no obvious shortage of the relevant cognition. So which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (5).
