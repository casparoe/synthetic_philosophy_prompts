In three weeks I give a forty-five-minute talk in a joint seminar co-run by our psychology department and the school of public policy, and I would like to walk in with the question period already mapped rather than improvised. My thesis is a deflationary reading of the gender-equality paradox — the recurring cross-national finding that countries scoring higher on composite gender-equality indices show larger, not smaller, sex differences in field of study and occupational choice, and larger gaps in expressed interest in people-centered versus thing-centered work. The usual gloss, in the press and in a good deal of the technical literature, is that once discrimination is removed, latent biological differences are free to express themselves, so egalitarian societies reveal what constrained ones suppress. I argue the pattern is at least as well explained without any appeal to latent differences, by a shift in which motive governs the choice. Sketch of the argument: where occupational earnings largely determine a household's standard of living, both sons and daughters sort heavily on income, which compresses the choice distribution and pulls women into fields they might otherwise avoid; where generous public provision and a compressed wage structure largely decouple individual welfare from occupational earnings, the income motive drops out of the decision and choice is dominated by non-pecuniary considerations — interest, identity, anticipated conditions of work — which are more gender-typed, because the standard indices measure earnings parity, political representation, and adult attitudes toward women's employment rather than the childhood scripts that generate interests in the first place. On this account the paradox tells us which constraint was lifted, not what lay underneath it. I intend to defend two claims as predictions rather than as post-hoc accommodations: first, a within-country reversal of the gradient, such that in high-egalitarianism countries daughters from low-income households sort less gender-typically than daughters from high-income households, because for them the income motive still binds; second, an interaction with the earnings structure of the field, so that the paradox is weakest where the earnings premium over the national median is steepest and strongest where returns are flat. I close on a normative point: a welfare state that removes the material reason to resist gendered sorting has not thereby vindicated the sorting.

The room is specific, and it is emphatically not the room that reads the op-eds. Two attendees are behavioral-genetic psychologists working with twin and adoption designs; they hold that sex differences in interests are moderately heritable, stable in direction across cultures, and best characterized by ratios of variance rather than of means, and one of them will want to know how a purely compositional account of motives produces anything like the male-skewed tails they study. Three are quantitative sociologists of work and occupational segregation, who will attack measurement invariance across countries and over time on the attitude items, the ecological step from a country-level correlation to an individual-level mechanism, and the endogeneity of wage compression to the very sorting I claim to explain. One is a labor economist who studies returns to schooling and compensating differentials, and will say that my "income motive" is just a sorting model with comparative advantage running the other way, and that without identifying variation I cannot distinguish it from preference heterogeneity — and that measured earnings premia misstate the relevant returns anyway. The person whose national index I use throughout will be present, and thinks her instrument measures gender-role attitudes, which cuts directly against my claim that it leaves socialization unmeasured. Finally there is a feminist political theorist who will object that I have quietly conceded the existence of pre-political gendered desires that merely await the right conditions to be expressed, and that treating preferences formed under one regime as revelatory while treating those formed under another as distorted is a double standard. For full disclosure on my own side: the data are cross-sectional, I have no instrument, and parental income in these samples is correlated with parental gender attitudes, which is one reason my within-country prediction is harder to read than I would like.

Prepare me for the discussion. Give me roughly eight objections in order of how much damage they would do to this thesis in this room — not damage to my slides, damage to the claim — each phrased the way the person raising it would actually phrase it, so that I can recognize it in flight rather than reconstruct it afterward. For each: a two-minute reply I can deliver without notes, an explicit flag on where that reply is soft or where it buys time by reframing, and the concession I should make in my first sentence rather than try to hold. Separately, tell me which of these objections is genuinely fatal to the mechanism as I have stated it, as opposed to fatal only to the empirical demonstration of it, since I would rather narrow the thesis live than lose the argument. Identify the single reply that has to land for the talk to succeed at all — the one that, if it fails, everything downstream collapses. And give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, the one that goes at whether my account is consistent with any possible pattern of results, together with the most honest answer I can give to it in public. Please leave out the objections a journalist or a general audience would raise — I have those on speed dial and they cost me nothing here; this room's objections will be technical, and I want them in their own vocabulary.
