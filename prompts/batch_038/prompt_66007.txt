Under the U.S. Endangered Species Act, the decision whether to list a species as endangered or threatened must be made "solely on the basis of the best scientific and commercial data available": the economic consequences of listing are, by design, kept out of that determination altogether. A few steps later in the same statute, the picture reverses. When designating critical habitat, the agency is directed to take economic impact into consideration and may exclude an area whenever it finds the benefits of exclusion outweigh the benefits of inclusion — an open cost-benefit comparison — with one exception: it may not exclude an area if exclusion would result in the extinction of the species. And where a federal project would jeopardize a listed species, a cabinet-level committee may grant an exemption after weighing the project's benefits against the loss to the species; in the case that created that committee — a nearly finished dam on a Tennessee river and a small fish — the committee refused the exemption, and Congress then exempted the project anyway by attaching a rider to an appropriations bill.

Reconstruct the decision rule this structure presupposes about choices under uncertainty. In particular: is the exclusion of economic evidence at the listing stage a claim about value (that a species' continued existence is infinitely valuable, or incommensurable with dollars, so that no expected-value comparison involving it is even well-defined); a claim about risk (a decision-weighting that gives the worst outcome disproportionate weight, so that even a small probability of extinction dominates a large expected benefit); or no first-order normative claim at all but an institutional one — a division of labor that assigns fact-finding to biologists and reserves the weighing of costs to political bodies, which is precisely what the exemption committee and then the appropriations rider proceeded to do? Note also that the extinction exception in the habitat provision is a threshold, not a weight: it does not tip a balance, it removes the question from balancing.

Answer with exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five points should settle, in order: (1) which of the three readings above the listing stage most plausibly presupposes, and why; (2) whether the habitat provision's explicit balancing and its extinction exception are consistent with that reading or require a different one; (3) what the exemption committee and the congressional rider reveal about whom the scheme treats as entitled to weigh costs, and whether that is a coherent allocation rather than a contradiction; (4) which elements of the practice are strictly required by the decision rule you have attributed to it, and where the practice is underdetermined, so that rival rules would license the same behavior; (5) what would have to change — procedurally, not just in the statute's rhetoric — if listing were instead governed by ordinary expected-value maximization with a large but finite value assigned to a species' survival.
