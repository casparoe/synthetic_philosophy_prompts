Our philosophy-of-medicine lab does a thing once a term where everybody posts their own account of health on one page and the rest of the room spends an hour taking it apart. Mine is below and I want to know where it actually cracks before I hand it over.

1. I'm a naturalist about disease in roughly Boorse's way: a disease is an internal state that makes a statistically typical subnormal contribution to survival or reproduction, relative to some reference class. No values anywhere in the definintion.
2. Those reference classes get carved by nature — age, sex, that sort of thing — not by whatever clinicians agree to count.
3. I was glad when the race coefficient came out of the eGFR equation. Race isn't a biological kind and it shouldn't be an input to an algorithm.
4. But I do think ancestry-linked differences, sickle-cell trait and so on, are real biology rather than social construction.
5. Whether you're diseased is a fact about you, not about which population you happen to be standing in. Same body, same status.
6. Verdicts I want to keep: infertility at 28 is a disease and at 45 it isn't; male pattern baldness isn't a disease; short stature isn't one either, however desperately the parents want it to be.

The bit I lose sleep over is whether 1-2 and 3 can live together — whether my naturalism is secretly a brief for race-based medicine. Is that the real problem? And more generally, go through the whole list and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do on first pass, and for each real one what the cheapest repair is and what it costs me. Rank them by severity rather than telling me all six are equally wobbly. Please don't just say give up naturalism unless you can show that I'm actually forced to.
