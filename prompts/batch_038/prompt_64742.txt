Teaching a seminar section on credit and priority and I want to run The Social Network against the multiple-discovery thesis — the Merton line that basically every discovery is a multiple, that ideas arrive in clusters because a field is ripe, so Newton/Leibniz and Darwin/Wallace aren't scandals but the normal case. The Winklevoss thread is a great test case: the twins have the idea for a closed Harvard social network, Zuckerberg builds one within weeks without them, and the film frames it as theft while Merton would frame it as exactly what you'd expect from a ripe field.

What I want from the reading is a position, not a plot summary. Does the film actually commit to whether the idea or the execution carries the credit? It seems to me it's more interesting than people give it credit for here — the opening establishes that Mark's motive isn't the twins' concept at all, and the features that make Facebook Facebook (the ranking instinct he'd already exercised on Facemash, the closed email-domain exclusivity) are his before anyone pitches him anything. If that's right, is the film siding with Merton — ideas are in the air, only implementation is scarce — or undercutting the multiples frame entirely by making the discovery hinge on one person's character and grievances, so that it wasn't inevitable at all? And what does the ending, where he's sitting there refreshing a friend request, concede about which of the two was actually the scarce good?

Then fold in something the film never mentions but the law has already settled twice, in opposite directions: independent invention is no defense to patent infringement, but independent creation is a complete defense to copyright. Which of those two pictures of ideas is the film implicitly running on, and does anything in the story break if you swap it for the other one?
