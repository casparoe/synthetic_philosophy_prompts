THE QUESTION (two-hour unseen paper, students answer both questions, roughly 1,000 words each):

Question 2.
(a) Explain how a moral realist should distinguish the thesis that moral claims are truth-apt and often true from the further thesis that the facts which make them true are wholly independant of human attitudes. Which of these two theses does Harman's challenge about moral facts doing no explanatory work actually target, and can a realist give up one while keeping the other?
(b) Suppose Dr Okonjo defends what she calls convergence realism, namely: moral claims are truth-apt and some of them are true, but what makes a moral claim true is that fully informed and ideally rational agents would converge on accepting it under good reasoning. Is convergence realism a form of moral realism? Say what turns on the answer.
(c) In seminar a student said that if two people are equally well informed about the non-moral facts and equally good at reasoning, and they still disagree about a moral matter, then neither of them has any reason to think they are tracking anything real. Assess this claim.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER (submitted on time, word count 1,030, anonymous candidate number so I've no idea who wrote it):

"Realism is the view that moral sentences are really true or false, in the same way that sentences about tables are, and that some of them are true. Pretty much everything else that gets called realism is just extra. The most important objection to realism is Harman's argument from explanatory impotence. Harman's point is that if moral facts existed and were independant of us, they would have to be the kind of thing that could causally interact with our beliefs, because otherwise we could never have any reason to think they are there. But moral facts, if they are non-natural like the non-naturalists say, can't cause anything at all. A physicist can explain why a cloud chamber shows a trail by citing the proton, but there is no moral observation like that. So moral facts are either not independant, or they are not really facts.

This is why I think the non-naturalist is in a bad spot. They want moral facts to be real and attitude-independant, but they also admit that these facts don't cause anything, and then their realism is doing no work. Once you see that, the space of options collapses down to non-cognitivism on one side and realism-with-causal-facts on the other, with nothing stable in the middle. Mackie is the classic example of a non-cognitivist, since he thinks we talk as though there were moral facts when really there aren't any, and his argument from queerness is basically Harman's point made earlier: it says that objective values would be entities of a very strange sort, and that this strangeness shows up in the fact that they couldn't figure in any causal explanation of our beliefs.

Moral disagreement is the strongest evidence for all of this. If there were moral facts and we could track them, you'd expect people to slowly converge on them over time, the way they converged on the germ theory of disease. Instead we have thousands of years of the same arguments about the same cases. So nobody is tracking anything real, and the realist owes us an explanation of why that isn't the case.

For part (b), convergence realism is obviously a realism, because it says moral claims are true or false and that some are true, and that's just what realism is on the definition I gave above. Also, what makes them true on Okonjo's view is a fact about reasoning rather than about anybody's actual attitudes, so it keeps independance in the way that really matters. I think Harman's challenge applies to it in exactly the same way it applies to any other realism, so Okonjo hasn't fixed anything, she's just relabelled the problem.

For part (c), the student is right. If two people are equally well informed and equally rational and they still disagree, then you can't explain the disagreement away by saying one of them has bad information or is reasoning badly, and that's just bad news for the realist, who thinks there's a fact of the matter that one of them is failing to track."
