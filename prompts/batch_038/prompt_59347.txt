Ok so I'm about 250 pages into a novel and the whole thing is built on Hofstadter's MIU puzzle, which I first read something like fifteen years ago and have been semi-misremembering ever since. Quick version of the book: my main character is a woman who used to write trading algorithms and has become convinced she's found a way to jump out of the system everyone else is stuck inside. I've been a little cute about the structure — Part One is a single chapter called MI, and every chapter title in Part Two is generated from the previous one by one of the four rules, in order, so a reader who knows the puzzle can follow the derivation down the page. Part Three is one blank page. That's supposed to be MU.

A friend read the draft. Smart guy, does something with distributed systems, not a philosopher, and he sent me this paragraph explaining what he thinks I've got wrong:

"Hofstadter's puzzle is Gödel's theorem in miniature. MU is a true statement the system can't derive, and the reason is that the rules can never quite get rid of the I's — you can shrink them down but never all the way to zero, which is exactly the shape of a Gödel sentence, something the system can point at but never reach. The only way out is to add a new rule licensing MU, and that's what he means by jumping out of the system. But you never really get out, because the enlarged system has its own unprovable sentence, so it just goes up forever. That's the real payoff, and it's why your character is basically right: people can step outside and see that their own Gödel sentence is true, and a program can't, because a program is nothing but the system it's inside. That's the anti-mechanism argument Penrose made such a meal of, and Hofstadter is on board with it."

Some of that I half believe and some of it makes my stomach hurt, and I cant tell which is which. Can you go through his paragraph line by line and tell me what's actually right, what's a defensible simplification for a novel, and what's just wrong? Three things I especially want pinned down:

Is MU a "true but unprovable" statement at all, or is that the wrong frame entirely — there's no arithmetic in this thing, just three letters being pushed around, so what would make MU true? And is there any self-reference anywhere in the MIU system, because I've stared at it and I can't see where it would come from.

He says the impossibility is a Gödelian limit you can only see from outside. But I vaguely remember the book also saying you could write a little program that settles, for any MIU string you hand it, whether it's a theorem — which sounds like it flatly contradicts him. Which of us is remebering right, and if we both are, what's the difference he's missing?

And is jumping-out-of-the-system really about adding a rule that lets you write down MU, or is that a different move from the one Hofstadter's actually talking about? Relatedly, does the no-escape-goes-up-forever bit even apply here, or is he importing a fact about arithmetic into a letter game?

The one that would change my ending: is that last claim — humans see their own Gödel sentence, machines can't — something Hofstadter argues for, or is he being conscripted into someone else's argument? I keep running into that line attached to different names and I've never sorted out who says what. And if it isn't Hofstadter's view, what does he land on, and is there anything left my character could honestly claim to do that a program couldn't? Because if the honest answer is "not much," I'm tempted to rewrite her as somebody fooling herself, which is probably a better book anyway. Either way I need to know whether the blank page at the end is a real gesture or just decoration.
