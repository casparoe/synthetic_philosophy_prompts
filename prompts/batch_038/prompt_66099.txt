I write a science-and-ideas column and every time I circle back to the Hart–Fuller debate the German informer case shows up and readers glaze over. So I want a fresh case, built to order.

Construct a thought experiment in which a post-authoritarian court has to decide whether some enactment of the old regime counts as law — and where the natural law answer (that enactment was so iniquitous it was never law at all) produces a practically worse outcome than the positivist answer (it was valid law, horribly unjust, and here is the choice we now face openly). Constraints:

- invented country. No Germany, no Nazis, no named real transition like Chile or South Africa.
- the iniquity has to be real but middling — discriminatory and humiliating, not exterminationist — so Radbruch's extreme-injustice threshold isn't obviously crossed.
- lots of ordinary, unobjectionable legal business happened under the same regime in the same years: marriages, contracts, property registrations, run-of-the-mill convictions.
- the bad consequence cant be stipulated, and it cant come from the natural lawyer being dim. It has to fall out of what denying legal status actually commits them to.
- scenario in at most two paragraphs, no quotations from Hart or Fuller, no citations or real cases, paraphrase only.

Then show the work: what a Hart-style positivist says the court should do, what a Fuller-style natural lawyer says, and where the two verdicts diverge in practice for the people affected. Name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, say what happens if you crank the iniquity way up (does it collapse back into the informer problem?) or way down, and tell me how a natural lawyer would attack the design — I fully expect them to say I've built a strawman, so say where they'd be right.

The point of the exercise is to argue that the natural-law move costs more than it buys on a real transitional docket, becuase the invalidation cant be contained to the nasty bit. A case that asserts that cost instead of generating it is useless to me.
