Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about who started the Cold War, and I came away unable to answer him. My own position is an ordinary one: the question is worth asking, the archival material that opened up after 1991 changed what could responsibly be said about it, and while structural factors — a bipolar postwar settlement, two incompatible systems of security, the vacuum left in the middle of Europe — did most of the explanatory work, that is perfectly compatible with particular decisions having been made badly, culpably, or in ways that made the confrontation worse than it needed to be. My friend, who reads a good deal of international relations theory, thinks this is incoherent, and he put his case as a chain rather than as a mood, which is why I have not been able to shake it:

(1) An agent is blameworthy for an outcome only if that outcome would not have occurred had the agent acted otherwise.
(2) For there to have been no confrontation between Washington and Moscow after 1945, at least one of the two leaderships would have had to act against the security imperatives of its own position in a bipolar system; neither could have done so and remained in power, let alone kept its state intact.
(3) Therefore neither side could have avoided the confrontation, and the particular moves historians quarrel about — the long telegram Kennan sent from Moscow, the mirror-image telegram his Soviet counterpart sent home from Washington, the doctrine and the recovery program announced the following year, the blockade of Berlin, the coup in Prague, the consolidation of a Soviet sphere — were substitutions for one another within an outcome that was already fixed.
(4) But if the outcome would have occurred regardless, then nobody started it, since to start something is to make the difference between its occurring and its not occurring.
(5) So the traditionalists, the revisionists, the post-revisionists, and everyone since who has argued about who began the Cold War have been arguing about a question with no possible answer; and the same holds for the argument about who is to be credited with ending it peacefully four decades later.
(6) Hence the vocabulary of responsibility should be dropped from the historiography of the conflict and replaced by an account of structural constraint, in which individuals appear as occupants of positions rather than as authors of events.

I find (6) unacceptable but I cannot say why, and my friend observes that everything I reach for seems to concede his point: whenever I cite a decision, he asks whether anyone else in that position would not have made the same one, and my only reply is that some decisions plainly made things worse. So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please give me your own diagnosis first, before turning to my hunches, and tell me honestly whether the hunches identify real weak joints or are distractions I should abandon. The hunches are these. First, that (1) may be stronger than ordinary blame actually requires — blame in practice seems to attach to contributions and aggravations as readily as to difference-making, and to come in degrees, whereas his premise demands all-or-nothing counterfactual dependence on a single agent's choice; but I cannot tell whether that is a distinction with content or just a watered-down notion of blame that has surrendered whatever made blame fair. Second, that (2) and (3) may run together two claims a structuralist is not entitled to run together: that a confrontation of some kind was unavoidable, and that this confrontation, with these timings, this ideological form, this nuclear dimension, and this spread into Europe's periphery, was unavoidable. If the inevitability holds only at the coarse grain, does that leave room for blame at the fine grain, and what precisely would such blame be about? Third, that the argument may be self-undermining, since (2) is itself a counterfactual about what leaders could and could not have done and gotten away with — exactly the sort of claim (5) declares unusable — and my friend appears willing to run counterfactuals when they support structuralism and unwilling when they support agency. Is there a principled line available to him here, or is the inconsistency fatal?

Then test the argument's reach, because I suspect that is where it breaks. Does the same reasoning show that nobody is responsible for the outbreak of the First World War, for the persistence of the transatlantic slave trade, or for the financial collapse of 2008 — systems in which, by hypothesis, each participant did what their position required? Does it show that nobody deserves credit for the manner in which the Cold War ended? If it does, I want to treat that as a reductio; if it does not, I want to know what is different about the case my friend picked. Relatedly, it would help to be told what each historiographical camp is actually answering when it says who started the thing, since I suspect some of them mean something closer to who made the first irreversible escalatory move and others something closer to who contributed more to the harm, and that this slippage between readings is doing quiet work in (4) and (5). If you conclude that the chain is valid and the real question is which plausible premise to abandon, say so plainly: I would rather keep (1) than give it up, because it looks to me like the backbone of any fair assignment of blame, and I would like to know exactly what I am paying for keeping it.

One request about how you present all this. Please separate what is settled among historians and philosophers of history who work on these questions from what remains genuinely contested, and do not write as though the field had concluded either that structural inevitability carries the day or that some particular leader bears singular responsibility; I do not believe either is close to consensus. Where your own reading of a premise is a judgment call, or where you are advancing a minority view, flag it as such rather than presenting it as the received position. I am not after references — I will assemble the reading myself — but I would like the argument restated in whatever form makes its weakest joint visible.
