I've been going back and forth on this for weeks and its driving me nuts, so let me try to hand the problem to you as a build job rather than a reading job.

The question: is Buddhist ethics actually a consequentialism? I know the standard moves — karma read naturalistically as a calculus of results, the three unwholesome roots as the loci of disvalue, cetanā/intention as the thing that makes an act the act it is — and I know the standing objection that all of this collapses into something closer to virtue ethics or a theory of the agent's own character. What I don't have is a case that forces the two readings apart cleanly. So build me one.

Specs:

1. One realistic situation, a paragraph or two. A workplace, a family, a small town, a clinic. No trolleys, no monks on mountains, no sci-fi.
2. Fully naturalized. No rebirth, no cosmic karmic bookkeeping, nothing the agent can't check. Every consequence in the case has to be one a secular hedonic utilitarian already accepts as real: ordinary psychology, ordinary social causation.
3. An act-utilitarian (hedonic, agent-neutral) says the act is clearly required — not merely permitted, and not a close call.
4. A Buddhist consequentialist on that naturalized reading says doing it is wrong, unskillful. That verdict has to fall out of the theory's own structure, not out of a deontological constraint I've smuggled in, and not out of the agent's felt guilt counting as extra pain on the utilitarian side.
5. The upāya escape has to be blocked, not just ignored. Which means the good outcome has to be caused *by* the unwholesome state of mind — the hatred or the greed is load-bearing, not incidental. It can't be a case where a bodhisattva could pull off the same result from compassion, and it can't be a rerun of the familiar ship's captain story where killing the would-be murderer gets you praised instead of blamed.
6. The residue has to be small and contained: she's back to her equanimous self within a day, no habit forms, no dispositions shift, nobody ever learns what she was actually feeling. State these stipulations out loud inside the case and mark them as stipulations, because I need to see exactly what work each one is doing.

Then, once the case exists:

- Run both theories on it explicitly, on the same stipulated facts, and point to the precise step where they part company.
- Name the single feature doing the work, and show what happens when you remove it. At least two removals: make the malice convincingly fakeable, and let the residue spread.
- Here's the twist I care about most. Suppose the Buddhist agent takes anattā seriously and denies her own future mental continuum any special weight — suffering is ownerless, so there's no principled reason to privilege her stream over a stranger's. Does the divergence survive that? If it does, the disagreement is axiological. If it doesn't, then the Buddhist consequentialist was doing prudence with extra steps. Show the reasoning, don't just tell me which.
- Say what she should actually do, given that spec 3 rules out any alternative coming close. Whatever you land on, tell me what it costs the theory.
- Now argue against your own case as the utilitarian would: attack the stipulations as fiats dressed up as facts, and press the line that talk of unwholesome roots is just shorthand for a disposition to produce suffering, so calling the occurrent state intrinsically bad is either reducible or question-begging.
- Finally: is the divergence about what's good, or about philosophy of mind and action — where an act is located, whether an occurrent intention is part of the act or part of its consequences? My hunch is the second one, and I'd rather be corrected than agreed with.

If you think the specs can't be jointly met without cheating — that the only way to get the divergence is to stipulate the verdicts instead of generating them from the theories — say so and explain where the construction breaks. That's an answer I can use too.
