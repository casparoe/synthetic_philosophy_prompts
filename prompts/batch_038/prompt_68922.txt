My partner and I have been going in circles for months about whether you can find out from someone else's word that a piece of music is worth hearing, and its become the most annoying argument in my life because we agree on almost everything. She writes reviews of contemporary classical stuff and she's usually right. We both agree I can't tell whether she's right until I actually listen. We both agree I do end up believing her, and that the belief usually turns out true. Where we split: she says that's all there is to knowing, I know the piece is great because she said so, full stop. I say I don't know it, I've got a true belief that came from a reliable source but isn't *my* judgement in any way that matters, and the knowing only starts when I listen and it lands on me.

Poking around the philosophy of aesthetic testimony it looks like the field is stuck on exactly this knot. One camp says testimony about beauty is epistemically defective -- you can't come to know or justifiably believe it from a critic's word, because aesthetic judgement requires first hand acquaintance with the thing. Another camp says that's massively overblown, testimony transmits knowledge about artworks as happily as it does about anything else, and what you're missing when you take my partner's word isn't knowledge but an experience or an appreciation, something non-epistemic. A third bunch say the whole puzzle is manufactured by an unrealistically strict picture of what knowing involves, and that if you'd just describe the case normally you'd see nothing weird going on. Three positions, decades, no movement that I can detect.

So what kind of disagreement is this actually? My hunch is that a lot of it is verbal -- that "know" is being stretched over two different things, having a reliably produced true belief about an artwork's value versus having been in the room with it -- and the camps only look like they're clashing because they keep trading the same cases back and forth and each side redescribes the case in its own preferred vocabulary. Is that right? Or is there a substantive question hiding under the verbal reading, something about whether aesthetic properties are the kind of thing that can be picked up second hand at all, or about what makes a belief an *aesthetic* one rather than merely a belief about an artwork?

One thing that bugs me and might be a lever: my partner says if I'm right about the music I have to be right that I can't know a person is generous from other people's reports either, and I have no good answer. Does the fact that the moral-testimony version of this looks structurally identical help decide whether the aesthetic dispute is about knowledge or about something else?

Diagnose rather than referee -- I don't want a verdict on who's right, I want to know what the shape of the impasse is, what the real fault line is, and what would count as progress: a distinction nobody's drawn, a class of cases one camp can't handle, a change of question.
