Two things landed on me within a fortnight of each other and they turn out to be the same question at different scales, which is why I want a proper look at whether my views fit together. First: after a genuinely bad eighteen months I signed up to the national online gambling self-exclusion scheme, five years, and it can't be switched off early — I did it in a clear-headed hour and I've been telling everyone it's the textbook sensible Ulysses contract. Second: I got a letter saying I'd been balloted onto the citizens' assembly on electoral reform, and part of our remit is to say whether anything should be entrenched against a future simple majority. My facilitator keeps saying the two problems are cousins and I've no idea whether my answers to them are consistent.

Here's the list, written down as I actually hold it:

(1) Akrasia is real. A craving isn't a revision of my values, it's interference with them, so a lock can protect me from me without disrespecting me.
(2) On identity I'm a reductionist — no deep further fact, just overlapping psychological connections, and across a decade those can run pretty thin.
(3) My exclusion binds me even if in year three I'm calm, in therapy, articulate and genuinely no longer want a bet. Relatedly, a mate is on the same scheme and I've said out loud that if he asked me next year to help him find a way round it I'd refuse, and that refusing would be the kind thing to do.
(4) What gives a precommitment its force isn't the authority of the earlier self but the bound self's own endorsement — anticipated consent, not ancestral command.
(5) For the assembly I'd back entrenching a core of rights an ordinary majority can't repeal, and I also think a rule is legitimate only if the people living under it now could ratify it. Jefferson's line to Madison in 1789, that every constitution naturally expires after nineteen years, doesn't move me.
(6) Entrenchment should be friction, not permanence: there has to be some hard procedure — supermajority, a gap spanning two elections, whatever — by which it can always be undone.
(7) I distrust open-textured clauses (dignity, the rule of law, the interests of future generations) because they hand the substantive decision to whoever interprets them in forty years' time. A precommitment should be specific enough to bind conduct rather than vocabulary. Connected to that, I think judicial review is democratically objectionable and I'd rather limits were enforced procedurally than by judges.
(8) And I think the assembly should be able to recommend entrenching provisions that a majority of its own members would vote against today if put to them directly — the point is to bind the same body's later, worse self, not just other people.

Each of those feels fine in isolation, which is exactly why I want somebody else to go through them and tell me which ones are quietly at war. Please don't just flag everything: rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what it costs me to give it. Then tell me seperately which pairings only look bad and are actually coherent, because I'm fairly sure at least one of them is. Finish with the single revision that would clean up the most, even if it's the one I'd most hate making. Lead with your bottom line in one sentence — does the package hang together, and if not, which commitment is the load-bearing mistake? Keep the whole thing under 450 words.
