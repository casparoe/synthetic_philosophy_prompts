I write about mathematics and the governance of AI for a general-interest science desk, and I am trying to build a piece around a collision between two literatures I usually cover separately. One is the inter-universal Teichmüller controversy in number theory. The other is the scalable-oversight literature in AI safety, and in particular the 2018 "AI safety via debate" proposal of Irving, Christiano, and Amodei: the claim that a judge who cannot verify a long or technical argument for herself can nonetheless be reliably informed by watching two capable, adversarially opposed agents argue about it, because exposing a flaw is easier than constructing and defending one — the reason, in their complexity-theoretic analogy, that debate under optimal play lets a polynomial-time judge settle questions in PSPACE, where direct judging reaches only NP. I want the controversy treated as a natural experiment on that claim, and the theory treated as something the controversy puts under pressure. I am emphatically not asking you to adjudicate the mathematics; I have neither the training nor any expectation that a verdict is available to a non-specialist.

The facts, so that nothing turns on your recall of them. In August 2012 Shinichi Mochizuki, an arithmetic geometer at the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences in Kyoto, posted four preprints totaling several hundred pages, written in a vocabulary he had largely invented, which he and a small number of collaborators maintain yield a proof of the abc conjecture. Workshops were held at RIMS, in Beijing, in Oxford, and again at RIMS between 2015 and 2016, the later ones drawing more than a hundred participants; none produced a broad consensus that the argument worked. In 2017 a group of mathematicians who had gone through it in detail converged on a single specific point they could not follow, near the end of the proof of Corollary 3.12 in the third paper. In March 2018 Peter Scholze and Jakob Stix spent five days in Kyoto with Mochizuki and Yuichiro Hoshi, and both sides then published accounts: Scholze and Stix a short report arguing that the gap was so severe that "small modifications will not rescue the proof strategy"; Mochizuki a response several times its length, maintaining that the objection rests on a simplified misreading and that the disputed step is legitimate only when the objects involved are read with the apparatus his theory requires — re-initialization of objects so that their prior history is not carried across, labels distinguishing versions of the same object, and a strict regard for an object's species or type. In 2021 the four papers appeared in Publications of RIMS, a journal Mochizuki edits; Scholze's review recorded that his concerns had not been addressed. The field has since split, with a mostly Japanese group treating the result as settled and most number theorists treating abc as open.

Now the analysis I want, which should run in both directions.

First, do the mapping rigorously, and say where it resists. Who is the judge here, and in what sense — the journal's referees, the workshop audiences, Scholze himself, or the community acting as a distributed judge whose verdict is whatever consensus eventually forms? Who are the debaters, and what exactly is the claim in dispute? Note the structural embarrassment: the framework's engine is adversarial incentive, and in this episode both sides were, so far as anyone can tell, trying to get at the truth rather than to win. Tell me whether that disqualifies the case as a test, or whether it does something more useful — prising the verification-asymmetry assumption apart from the incentive assumption so we can see what each contributes on its own.

Second, interrogate the idea of a weak judge. Scholze is not weak in the sense the theory means: he is among the strongest verifiers the relevant field contains, and he still could not check the argument. What does that imply about a model that treats the judge's limitation as a computational budget? My suspicion is that the literature's word "verify" quietly presupposes a shared background of definitions and intuitions, so that the judge's limit is not only time and attention but membership in an interpretive community — and that the presupposition stays invisible in the complexity analogy because there the language is fixed in advance and the objects come pre-constituted. Say whether you think that is right, and what it costs the proposal if it is.

Third, take Mochizuki's position seriously on its own terms. He says the critics have not entered the framework, and that entry requires years of apprenticeship in its stipulations. Is that an honest description of a genuine conceptual prerequisite, an unfalsifiable shield, or something the debate vocabulary simply cannot express? Then push it into the AI case, which is what my piece is actually about. Suppose a system produces an argument whose crucial steps hold only under stipulations the system itself introduces, and defends their legitimacy by appeal to a way of reading the objects that the critic has not adopted and cannot adopt without rebuilding its intuitions. Is that obfuscation as the debate literature uses the word, or a distinct failure mode that the literature folds into obfuscation and thereby misdiagnoses? I would rather have your term than mine.

Fourth, run the contrast. With the four-color theorem the obstacle to human verification was volume and tedium, and the community's unease was eventually retired by a machine-checked formal proof. With the classification of the finite simple groups the argument was scattered across many authors, a genuine gap surfaced years afterward, and it was closed by a book-length treatment that specialists could then survey and accept. Both controversies converged. This one has not. My working distinction is obfuscation by volume versus obfuscation by re-basing, the second being a case where nothing can be checked until the checker has accepted a new way of constituting the objects being checked — but I expect you to improve on that pair or reject it. Whichever distinction you settle on, say which of the two kinds the debate framework's complexity results actually speak to, and whether the framework has anything at all to say about the other.

Fifth, and most important to me: what does the episode do to the theory, and what would the theory have to add in order to survive it? Be honest about the disanalogies — human debaters cannot run arbitrarily many rounds, the rounds here took six years, there is no payoff structure to exploit — and then tell me what remains once those are set aside. If the optimistic claim is that a compelling but false argument is intrinsically harder to defend than a true one, is there anything in this episode that bears on that claim independently of the incentive question? And specify what development over the next decade would count as evidence for your reading rather than mine, so that I am not publishing a thesis that cannot be disconfirmed.

Where the theory is silent, say so plainly rather than drafting language on its behalf. And engage the argument directly; I am not looking for a list of things to go and read.
