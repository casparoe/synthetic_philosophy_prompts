I sit on the appeals committee of a trade union whose rulebook was drafted in the late 1940s. Its opening declaration of objects states that the union exists to secure the mutual protection and advancement of everyone working in the trade. A later clause, carried over unchanged from the founding draft, provides that only members employed at the works listed in Schedule B may stand for executive office; those works closed decades ago, and the practical effect is that eligibility has narrowed to a small, aging, historically all-male group of craftsmen. Half the committee says the rulebook means what its authors meant in 1948 and that only a full membership meeting can change it; the other half says the declaration of objects governs and the Schedule B clause should be treated as spent.

Please set out the most important implications of Frederick Douglass's mature constitutional interpretation — his insistence that a written instrument be read by the objects it declares for itself rather than by the intentions of the men who framed it, and that where a text admits of two readings, one consistent with justice and liberty and the other not, we are bound to adopt the former — for a dispute of this shape. I would like the analysis to address three points in particular: what his view implies about who is entitled to settle the meaning of a text whose authors can no longer be questioned; whether the discreditable intentions actually embedded in a document's history can be interpretively decisive, or must be set aside in favour of what the words themselves were made to do; and whether his rule of preferring the just reading is genuinely a principle of interpretation or a licence to rewrite the instrument — and what would tell me which of the two it is in my union's case.
