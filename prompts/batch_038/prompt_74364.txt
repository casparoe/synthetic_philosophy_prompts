I teach an undergraduate seminar in early modern philosophy, and next week we read the first of Berkeley's *Three Dialogues*. Before the students meet the vocabulary, I want them to meet the argument, and specifically to see its hinge rather than be told where it is. So I am looking for a short poem to set at the head of the handout.

The hinge is the argument commentators have nicknamed the master argument. Philonous challenges Hylas to conceive of anything existing unconceived, outside every mind; Hylas tries, and finds that in the trying he has only furnished his own mind with the tree he meant to liberate; and from his admission that whatever he conceives he conceives in his mind, the impossibility of unperceived existence is supposed to follow. The obvious reply is that the admission proves nothing: of course framing an idea of a tree puts a tree in a mind, but that says nothing about whether trees get on without one. And Philonous's parry, as I read it, is that the objector has quietly slid from conceiving a thing to conceiving a thing *as* unconceived — a different act, one the first never called for. I want that slide, and the parry, to be the poem's mechanism rather than its subject matter.

Here is the device I have in mind. One clause — Hylas's concession, in your own wording — must appear twice in the poem, word for word identical both times, but broken across lines differently. Nothing may be added, dropped, or reordered within the clause; the entire difference in scope must be carried by where the lines fall and what punctuation you use. Read one way, the clause should report the act of conceiving and sound like a proof; read the other way, it should describe what was conceived and sound like a shrug. Free verse, one or two speakers, no more than twenty lines, no rhyme or meter required.

Three things I do not want. Do not let the poem settle into the old joke about a deity who is always around the quadrangle: that answers a different question — whether unperceived things persist — and dodges this one, about whether unconceived existence can be conceived at all. Do not let Philonous simply win; a serious poem should register that if the best we ever get is the tree with the perceiver struck out, a relative or negative conception, then the challenge can be reissued as a demand for something positive, and the parry begins to look like a victory about words. And do not use the words *subjective* or *objective*, or any line whose only job is to announce the conclusion.

Send the poem and nothing else, except that you may append at most two sentences — fifty words or fewer — identifying the line where the clause returns and saying what the second lineation is meant to expose. The entire response should stay under three hundred words.
