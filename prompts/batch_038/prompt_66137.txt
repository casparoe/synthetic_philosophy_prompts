spent 30-odd years running a neutron diffractometer, and only got into this stuff properly since retiring. On our beam-time committee we kept hitting what I now recognise as a two-person bargaining problem: two proposals both graded excellent, not enough shifts for both, so the committee would lay out two or three feasible splits and pick one. Both groups signed off beforehand on a common scale for what an outcome was worth to them, and 0 means each walks away with its fallback.

The calls I remember cleanly (first number = value to group 1):

- A and B: options (8,2), (5,5), (2,8). Took (5,5) — the products tied, and the chair broke it for the group that stood to gain less.
- C and D: options (3,7), (6,3), (7,1). Took (6,3).
- E and F: options (2,5), (4,4), (5,2), plus (3,3), which got withdrawn before the vote. Took (4,4).

One member used to say the committee shouldn't be moved by an option nobody was going to pick anyway; another said no group should profit from the disappearence of something that was never in contention. They never settled it, because in 12 years it never bit.

Now C and D are back, and (7,1) is gone — the furnace it needed was decommissioned — so the menu is just (3,7) and (6,3). What should the committee do to be consistent with its own record, and which precedent is the closest analog? If the record doesn't actually settle it, say so plainly: lay out the rules that fit all three cases, what each one says here, your best guess and why, and what principle the committee adopts either way. And tell me the case that would have exposed their rule years ago — sketch it with numbers.
