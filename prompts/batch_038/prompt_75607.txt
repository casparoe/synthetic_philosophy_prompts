I've been going round in circles on something and I want a proper conceptual unpacking of it, not a take on whether homeopathy works — take it as read that it doesn't.

My problem is that there seem to be at least three distinct official stances on homeopathy in this country and they don't look like they're built on the same idea of what makes a thing scientific.

First, NHS England's guidance on items that shouldn't routinely be prescribed in primary care — this goes back to the 2017 consultation — parks homeopathy in the same category as glucosamine and chondroitin, omega-3 compounds, herbal treatments and silk garments, under a heading to the effect that there's no evidence of clinical effectiveness for any patient group. So administratively it isn't in a special "not science at all" box; it's in the same box as things that are merely unproven or poor value.

Second, medicines regulation has long had a separate, simplified registration route for homeopathic products that doesn't ask for efficacy data at all, provided the product is diluted past a certain point and doesn't make any therapeutic claims on the label. The state will register it, on condition that it says nothing.

Third, anyone can set up in practise as a homeopath here — no statutory regulation of the occupation, just voluntary registers — and the advertising regulator's role is confined to the claims made for products and practitioners.

So: what criterion of demarcation does each of those three presuppose, and is it the same one? For each, tell me what it only makes sense under, and which rival pictures it quietly rules out. I'm particularly interested in whether any of them is actually falsificationist. It strikes me that homeopathy is unusually falsifiable and has been falsified repeatedly, so a Popperian criterion ought to give it a good mark for boldness — which makes me suspect the real criterion in play is something about evidential support rather than testability, and that the whole thing gets described as "science vs pseudoscience" when it's doing something else. Is that right, or is there a sense in which the NHS position really is Popperian?

Also: does the prescribing category presuppose that reliable benefit counts for nothing unless it comes from the specific effect, and is that a philosophical commitment or just a trial-design convenience? And what does the register-it-but-don't-let-it-claim-anything approach presuppose — that demarcation attaches to claims rather than to substances, or something about informed adults spending their own money?

Please distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, flag where they're underdetermined so that several rival theories would license the same policy, and say whether the three parts are mutually consistent or whether one undermines another. Then tell me what would actually have to change in each of the three if we swapped the implicit criterion for a strict falsifiability test, for a Lakatosian research-programme view, and for a no-sharp-boundary view on which there are only degrees of evidential support.
