I'm in the third year of a philosophy PhD, working on the foundations of subjective probability, and the second chapter of my dissertation is about how much normative work the Dutch book arguments can be made to do once you stop assuming an idealized betting setup. For a joint seminar I co-run with the economics department — half the room has had a graduate asset-pricing sequence, none of it has had a philosophy of probability course — I want to build the chapter's central case study around Long-Term Capital Management in 1998. I don't want a retelling of the collapse; the history is well documented and I know it. I want the case analyzed through the coherence framework, in both directions: what the framework says the actors faced, which features of the episode it captures and which it misdescribes, and what the episode in turn does to the framework as a normative argument.

Take the following as given, so that nothing turns on your recall of the details. LTCM was founded in 1994 by a former head of Salomon Brothers' bond arbitrage desk, and its principals included the two economists who shared the 1997 Nobel for the option-pricing work. Its core business was convergence trading: identify two closely related instruments whose price relationship had deviated from what the model said it should be, take offsetting long and short positions, borrow heavily against them in the repo market, and wait for the relationship to snap back. Its balance sheet leverage was on the order of twenty-five to thirty to one, with derivative notional exposure far larger still. After three years of very strong returns it returned some capital to investors in early 1998; then, following Russia's August default on its domestic debt, correlations spiked, virtually every one of its spreads widened at once, and the fund lost something on the order of four and a half billion dollars in under four months. On September 23, 1998, fourteen financial institutions injected roughly 3.6 billion dollars in a recapitalization arranged by the New York Fed, which contributed none of its own money; the existing partners were effectively stripped of control, and the positions were unwound and the fund dissolved by early 2000. Most of the convergence trades did eventually converge.

Here is what I want from the analysis.

First, the mapping, done strictly and without forcing. In the canonical Ramsey–de Finetti setup an agent posts betting quotients for a set of propositions, is obliged to accept bets at those quotients in either direction as the bookie chooses, at stakes of any size, with payoffs linear in money, and with everything settled once, at the end. Tell me what each of these conditions corresponds to at LTCM, and where the correspondence fails. Which party is the bookie and which is the bettor, and is that question even well posed when the fund is simultaneously taking both sides of thousands of offsetting positions? What plays the role of the betting quotient — the quoted spread, the model's fair value, or the price at which the repo desk will lend? What is the stake, and what does acceptance at arbitrary stake size become once stake size is bounded by a collateral haircut? Above all: what does settlement-once-at-the-end become when the position is marked to market daily and can be closed against your will?

Second, the puzzle that makes the case interesting. Coherence is supposed to guarantee immunity from sure loss — that is the converse direction of the Dutch book result, and it is what gives the framework its normative bite. LTCM's prices look, on the fund's own account, to have been coherent: a single valuation model, offsetting legs, positive expected value, no state of the world in which the whole book loses by construction. Yet it was ruined. Either the coherence claim failed, or one of its idealizations did the work. Identify precisely which, and rank the candidates: non-linearity of utility in money, bounded stakes, sequential rather than single settlement, transaction costs and financing frictions, or the assumption that the agent's quoted prices are ones it must actually trade at. Note that a marked-to-market leveraged position converts a terminal-payoff bet into a bet whose effective stake is renegotiated continuously by parties who did not agree to your quotients — and say whether that is a repairable gap or a different game entirely.

Third, run the analysis on the other side of the trade. In September 1998 the prices across related instruments arguably implied something incoherent, in that no single pricing measure was consistent with all of them. Nobody could make a book against that incoherence. Explain why: what has to be true of a price vector for it to behave like an agent's quotients, and what is missing when the counterparties are dispersed, when prices are not two-way commitments, and when there is no obligation to accept the other side? Then address a point my examiners will press: no-arbitrage pricing theory looks like the same idea transplanted — absence of arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of a coherent pricing measure — and its idealizations appear to fail in exactly the same places. Are these two coherence theorems really one theorem, or does the analogy trade on an equivocation between consistency of an agent's dispositions to bet and consistency of a vector of market prices?

Fourth, do something constructive. Could one state a pathwise or solvency-relativized version of the Dutch book result — coherence conditional on a collateral constraint, or immunity defined over trajectories rather than terminal states — that would have predicted LTCM's fate rather than merely accommodating it after the fact? Sketch what such a theorem would have to say, what it would have to give up, and whether the resulting principle still supports probabilism or has been drained of the content that made the argument worth making.

Fifth, be honest about the limits. These traders were not, in any straightforward sense, reporting credences in propositions they understood; they were running a statistical model of mean reversion with a financing overlay. Say where that objection bites, where it can be answered by treating the model's outputs as operational subjective probabilities, and where the case is simply outside the theory's scope. I would rather be told that the framework is silent about LTCM than given a strained reading in which it speaks.

Finally, land the philosophical point. There is a deflationary line of criticism — associated most prominently with Hájek — holding that the Dutch book argument merely dramatizes an inconsistency in an agent's attitudes, and that the guaranteed-loss protection evaporates once utility is non-linear or bets are indivisible or the agent's circumstances change. Does this episode support that deflationary reading, or does it instead suggest that the idealizations are not cosmetic but doing the substantive normative work? And is there anything left of the popular saying, usually credited to Keynes though nobody has ever located a source for it, that markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent — or is that just an unrigorous restatement of the sequential-settlement problem you identified in the second part?

Please don't give me a reading list, citations, or a survey of who has written what; I have all of that. Work the argument out from the framework itself. Where a factual feature of 1998 matters to one of your conclusions and you are unsure of it, flag it and show me how much of the conclusion rides on it.
