I am a junior at a public high school in the American Midwest, and I spent last school year on an exchange in a mid-sized town in southern Germany. Two small incidents from the end of each year have stayed with me, and I cannot stop comparing them. At my German host school, the year-end report was handed to each student individually, in private, and there was no such thing as class rank, no honor roll, and no ranking read aloud at any ceremony; when a substitute teacher once mentioned how well one girl had done on a physics exam in front of the rest of us, two parents complained to the administration and he was formally reminded of the rules. At my own school here, by contrast, the honor roll was printed in the local newspaper with everyone's names and grade-point averages, our class rank was calculated and distributed, the top-ranked student was announced by name on the graduation program weeks in advance, and nobody thought any of this was even a question.

The part that puzzles me is that the two countries seem to run their privacy in opposite directions, and not just in schools. My host father spent a month trying to obtain the file behind a road project a few streets from our house, and he kept being told that various parts of it could not be released; he described the default attitude as being that a document belongs to the office that produced it. Here, a student reporter I know got the entire district's discipline-referral data in about two weeks with a form and a small fee, and the superintendent's salary, every teacher's pay band, and the school board's emails are all things anyone can ask for and expect to receive. So Germany appears to treat information about a person as almost untouchable while treating information about the state's own business as comparatively hard to reach, and my country does the reverse: the individual student's record is published for the town to read, while the government's records are handed over on request. My host father calls this "data protection," but I think he means something much stronger by it than we mean when we use that phrase here.

In civics class we also spent a week on the Chinese system for people who ignore court-ordered debts: their names, ages, home cities, and partly redacted identity numbers go into a public database, they are barred from buying airline tickets or high-speed rail tickets and from various other ordinary activities, and some courts have gone further and put the names on public display screens. Our teacher presented this as simply the extreme end of the same spectrum, which struck me as too quick, because it looks to me like a third and different idea about what a person is and what a record of her conduct is for. So: what theory of the person, and of the relationship between a person and her government, does each of these three practices actually presuppose? Is the German practice committed to the view that information about me is a kind of property I own, or to the view that the state is presumptively an adversary to be kept at a distance, or to something about a person's dignity that cannot be reduced to a measurable record? Does the American practice presuppose that anything a publicly funded institution produces belongs to the public, and that merit or reputation only functions if it is visible? Is the Chinese practice committed to the idea that a person just is the sum of her recorded conduct, with visibility doing the work that prisons and fines would otherwise do? Please tell me what each practice strictly requires versus what it merely happens to fit well with, whether any of the three is internally inconsistent with other things the same legal culture insists on, and what would concretely have to change in each country's schools and record-keeping if it adopted the other's presupposition. I would like the answer to end with a single line beginning "Presupposition:" giving, in one sentence, the one commitment the German report-card practice cannot survive without, or the single word "Underdetermined" if no one commitment is in fact strictly required.
