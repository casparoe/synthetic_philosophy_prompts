I sit on my company's Post-Incident Review Board as the platform team's representative. The board owns our postmortem template and adjudicates incident reports that teams dispute, and it votes next week on a substantial change to that template. I've been asked to circulate a supporting memo beforehand. I'm a staff software engineer with no philosophy training, so please write for someone who can follow a precise argument but should not be assumed to know the vocabulary; where a distinction from that literature genuinely does work here, introduce it in plain terms and say what it settles, rather than naming it and moving on.

Here is the incident that prompted the proposal. On 14 March a feature flag was flipped in production to enable a new pricing path. That path acquired a database connection per request inside a retry loop instead of reusing one from the pool. Separately, six weeks earlier we had lowered the connection-pool ceiling on that service as a cost measure, and four months earlier we had raised the circuit-breaker threshold after an unrelated incident, because the old threshold tripped during legitimate traffic spikes. Our deploy pipeline classifies flag changes as configuration, so no canary ran. The service shed traffic for 47 minutes.

Our data team can also show that a scheduled cache flush was due twenty minutes later and that, in staging, that flush on its own drives connection demand above the lowered ceiling. So the outage appears to have been coming anyway, on a slightly different timeline, with or without the flag change.

The proposed template change does three things. It deletes the single Root cause field. It requires a directed graph of contributing conditions, each node tagged with whether we actually hold a deployable lever on it — a setting, a code path, a process gate — that would have changed the outcome. And it restricts the graph to conditions that were abnormal at the time, so that the existence of our legacy retry code, or of electricity, cannot enter it.

The board is split. One group holds that the root cause is the flag change, because had it not gone out we would have been fine that day, and because once every condition is admitted to the graph nothing is the cause and nobody is answerable for anything: the report ends by indicting the organisation. The other group holds that the root cause is the lowered ceiling together with the raised breaker threshold, because flag changes will keep happening and those two are what we can set differently next time; they add that the first group's test is arbitrary, since by the same reasoning the cause is whoever wrote the retry loop in 2019, or the day we picked this database.

Two further constraints. Our external compliance auditor requires each major incident report to state a single root cause in one sentence, and we cannot drop that unilaterally. And an SRE has proposed building a counterfactual replay harness that reruns the incident in staging with one condition held at its old value, to settle disputes like this empirically; one board member objects that such a replay cannot isolate a single condition, since changing the flag also changes load timing, on-call behaviour, and whatever else was in flight.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It must take a position — adopt, adopt with named amendments, or reject — and give reasons the minority can argue back against, not a neutral summary of both camps. Specifically, I want it to state what the board should mean by root cause once it has settled how it will use the term; how the auditor's one-sentence requirement can be satisfied without misdescribing the incident; what rule the graph should follow in the case where the outage was coming regardless; and how much evidential weight the replay harness deserves given the objection to it. Say plainly where the two groups disagree about what a cause is and where they disagree only about what a postmortem is for. Give the strongest consideration against your own recommendation, in wording the minority would recognise as their own. Close with a checklist a reviewer could apply to the next disputed report without coming back to the board.
