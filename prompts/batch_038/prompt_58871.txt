It's about whether arithmetic might turn out to be inconsistent.

His line: we can't prove PA consistent from within, and what we've got instead is (i) nobody has ever found a contradiction, and (ii) we can build a model of it inside set theory, which itself can't prove its own consistency. That's thin. So there's a live, non-negligible chance that some sentence and its negation are both provable, and then classical logic hands you explosion: every sentence becomes a theorem and maths stops being about anything at all. His conclusion is that we should hedge — retreat to something constructive or finitist, or move to a paraconsistent logic where one contradiction doesn't take the whole house down.

My line, which is what I want argued for: the hedging isn't just unnecessary, it's aimed at a threat that has no possible shape. The core of it is this. Being inconsistent means there's some particular finite proof of a contradiction, and there's no reason whatsoever to think the shortest such proof is anywhere near the scale of human or even cosmic effort — it could be coded by a number with more digits than there are particles in the observable universe. So you have to split his scenario in two. In the first, PA is inconsistent and the proof is short enough that somebody actually finds it: that would be devastating, but it's also the scenario nobody really believes in, and no amount of hedging would have saved us from it anyway. In the second, PA is inconsistent but every contradiction is permanently out of reach: nothing changes for anybody, ever, and the theory keeps working exactly as it does now. He has to tell me which of the two he's worried about, and I dont think he can — the first isn't what his own argument supports and the second isn't a worry.

Build in this bit too, it's the part I liked best. The gap between what PA proves and what anyone could ever actually check is already enormous, no inconsistency required. PA proves that certain specific computations halt — computations so long that nothing could ever run them or inspect them step by step — and it proves plenty else that we only trust because it's never let us down. Provability has always outstripped verification by an unimaginable margin. So going from 'outstrips us by an unimaginable margin' to 'outstrips us outright' isn't the difference between a working theory and a broken one; it's the same difference we've been living inside all along, just pushed further out.

What I want argued: that hedging costs real mathematics — theorems we'd give up, constructions we couldn't carry out — in order to buy protection against something that could never be discovered, used, or felt; and that you can't rationally act on a risk which has no possible expression in practice.
