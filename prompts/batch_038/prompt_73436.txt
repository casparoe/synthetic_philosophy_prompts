I TA an undergrad decision theory seminar and this week was practical reason and incommensurability, and Buridan's ass came up like it always does. One of the students is an econ double major and he basically scoffed: the donkey isn't a paradox at all, it's just a consumer sitting on an indifference curve between two identical bundles, and nobody in Econ 101 starves on an indifference curve, because you can just take either one, and if you're bothered by being arbitrary you can randomize, which is sometimes straight-up better anyway. His line was that the medievals made a catgory error because they didn't have utility functions, and once you write the problem down properly the starvation evaporates. Half the class found this convincing and I didn't push back well in the moment, partly because I had already built the demo he was implicitly appealing to: a toy agent with a numeric utility function over two bales of hay (distance, freshness, whatever), an argmax, and a small epsilon of random noise as a tiebreaker. It picks a bale in under a second, every run. It feels like it proves his point and also like it's cheating, and I can't articulate which.

So: audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the two bundles, the indifference curve, the budget constraint, the utility function, the act of choosing, the starvation — and then walk through where the mapping breaks. Sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually bear on his conclusion and the ones that are just decoration, and tell me whether the category-error verdict survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying the whole thing.
