A mid-sized port city operating under its own charter has recently reformed its franchise in two steps. First, it extended the municipal vote to every adult resident of five years' standing, irrespective of nationality. Second, and more unusually, it created a supplementary roll of non-resident "externally affected persons": people living downstream on the same river, under its flight paths, or in neighbouring jurisdictions whose tax base, housing market, or water supply is measurably moved by the city's zoning, port, and aviation decisions. Membership on this roll is determined by a standing Impact Assessment Office, which estimates the magnitude of the cost or benefit borne by each group it can identify; roll members vote in municipal elections with weights proportional to those estimates, subject to a cap ensuring the roll as a whole never carries more than twenty percent of total voting weight. The reform was publicly justified by appeal to the idea that everyone affected by a decision ought to have some say in making it.

Here is the difficulty I want you to engage. The Impact Assessment Office's methodology, its threshold for what counts as an identifiable group, and the cap itself are all set by ordinary municipal legislation — that is, by a body that already includes the residents and the currently enrolled external voters. The criterion is therefore being used to constitute a constituency by a constituency that the criterion itself helped constitute. When someone objects that the flight-path residents are mis-weighted, or that a downstream community has been wrongly excluded, the objection is adjudicated by the very body whose composition is at issue, and no higher forum exists.

With that case in view, give me your credence — a single number — in the following claim:

There exists a formulation of the principle of democratic inclusion which, applied to a contested-boundary case of this kind, yields a determinate answer to the question of who may decide who may decide, without either (i) depending on a prior decision taken by a body whose own standing is presupposed rather than justified, or (ii) terminating in an acknowledged fiat — a stipulation that the question is settled because some group has in fact settled it.

I do not want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or attributed positions; work directly from the argument. I do want the derivation made explicit, in the following order:

1. Four to six sub-questions on which your headline number depends, each assigned a rough probability with no more than two sentences of justification. Candidates I would expect you to consider, though you should substitute your own if you think mine are wrong: how determinate "being affected" is as a pre-institutional property, ascertainable before any decision procedure exists; whether a coercion-based criterion — the demos consists of those subject to the state's coercive authority — escapes the regress better than an affectedness-based one, on the ground that the coercion is already there to point at independently of anyone's judgement; whether a self-applying or fixed-point criterion, one that governs its own extension, could be principled rather than circular; whether the constitutive-historical route, on which a people simply establishes itself and legitimacy is conferred retrospectively by its subsequent performance, is a genuine answer or a restatement of the fiat; and how much of the problem is verbal, dissolving once the justification of a decision rule is separated from the causal explanation of how that rule came to be adopted.

2. An account of how those sub-probabilities combine into your headline number — multiplicatively, as a weighted average, as a minimum over the terms, or by some other rule — together with a defence of why that combination rule is appropriate here rather than merely convenient.

3. The single term to which your final number is most sensitive: where a fifteen-point shift would move the headline furthest, and why the claim is more exposed there than elsewhere.

4. A separate and sharper judgement on the case itself: your probability that the city's arrangement aggravates the circularity rather than mitigating it — that giving the affected roll a legislative hand in determining its own future composition and weighting is a genuine defect in the design, rather than the most honest available response to an unavoidable one.

5. Finally, suppose two colleagues have examined the same case and one puts 0.75 on the headline claim while the other puts 0.2. Identify which of your sub-questions most likely carries their disagreement, and describe what a single argument or piece of evidence would have to look like in order to move you the greatest distance.
