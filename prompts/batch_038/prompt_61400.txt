I run a 20-person nonprofit that places people coming home from prison into jobs. When an employer calls about a candidate, one of my four placement officers has to decide within about a day whether to vouch — whether to actually believe, and then say out loud, that this person will hold the job. We do maybe fifteen of these a month and the stakes cut both ways: a bad placement costs the employer real money and tends to poison that employer, sometimes the whole sector in our town, for everyone who comes after; but declining to vouch because the evidence isn't there pretty much guarantees the person doesn't get hired, since nobody hires out of our program on a shrug. My staff have split into two camps — one wants a hard evidentiary threshold before we say anything, the other says there's never evidence about an individual person and that waiting is just a polite way of saying no.

Write me a decision procedure my officers can actually run in half an hour with a candidate file and a phone call, built out of what James and Clifford genuinely disagree about rather than generic "weigh the risks" advice. Specifically: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops at no, where it hands the call back to a human because it's a values question and not a factual one, and which of our decisons actually have the structure James thinks licenses belief ahead of the evidence — can't be settled by waiting, and the waiting itself guarantees the worse outcome — versus which are just impatience wearing that language. I also want a distinction they can apply between believing something and merely acting as if it's true, and what changes when we can hedge: trial period, our own money at risk, weekly check-ins. Hedging seems to buy evidence faster than waiting does, and I want the procedure to exploit that instead of treating it as a dodge. Include a logging step and a 90-day review of what we predicted versus what happened, because I want to know whether our judgments track anything at all. Anything flagged goes to me for signoff, and our funder audits retention numbers, so whatever they do has to leave a record.

Then run it on two cases. One: a 24-year-old with no real work history, two short-lived placements, a strong interview and a single good reference from a volunteer site, up for a warehouse job with an employer who's hired from us twice and had one placement fail. Two: a candidate with eleven clean months and solid references, plus an unverified tip from a former supervisor that something is off, up for a job at our single biggest account. I expect the procedure to come out against me on the second one; say so plainly instead of softening it.

Finally, be honest about where the thing will misfire, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what this whole setup just can't do.
