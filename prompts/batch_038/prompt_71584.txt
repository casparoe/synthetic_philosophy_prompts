ok so my friend Dana and I got into this for like three hours last night and neither of us moved an inch. Grade the arguments, not the arguers.

Background we both accept: the standard bubble/echo-chamber split. A bubble is what you get when people simply never hear the other side — exclusion by omission, nobody's saying anything bad about outside sources, they're just not in the room. An echo chamber is worse in kind, because it actively teaches members to distrust outside sources; you get out of it by rebuilding trust, not just by adding exposure.

Dana spends a lot of time in an online community of parents whose kids have a particular rare condition (I'll leave the specifics out, they don't matter), and she thinks it fits neither box, so she's coined a third one. She calls them exit-cost communities. Nobody in there ever disparages an outside source. Members will readily say the mainstream clinic's guidance is credible, links get posted constantly, exposure is basically maxed out. But if you actually change your mind and adopt one of those outside claims, you lose standing — you get cold-shouldered, your posts stop getting answered, you're treated as a defector who's gone over. So belief stays locked without any distrust ever being manufactured.

The fight, roughly in the order it happened:

1. I said this isn't a new category, its just one of the mechanisms an echo chamber runs on. Discrediting a source doesn't have to be done with arguments; making it socially expensive to believe the source *is* a way of discrediting it. She's found a subtype, not a third kind.
2. She said no, and that the diagnostic difference is the whole point. If you go in with the standard checklist looking for distrust-of-outsiders rhetoric, you find none, you classify the group as open and healthy, and you prescribe the wrong medicine. Her category is supposed to explain why the obvious fix — more sources, more links, more exposure — does literally nothing there.
3. I said then it's not a problem about epistemic structure at all, it's a psychology problem. If members privately agree the outside source is reliable and still don't update, that's akrasia under social pressure. It belongs in a story about why people cave to their peers, not in a taxonomy of information environments — those are about what's available to be believed and how it's presented.
4. She said the test for whether a category belongs in a taxonomy like this is whether it changes what you'd do, and hers does: it tells you to stop working on the group's information supply and start lowering the price of changing your mind — anonymous posting, off-ramps that don't cost you your relationships, public credit for people who update.
5. I said fine, but then every close community is an exit-cost community and the category picks out nothing. Leaving a lab, a church, a union, a friend group costs you. And worse for her: some exit costs are good. A physics department penalises you for adopting a claim that flatly contradicts well-established results on the strength of a blog post, and that penalty is doing real epistmic work. So she can't just say "costs on changing your mind = pathology."
6. Here's where we got properly stuck. She wanted to draw the line between being punished for leaving altogether and being punished for adopting one specific outside claim while staying — the second is the pathological one. I said that doesn't survive the department case either, because that's exactly what happens there: nobody expels you, you just lose standing, and rightly so. She said then the real difference must be whether the penalty tracks the evidential quality of the belief or only its social direction. I said that criterion is only usable from outside, by someone who already knows which beliefs are right, so neither the members nor an auditor can actually apply it — and if you need the truth first, the category isn't helping you find anything. She said I was begging the question and we went around again.
