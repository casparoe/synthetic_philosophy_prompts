For two years our reading group has failed to make progress on a question that ought to be decidable: whether Bongard's hundred problems — twelve small figures per problem, six on the left page exemplifying an unnamed concept and six on the right page exemplifying its absence — constitute a legitimate testbed for concept induction, or a benchmark whose results are artefacts of its own construction.

The first position takes its cue from Hofstadter's remark in Gödel, Escher, Bach that the skill of solving these problems sits close to whatever pure intelligence amounts to, and from Chollet's argument that intelligence is skill-acquisition efficiency rather than skill at any one task. On this view, a system that names the intended concept for a large fraction of the battery, given only the twelve figures and no task-specific engineering, has thereby demonstrated general concept formation; the number of examples required is the measurement, and sample efficiency on a fixed battery is a fair basis for comparing a machine with a human.

The opposing position holds that this is confused twice over. First, the figures were authored by human beings and retained only because human beings could solve them, so a battery of them is a sample drawn from the designer's prior rather than from the space of concepts; performance therefore measures the overlap between a learner's inductive biases and the designer's, and no score means anything without an explicit statement of which priors the learner was granted. Second, and independently, any split of six against six is underdetermined: many mutually inconsistent rules separate the two pages, so solving amounts to guessing which rule the author meant, and the task tests a pragmatic mind-reading competence rather than induction. A third strand, closer to machine learning than to either, says both sides are missing the point: what explains human performance is a particular mechanism — search over a compositional vocabulary of low-level visual features weighted toward short hypotheses — and mechanism claims of that sort are neither supported nor threatened by benchmark scores.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than accepted, is that the impasse is largely verbal: generality in the first position means prior-independence within a battery held fixed, whereas in the second it means robustness across the space of possible batteries, and once those are separated most of the heat disappears, leaving a narrower question about which priors may be granted without a comparison becoming unfair. I am not confident of this. The real disagreement may instead concern standards of evidence (fixed-battery sample efficiency versus transfer across battery families), or whether a benchmark score can ever bear on a mechanism claim, or the status of authorial intention — whether the intended concept is a real feature of each problem or a useful fiction that both humans and machines happen to converge on.

So that your diagnosis has something concrete to bite on, here is a finding of ours. For sixty of the problems we constructed two transformations: a surface remapping that preserves the intended concept while exchanging the roles of shape, size, orientation and shading, and a polarity inversion that makes the concept exemplified on the right page the one exemplified on the left. Members of our group converge on the same verbal concept after remapping in the overwhelming majority of cases and are largely unbothered by inversion, whereas our program-synthesis implementation, which solves the originals at a respectable rate, degrades sharply on remapped versions and after inversion frequently reports a different concept — usually one that also fits the original twelve figures. We cannot tell whether this supports the first position (humans have a mechanism that tracks the designer's concept) or the second (humans and model alike are reading the designer's mind, differing only in how wide their priors are).

What kind of disagreement is this, then? Please diagnose its structure rather than adjudicating between the two positions or surveying how the literature divides, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a redesigned experiment, a formal result such as a bound on what any learner with a stated prior can achieve across a family of batteries, or a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why.

Give your answer as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. The items should cover, in this order: the single claim on which the two positions genuinely conflict; your classification of the disagreement; the best evidence for that classification drawn from what each side actually says, where our remapping and inversion results may be used; what would count as progress; and whether you think that progress is in fact achievable.
