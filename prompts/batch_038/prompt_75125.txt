My brother died in March and for about fifteen years he'd been building a world on a privately hosted server — terrain, towns, a rail network he was weirdly proud of. My mum cant stand the thought of it just sitting there and wants the server switched off; I've been arguing that that isn't like deleting some old photos, it's destroying something that was actually there. But when I try to say why I get tangled, and I suspect part of my argument is grief dressing itself up as metaphysics. What I want to find out is whether "it's real" and "it's a loss" can come apart at all — because if they can't, my row with my mum is about something else entirely.

So build me a thought experiment that tests that. I want one case, one paragraph, present-day tech, in which a virtual object is genuinely destroyed — real on the terms of the view that says virtual things are real digital objects rather than pretend ones, the sort of position Chalmers defends — and yet nobody whatsoever is harmed by its destruction. Constraints: no full-dive VR, no uploads, no NPCs with inner lives, nobody deceived about anything, and you can't get to the verdict just by stipulating that nobody cares or that it holds no sentimental value; the case has to earn that. Phrase it so I could read it out to my family without saying the word ontology.

Then show the working: what a virtual realist says about the case and why, what a fictionalist says and why, and whether they land on the same verdict for reasons that can't both be correct. Point at which single feature is doing the work, tell me what happens to both verdicts if you put that feature back — say, one person who logs in once a year — and finish with how someone who thinks virtual harms are real harms would attack the case. If the spec turns out to be unsatisfiable, say so and say which constraint you'd have to drop, rather than quietly softening one to make it work.

British English please.
