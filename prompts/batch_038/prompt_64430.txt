I must explain how I come to ask this, because it is not the usual sort of question. For thirty-eight years I worked as a physical chemist, the last twenty of them at a national measurement laboratory, where my days were given over to primary standards for mass and electrical quantities and to the uncertainty budgets that accompany them. I retired not long before the kilogram ceased to be a lump of platinum–iridium in a vault outside Paris and became instead a fixed numerical value in an equation — an event that struck me at the time as rather more philosophical than my colleagues allowed. Since then I have read seriously and rather slowly, and for the past year I have been going through Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* with three former colleagues; we meet on Thursday afternoons in a back room of the municipal library. We have now reached Book V, and I am stuck in the middle of chapter 5, the discussion of exchange and money. The one classicist in our group is wintering in New Zealand, so I have no one competent to interrogate. I should add that I have no access to a university library and no intention of acquiring the secondary literature: what I want is your own close reading of the text in front of us, not a survey of who has defended which interpretation. If you think one of my questions rests on a misreading of the Greek, or on an artefact of translation, please say so plainly rather than steering around it.

Here is the passage, in the Rackham translation as hosted by the Perseus Digital Library, at roughly 1133a–1133b. I quote three stretches.

(i) "Now proportionate requital is effected by diagonal conjunction. For example, let A be a builder, B a shoemaker, C a house, and D a shoe."

(ii) "Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. As therefore a builder is to a shoemaker, so must such and such a number of shoes be to a house, [or to a given quantity of food]; for without this reciprocal proportion, there can be no exchange and no association; and it cannot be secured unless the commodities in question be equal in a sense. It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

(iii) "Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant. Hence the proper thing is for all commodities to have their prices fixed; this will ensure that exchange, and consequently association, shall always be possible. Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes. There must therefore be some one standard, and this accepted by agreement (which is why it is called nomisma, customary currency); for such a standard makes all things commensurable, since all things can be measured by money. Let A be a house, B ten minae and C a bedstead. Then A=B/2 (supposing the house to be worth, or equal to, five minae), and C (the bedstead) =B/10; it is now clear how many bedsteads are equal to one house, namely five. It is clear that before money existed this is how the rate of exchange was actually stated—five beds for a house—since there is no real difference between that and the price of five beds for a house."

My difficulties are these.

1. Is there one standard or two? In (ii) the single standard by which all commodities must be measured "is in reality demand"; in (iii) the single standard is "accepted by agreement," and "all things can be measured by money." I can read this either as a flat inconsistency or as a hierarchy in which need is the measure in principle and coin its conventional deputy, and I cannot decide which. What force do you take the words "in reality" to carry? Are they correcting ordinary opinion in the way Aristotle elsewhere corrects the opinion that money is wealth, or do they merely mark what is true in the normal case?

2. What kind of impossibility is conceded in "it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense"? I want to know whether "commensurable" is being used in its mathematical sense of two magnitudes admitting a common measure, and if so whether the failure he grants is a failure of kind — a house and a bedstand are not quantities of one and the same magnitude, so no ratio is even definable between them — or merely a failure of precision, the ratio being definable but not determinable. In my old profession the distinction was everything: an instrument may be biased, in which case you calibrate it, or the quantity may be undefined, in which case no calibration helps. And if the fallback is that demand is "sufficiently accurate for practical purposes," is that simply the methodological allowance he makes early in the *Ethics*, that one must not demand the same exactness of every inquiry? If so, does his account of justice in exchange then deliver only an approximation, and is that a defect or the whole point?

3. "Diagonal conjunction" plainly presupposes some figure. I imagine a rectangle or parallelogram with the four terms at its corners and the crossed pair joined by its diagonals, but my text prints no diagram, and I cannot see whether the geometry is load-bearing or a mere device for indicating which terms are to be paired. Is there any reason to suppose the choice of a diagonal is more than bookkeeping?

4. When he says money "does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will," what alteration does he have in view — debasement of the coinage, a legal revaluation, or simply the withdrawal of a community's willingness to accept it? And does the sentence commit him to the view that what makes silver money is a decision of the polis rather than anything about the silver? I seem to recall that in the *Politics* he describes money as growing out of the necessities of exchange, people settling on some intrinsically useful and portable commodity, and I cannot easily square that genealogy with so strong a conventionalism here. The 2019 redefinition of the kilogram is, of course, exactly the case Aristotle describes: the standard was altered at will, by agreement, and nothing about the metal in the vault changed. Does his framework have any way of saying whether such an alteration is a change of measure or a change of what is measured?

5. Finally, the two remarks about stability and about the future. Money is a "guarantee of exchange in the future," and it is "liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities ... but it tends to be comparatively constant." To my ear this is the earliest statement I have met of the idea that a standard need not be invariant, only more stable than the things measured against it — a drifting reference rather than a fixed one. Does Aristotle appear to notice that he is describing drift, and does he think the drift can be corrected, for instance by the price-fixing he recommends in the same breath? Relatedly, what work is the house-and-bedstead arithmetic in (iii) doing? He concludes that even before money existed the rate was stated as "five beds for a house," which seems to make coin a mere recording device for a barter ratio that was already there; yet the calculation only runs because both sides are expressed in minae. Which is it — does the mina do explanatory work, or does it merely transcribe need?
