Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the donor-conception records regime, and I still cannot settle it in my own head. I would like you to act as an expert in both the metaphysics of personal identity and the ethics of assisted reproduction, think carefully through the distinctions involved before you write anything, and tell me what this body of practice actually presupposes.

Here is the practice, as concretely as I can state it. In the United Kingdom, gamete donors registered from April 2005 onward are not anonymous: their offspring may apply for identifying information on turning eighteen, and the first cohort conceived under that rule came of age in 2023. The Australian state of Victoria went further in 2016, extending the entitlement retrospectively to every donor-conceived person regardless of when they were conceived, without the donor's consent, overriding assurances of anonymity given decades earlier. In countries with no such law, sperm and egg banks nonetheless sell "identity-release" donation as a premium product, and market donors by traits — height, education, test scores, resemblance to the recipient's partner. Separately, private registries match donor-conceived people who share a donor, and these "donor siblings" frequently form lasting relationships with one another and sometimes with the donor. All of this sits alongside clinic counselling norms that insist genes do not make a parent, that families are made by care, and that recipients should tell their children early precisely so that the donor is never mistaken for a father.

My friend's claim was that the first two items are Kripke's argument for the necessity of origin — that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg — smuggled into family law and converted into an entitlement. My reply was that this is a category error: a modal thesis about which gamete pair a person actually came from says nothing at all about whether knowing that pair matters to who one is. Her answer was that if it is not essentialism, the asymmetry is inexplicable — why is the entitlement always traced to the gamete providers, never to the woman who gestated the pregnancy, never to the people who named and raised the child? Neither of us budged.

Please work through the following, in this order, at roughly these lengths. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, and do not quote anyone; paraphrase any view you attribute.

1. **Bottom line** (about 80 words): your verdict on whether the regime is committed to origin essentialism, and who won the argument.
2. **First moves** (about 150 words): the candidate theories of identity and of origins you considered — including at least one you set aside — and why you kept or discarded each.
3. **What the regime strictly presupposes** (about 400 words): the minimal commitments the law, the registries and the trait-marketing cannot be stripped of without the practice losing its point.
4. **What it merely fits with** (about 250 words): rival accounts — medical history, prohibition of deception, narrative completeness, recognition by others, simple curiosity — that would equally license release of records, and how one could tell them apart from the practice itself.
5. **Two claims that look like one** (about 250 words): separate the necessity of a particular origin from the claim that origins explain or constitute a person, and separate gametic from genetic from gestational origin. Say which of these the practice actually invokes at each point.
6. **The asymmetry and the inconsistency** (about 250 words): whether the gamete-centred entitlement is coherent alongside counselling that denies genetic parenthood, or whether the two parts of the regime presuppose contradictory things.
7. **If the presupposed view were replaced** (about 250 words): what would have to change in law, clinic practice and registry design if the entitlement were rebuilt on a non-essentialist foundation — and what would become unintelligible.

Where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, say so plainly rather than forcing a single theory onto it.
