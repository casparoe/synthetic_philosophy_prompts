My reading group has been stuck on Meditation II for something like six weeks now and it's split into two camps that have completely stopped hearing each other, so I want to try to get them to write down what they actually share. I've taken to calling camp A the proprietors: they hold that what the cogito delivers is an owner of the thinking — a single subject that's the same thing across the doubting and the asserting — and that it delivers this without leaning on any general premise, because the certainty comes from performing the act rather than from reasoning to a conclusion. Camp B I've taken to calling the weather reporters: they hold that all you're entitled to is that thinking is going on, right now, and that the honest way to say it is impersonally, the way you'd report that it's raining; on their view the first-person pronoun is either a leftover from subject-predicate Latin or a smuggled step from a general premise to the effect that whatever thinks exists, and either way you get no subject and definately no subject that lasts. One of our weather reporters has done years of long retreats and insists there are stretches where she'd sincerely assent to there's-thinking and dissent from I-am-thinking, which she offers as data rather than doctrine, and the proprietors keep saying that's a report about the felt sense of ownership, not about whether there's an owner. Nobody is converting anybody.

So: please draft the joint statement both camps could put their names to. Start with a numbered section of agreed claims — I'd like at least eight — covering what the cogito does and doesn't establish, what the doubt from Meditation I leaves standing, what a first-person present-tense self-ascription is supposed to be doing, and where Descartes has to get his persisting self from if not from here (the continuous-creation business later in Meditation III comes up in our group a lot). Hard constraint: a claim doesn't count as agreed if both sides can only sign it by reading it two different ways. If you find yourself tempted to include one of those, put it in a separate flagged list and say exactly where the ambiguity sits. Then a disagreements table with these columns: the disputed claim in the proprietors' own preferred wording; the same claim in the weather reporters' own preferred wording; whether each side could live with the other's wording and why or why not; whether the row is a genuine crux or merely verbal; and for every genuine crux, one concrete case or argument both sides agree actually bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance.

Feel free to use the material we've been throwing at each other: thought-insertion reports, where someone is certain a thought is occurring but denies that it is theirs; people who insist they have no inner monologue at all, and what the cogito is supposed to come out as for them; the difference between being wrong about which thought you're having and being wrong that you're having one; whether the "I am, I exist" formulation in Meditation II — which is framed as coming out necessarily true each time it's asserted or conceived in the mind — even presents itself as an inference, and if it doesn't, whether the proprietors have already won that much; and whether either camp needs a diachronic premise to get anything interesting out of the whole move. I want at least five rows, and at least one crux where the two predictions would genuinely come out differently — if both sides predict the same thing it isn't a crux, it's a difference in taste, and it belongs in the verbal pile. Finish with a short blunt section saying which disagreements are verbal and why; if you think one camp's central claim is empty rather than false, say that in the document's own terms instead of hedging around it. Two or three pages or so, and no bibliography, no citations, no list of philosophers to go read — just the statement.
