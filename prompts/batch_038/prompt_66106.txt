I convene a fourteen-person graduate reading group in moral psychology — roughly half philosophy doctoral students, half psychology students who work on judgment and decision-making — and we meet for seventy-five minutes a week in a windowless seminar room that has two whiteboards and no projector. This term's block is on the relation between moral evaluation and the ascription of mental states, and everyone in the room has already read the founding paper on the side-effect effect: they know the two chairman vignettes cold, they know that something like four out of five people call the environment-harming chairman's side effect intentional while fewer than a quarter say the same of the environment-helping chairman, and they know the two stock families of reply. On the first, the asymmetry is an affective bias — moral outrage contaminating what ought to be a purely descriptive judgment about the agent's state of mind. On the second, the deep-self alternative, what really drives intentionality ascriptions is whether the side effect expresses the agent's own deeper values, and in the original pair of vignettes that factor is perfectly confounded with the valence of the outcome, because the chairman's professed indifference is callous when the upshot is harm and merely unmoved when the upshot is benefit. So the standard classroom move — distribute the two vignettes, take a show of hands, let the asymmetry materialize — is dead for this group. They would know what I was doing before I finished reading aloud.

Design an in-class activity that instead turns the room into a subject pool for a design capable of pulling those two explanations apart. My own instinct, which you are free to reject or replace, is a crossed design: the moral valence of the side effect crossed with whether the side effect expresses the agent's stated deeper commitments — a harm case whose chairman is distressed that the program will damage something he genuinely cares about, a help case whose chairman is a longtime partisan of the cause being helped, alongside the two familiar indifference versions. If the affect account is right, the harm cells should stay high whatever the chairman says he values; if the deep-self account is right, the valence asymmetry should shrink or flip once expression and valence come apart. What I need from you is the whole apparatus: new scenario content, since the profits-and-environment setting is compromised in this room and needs replacing with something that carries the same logical structure but different clothes; a counterbalancing and randomization scheme for fourteen people, bearing in mind that everyone will eventually hear all four conditions and can therefore contaminate one another, so tell me explicitly how you are sequencing exposure and whether you are running the group as a whole or in isolated clusters; a written prediction round before any data is collected, in which each participant stakes a specific number on what the group will say in each cell, scored by some simple points scheme that costs me nothing but gives them a reason to be invested in being right; a procedure for recording and tallying responses on the spot with nothing but whiteboards and paper slips; and a debrief that walks from the tallies to the two explanations and then on to the harder question of what would count as evidence for either.

Then give me the engineering notes without softening them. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — my guess is the prediction round, small-sample noise making the four cells look like a random scatter, and at least one participant answering strategically rather than naturally — and what should I do about each? How do I keep the conditions from bleeding into one another? And which features of the actual research program can a seventy-five-minute session with fourteen informed people simply not reproduce, so that I know what I have to supply in words instead of pretending the activity delivered it? There is one question I want the debrief to force rather than dodge: whether the numbers this room generates are evidence about the folk concept of intentional action at all, or only evidence about how theory-laden respondents behave once they suspect what is being tested. Build that into the structure if you can, rather than raising it only in discussion. Finally, please commit to your choices. I do not want a menu of alternative designs or a survey of interpretations with the decision left to me: pick the scenario content, pick the counterbalancing, state a definite prediction about which cell pattern we will most likely see and argue for it, and say what evidence would make you revise that prediction. And skip the reading list — I have the literature on my side of the table.
