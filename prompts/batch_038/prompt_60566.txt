I'm a doctoral student in a joint history-and-philosophy-of-science programme, and I've been asked to prepare a seminar handout on the methodology of scientific research programmes for a group who have already read Lakatos on progress and degeneration. The convenor has explicitly banned the casebook examples — no phlogiston, no Ptolemy or Copernicus, no Newton-versus-Einstein, no Freud, no Marx — on the grounds that the students recite the verdicts without ever applying the criteria. So what I want from you is a designed case, not an exposition: a fictional but entirely plausible empirical discipline, together with a documented timeline, engineered so that Lakatos's progress criteria deliver verdicts that cannot simply be read off the labels in the story. The point of the exercise is to force the students to do the novelty bookkeeping themselves, and in particular to make them notice that "a novel fact" may be ambiguous between two senses — a fact that was not yet known to anyone when the theory was published, and a fact that played no part in the construction of the theory. I'm fairly sure the literature has names for these two senses (something like temporal novelty and use-novelty, with Zahar arguing early on that the second is the one that matters), but please don't take my word for it; where the two come apart in your case, say so explicitly and tell me which sense Lakatos's own criterion of empirical progress actually requires, if his formulation settles the question at all.

Here are the specifications. Invent one research programme with a hard core you state in a single sentence, and four successive theories, T1 through T4, spanning roughly thirty-five years, each modifying the protective belt while the core stays untouched. For each shift, give me a compact timeline entry recording the year the theory was proposed, the year the relevant datum was first documented, the year it was generally accepted as corroborating, and what was known to whom at each point — including at least one instance where a researcher had access to unpublished measurements that never entered the public record. The crucial constraint, and the reason I'm asking rather than writing it myself: exactly one of the three shifts must be judged empirically progressive under the temporal reading and degenerating under the use reading, or the reverse, and you must build that shift so the divergence is innocent rather than scandalous — for instance, the theorist fits to analogue measurements from a laboratory system while publishing a prediction about the target system before anyone has measured it there. The other two shifts must be unambiguous, one progressive and one degenerating, so that the students have a control. Every protective-belt modification must be independently motivated and non-ad-hoc in the ordinary sense — no auxiliary hypothesis invented purely to block a counterexample, no parameter quietly tuned after the fact, no suppressed data, no fraud. The degeneracy verdict in the case where you deliver it has to come entirely out of the novelty bookkeeping and not out of any ad hocery, because that is precisely the distinction the handout is meant to sharpen. As a seed for the discipline, though you may replace it: the mid-century study of why steep gravel river-beds develop a surface layer coarser than their substrate, and the competing accounts of how much sediment such a bed can transport. Keep the whole scenario — discipline, four theories, timeline — under about seven hundred words, and write it so it can be handed out cold, without your commentary embedded in it.

After the scenario, and clearly separated from it, do the checking work. First, apply Lakatos's criteria to each of the three shifts in turn: state whether the new theory carries excess empirical content over its predecessor, whether any of that excess is corroborated, and therefore whether the shift is theoretically progressive, empirically progressive, or degenerating — and repeat the verdict for each shift under both readings of novelty, so that the divergence is visible on the page. Second, exploit the timeline you built: give the assessment twice, once as a snapshot taken eight years after T2 and once as a retrospective assessment twenty-five years later, and tell me whether the verdict changes and why; if it does, that is a feature I want the students to see rather than a flaw to be hidden. Third, identify the single feature of the pivotal case that generates the divergence between the two readings of novelty, state it in one sentence, and then say what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed or varied — if, say, the unpublished analogue measurements had never existed, or had been published first. Fourth, give the strongest reply available to a defender of the reading on which the pivotal shift comes out badly, and say whether that reply rescues the criterion or merely restates it. Finally, tell me honestly whether your case is one a real discipline could plausibly produce or whether you had to bend the setting to satisfy my constraints, since I'd rather know before a student spots it. No bibliography and no reading list, please — I have the references, and I don't want you guessing at page numbers.
