so, i've read one too many posts making the standard move on sunk costs — you know the one, the money's already gone so it's not in the decision, only the forward-looking payoffs are, and anyone who keeps going is just displaying a bias the psych lit has named and shamed. i don't want to re-litigate that with the usual escape hatches either (reputation, commitment devices, "well actually your preferences change endogenously"). i want a real argument for the other side, and i think the cleanest way in is the Ship of Theseus.

Take the full version: a ship gets re-planked one timber at a time over decades, every removed plank gets stored in a shed, and at the end somebody reassembles all the original timbers in the same arrangement in a drydock. Now there are two candidate ships and the literature spends all its time on which one is *identical* to the original. I want you to argue about which one is *worth anything*, and to use that to make a case that a certain kind of sunk-cost-respecting attitude isn't a bias at all.

Here's the seed I want you to build on and make rigorous, push back on if you think it needs it: the two ships are molecule-for-molecule and arrangement-for-arrangement the same at the end. Anything a purely forward-looking agent can see about them — what they'll float like, what they'll fetch at auction as hulls — is the same too. And yet it seems plainly not arbitrary to rank the one that got there by continuous sailing above the one that was reassembled out of a shed, and to rank it above *because of* how it got there. If that's right, then "the value of a thing is a function of its current state plus your future decisions about it" is false — a thing's value can depend on the whole causal-historical path, and the path is made out of costs that were, at the time, sunk. The forward-looking orthodoxy smuggles in a claim about what value supervenes on, and the ship case is a counterexample to it.

What I want from you:

- Lay the argument out premise by premise, so that someone who thinks i'm wrong can point at the exact premise they'd reject rather than just shaking their head.
- Make it a *normative* claim, not a therapeutic one. I'm not asking you to defend "humans are wired this way so be gentle with yourself." I'm asking you to argue that in cases with this structure you genuinely ought to let the past spend bear on the decision.
- Deal with the two objections I'd raise immediately, and deal with them properly, not with a wave: (a) even granting that the continuous ship is the more valuable one, there's nothing you can do about it now — the planks are already gone, so at the moment of decision the extra value isn't purchasable and therefore can't be a reason; (b) you haven't found a rational requirement, you've just described an aesthetic preference and given it a fancy metaphysical haircut.
- Tell me where the disagreement actually bites. Give me a decision — some real, non-ship thing, a project or a program or a relationship or an institution — where the forward-looking view and your view come apart in what they recommend. If you can't produce one, say so, because that's honestly the main thing i'd want to know.

Argue from reasoning only. No citations, no references, no invented studies — i don't want a bibliography and i don't want you guessing at one. 700–1000 words. Tone can be informal but the dialectic should be tight, and if you think my seed premise is false, the most useful thing you can do is say exactly why and then argue the strongest version of it anyway.
