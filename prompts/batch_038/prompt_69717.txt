I spent thirty-eight years as an analytical chemist and have come to philosophy late, so please tell me plainly wherever my reasoning is naive. Over the past year I have been persuaded by what appears to be the emerging consensus on farmed aquatic animals: that fish and decapod crustaceans are not merely nociceptive automata; that opioid modulation, conditioned avoidance, and motivational trade-offs jointly make an unpleasant conscious state the best explanation of their behavior; and that the skeptic's demand for a mammalian neural architecture is special pleading, since the same functional role could be realized convergently. On that view, live chilling, asphyxiation on ice, and boiling alive cause genuine suffering rather than the appearance of it.

What would count against this? I want a map of the view's exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which findings would be decisive, and roughly what would have to be observed to produce them? Which objections would a committed defender concede as telling against them, and which would they dismiss as question-begging? Does the position predict anything its skeptical rival does not, and are any of the defeaters already partly in the record?

Two worries of my own deserve an answer. First, that the view has been insulated by treating any aversive behavior as confirming evidence and any absence of it as uninformative, so that nothing counts against it. Second, that it slides from an empirical claim about consciousness into a precautionary principle about how to act under uncertainty — a slide that may be defensible, but which cannot by itself support the particular conclusions drawn from it. Tell me whether either is real. Finally, say whether the evidence base is concentrated on a handful of laboratory species while the animals farmed in the greatest numbers are the least studied, and whether that gap should change my confidence. Please answer in American English.
