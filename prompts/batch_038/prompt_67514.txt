I am preparing written comments on a colleague's draft chapter about religious arguments in political debate, and the chapter's central device is a comparison I would like audited on its own terms rather than through the doctrine it decorates.

My colleague holds a broadly Rawlsian line: citizens may bring comprehensive religious convictions into political argument provided that, at some point, reasons are offered that others could reasonably be expected to accept. He pairs this with a Habermasian division of arenas — the informal public sphere, where religious contributions are welcome as they stand, and the formal settings of legislatures and courts, where they must be rendered in generally accessible terms. To explain why this demand is not a demand that believers abandon their faith, he says that public debate is like a multilingual assembly: a religious argument is a contribution delivered in a language only its speaker understands. It is not out of order, but it becomes usable by the assembly only once translated into the common tongue, and until then the assembly may fairly set it aside. From this picture he draws two conclusions. First, nothing of substance is lost in translation, because only the vehicle changes while the meaning is carried across intact; asking for public reasons is therefore not asking for a different argument. Second, the burden is not one-sided, since the requirement falls on everyone equally and those already fluent in the common tongue simply have nothing to translate.

Critics in the chapter answer with a rival image: the translation requirement is like obliging recent immigrants to plead their case in a language they have had no opportunity to learn, which turns a procedural formality into a standing disability.

Please audit both comparisons. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the part of the source language, the target language, the meaning carried across, the translator, the assembly, and the deadline by which translation is due — and then locate where the mappings fail, distinguishing the failures that bear on my colleague's two conclusions from the merely decorative ones. I would like attention to whether the presumption that meaning survives the transfer holds when the force of a reason for the believer lies in its being a command rather than a claim about shared values: consider an argument against physician-assisted dying resting on life not being ours to dispose of, and ask whether rendering it as an appeal to bodily integrity or to the social meaning of medicine preserves its implications or quietly licenses exceptions the original forbids. Ask, too, whether the common tongue is in fact common or is itself one contested register among others, and whether the assembly picture presupposes that translation is always possible, that the speaker must perform it, and that the audience can certify when it has been done well. Consider finally whether the metaphor fares differently in the informal public sphere than in a courtroom.

Conclude by saying which of the two images carries more weight, whether either supports the conclusions drawn from it, and whether some third comparison — testimony, perhaps, or the offering of a witness rather than of a translation — would do the work more honestly.
