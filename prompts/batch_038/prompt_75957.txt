> Anatta, usually translated as no-self, is one of the three marks of existence alongside impermanence (anicca) and suffering (dukkha). The claim is that what we call a person is not one thing that persists but a bundle of five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness — which are causally linked and constantly changing. The standard illustration is the chariot, from the Milindapanha, where the monk Nagasena walks the Indo-Greek king Menander through the parts of a chariot and gets him to admit that "chariot" is just a name we apply to the assembled parts, and that there is no chariot over and above them. Same with persons: "Nagasena" is a designation for a process, not a thing.
>
> It matters that this isn't only a metaphysical curiosity. The Buddha refused to answer when asked directly whether a self exists, which shows that anatta is really an eliminativism: persons don't exist, only aggregates do, and the belief that they do is the root of clinging and therefore of suffering. Getting rid of the belief is the whole point of the exercise.
>
> Parfit arrives at the same place by a very different route. In *Reasons and Persons* he defends reductionism: personal identity over time just consists in ordinary physical and psychological facts, and there is no "further fact" such as a Cartesian ego. What matters is Relation R, psychological connectedness and continuity with the right kind of cause, and his famous conclusion is that identity is not what matters. The thought experiments do the work. In the teletransporter case the original is destroyed and two Replicas are produced, which shows that identity is indeterminate; in the division case, both brain hemispheres are transplanted and both survivors are R-related to me, so psychological continuity can't be identity — because continuity isn't transitive, and identity has to be.
>
> Hume got there first, and the same way. In the *Treatise* he argues that when he looks inside he only ever finds particular perceptions and never a self, so the mind is a bundle; he then uses cases like fission to show that our identity ascriptions are fictions the imagination smooths over.
>
> Both views face the same objection and neither has a clean answer. If there is no continuing subject, nothing can bear responsibility: the person who committed the act isn't the person who gets punished, and the being who is reborn isn't the being who acted. Parfit and the Buddhist tradition both simply concede this — Parfit thinks morality has to be rebuilt on what matters rather than on identity, and Buddhism drops karma along with the self. So, yes: same objection, same concession, and both end up with a deflationary ethics where survival and responsibility are matters of degree.
