I was recently appointed to my city's planning commission — a seven-member volunteer board that votes on zoning, variances, and a handful of land-use contracts — and, having no philosophy training at all, I did what I usually do before taking on an unfamiliar system: I read the documentation. That sent me into public reason liberalism, and I now find that my own verdicts on concrete cases do not behave like a single rule. I would like to know whether they add up to one, and if not, which of them I should discard.

Here are my verdicts, mostly formed before I had any vocabulary for them.

1. A commissioner votes against a variance that would put an adult bookstore beside a middle school. She gives traffic and minors' exposure as reasons, and then adds that the business is sinful and that the neighborhood's moral character is a legitimate object of public concern. I think she did something wrong in voicing that last part, even though her other reasons were sufficient and even though I agree with her vote.

2. The same commissioner casts the same vote, mentions only traffic, and tells me afterwards that the traffic argument was a cover and that her real reason was religious. I think this is worse than case 1, which startles me: the public record here contains only reasons I called acceptable.

3. A Quaker commissioner votes the same way and says plainly that his conscience is bound by scripture, offering nothing else. I think he has done nothing wrong — if anything, something I admire. I cannot reconcile this with case 1, where stating a religious reason alongside adequate public ones seemed to be the offense.

4. A church circulates a leaflet against a ballot measure on medical aid in dying, arguing entirely from religious premises. Fine. A legislator votes against the implementing statute and gives only the same religious premises from the floor. Not fine. My rule seems to track the institution rather than the content, and I cannot say why.

5. Our council opens each meeting with a prayer given by rotating local clergy. Fine. The city seal carries a motto invoking God. Not fine, and I feel this more strongly than about the prayer, which anyone can decline to attend. Neither is coercive in any obvious sense.

6. A commissioner has honestly looked for a secular case against a policy, cannot find one he believes, and would rather vote his conscience than abstain or manufacture a reason he does not hold. I think voting is the right call. But if that is right, I do not see what my requirement was ever demanding of him.

7. A commissioner supports a policy because he holds a sectarian naturalism about the direction of history — premises no more available to my devout neighbor than scripture is to me. Not fine. A commissioner supports the same policy because he thinks it maximizes aggregate welfare. Fine. I cannot articulate what makes the arithmetic public and the philosophy of history non-public.

8. A religious objection to a municipal surveillance contract, once translated into security and budget language, becomes weaker and vaguer, and the commission decides worse for having heard the translated version only. Here I think the translation requirement actively degraded the outcome.

My tentative principle is: you may be moved by whatever you like, but you owe your fellow citizens a justification they could assess without adopting your metaphysics. I suspect it is too weak — anything satisfies it with a fluent enough gloss — and that it smuggles the whole question into the word assess.

So: is there a principle, stated as precisely as you can, that delivers all or most of these verdicts? Give it as explicit clauses and say which case motivates each clause. If none fits, identify my outlier verdicts and tell me what it costs to give them up rather than to complicate the rule. Then tell me what the surviving principle says about two cases I have not settled: a commissioner who supports a lending restriction because his tradition forbids interest, arguing the point in terms of predatory lending; and a judge who writes a concurrence invoking natural law alongside constitutional text.

Two further questions I care about as an engineer. First, is the requirement about the reasons offered or the reasons actually held? Only the agent can tell those apart, so if it is about the latter I do not see how it could function as a rule anyone applies to anyone else. Second, if it turns out to be a constraint on phrasing rather than on justification — a style check, not a correctness check — is that a defect in the view or the whole of it?

Finally, locate me. I have come across Rawls's proviso, Audi's requirement of a secular rationale, Habermas's proposal that translation be demanded institutionally rather than across the board, and the objection that all of this imposes an asymmetric burden on religious citizens. Say which of these my verdicts already commit me to, which they rule out, and whether any of them handles cases 2 and 7 better than my own formulation does.
