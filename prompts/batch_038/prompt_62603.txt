I cover brains and courtrooms for a magazine — mostly the steady drip of "his lawyer says the implant made him do it" pieces — and I fell down the Frankfurt-case rabbit hole last month trying to work out what I actually think about free will. I've ended up with a pile of gut verdicts on the cases and on variants I made up myself, and I can't tell whether there's a principle underneath them or whether I'm just being inconsistent in a mildly interesting way. I'm on deadline and I don't want a reading list or a tour of who said what — I want the principle, stated properly, and I want to be told which of my verdicts have to go.

Here's what I've got. Numbering them so you can talk back to me.

1. The original. Black wants Jones to take the money, will step in if Jones shows any sign of not doing it, never has to. Jones takes it on his own, and could not have done otherwise. Blameworthy, full stop. I'm solid on this one.

2. Same, except Jones is told afterwards that Black was sitting there the whole time ready to move. Still blameworthy, and to exactly the same degree as in 1. Finding out doesn't retroactively adjust anything.

3. Same, except Jones knows from the outset that Black is there. Takes the money anyway. Still blameworthy — and if anything a shade more so, because now there's some defiance in it.

4. The version where Black is reading something in Jones first: a flush, a neural marker, whatever, a couple of seconds upstream of the choice, so the outcome is effectively settled before Jones "decides". Here my nerve goes. Responsibility feels thinner. Not gone, but if Black is already reading the first movement of the act, what's left for Jones to own?

5. The buffered one — the fix designed to get around the objection to 4. Device installed at birth, fires at t if Jones hasn't already made the decision under his own steam, and Jones does make it under his own steam, undetermined, a beat before t. Everyone I've read says this is the cleanest case of the lot, the one where Jones is most obviously the source of the act. I find it worse than 4. Something about a machine having sat in his skull for forty years waiting its turn makes the decision feel borrowed. I know that isn't an argument.

6. Same as 1, but Black wants the money taken for the cash and Jones takes it out of pure spite. Same act, different reason. Responsible, but responsible for something other than the thing Black was steering him towards, and the fact that the two line up is luck.

7. The one where the device is dodgy and would have failed, so Jones genuinely could have done otherwise. He's responsible here — but I don't feel any more responsible-for-it than in 1, which rather spoils the point of the case.

8. Two versions of 1 differing only in what's doing the watching: a black box on a table, versus Black's devoted assistant who would have stepped in and is gutted never to have had to. With the person, I feel Jones gets a sliver of credit for the fact she never had to move. With the box, nothing at all. I'm aware this one is probably embarrassing.

9. The neuroscientist actually does intervene and causes the choice. Not responsible. So the entire difference between 9 and 1 is that in 9 something happened and in 1 nothing did, and "nothing did" is doing a hell of a lot of work in my ledger.

10. Attached to 9, though it pulls the other way: I can't shake the feeling that Black's presence isn't nothing. He's the reason there was no other door out of the room. "He never touched him" and "he was never there" sound like two ways of describing the same situation to me, but they plainly aren't, and my gut knows the difference.

So: is there a principle — or a small handful of them — that delivers these, or as many as it honestly can? I'd like it written out precisely, clause by clause, with each clause tagged to the verdict that motivated it, so I can see what work each bit is doing and whether I'd still sign up to it once it's naked like that.

Then tell me where it lands on the pairs I can't reconcile: 4 against 5, and 1 against 7. Which am I actually tracking — sourcehood, robustness of the alternatives, or some third thing I've been calling both? And 8 and 10 pull in opposite directions in a way I can't articulate: 8 looks like pure anthropomorphism on my part, and 10 looks like the same anthropomorphism refusing to go away when the case is stated properly.

Which verdicts does your principle have to overturn? If you overturn one, tell me what it costs to keep it instead, because I'd sooner have a baroque principle that rescues 8 and 10 than a tidy one that bins them — but say so plainly if that's not the real trade.

And predict three cases I haven't listed. Specifically: one where the marker Black reads is caused downstream by Jones's own deliberation rather than sitting upstream of it; one where Jones finds out afterwards and is relieved; and one where the device would have made Jones do something considerably worse than what he actually did.

If the honest answer is that 5 is just squeamishness about hardware and 8 is just my tendency to give people credit for the weather, say that — but then show me what the principle that explains them as squeamishness and sentiment looks like, because my suspicion is that it's the same principle that can't make sense of 10.
