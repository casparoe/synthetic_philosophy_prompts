I am convening a small workshop at the intersection of decision theory and the philosophy of economics, and two of the participants — one a committed partisan of the Nash bargaining solution, the other an equally committed partisan of the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution — have at last agreed to an adversarial collaboration rather than another exchange of exasperated seminar remarks. My task is to hand them a document both can sign. I should like you to draft it.

The shared setting, which both accept and which you may assume throughout: two-person bargaining problems (S, d), with S a compact convex set of utility pairs, d the disagreement point, and utilities cardinal but unique only up to a positive affine transformation of each player's scale. Both prescriptions are Pareto optimal, symmetric, and invariant to positive affine transformations; they part company over the fourth axiom. The Nash solution is characterized by those three together with independence of irrelevant alternatives, and equivalently maximizes the product of gains over d. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution replaces independence of irrelevant alternatives with a monotonicity requirement, and selects the maximal point of the intersection of S with the ray from d through the ideal point, that is, through the pair consisting of each player's maximal feasible utility. Both participants know this material cold; do not spend words re-deriving it.

The disagreement I want them to confront jointly is best fixed by a case, which you should treat as stipulated. Let S be the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), and (0,1), and let d = (0,0). Let S′ be the convex hull of (0,0), (1,0), (0, 1/5), and (1/2, 1/2) — S with its upper-left corner cut away. Then S′ is contained in S; the point (1/2, 1/2) is feasible and Pareto optimal in both problems; and neither prescription selects any of the alternatives that were removed. Nevertheless the Nash selection is (1/2, 1/2) in both problems, while the Kalai–Smorodinsky selection is (1/2, 1/2) in the first and (2/3, 1/3) in the second, because the second player's ideal payoff falls from 1 to 1/2. (Should either party object that S′ is not comprehensive, substitute its comprehensive hull; neither selection changes.) I have checked the arithmetic and so have they; the dispute concerns only what it shows.

Please produce the joint statement in four parts.

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These must be non-vacuous — nothing of the order of "bargaining involves two parties." Include at minimum: that independence of irrelevant alternatives and the monotonicity requirement are incompatible, so no selection rule enjoys all five properties; where the two prescriptions coincide, symmetric problems being the obvious case but not the only one; that their dynamic counterparts run in parallel, the concession process in which each party holds out until the parties' willingness to risk breakdown is equalized terminating at the Nash point, while a process in which both make equal proportional concessions from their ideal claims terminating at the Kalai–Smorodinsky point; and that the non-cooperative results — a demand game with vanishing breakdown risk, and an alternating-offers protocol in the limit of perfect patience, both converging on the Nash point — are theorems they agree on while disagreeing entirely about what the theorems establish normatively.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should give the disagreement, a formulation the Nashian would sign, a formulation the Kalai–Smorodinsky partisan would sign, and a verdict on whether it is substantive or merely about words. Candidates I expect, though you should add or discard rows as you judge: whether the ideal point is a genuine normative reference or an artifact of how the choice set happens to be described; whether monotonicity articulates a fairness intuition or begs the question by treating unattainable aspirations as claims; whether a non-cooperative implementation vindicates axioms or merely describes a process; whether the case above shows the Kalai–Smorodinsky prescription to be hostage to the destruction or addition of options nobody would choose, or shows instead that the Nash prescription is blind to facts that fairness plainly tracks; and whether equalizing proportional gains above the disagreement point already smuggles in an interpersonal calibration that affine invariance was meant to preclude.

3. Cruxes. For each, state the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party; the argument, thought experiment, or elicitation both would accept as bearing on it; each side's prediction recorded in advance; and what each would concede if the prediction failed. Include a crux on the canonical description of the feasible set — whether fixing a protocol for what counts as an alternative dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it; a crux on three or more bargainers, where the natural extension of the proportional-concession idea is contested and rival rules compete to inherit the two-person axioms; and a crux on whether any of this is settled by how people actually divide a surplus when instructed to be fair.

4. A closing paragraph stating, for each party, what it would take to abandon its favored axiom, and whether anything exists that both agree would be decisive. If you conclude — as I suspect — that a portion of this dispute is verbal, say precisely which portion and why, and characterize what remains once it is stripped away.

Two constraints. The document must be one both could put their names to: no strawmen, no point-scoring inside the agreed section, and no formulation of a disagreement that its own side would repudiate as a caricature. And please cite nothing — no papers, no attributions, no bibliography. I want the whole thing argued from the definitions and the case stipulated above, since that is the only common ground the two of them reliably share.
