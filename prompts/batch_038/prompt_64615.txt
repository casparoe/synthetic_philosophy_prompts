I am the head of policy at a mid-sized social platform headquartered in the United States. We have roughly forty million monthly users, about a fifth of them in the European Union. We are not large enough to attract the Union's regime for very large online platforms, but we fall squarely within the ordinary obligations of the Digital Services Act, including a notice-and-action mechanism and a written statement of reasons to any user whose content we restrict. Because we are American, our counsel also reads every moderation policy against First Amendment doctrine, and our published terms have for years described American free-speech norms as our baseline.

One category in particular arrives with two legal baselines that do not coincide and sometimes point in opposite directions: public speech attacking a racial, religious, ethnic, or national group. American doctrine protects advocacy of unlawful conduct unless it is directed at inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it. European human-rights law, and the Union instrument requiring member states to criminalise certain forms of public incitement to hatred, permit criminalisation without any such imminence requirement. The structured threshold tests developed in the international human-rights literature — weighing context, the speaker's position, intent, content and form, extent of dissemination, and likelihood of harm — reliably yield more removals than the American test, and I am not confident they yield a stable subset of it.

We currently review about fifty escalated cases in this category per week. Most carry a twenty-four-hour clock; those flagged as involving a credible threat of violence carry six. Two full-time reviewers, neither of them legally trained, make the first-pass call, and we have four hours per week of outside counsel time, which I would rather spend on ten cases a month than on fifty a week. Every outcome becomes a statement of reasons that the user, an internal complaints body, an out-of-court dispute settlement entity, and potentially a regulator will read. Over-removal generates appeals, press criticism, and — where the speaker is a dissident or a persecuted minority — real danger to that speaker. Under-removal generates fines and, in the worst case, violence.

What I need is a procedure, not a position paper. It should be grounded in whatever these two bodies of law and the human-rights literature between them actually establish that is usable at our scale, but written so that a reviewer who has never studied law can run it in ten minutes and produce the statement of reasons in three sentences an ordinary user will understand. Please give me:

1. The questions in the order they should be asked, with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each — and where the procedure stops with a "no removal" verdict rather than continuing.
2. The points at which the question is one of values rather than fact and must therefore be handed to a person, identified by name of role rather than left to the reviewer's judgment.
3. Which distinctions from the underlying doctrine must survive the simplification (imminence, intent, speaker status, reach, and whatever else you consider load-bearing) and which can be dropped, with reasons for each decision. I want to know what the procedure is no longer tracking once it is short enough to use.
4. The output format. Does the procedure return a single verdict, or a verdict plus jurisdiction? Our internal debate is unresolved: I have argued for one global standard set at the stricter of the two baselines, on the ground that running two is expensive and that we should not be in the business of deciding which users merit legal protection. One colleague replies that a globally stricter standard exports the most restrictive member state's law to users everywhere and contradicts our own published terms. Another says a US-calibrated global standard will get people killed where these European rules exist for a reason. Your procedure should either sit cleanly on top of any of these choices or tell me that the choice is forced — and note where a "no removal" outcome might be legally compelled rather than merely permitted, since some US jurisdictions may yet require us to carry content we would otherwise take down.
5. The case the procedure handles worst, and the direction in which it will err systematically. If it errs toward removal, tell me what that costs; if toward retention, tell me that instead.

Then run it on three cases.

Case one. An elected official in a member state posts, on a verified account with roughly six hundred thousand followers, a message about a group of recent arrivals that describes them as an infestation and says the country "will not survive" their presence. There is no call to action of any kind. In the three weeks since the post, two shelters housing members of that group have been firebombed in the same region. Our reviewers access the text only through machine translation.

Case two. A US-based account with about two hundred followers posts denial of a documented genocide together with praise for its perpetrators. The account's audience is overwhelmingly German; the user travels to Germany twice a year; and the post is visible to descendants of survivors living there, several of whom have reported it.

Case three. An account operated by members of a persecuted religious minority in a third country — neither the EU nor the US — posts about the majority community in the same dehumanizing register, calling them vermin and saying the country would be better without them. The minority has no state power, no armed capability, and no realistic prospect of acting on any of this. The account has forty thousand local followers and is currently the only channel through which evidence of atrocities committed against that minority reaches the outside world. My instinct is that this stays up, and I cannot see what in a threshold test built for European conditions makes it stay up other than the fact that they are the victims. If your procedure removes it, say so plainly and tell me which step did the work.

Finally, build in a step for the recurring problem where a term is a slur in the country the speaker lives in and a neutral demonym in the country they came from, and where the diaspora community and the home-country community disagree about which reading applies. Say who resolves that disagreement and on what evidence.

Please work from your own understanding of the doctrine and give me its content. I do not want a list of cases, instruments, or scholarship to go and read, and I do not want citations; I want the substance those sources support, expressed as something my reviewers can execute.
