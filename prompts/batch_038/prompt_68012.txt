I convene a final-year undergraduate module in the philosophy of medicine. Students arrive having already worked through the standard accounts of health and disease — Boorse's biostatistical theory, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, and the ability-based account on which health consists in having the ability to achieve one's vital goals — together with the naturalism/normativism dispute that frames them. They can recite all three definitions reliably, which is precisely what I do not want to reward. I need to contribute one compulsory essay question to the two-hour final examination paper, and I would like you to draft the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question must be built around the following invented case. Please reproduce it in the question stem, in polished prose rather than as a list, tightening the wording as you see fit but preserving every substantive stipulation, since the marking scheme turns on them:

1. A cheap, safe, orally administered synthetic compound — call it Compound S — has been taken voluntarily and daily by roughly four-fifths of the adult population of the country for two generations, and is now routine paediatric practice.
2. In users, mean systolic blood pressure sits about 12 mmHg lower than it did three generations ago, and lifetime incidence of major cardiovascular events is roughly halved. Users report no symptoms, show no lesions, and exhibit no measurable change in any other functional ability.
3. The fifth of the population who never take S are, by every clinical standard available three generations ago, entirely within normal limits: no lesion, no symptom, no mechanism that fails to work, no functional ability reduced relative to the pre-S population. They simply sit at the upper end of what used to be the normal range.
4. Relative to the present population, however, they are statistical outliers on the high side, and their excess cardiovascular risk is now the single largest preventable cause of death in the country.
5. The national health service will fund S as a treatment only for a state that qualifies as a disease. The regulator must therefore decide whether the non-users are diseased, and its decision will determine who receives subsidised access.
6. For the second part of the question: S has one side effect. In users it depresses one component of peripheral thermoregulation, so that at ambient temperatures below minus ten degrees Celsius they lose manual dexterity measurably faster than non-users do. In the climates users actually inhabit this effect is never felt: it produces no symptom, and it does not reduce any ability they ever exercise. Non-users lack the deficit. Because four-fifths of the population carries it, it is now statistically typical.

The question should have two parts, worth 15 and 10 marks respectively, for 25 marks total, with a 45-minute budget and an expected answer of about 900 words. Part (a) should ask whether the non-users are diseased, and should be framed so that a full answer must state a determinate verdict from each of the three accounts and then identify, for each account, at least one input the case forces it to fix that the account itself does not determine — for the biostatistical theory, how the reference class is drawn and at what time the relevant statistics are taken; for the harmful dysfunction analysis, whether an epidemiologically real excess risk with no failed mechanism can supply the dysfunction component; for the ability-based account, whose vital goals are at issue and in what environment they are assessed. Part (b) should introduce the side effect from stipulation 6 and ask what the accounts now say about the users, which account, if any, ends up classifying the majority of the population as diseased, and whether the contrast between the two parts shows the naturalism/normativism dispute to be substantive or merely verbal.

Please deliver the following three items.

First, the examination question exactly as it should appear on the paper, including the case, the two parts, the mark allocations, and the rubric.

Second, a model answer of 800 to 950 words, written as a strong candidate would write it under examination conditions — continuous prose that argues for a single thesis, not an outline or a set of annotated notes. It should not be a survey of the three accounts; it should use them to make an argument about the regulator's question.

Third, a marking scheme usable by a graduate teaching assistant marking a hundred scripts in a day. It should contain: five must-hit moves, each with the marks it carries, summing to 25; an explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a strong second-class answer, expressed in terms of specific moves rather than adjectives like "nuanced" or "critical"; four characteristic errors, each with a one-line note explaining why the error is an error and not merely something left out; and a rule for capping answers that define the three accounts correctly but apply them to the case only in passing.

Two further constraints. The paper is closed book, so neither the question nor the model answer may require or supply publication dates, page references, or a bibliography, and students should be told that naming a position and its author is sufficient. And please confine the facts to those stipulated above: do not import real epidemiological data, real guideline revisions, or invented studies, and do not have the model answer appeal to a view held by a named contemporary philosopher that goes beyond the accounts already described.
