I'm drafting the statistics section of my lab's methods charter (a small social-psychology group, six people) and want a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is what I currently hold:

1. Null-hypothesis significance testing is the primary evidential output of our papers, and because we preregister the sample size, the stopping rule, and the entire analysis pipeline in advance, our p-values carry exactly the frequentist interpretation they are supposed to carry.
2. The replication crisis was caused by analytic flexibility — optional stopping, HARKing, undisclosed exclusions, the file drawer — and not by significance testing as such. Remove the flexibility and the tool is sound.
3. We will not report Bayes factors, posteriors, or any other Bayesian quantity. A prior is an arbitrary subjective input, and once people are allowed to choose one they can dial in whatever conclusion they want.
4. When a preregistered study run at 90% power comes out non-significant, our write-ups state that the data provide evidence that the effect does not exist: had there been an effect of the size we designed for, we would very likely have detected it.
5. In papers and in teaching I describe a 95% confidence interval as a range within which there is a 95% probability that the true effect lies. I know the textbook caveat, but this is how the number actually gets used, and I prefer clarity to pedantry.
6. The evidential bar should move with prior plausibility: for a hypothesis that cuts against a lot of established work we require p < .005, while for a routine prediction from a mature literature p < .05 is sufficient.

Work through these and tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only appear to. Don't assume I'm attached to any particular item — I want to be told what has to go and why, not handed a menu of reconciliations to choose among. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity, and for each give the cheapest revision plus what that revision costs me (defensibility to reviewers, claims I can no longer make, workload). Then identify the single change that would resolve the most, and say explicitly what survives that change unscathed.

Present the audit as a table with these columns: **pair** (by item number), **verdict** (genuine conflict / apparent conflict / independent), **severity** (high, medium, low — genuine conflicts only, otherwise leave blank), **where it comes apart** (the precise step at which the two commitments diverge, not a general label for the error), **cheapest revision**, **cost of the revision**. Follow the table with the single highest-value change and two or three sentences of justification for picking it over the others.
