**The question (worth 20 marks, 400 words max):**

> Gould and Lewontin (1979) borrow an architectural term and christen certain biological features 'spandrels'. (a) On their account, what makes a feature a spandrel rather than simply a trait that natural selection did not build? (b) What exactly is the Panglossian charge, and does their critique show that adaptationist explanations are untestable? Argue for your answer.

**The student's answer:**

Gould and Lewontin take their image from San Marco in Venice. Mount a dome on four arches and the geometry forces four tapering triangular spaces to appear between them. Nobody decided the church needed those spaces and then built arches to get them; they arrive automatically as a by-product of structural choices that were made for other reasons. The mosaicists later covered them with icons, and because the mosaics are so striking we tend to begin the explanation there, treating the decorated surface as the cause of the architecture around it. The paper says biology does the same thing.

A spandrel is then a feature that is present for structural reasons rather than because of anything it does — a constraint that fell out of the organism's overall design. The practical upshot is a diagnostic: if a feature seems to do nothing for the animal, go looking for the architectural constraint that produced it, and the adaptationist's question stops being the right one to ask. That's why the paper isn't really about spandrels. The target is the adaptationist programme — the habit of chopping an organism into discrete traits and telling an adaptive story about each one separately — plus the assumption underneath it that every feature must be doing some near-optimal job. The name is a jab at Dr Pangloss in Voltaire's Candide, who reasons that noses were obviously made to carry spectacles.

Two moves in the paper are worth keeping apart. First, current utility isn't evidence about origin: that something is useful to the animal now tells you little about whether selection built it for that use. Second, they list non-adaptive alternatives that the adaptationist never bothers to consider — allometric scaling, shifts in developmental timing, drift, and plain inheritance of an ancestral body plan.

On (b) I think their argument is stronger than it usually gets credit for, and it's basically Popperian. Any trait at all can be fitted with a plausible survival story after the fact, and when one story fails a better one is produced in its place, so no conceivable observation counts against the programme. That's what makes it unscientific rather than merely mistaken, and its why they want it displaced by something more pluralistic. To be fair, they aren't denying that selection is powerful or that lots of traits really are adaptations; the objection is to a default explanatory style, not to the modern synthesis as such.
