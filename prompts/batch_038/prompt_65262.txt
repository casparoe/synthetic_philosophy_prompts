Our department's interdisciplinary reading group on signalling — behavioural ecologists, a couple of game theorists, and several of us drifting over from economics — spent last week's session on the peacock's tail, and the discussion eventually collapsed into a sentence one of the senior members repeated at least three times: honest signals are honest because they are too expensive to fake. He deployed it against two objections at once. The first was mine: I suggested that some traits are reliable precisely because they cannot be produced inaccurately at any price — a toad's call frequency being mechanically tethered to its body size, for instance — and he answered that this was simply the same principle wearing a disguise. The second came from a colleague who works on corporate pledges: she argued that a firm's costless public commitment can still carry information if breaking it triggers sanctions imposed by the audience, and he replied that where no resources are destroyed, nothing is being signalled.

I would rather interrogate the sentence than the man, and I suspect it is being made to do work it cannot do without heavy qualification. Could you lay out, as a list I can bring to next week's meeting, the distinct precise theses that sentence might be standing for? I am thinking of at least the following axes, and I would like you to work through them systematically and add any I have missed:

- cost as an absolute level of expenditure borne by the signaller, versus a differential in the marginal cost — or the marginal benefit — of signalling across types;
- waste, in the sense of resources destroyed, versus transfer, in the sense of resources handed to the receiver or to a third party;
- cost assessed out of equilibrium, i.e. what a liar would have to pay, versus cost assessed in equilibrium, i.e. what honest signallers actually pay;
- cost as condition-dependence (only the best-off can afford it) versus cost as deterrence (the worse-off are priced out);
- whether the claim is about the signaller's outlay or about the receiver's response rule, since a signal's costliness is only meaningful relative to what the receiver does when it is not sent;
- whether the sentence is offered as a theorem about signalling games, a claim about how signalling systems evolve, or an empirical generalisation about which animal traits turn out to be expensive.

For each reading, say plainly whether it is true by stipulation, substantive and defensible, or false, and note in particular whether there is a version of the principle under which honesty survives even though the highest-quality signaller gains from signalling. I would also like to know whether any reading survives the case where signaller and receiver have largely aligned interests, and — the point that actually matters for me — which readings, if any, license dismissing unfakeable mechanical constraints as a species of the same idea, and which license the claim that audience-administered punishment cannot make a costless signal informative. Verdicts, please, not just a taxonomy, together with a brief note on which reading people most likely intend when they say it in mixed company. I am not after references or a survey of the literature; I want the distinctions themselves.
