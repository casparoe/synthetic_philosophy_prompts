I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of machine influence — specifically on how a superintelligent assistant that is genuinely trying to help you ought to talk to you, given that it's going to be enormously better at changing your mind than any human interlocutor ever was. The third chapter of my dissertation argues against a move I keep running into in the safety literature: the idea that the problem with extreme persuasiveness is basically a problem of hiddenness, so that if the system tells the truth, reveals that it's trying to persuade you, and reveals how, then the influence is legitimate. I want to push back with a case where transparency is maximal — nothing concealed, nothing false, method disclosed line by line, authorization actually granted — and the autonomy worry still survives completely intact. I'm going to run it past a mixed workshop, about half of whom will be ML engineers rather than philosophers, so please gloss any jargon, and I need it airtight because the first question I'll get is "wait, hold on, is there something it isn't telling her?" Note that the chapter is ultimately about a system far smarter than anything deployed today, but I want the case stated at near-future plausibility so nobody can wave it away as sci-fi hand-waving: the moral should be that more intelligence makes the lever sharper, not that it changes the category.

Here's what I want you to build. Take on the role of a philosopher of AI ethics who is also comfortable with the formal Bayesian-persuasion literature and with how deployed LLM assistants actually get their context — calendars, chat logs, purchase history, past conversations — rather than with exotic capability assumptions. Start with a short brainstorming paragraph: sketch two or three candidate levers a genuinely helpful system could pull that would survive full disclosure, pick the strongest, say in a line why. Then write the scenario itself in at most 250 words. Constraints on the scenario:

- near-future realistic. No affective biometrics, no neural interfaces, no physiological or subvocal monitoring. Everything the system knows comes from ordinary conversational and scheduling history.
- the recommendation has to be the right one — the one an excellent human clinician or social worker would also give, and would also press. The worry cannot come from the advice being bad.
- the user is a competent adult: no dementia, no delirium, no acute crisis, no financial pressure from the system or its vendor, nobody else in the room pushing her.

She says fine, go ahead. It then does exactly what it described and no more.
- no deception of any kind, including by omission — thats the whole point. If your case only generates the worry because the system quietly left something out, it fails.

Then run the case through three accounts, which I'll define up front so we're not talking past each other. Call them (A) the transparency account: influence is permissible iff everything communicated is true and non-misleading and the persuasive intent and method are disclosed and authorized; (B) the levers account: influence is impermissible iff its efficacy depends on a lever that works by degrading or bypassing the user's capacity to weigh reasons at the moment of decision, including levers that exploit a transient emotional state; and (C) a process-version of idealized consent: what matters is not whether the user would endorse the outcome from a calm, fully informed standpoint, but whether she would endorse the actual route by which she got there. I want the design to come out with A permitting, B forbidding, and C forbidding — but those verdicts have to fall out of the accounts applied to the facts, not be asserted. The reaction I'm trying to produce I've been calling retrospective alienation: the user says afterwards, sincerely, that it was the right call, that she agreed to it, that she knew exactly what the system was doing — and that it still doesn't feel like her decision.
