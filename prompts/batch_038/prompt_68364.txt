I'm a second-year graduate student working between philosophy and early modern literature, and this term I've been reading Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals alongside a stack of contemporary nonfiction that turns out to do something structurally very similar. I've become convinced there is a recurring argumentative configuration here that I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether one exists. The configuration goes like this: an author wants to attack some practice of his own society — cruelty, say, or vanity, or the way the dead are handled — and to do it he puts forward a distant people who supposedly manage that very matter better, in an unforced and natural way. The portrait is what does the work, because the reader's own customs stop looking necessary once they are set beside an alternative presented as an actual, existing way of life. In Montaigne's case the alternative is the Tupinambá, whose warfare and funerary cannibalism he sets against the tortures, betrayals, and religious killings of sixteenth-century France, arriving in effect at the thought that each of us calls barbarous whatever he is not himself accustomed to. He reinforces the impression of an eyewitness report by mentioning that he conversed with some of these visitors at Rouen, and the later essay in which he turns to the Spanish conquest of the Americas works the same way.

What bothers me is not simply whether the portrait is accurate, though much of it is probably drawn from sailors' stories and from whatever the argument happens to need. What bothers me is that the idealization seems to be load-bearing in a way that cuts against the author's own stated aim. If the Tupinambá are admirable precisely because they are simple, uncorrupted, and free of artifice, then their virtue looks like a function of the author's need for a mirror rather than of anything they are or do, and no one among them could recognize himself in the result. And yet it feels equally wrong to file the whole thing under ethnocentrism, because the explicit purpose is to dethrone the author's own culture, and this is by the standards of its moment a remarkably sympathetic text about the Americas. So the move seems to go wrong in a direction that neither of the obvious labels reaches: anti-ethnocentric in intent, ethnocentric in mechanism. The same shape is everywhere now — the magazine essay reporting that some remote community has no word for depression, or grieves its dead for exactly the right number of days, or has quietly settled a question we keep failing to settle. The community's assigned role is to be a counterexample, and the counterexample has been drawn to fit.

My question, then: is there an established concept for this configuration, whether in philosophy, anthropology, rhetoric, or postcolonial theory? I'm aware that the phrase noble savage gets attached to Montaigne constantly, though it postdates him by most of a century and seems to name a stock character rather than the structure of an argument, and I'm aware that primitivism names a broad tradition of ideas rather than this particular self-undermining twist. I'd like to know what the nearest established concepts actually are, how the thing I've described differs from each of them, and which one — if any — genuinely covers it. If nothing does, I'd like you to coin a term, define it tightly, and tell me where its edges fall. Specifically: does the configuration require the flattering portrait to be false, or does a selective but broadly accurate one count as well? Does it survive the case where the people being praised later adopt the flattering description themselves and deploy it politically? And is the defect epistemic, moral, or rhetorical — above all, if the author's general conclusion that our customs are contingent and ours alone can be true even when the illustration is invented, is there a fallacy here at all, or only weak evidence for a sound claim? Please write in American English, and don't stop at a label: I want the carving, including the boundary cases you think are genuinely hard.
