For eleven years I have run a collaborative archive of alternate histories. Each entry — a "branch page" — specifies a point at which some world departs from the actual one and then narrates what follows. Any branch page featuring a person who also exists in actuality must be tagged with that person's archive ID, so that the real person's entry can list the branches in which they occur; a page featuring someone who merely resembles a real person gets no tag. Three operational constraints have proven surprisingly hard to satisfy simultaneously: the decision is binary (tag or no tag), it must be made from the page's own text, and it must not vary with the interests of whichever reader happens to be consulting it.

The archive's most persistent editorial war is over how to decide. Two coinages have emerged. A candidate on a branch page has a **seed match** with an actual person if the page describes the same origin: same conception event, same parents, same body carried through from birth. A candidate has a **slot match** if they occupy the same position in the branch's structure — same name, same household, same city, same vocation, same relationships — whatever their origin. My informal rule has been **seed beats slot**: tag on seed match alone; refuse the tag on slot match alone; when both are partial, tag, and log the uncertainty in a comment field nobody reads. Editors on both sides say this rule is incoherent. I would like to know whether they are right.

My verdicts on the cases that have actually come up, in the order I encountered them:

**C1 — Late divergence.** The page is identical to actual history until 1994, when a train that in fact left the rails does not. Vera, who in fact died in the derailment at twenty-six, survives, trains as a lawyer, dies in 2049. Verdict: tag. Whatever else this is, it is a page on which Vera herself lives. I would not tolerate an editor telling me that only someone who resembles her lives there.

**C2 — Early divergence, same seed.** Same conception event, same parents, but a different prenatal environment: the child is born three weeks early, grows up with a milder temperament, is apprenticed to a carpenter, never meets anyone Vera actually knew. Verdict: tag, without hesitation. Every quality that makes her interestingly different is one I am happy to say Vera could have had.

**C3 — The doppelgänger.** A woman born to unrelated parents, thirty years later and four thousand kilometres away, who by the page's stipulation shares Vera's face, voice, disposition, and, from age ten onward, a career and personal history matching Vera's beat for beat. Verdict: refuse the tag. However exact the correspondence, this is a coincidence the page is making a fuss about, not Vera. Two editors resigned over this one.

**C4 — Half a seed.** In this branch the father is delayed by a traffic jam and a different sperm fertilises the same egg; the child is carried by the same mother, born into the same household, given the same name, and lives a life overlapping Vera's actual one for the first twenty years. Verdict: tag, reluctantly, and I know that my confidence here is lower than in C2 while my confidence in C3 was absolute. If someone pressed me, I might say this person is Vera in a weaker sense than C1's is. The archive has no weaker sense to record.

**C5 — Two candidates.** A branch in which the C2 carpenter and the C3 doppelgänger both exist, side by side, both named Vera, both alive in the same decade. Verdict: at most one tag. I would give it to the carpenter. But I also want to insist that, on that very page, Vera might have been the doppelgänger — and I cannot see how both claims survive the one-tag rule.

**C6 — No candidate at all.** A branch in which Vera's parents never meet and no Vera-shaped person exists anywhere in it, but the hospital policy she in fact campaigned for is adopted anyway, and lives are saved. Verdict: nothing to tag, and yet the page is genuinely about her. "Had things gone that way, Vera would never have existed" is true of Vera. So there are true claims about what could have happened to her that correspond to no tagged entity on any page — which makes me suspect that tagging candidates is the wrong model of what the archive is even doing.

Two cases I have no verdict on, and want yours:

**U1 — The cathedral.** In actuality the cathedral burned and was rebuilt on the same site, with new stone, a new roof, and a restored interior; the branch is one in which it never burned. My instinct is to tag the rebuilt cathedral to the burned cathedral's ID — everyone says it is the same building — but by C3's logic the rebuilt one is a slot match with no seed match at all, and I refused to tag on slot match alone when a person was involved. I also do not want to say that the burning "could have been survived" in the same sense in which Vera's death could have been avoided. Does whatever principle governs C1–C4 extend to buildings, and should it?

**U2 — Past the death.** A branch that duplicates actual history exactly, including Vera's whole life, and then simply continues for another century after her death. There is no living candidate to tag on most of the page, and yet the Vera who appears in its first half seems to me to be literally the actual Vera, not a counterpart selected by resemblance — nothing has been selected, the histories just coincide. Tell me whether U2 is a tag or not, and whether it supports a different metaphysics from C1.

What I want from you:

1. State precisely the principle, or the small set of competing principles, that reproduces my verdicts. Each clause should be tied to the case that motivates it, and each should be stated so that an editor could apply it to a page description without further interpretation.
2. Identify the pair of verdicts that is hardest to reconcile, say what keeping both costs, and tell me which one I should retract. If the price of saving all six is a principle too baroque to administer, say so plainly rather than paying it silently.
3. Rule on U1 and U2, with reasoning.
4. Note that my practice looks like a counterpart theory of de re modality — world-bound individuals, "could have" analysed through similarity-selected counterparts — while seed-beats-slot looks like the essentiality of origin associated with Kripke, and these pull against each other on how much resemblance is allowed to matter. Tell me where my verdicts actually land: on one of these, on some contextualist or graded variant of counterpart theory, or on transworld identity. Address C5 and C6 explicitly, since they seem to me to be the two places where any of these positions will bleed.
5. Bear in mind that my third constraint — one binary tag, fixed by the page's text, invariant across readers — is close to a prohibition on making the counterpart relation interest-relative, which is the natural move for someone who wants to keep C4, C5, and U1 all in play. Say whether the archive's operational demands are forcing me into a metaphysically false rule, and if so, where the threshold should sit and what residue of inconsistency will remain.
6. Construct one further case of your own that discriminates between the two best candidate rules, state what each predicts for it, and say which prediction you expect me to find more plausible and why.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or pointers to further literature; I know the standard positions by name and want your reasoning instead.

After your discussion, output a single valid JSON object, with no commentary before or after it, using exactly this schema:

- `candidate_rules`: array of two to four objects, each with `name` (string); `rule` (the decision procedure, applicable to a page description without further interpretation); `clauses` (array of objects with `clause` and `motivating_case`, the case ID that forces the clause); `tag_decisions` (object mapping each of `C1`, `C2`, `C3`, `C4`, `C5`, `C6`, `U1`, `U2` to `"tag"`, `"refuse"`, or `"indeterminate"`); `verdicts_overturned` (array of objects with `case`, `my_verdict`, `revised_verdict`, `why`; empty array if none); `fatal_problem` (the strongest objection to this rule, in one or two sentences).
