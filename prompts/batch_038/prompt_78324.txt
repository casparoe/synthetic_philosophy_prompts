So for the third cycle running I'm the token philosopher on a review panel at a smallish private foundation that funds interdisciplinary work on complex systems — mostly physicists, one molecular biologist, a sociologist, and me. Two of the others have told me, not unkindly, that my job is to stop proposals that use the word emergence as if it were a research method, and this year the chair has actually asked me to bring something to the September meeting: a screening tool the whole panel can run at triage, where each of us gets maybe fifteen minutes per letter of intent and their about fifty of them a cycle. Nobody on the panel has read any philosophy, so it can't trade on jargon, and it can't ask reviewers to do something they won't do, like reconstruct an argument charitably before scoring it. What I want is a decision procedure — an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the branch each answer sends you down, and the point where it stops and hands the call to a human — that sorts a proposal into one of: (i) ordinary reductionist work with emergence language bolted on as decoration; (ii) a real and reasonably tractable claim about explanatory autonomy, meaning non-derivability, multiple realization, coarse-grained variables carrying predictive weight the micro description doesn't cheaply reproduce; or (iii) a strong claim that the higher level has causal powers of its own, downward causation and all. And it has to be honest about a tension I can't see a way around, so please engage with it rather than papering over it: a tool that just asks whether the higher-level facts are derivable from the micro facts will let nearly everything through, since hardly any interesting system is derivable in practice, while a tool that asks whether the higher level does causal work the lower level can't will fail everything for anyone who takes the physical level to be causally closed, on the exclusion reasoning Kim pressed. So the questions have to do something subtler than picking one of those two axes — tell me what the third thing is and how the tool actually uses it. Say also which of the familiar distinctions must survive being flattened into a checklist item and which ones I can safely throw overboard at this stage, and flag every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedural costume. No bibliography, no reading list, please — I know you can't verify any of that and neither can we, and half the panel would resent being told to go read something.

Then run it. Three letters of intent, all realistic for this foundation, and I want the tool applied to each with the questions in order and the actual answers you'd accept or reject rather than a summary. First: a soft condensed matter group working on driven granular media and active colloids, large simulations plus a tabletop rig, pitching the collective modes as emergent on the grounds that they can be predicted from a coarse-grained hydrodynamic-like description but not feasibly from the particle-level model, and listing three specific predictions where the two descriptions diverge within the run times they can afford. Second: a computational neuroscience group claiming that network-level oscillations exert downward causation on the spiking of individual neurons, stating plainly that the neural level is causally closed, and promising a rigorous demonstration that the oscillation variables carry independent causal power — I want to know exactly what the tool demands of them, what would have to be added before it's fundable rather than confused, and whether the two things they assert can even be held together. Third, and this is the one where I expect an answer I won't like: an institutional analysis proposal co-written by a former student whose work I think is genuinely excellent, arguing that the micro-level description of individual behaviour is in principle unavailable for the systems they study, so no bridge principles could ever be supplied, so nothing could ever be derived, and that this very non-derivability is what establishes both the autonomy and the causal efficacy of the institutional level. I have a bad feeling about that inference and I want the tool to say so in terms I can put in front of a panel without sounding like I'm just defending reductionism by reflex.

Deliverables, then: the procedure proper; a one-page version we could print and keep next to the score sheet; the short paragraph I'd send the chair explaining where the tool misfires, which categories it can't cleanly separate, and what it can't settle at all; and — because we've been burned twice now — an account of what a determined applicant who had seen the checklist would write to fake their way into category (ii), and which single question catches them. Its fine if the honest answer is that no fifteen-minute screen can do this reliably and the tool should mostly be sorting proposals into "review normally" versus "send to the philosopher"; if that's your conclusion, build the tool that way and tell me what I'm giving up.
