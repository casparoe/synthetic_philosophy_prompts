I'm a postdoc in population genetics — drift, effective size, the coalescent, that whole toolkit — and I got dragged into a reading group argument about whether generations are actually real kinds. Like, is a Millennial a thing in the world, the way a species or a chemical element is a thing, or is it just a label someone slapped on a stretch of birth years and then sold back to us?

The debate went right over my head, honestly. On one side: generations as natural kinds with fuzzy-but-real boundaries. On the other: generations as measurement artifacts or marketing inventions, no more real than dividing people into those born on odd vs even years. And underneath both, the usual suspects — the boundary problem (why does someone born in December 1996 count as a Millennial and someone born in January 1997 as Gen Z, when they had near-identical childhoods?), the performativity worry (once you name a cohort and target it, do you manufacture the very thing you claimed to find?), and some cyclical theory about history running in ~80-year waves of four generational types that I couldnt follow at all.

Here's what I want from you. Restate that whole debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually think in. Allele frequencies and their variance, drift versus selection, coalescent trees and common ancestry, effective population size, the difference between a sample and a deme, structured populations and migration, the fact that a lineage's "generation" is a counting unit defined by parent-offspring steps rather than a calendar. Use it properly, not as decoration — I can tell when someone's just borrowing words.

Then give me the residue report, sorted into three piles: what translates cleanly, what only translates if you bend something out of shape, and what has no expression in my vocabulary whatsoever.

Two specific things I want you to be careful about. First, no smuggling. I can see how tempting it is to quietly redefine "generation" as a deme or as a cohort defined by common ancestry, declare the translation successful, and then announce that the philosophers were confused. If you do that, say so out loud and tell me what got lost in the redefinition. Second, don't let my framework's own habits do the arguing — in my world, whether a grouping is real often just depends on whether it predicts, and I suspect that's a substantive position about kinds rather than a neutral translation, so flag it if it's creeping in.

And a genuine question, because I think it might cut both ways: my field's demographers cousins already talk about cohort effects, and the identification problem where age, period and cohort are perfectly collinear so you can't estimate a cohort effect without extra assumptions. Does "generations are real" just reduce to "there's a non-reducible cohort effect"? If so, does the identification problem mean the question is underdetermined by data, or does it mean something subtler about what kind of claim it is?

End with a verdict on whose side the residue falls on — is my vocabulary missing something it ought to be able to say, or does the failure to translate show the original debate was less substantive than it sounded? Tell me how confident you are in that call and in the main pieces of the translation, and single out the one point you're least sure about, especially anywhere you might be leaning on a reading of the generations literature that's contested rather than standard.
