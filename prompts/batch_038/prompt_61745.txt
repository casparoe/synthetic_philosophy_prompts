I run a little reading group in my living room — six of us, mixed backgrounds: a physics PhD student who insists all of this is "just Bayesian conditioning with extra steps", a lit PhD who thinks it's really about language games, two people who did a philosophy minor fifteen years ago and still bring up the Matrix at every meeting, and me, who is basically self-taught off SEP entries. We spent a month on transcendental arguments — Kant's move against the idealist, Strawson's descriptive metaphysics, Putnam's brain-in-a-vat bit, the Apel/Habermas idea that you can't argue without already presupposing the norms of argumentation, Korsgaard's constitutive stuff about agency, plus the naturalist response that all of these are just inference to the best explanation wearing a funny hat — and it turned into six people using "transcendental argument" to mean six different things. Half our fights were about whether the conclusion is supposed to be about the world or only about what we can't help believing, and nobody ever noticed they'd switched which one they meant mid-sentence. Also, whenever somebody pressed one of these arguments, the same dodge showed up: just substitute a weaker conclusion and the reasoning still goes through, so nothing was ever actually established against the skeptic.

So I want to build a "which version are you actually running?" quiz for next week — eight questions, nine max — each one a concrete situation or a reaction to a bit of reasoning rather than a definition check, two or three answer options apiece, then a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the named positions. Treat yourself as someone who has taught a Kant-and-after seminar for ten years and who also designs diagnostic quizzes for a living, and please think carefully and brainstorm out loud first: list the axes these positions genuinely differ on before you write a single question. The axes I can see are which capacity is doing the work (experience, thought, language, actual discourse, agency); whether the conclusion is world-directed or only about our conceptual scheme or only about what we're committed to believing; whether the "you can't coherently doubt this" is a normative point or just a psychological one; whether the argument is meant to defeat the skeptic or only to describe us; and what's supposed to happen when someone grants every premise and shrugs. Add any I've missed, then make the questions probe those axes, and make the axes visible in the key so I can tell my group "you came out scheme-directed on six of eight, that's why you keep talking past Dave."

Hard constraints, because these are the things that always ruin this kind of quiz. No question may name a philosopher or use words like "transcendental", "a priori", "constitutive", or "performative contradiction" — someone who has never read any of this has to be able to answer from the case alone, and the quiz should sort people by what they'd actually say, not by which label they like the sound of. Keep the options inside a question roughly equally attractive and equally well-written; I don't want one to read like the textbook answer just because it's longer. Include at least one question that forces a choice between "the skeptic is straightforwardly wrong about the world" and "the skeptic might be right about the world but has misdescribed what he's doing when he doubts". Include at least one where two positions you'd expect to be far apart give the same answer — I'd guess Strawson and Putnam, but tell me if you'd pick a different pair — and more generally flag any pair of leaves your instrument can't really seperate rather than pretending it can. Then after the key, three things: (a) tell me what to do with the person whose answers come out inconsistent, since at least three of us will be that person; (b) list the logically possible combinations of answers that no question-path ever leads to, and for each say whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, because its unattractive, or because nobody has thought of it yet — I'd bet at least one cell is genuinely unoccupied and interesting; and (c) don't tack on a reading list at the end. I've had models confidently recommend chapters and books that don't exist, and I have no way to check, so if you're not certain a work is real just leave that part out entirely.
