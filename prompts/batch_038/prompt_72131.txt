I sit on the board of a small heritage trust in a market town, and we fund the midsummer lamplight procession that all our brochures say has run every year since 1812. A grad student we hired for an archiving project has just shown that it was actually started in 1927 by the chamber of commerce, lifting a couple of motifs from an unrelated harvest custom that had already died out, and that the 1812 date traces back to a newspaper item that looks like it was planted by the same people. She wants the wording corrected. The volunteer committee says correcting it hands the story to the neighbouring town, which is bidding for the same regional tourism pot, and anyway the procession is the one week a year when the place actually feels like a town to the people who live here — how it started isnt what they turn out for.

Two complications: our deed commits us to supporting customs of "demonstrable historic continuity," and three other things we fund have provenance questions that will surface the moment this one does, so whatever we decide here sets the pattern.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Take a position — keep funding unchanged, fund with corrected materials, withdraw, or an option I havent listed — and give the board something they could actually use on the other three cases that isn't just gut feel. And deal honestly with the volunteers: is a public body spending money on a story it now knows to be false a different kind of problem from a town telling itself a flattering legend?
