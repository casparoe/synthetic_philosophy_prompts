I'm a senior in a seminar on the philosophy of work, and I've been asked to give the ten-minute address launching a flexible-scheduling pilot in my university's dining and custodial services. The room will hold about 120 hourly staff, many of them on split shifts or holding second jobs, plus the vice-president and two trustees who funded the pilot, the union representative who negotiated it, and a scattering of faculty. The union rep has asked me not to talk about the dignity of labor; the VP's office has asked me not to turn the occasion into a critique of the university.

I want the speech built on a historical claim rather than an inspirational one: that treating busyness as a virtue is contingent, that ascetic Protestantism turned ordinary labor into a calling, that the ethic outlived the theology that produced it, and that we are left holding the residue, in which rest must be earned rather than taken. Three facts should carry argumentative weight rather than serve as decoration. When France moved the legal week from 39 hours to 35 at the turn of this century, the stated purpose was above all to cut a record unemployment rate by sharing work out, not to give anyone a fuller life outside the job. Studying the reform afterward, economists found that shift-work firms preserved output largely by intensifying night work and adding overtime rather than by shortening anyone's day. And the first death publicly reported in Japan as karoshi, in 1969, was a 29-year-old worker in the shipping department of the country's largest newspaper company, killed by a stroke.

Hard constraints: never promise the pilot will give people more free time, since for someone paid hourly flexibility can mean fewer hours or the same hours rearranged; never use the phrase work-life balance, which concedes the premise I mean to dispute, that work is the default and life is the remainder; leave room for the people in the room who genuinely like their jobs without commending them for liking them; and if you invoke karoshi, make it the far end of a continuum that includes this room rather than a shock from elsewhere.

Deliver it as exactly five numbered movements: for each, one sentence saying what the movement is doing, followed by the words I will actually say, about a hundred of them. No introduction, no title, and nothing after the fifth.
