I'm a high school senior presenting a twenty-minute paper at a regional student humanities symposium, and the question period will be handled by a panel of four: two school counselors (one of whom runs restorative-justice training for our district), a clinical psychology professor who teaches the questionnaire research on guilt-proneness and shame-proneness, and a former debate coach who now judges student papers and cares about definitions and slippery slopes.

My thesis is that shame, not guilt, is the better moral emotion for a community like a school, and that a community can deliberately choose it. My argument in brief: (1) guilt is directed at an act and can be discharged by repair, so at best it restores the status quo, whereas shame is directed at the self as seen from the standpoint of people whose opinion matters to you, and therefore aims at becoming a different person; (2) guilt's accuser is internal, so it can be satisfied privately and licenses concealment, while shame is ineliminably about being seen and so trains candor; (3) because shame is constituted by whose gaze you have internalized, a community that arranges the gaze on purpose — ceremonies, public acknowledgment, restorative circles, who is permitted to speak for the group — is engineering its members' moral emotions, and that is what it means for a culture to choose, within limits set by temperament and by what the community already rewards.
