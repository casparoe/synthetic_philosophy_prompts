Background: not an academic, I do data engineering, and I've been reading the consciousness literature in the evenings for about a year — mostly around the access/phenomenal distinction and the global workspace people, plus a lot of the applied arguments about whether AI systems might be conscious. My partner thinks I've gone down a rabbit hole. Possibly. Anyway, there's a pattern I keep running into that I don't have a word for, and I've started to think the reason I can't find one is that it's hiding in the seams between steps rather than in any single step.

Here's the shape of it. You want to know whether some system is conscious, or which of two brain states is conscious. Consciousness isn't directly measurable, so you pick a marker: verbal report, a button press, confidence ratings, flexible use of the information, whatever the paradigm happens to make available. And the reason you picked that marker is largely practical — it's the thing you can actually get out of a subject in a scanner, or read off a model's outputs. Totally reasonable, you have to start somewhere. Then the marker does the real work downstream: state X shows the marker, state Y doesn't, so X is conscious and Y isn't; or this architecture has the marker, so it might be conscious. And then when someone objects that there could be consciousness without the marker — unreportable states, the local-versus-global broadcasting argument, the whole overflow business about whether iconic memory holds more than you can report — the objection tends to get waved off as untestable, which is treated as a decisive point rather than as a symptom. What bugs me is that every one of those steps looks fine on its own. Picking a measurable marker is just good practice. Inferring from the marker is just applying the theory. Rejecting the untestable objection is just methodological hygiene. It's only when you line them up that you can see the warrant for the conclusion traces all the way back to a constraint about what's convenient to measure, and that constraint did essentially all the work while looking like it did none.

Two instances to make it concrete, since the abstract version sounds like a complaint. First: contrastive paradigms where "saw the stimulus" is defined operationally by the subject's keypress, so the thing you end up isolating in the data is a correlate of reportability, and then the paper's discussion section talks about having found the neural correlate of the experience, no caveat, as if the swap happened for free. Second: the lists of indicator properties people put together for machine consciousness, where each property makes it onto the list for two reasons at once — because some theory says that property matters, and because it's the kind of property you could actually check in a system you have access to — and those two justifications get blended together so that by the end nobody can say which one earned the property its place, and the list gets used as if it were theory-driven when a chunk of it was access-driven.

So: does this have a name? I'm not asking about operational definitions in general, and I know "you get what you measure" is a bumper sticker, not a concept. And I don't think it's just question-begging either, because the interesting part is that no single step begs anything — the circularity is spread across the chain, and each step is individually defensible. What I'm after is the structure where a pragmatic constraint enters at step one and has been converted into an epistemic one by step three, with every conversion looking locally legitimate. If there's an established term or a literature that has exactly this, point me at it and tell me where my description overreaches. If the nearest things are only cousins, I want to know which cousin is closest, what each one actually covers, and specifically why mine isn't that one. And if nothing fits, coin it: give me a name, a definition tight enough that a third party could apply it to a case I haven't described, and at least two things it should deliberately not be used for, so the edges are sharp. A bare label is useless to me — the carving is the whole point.
