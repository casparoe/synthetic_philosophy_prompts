My mum died three weeks ago at 88 and I'm doing the eulogy at her service on Saturday. She was a George Eliot person in the most literal sense — reread Middlemarch once a decade, and she had taken Eliot's own position on religion completely to heart and lived it for sixty years without ever calling it a position. Which is: the doctrines are false, nobody walked on water, there's no one waiting on the other side of this, and you still go to church anyway, every Sunday, and say the creed out loud along with everybody else. Not ironically, not as a bit, and not to keep up appearances — because she thought that's where a place keeps its moral imagination, and that if you pull the forms out you lose something you can't put back with a good lecture. She knew perfectly well that Eliot had translated the Strauss and the Feuerbach, the two books that took the miracles apart, and still sat in a pew. She used to bring up the essay where Eliot goes after Dr Cumming, and her point about it was always that the quarrel wasn't evidential — it was that a religion aimed at your own rescue in the next world makes you smaller and less generous in this one. Mum thought that was the sharpest thing anybody had ever said about religion and she would say it at christenings.

She'd also have despised being called spiritual but not religious.

Here's the problem. The service is in her parish church, same building for 43 years, and the vicar officiating buried my dad, knows exactly what Mum thought, and has asked me — politely, but he has asked — not to turn it into a platform. About half the room will be that parish: the choir, the flower rota, the woman she sat next to every week, all of them about to hear a eulogy telling them their friend didn't believe a word they'd been saying together. The other half is family and old colleagues, and two of my nephews are athiests of the loud variety who have already told me the whole church business was cowardice and that she owed it to them to have said so publicly. So I have to do the impossible bit: explain to the parish why she came, in a way that lands as love for them rather than a correction of their theology, and explain to my nephews why she wasn't a hypocrite or a frightened old woman, without handing them a win either.

Six minutes spoken, so call it 900 words. Nothing in it that she'd have considered false — so no "she's with Dad now," and equally no smug line about her having known better than the room. Don't put any of Eliot's actual words in quotation marks; Mum hated being quoted at and I'm not starting now, so if you want to gesture at the novels or the translations, gesture, don't cite.

And the part I can't do myself: she said the creed out loud every single week and meant it in a sense she was perfectly happy to explain to anyone who asked, but which almost nobody in that building would have accepted as belief. I need one or two plain sentences that make that sense available to the room — something they hear as a description of how she loved them, not as a philosophy talk — and then I need to get out of the way so the vicar can say the committal without feeling like he's been ambushed in his own church.
