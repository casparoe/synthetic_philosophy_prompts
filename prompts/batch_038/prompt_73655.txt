I run the standards desk at a national heritage and tourism board, and I need a working procedure rather than an argument. Three things come across my desk every week: applications from tour operators, municipalities, food producers, regional museums, and festival committees for our official Heritage Experience listing; copy for the national tourism website and the visitor app; and proposals for segments of the national-day broadcast. In nearly all of them the selling point is age and continuity — a ceremony that predates written record, a dish cooked the same way for five centuries, a festival held without interruption, a national costume unchanged across generations. The volume is roughly sixty listing applications a year plus several hundred pieces of copy. My staff are three editors with no training in history, and each application gets about ninety minutes before it goes to a five-person committee for sign-off: two academic historians who distrust the entire certification enterprise, one industry representative who wants approvals, our legal officer, and me. We have been criticised in the press twice for certifying claims that turned out to be products of the last century, and the legal officer's constraint is that we may not publish findings that call an applicant's claim false. We only control what we ourselves say.

Please write the procedure my desk can run on an application, grounded in Hobsbawm and Ranger's account of invented tradition and in Anderson's account of imagined communities. From the first I want the distinction between invented tradition — invariant, ritualised, asserting a continuity with the past that is largely factitious — and custom, which is adaptable and serves practical ends, together with the observation that bursts of invention cluster at moments when older social fabrics are breaking up. From the second I want the point that a community larger than face-to-face is imagined without being for that reason false, the modularity of national forms, the role of print in fixing them, and the census–map–museum apparatus through which states manufacture heritage. Tell me plainly where the procedure is borrowing beyond what these authors intended: Anderson was not writing an authentication toolkit, and I want to know which of my tests would misrepresent him if I claimed his authority for them.

Deliver, in this order: an ordered sequence of tests, each with the question the editor asks, what counts as a sufficient answer, what an insufficient answer triggers, and a rough time cost, plus a note on why the tests sit in that order and where an application can be short-circuited to save the ninety minutes; a short label vocabulary the desk can attach to a claim — something on the lines of continuous, adapted, revived, reconstructed, invented while claiming antiquity, and invented without claiming antiquity — with the rule for which labels may be listed and under what public wording; three or four approved sentence templates for our own copy that stay truthful without calling anyone a liar; an explicit marking of the points where the procedure stops and hands the case to the committee because what remains is a question of value or policy rather than fact; and a candid list of which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, above all the choice of how old an invention must be before it becomes a tradition.

Tests I would expect to see, though you should revise, drop, or add: the first-attestation date of the practice as distinct from the first-attestation date of the claim that it is ancient; who codified, choreographed, or wrote it down, and when; whether the form matches a template that appears in unrelated regions within the same decade; whether invariance is itself a marker of recency, so that a practice which has not drifted may be younger than one which has; dependence on a rulebook, script, published collection, newspaper campaign, or broadcaster; the size of the coeval community the claim asks an audience to imagine; whether the heritage exists chiefly in state inventories, museum cases, and protected-status files; and who gains from certification, which should raise the evidentiary bar rather than settle anything.

The procedure must handle these hard cases explicitly: an invention now itself a hundred and thirty years old; a form imposed by a colonial administration and subsequently adopted as the community's own; a food claim where the ingredients are ancient but the prepared dish is documented only from the 1930s, so the age of the substrate must be separated from the age of the practice; and an oral tradition from a dispossessed community with no documentary trail whatever. On the last of these the procedure must state outright that absence of documentation is not evidence of invention, and say what to do instead.

Then run it on three applications, supplying the analysis yourself from the facts I give. First: a highland gathering festival whose earliest documentation is a 1908 handbill from a railway company's promotional office, held continuously since 1946, whose programme of competitive dance, livestock judging, mass singing, and a closing beacon-lighting by a local dignitary matches formats that appear in at least three other regions of the country within the same decade, and whose applicant copy asserts the festival predates any written record. Second: a woven garment the broadcast proposes to describe as worn continuously for five centuries, where the weave technique appears in regional archaeological material, but the garment's cut, its colour coding, and its association with national dress all trace to a craft-revival school founded in the 1920s. Third, and the one I expect to lose: our own flagship asset, the costume the board has promoted for forty years as the ancient national dress, which as I understand it was designated in the 1930s by a state committee that selected and standardised elements from several regional variants; if the procedure requires us to reword our own materials, say so and draft the replacement copy.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it cannot distinguish an invented tradition from a revived one on the evidence typically available, why it will systematically misfire on oral traditions even with the safeguard above, and why it cannot answer the question the committee will actually fight over, which is whether an invented tradition deserves listing once it has taken root. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; my staff will never read the originals. I want tests, labels, wording, and the places where the tests run out.
