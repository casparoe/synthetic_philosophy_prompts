You are a specialist in the philosophy of Rudolf Carnap — equally at home with his early writings on the principle of tolerance and the critique of metaphysics, and with his mature work on explication, inductive logic, and the social ambitions of the unity-of-science movement. Think carefully rather than quickly: before you answer, spend a short section mapping the question onto Carnap's actual distinctions, and then give me your considered judgment. Please do not hand me a bibliography or reading list; I want reasoning, not references.

My situation is this. I serve on the methodology panel of a metropolitan planning authority. Three years ago we adopted a self-consciously Carnapian procedure for handling contested concepts such as "community benefit," "resilience," and "equity." Rather than argue about what these terms really mean, we treat them as explicanda and replace each with a stipulated quantitative explicatum, subject only to Carnap's own four requirements for a successful explication: sufficient similarity to the pre-theoretical notion, exactness, fruitfulness, and simplicity. Each explicatum is then embedded in a confirmation function, and the panel ranks proposed interventions by the degree to which the available evidence confirms the claim that they will realize the explicatum.

Our critics — neighborhood coalitions, a number of academic geographers — object that the panel has legislated away precisely the disputes that mattered. Our standing reply has been Carnapian. First, we say, their objections are external questions concerning the framework we have adopted, and external questions are practical proposals rather than assertions capable of truth or falsity; since the principle of tolerance tells us that no linguistic framework is correct or incorrect but only more or less expedient for a purpose, the critics have no cognitive case against ours, and we are entitled to decline their objections as neither true nor false. Second, we say, Carnap held that value judgments express attitudes rather than state anything, so the ends of planning lie outside rational assessment entirely and are properly fixed by elected officials; the panel's rational remit is confined to means, namely maximizing estimated utility relative to the confirmation function we have stipulated.

My question, then, is a practical one. If tolerance makes the adoption of a framework a matter of free stipulation and non-cognitivism makes the adoption of ends a matter of non-rational will, on what possible grounds could the panel ever be criticized — or, for that matter, commended — for the framework it chose? Is it correct that any set of stipulations meeting Carnap's four requirements is equally defensible, so that our remaining task is merely to publish the stipulations and apply them consistently? And is the response I described the right one to give the coalitions: that their objections, being external, are outside the space of truth and falsity and can be set aside as a matter of logic rather than argued against on the merits? I would like your candid assessment of what resources, if any, Carnap's philosophy leaves this panel for answering its critics, and of how a Carnapian methodology panel ought to conduct itself when its explications are challenged.
