I am a software engineer with roughly twelve years of experience in distributed systems. I have no formal training in philosophy. Over the past year I have been reading the units-of-selection literature on my own — Dawkins and Williams on the genic view, Sober and Wilson's rehabilitation of group selection, Okasha's book on the levels of selection, and a handful of papers on contextual analysis — because a system I maintain raises what looks to me like exactly that question. **Draft: "The Copy Set"**

**1. The system.** I own an evolutionary search platform. Candidate solutions are serialized policy graphs; they are organized into demes of sixty-four. Two features are nonstandard. First, lateral transfer: each deme maintains a shared pool of reusable subgraph motifs, successful individuals write entries into it, and any individual in the deme may pull entries out and splice them into itself. Second, deme forking: every two hundred generations the highest-performing demes are copied whole — all sixty-four individuals, plus the pool, plus the deme's accumulated configuration — and launched as new demes, while the lowest-performing demes are terminated outright.

The question I keep being asked at design reviews is which of these things is being selected: the individual solution, the deme, the pool, or the motifs.

**2. What I take the literature to say.** Dawkins separates replicators, which are copied, from vehicles, which house them and are not copied; Hull reformulates the second role as the interactor, the entity whose dealings with its environment make the difference in copying. Williams argues that group selection is an unnecessary multiplication of explanatory machinery, available only under conditions that rarely obtain. Sober and Wilson argue that trait-group structure makes those conditions obtain more often than supposed. Okasha distinguishes two families of multilevel models: MLS1, in which groups themselves reproduce, splitting to found daughter groups and being assigned a group-level fitness, and MLS2, in which groups do not reproduce but merely differ in the average output of their members. Contextual analysis is then used to estimate how much of the selection differential sits at each level.

**3. My proposal.** At any reproduction event, some part of the parent's state is written into the offspring; the rest is discarded, recomputed, or supplied from outside. Call the transmitted part the copy set. My claim is that an entity is a candidate unit of selection if and only if it is, or is constituted by, something inside the copy set of some reproduction event, and an actual unit of selection if variation in it makes a difference to how many copy events it participates in. A corollary: a vehicle can never be a unit of selection, because by definition it is not transmitted; what makes my demes more than vehicles is precisely that forking puts them inside the copy set.

The reason I find this convincing is that I can move the boundary by editing code. When I disabled forking and rebuilt each deme's pool from scratch, the pool left the copy set; between-deme variance collapsed within a few thousand generations and deme-level properties stopped predicting anything. When I turned forking back on, the pool re-entered the copy set and demes became variable, heritable, fitness-differing things. Same individuals, same fitness function, different answer — because a different set of state variables was serialized.

**4. Consequence for the statistics.** The Price equation is an algebraic identity: it can be applied at any level, and it always yields a between-group and a within-group term. Anything that can be applied anywhere cannot tell you where selection is; it is bookkeeping rather than ontology. The same goes for the criterion I have seen attributed to Sober, on which one settles the question by finding the level whose characters explain the largest share of variance in fitness, and concluding that selection is at the group level when group characters explain more than individual ones do. On my account none of this estimation is necessary. You do not measure the unit of selection; you read the reproduction code. And since any between-group covariance can be restated as a change in gene frequency, I take the genic view to be true but uninformative.

**5. The objection I expect.** Someone will say this is pluralism wearing a systems-engineering costume: that the copy-set facts are just what the statistical methods are trying to measure, so I have redescribed the problem rather than solved it. My reply is that the copy set is available without measurement, and in an engineered system it is a design decision that is prior to any statistics.

The more interesting case is where biology's copy set is not clean. A host and its microbiome reproduce together, but the microbiome is partly transmitted vertically and partly acquired from the environment. My criterion says the holobiont is not a unit of selection; the unit is whichever fraction of its state is transmitted, and that fraction is measurable by the same intervention I ran in software — sever the vertical channel and see whether the group-level character remains heritable.

**6. Conclusion.** The units-of-selection debate is not a disagreement about how to describe the world. It is a disagreement about which mechanisms count as reproduction. Fix the copy set and the unit follows. Selection acts on what gets serialized.
