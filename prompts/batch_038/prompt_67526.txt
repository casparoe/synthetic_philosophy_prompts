I volunteer on our city's citizens advisory committee, which mostly means a dozen of us show up on Tuesday nights and read planning documents nobody else wants to read. Last week's meeting ran two hours over because of a drainage dispute, and afterwards two of the other members — Gerald, who runs a print shop, and Nadia, who teaches high school civics — got into it in the parking lot about something that's actually going to be in front of the council in the fall. There's a proposal to build 240 mixed-income units on the vacant city-owned lot by the rail yard, the council has kicked it down the road four times now, and a group of residents wants to force a citywide referedum on it in November. I was leaning on my car pretending not to listen, and honestly I've been mulling the whole thing for a week. I'm paraphrasing from memory so the exact wording isn't theirs and I might have the order of one or two of these slightly off, but I think I've got the substance right.

(1) Gerald opened by saying the council's deadlock is the argument for asking the voters directly: polls show the project has majority support citywide, but the two members from the affected district kill it every single time, and a referendum would settle it in a way a 5–4 council vote can't, because nobody could claim the fix was in. Legitimacy, he said, comes from the fact that everyone got an equal say, not from the fact that the answer came out right.

(2) Nadia said polls measure uninformed preferences, that nobody reads the fiscal note in the ballot pamphlet, and that the result gets set by whoever buys the most advertising and by how the question happens to be worded. Her example was Prop 13: passed by nearly two to one in 1978, wrote a one percent cap and reassessment-only-on-sale into the state constitution, required a two-thirds legislative majority for any future tax increase, and California has been living with the consequences for four decades, schools included. Her general point was that referendums are most dangerous exactly where the decision is hardest to reverse.

(3) Gerald said that's an objection to entrenchment, not to asking. Fix the design: make every citizen initiative an ordinary ordinance so a later council or a later vote can undo it; require a plain-language fiscal estimate in the pamphlet; and put a council-drafted alternative on the same ballot so people are choosing between two texts rather than voting yes or no on one. He said that's basically the Swiss arrangement — parliament can set a counter-proposal beside an initiative, and constitutional changes need a double majority, of the people and of the cantons, which forces a proposal to win broadly instead of narrowly.

(4) Nadia said two things were wrong there. First, Switzerland isn't evidence that referendums work, it's evidence that they work in Switzerland: small, wealthy, four languages, voting several times a year for a century and a half, so Swiss voters already possess the competence the measure is supposed to produce. You can't cite a practice that only functions because of the habits it has already generated as proof that importing it will generate those habits. Second, the double majority is a minority veto — a proposal can win the popular vote and still lose — which is a reasonable device for a federation but is not "letting the majority decide," and he had identified deadlocked minorities as the problem ninety seconds earlier.

(5) Gerald said weighting isn't a veto on the majority, it's a rule about which majority counts when the units are wildly unequal in size, which is the same reason the Senate exists; if all weighting is illegitimate then the Senate is illegitimate, and he doubted she was ready for that.

(6) Nadia said the Senate analogy fails because equal state votes were a bargain among pre-existing sovereignties that could have walked away from the deal, and it's entrenched so that it can't be amended without a state's own consent, whereas our neighborhoods never negotiated anything and weighting votes by district here just hands the people who own houses near the lot a veto over the renters who would live in it. She said the deeper problem anyway is who writes the question: whoever drafts the wording controls the outcome, and a council counter-proposal doesn't cure that, it just hands agenda control to the very body whose deadlock he was trying to escape.

(7) Gerald said then don't let the council write it — seat forty residents chosen by lot, pay them, give them staff, expert testimony and a few months, and let them draft both the question and the alternative. If the complaint is competence, that's the competence fix, because a small body with time and information frames things better than either a deadlocked council or a signature-gathering campaign. He said Ireland did precisely this on a constitutional question its politicians had dodged for thirty years and it worked.

(8) Nadia said he'd just conceded her point: the thing worth preserving is deliberation among informed people with time, and he's now getting it from forty people rather than from two hundred thousand at a ballot box, so the referendum has become a rubber stamp and the assembly is the democracy. She added that the assembly isn't neutral either — the Irish one lost seventeen members before its first working session and eleven more before the final abortion weekend, and seven replacements had to be pulled when it emerged they'd been recruited through a polling firm employee's contacts rather than at random — so random selection is only as good as the subset who bother to say yes.

(9) Gerald said fine, then they agree: drop the referendum, seat the assembly. (10) Nadia said no, because he'd still be handing the assembly's text to a campaign he can't control, and that he had improved the question and left the answer to the money.
