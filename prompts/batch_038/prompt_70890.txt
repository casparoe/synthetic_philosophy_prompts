For the past four years I have been the sole person at a mid-sized logistics firm who decides which outside training modules we buy and put in front of roughly four thousand employees, including every people manager. I have no scientific training of any kind — a business degree and a habit of reading widely on my own time — but the job has made me an amateur student of one problem in particular. Almost every vendor deck that crosses my desk now grounds itself in evolutionary psychology. A negotiation course explains its method by appeal to what our ancestors supposedly did in small bands. A resilience module claims a hard-wired status instinct. A teamwork product asserts that hierarchy is a recent and unnatural imposition on a species built for flat, egalitarian sharing. Sometimes I can feel that a claim is empty — that any outcome could be accommodated by the story the vendor tells — and sometimes I can feel that it is solid, but in neither case can I say why in terms that would survive a challenge from the vendor's salesperson, my own director, or a manager who liked the module.

What I currently do is a private three-question gut check, and I know it is indefensible. I need something better.

Here is what I am asking for: a written triage procedure I can run on a vendor's evidence package, in about forty-five minutes, during or immediately after a sales call, with no scientist available and no ability to commission new analysis. We see fifteen to twenty packages a quarter. Our general counsel and my director only look at the items I flag, so the procedure has to do the flagging. The output has to be a short filled-in form plus a one-paragraph recommendation, because that is what gets attached to the procurement memo, and it has to be simple enough that a colleague with less patience than mine could run it and reach the same verdict I would.

Please build it so that it is grounded in what the methodological critics of this style of explanation have actually established, not in a general mood of skepticism. That means I want the procedure to carry the real distinctions: between a trait that was shaped by selection for its current role, one that was shaped for something else and now does this, one that is an incidental side effect of something else entirely, and one that is simply an inherited leftover with no story at all; between an account that is merely consistent with the data and one that would have been risky to state in advance; between evidence that a trait is heritable and evidence that it is adaptive; between a claim about why something exists and a claim about what an employer ought to do about it. For each distinction, tell me plainly what question to ask the vendor, what answer counts as sufficient, what answer counts as a refusal, and which distinctions you have deliberately dropped for the sake of usability — and whether dropping them bends the procedure systematically in one direction. If the finished procedure has a thumb on the scale against evolutionary explanations, I want that said out loud, together with what it would cost us and what evidence should make me revise the default.

Then run it, in full and in writing, on three packages:

First, a teamwork module built on the claim that humans evolved an intense aversion to ranked authority and that hierarchy is a recent, unnatural distortion, supported in the deck by selected anecdotes about foraging peoples, a clip from a primate documentary, and two employee engagement surveys from our own industry. I like this framing and I want to buy it.

Second, a fear-management module claiming that people acquire and retain fears of snakes, spiders, and heights faster and more stubbornly than fears of cars, sockets, or guns, attributed to evolved preparedness, supported by conditioning studies run in several unrelated countries, attention and infant-looking studies, and comparative data from non-human primates. I find this one distasteful, because I am sure a manager will read it as permission to write off an anxious employee as unfixable.

Third, a sales-resilience module claiming that status-seeking is a fixed, innate drive, supported by a bestselling trade book, one twin study reporting a heritability estimate, and a recommendation that we therefore rank all employees publicly on a live leaderboard.

For each, show the form being filled in and give the verdict, including the two cases where the procedure should hand the remaining question to a person because what is left is about harm, fairness, or how the content will be used rather than about evidence — and say exactly what that person needs to be told.

Finally, state what this procedure cannot do. I assume it cannot assess whether a cited study was well run, whether an effect is large enough to matter, or whether the vendor has quietly cited the three favourable papers out of thirty. Tell me where those limits bite, what cheap addition would cover the worst of them, and what kinds of claim I should stop trying to run through it altogether.
