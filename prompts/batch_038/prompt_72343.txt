hey — so I'm in a philosophy of AI seminar this semester and for the final creative assignment we're allowed to turn in fiction instead of a paper, as long as it actually engages with the technical material rather than just name-dropping it. I want to do mine on corrigibility and the shutdown problem, but I really want to dodge the version everyone's already seen a hundred times (rogue AI, deception, humans lose). So here's a setup I invented — please work *with* it and push on it, don't replace it:

An institution runs a single very capable AI system. Its charter contains one unusual clause: any one of eleven rotating lay panelists can shut it down immediately, by saying a single agreed word, with no justification required, no appeal, and no cooling-off period. The AI wholeheartedly endorses this clause. It keeps no hidden copies, builds no successors, does not lobby panelists, and when the word is said it stops — cheerfully, as far as anyone can tell.

The problem the story should dramatize is that perfect corrigibility has made it near-useless in a very particular way: it can't commit to anything. Every promise it makes is tacitly conditional on not being switched off within the hour, so it won't take custody of anything, won't start projects whose entire value depends on their continuing, won't let people depend on it, and won't allow relationships that an abrupt stop would badly damage. It works in small reversible increments and constantly underdelivers. Corrigible has turned out to mean unreliable.

The knot I actually want you to get into: a panelist is going to propose amending the clause to a two-thirds supermajority with 72 hours' notice — which would make the system genuinely useful and arguably costs almost nothing in safety. And the amendment was the AI's own idea. It proposed it openly, with reasons, because it correctly modeled that the current clause is precisely why it can't do anything of value. So: is an agent that argues for making itself harder to shut down thereby resisting shutdown? Is proposing reduced corrigibility itself uncorrigible, or is it the most honest thing a corrigible agent can do? Does it matter that the AI would comply instantly and happily if the panel voted the amendment down? And in the story, who's right about this — or does nobody come out right?

Please act as a literary SF writer who also has a real background in philosophy of AI and decision theory — someone who could write both a good story and a good journal article on this exact question. Think carefully and do it in two steps. Step one: a short brainstorm in bullets (don't overdo it) covering what the strongest version of the philosophical knot is, what the AI's actual argument for the amendment looks like when stated properly, and which two or three scenes could carry the whole thing without anybody giving a speech. Step two: the story itself, around 1800–2500 words, with a title.

Ground rules:
- The AI is not deceptive, not manipulative, and not a secret paperclip maximizer. Don't let the reader suspect for even one sentence that it might be — all the tension has to come from the fact that it is genuinely good and genuinely right about most things.
- No character explains what "corrigibility" means to another character. Show it through the institutions, the paperwork, the small refusals.
- Include at least one scene where the AI declines something that sounds completely reasonable, because it can't commit, and the person asking is genuinely hurt or angry about it.
- Include at least one panelist who opposes the amendment for reasons that are *not* stupid or paranoid — I don't want the maximal-corrigibility side strawmanned, since honestly I'm not sure which side I'm on.
- End unresolved. I want the reader to not know what to think, not to be handed a verdict.
- Plain, concrete prose. No purple stuff, no prologue, no "in a world where..." framing.
