My partner's book group roped me into leading next month's session — twelve people, mostly fifties and sixties, zero classics background, and we meet in the back room of a wine bar, which given what I've been assigned is either perfect or a catastrophe waiting to happen. My slot is the stretch of Plato's Laws where the Athenian stranger starts talking about deliberately getting citizens drunk. Not the Republic, not the Symposium party scene — the bit in the first book where the three old men are walking to the cave of Zeus and the Athenian floats the idea that wine should be treated like a drug: if there were a potion that produced fear, he says, a lawgiver could hand it out in small doses and see who could hold steady, and since wine does roughly the opposite, it's a cheap and available version of the same test. Then the whole business about who's allowed to run a drinking party and why the person in charge has to be the sober one and not the youngest, the dig at lawgivers who just ban the temptation without training anyone to meet it, and the late reversal where wine stops being a hazard for the young and turns into a gift for the old.

Twenty five minutes of me talking, then discussion for as long as it holds.

Don't explain any of this to me. I've read it, I know the moves, what I need is a plan for explaining it to *them*. So:

- Order. What has to be in place before what, and why. My instinct is to open with the fear-drug analogy because it's the most vivid thing in the passage, but I suspect that's exactly wrong — until they've got that for this guy virtue is a trained disposition and not a list of prohibitions, the wine test reads as a hazing prank or a state-run bender. Tell me what the actual dependency chain is and where the analogy belongs in it.
- The one case that carries the whole thing. The wine bar is sitting right there and I'm very tempted to point at it, but I worry that turns the session into "so Plato would have approved of happy hour" and I lose the supervised-exposure structure completely. Say what the carrying example has to have and what it absolutely cannot have, and whether a modern one or something from inside the text's own world works better for a room that's holding glasses.
- The misconceptions. The ones they'll walk in with, the ones they'll form mid-explanation, and the exact point in the sequence where each gets headed off. I can already hear some of them: the Greeks were just drunk constantly and he's rationalizing it; this is obviously totalitarian, the state dosing its own citizens; he doesn't mean it, it's ironic, it's a thought experiment; and it's about the guardians again, isn't it. The one I'm actually dreading is somebody whose brother drank himself to death saying, quiet, "so the government should be handing out alcohol as a character test." I need to know whether that has to be defused *before* wine gets mentioned at all, and how you defuse it without lying about how the passage reads.
- What I refuse to cover at this level, said out loud so it doesn't look like I lost the thread. I'm guessing the Athenian-versus-Spartan-lawgiver argument that sets all this up, and anything downstream about the night council — but tell me if one of those is actually load-bearing for the wine bit and I can't skip it.
- One question to ask at the end. Not "so what did we learn," and not a comprehension check on the text — a new case they haven't seen that comes apart one way if they've absorbed the trained-disposition-plus-supervised-exposure structure and the other way if they've only registered that Plato said something shocking about booze. Tell me what each answer would diagnose.

No readings, no bibliography, no suggested translations — I am not sending twelve people in a wine bar home with homework.
