Been arguing with a friend since dinner about whether the butterfly effect has any business in historical explanation — she thinks it dissolves counterfactual history, I think shes using "chaos" as a vibe word — and now I've fogged myself up right before a grading session, so I want a read on something else. I TA a philosophy of history seminar. The short-answer question on the midterm was:

> Two claims get run together all the time: (i) small events can have enormous historical consequences — a driver's wrong turn, a missing horseshoe nail; (ii) because history works like that, we can't make reliable counterfactual claims about it ("would there have been a war anyway?"). Say what (i) has to be a claim about, what (ii) has to be a claim about, and whether (i) actually supports (ii).

Here's the answer I'm on the fence about:

> (i) is a claim about counterfactual dependence, not about probabilities: the war would not have happened if the car hadn't taken that turn. (ii) is a different kind of claim, about what we can know. Systems like this are chaotic in the technical sense — deterministic, but with sensitive dependence on initial conditions, so tiny differences get amplified exponentially and long-run prediction breaks down. History plausibly has that structure, since there's feedback everywhere: mobilization timetables, alliance commitments that ratchet, newspapers responding to each other. So (ii) follows from (i) fairly directly.
>
> The consequence people miss is that a chaotic history is also an overdetermined one. There were so many independent pressure points in 1914 — nationalism, the arms races, the alliance system — that the war was coming regardless, which means an event like the wrong turn wasn't really a cause of it at all. So the great-man picture is wrong twice over: individuals can't move outcomes much, and even if they could, we'd never be able to say what would have happened otherwise, so counterfactuals about the war are neither true nor false. The most we can do is describe the actual sequence. What chaos does not imply, though, is that everything is chaotic — you need amplification and feedback, and there's no reason to think slow institutional change has that structure.
