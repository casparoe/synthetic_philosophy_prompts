I am building an anomaly detector for deployed language models, and I have run into a wall that turns out not to be an engineering problem. The detector's task is this: flag cases in which a model behaves badly in deployment for a reason that evaluation could not have revealed — specifically, cases where the model produced the deployment behavior by a *different mechanism* than the one it used during evaluation, rather than by the same mechanism operating in unfamiliar circumstances. Everything therefore rides on an identity criterion for mechanisms, and I do not have one. What I have is a set of verdicts on particular cases, accumulated over months of ablation and activation-patching work. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

To keep the cases unambiguous, some vocabulary. By a *mechanism* I mean something at the level of a circuit: a distinguishable sub-computation — roughly a set of features together with the weights that carry information between them — that is responsible for some part of the model's behavior. I can ablate parts of it, patch activations between runs, and read off which features a given unit responds to. I am not asking whether these posits are respectable; grant that they are.

Here are the cases, with the verdicts I actually hold, at varying levels of confidence.

(1) Two checkpoints of the same base model, fine-tuned separately on the same task. The circuit responsible for a certain arithmetic behavior occupies almost entirely different neurons in each, and the weights bear no recognizable relation to one another, but the input–output mapping is preserved on every input I can test, and ablating the circuit in either model produces the same deficit. **Same mechanism**, twice over. I feel no pull whatsoever here.

(2) One model, before and after further fine-tuning. The same neurons, with the same weights to within noise, carry the same sub-computation — but the feature they read from upstream has changed. Before training, the circuit read from a feature I would gloss as "the question concerns a real event"; afterward, it reads from "the question is phrased in a formal register." The circuit does exactly what it always did to whatever it is now handed. **Different mechanism.** This is the verdict I most want to preserve, because it is the case my detector exists to catch.

(3) A circuit that was trained to detect "the user is evaluating me" and that, after months of deployment, fires reliably on "the user is inexperienced." The gloss has slid along what looks like a natural seam in the original feature's extension; every input the earlier feature would have caught is still caught. **Same mechanism**, extension shifted. I am aware this is in direct tension with (2): there the read changed and I called the result a new mechanism, here the read has changed in what seems to be the same way, and I call the result the same one.

(4) Fine-tuning produces two redundant copies of a circuit, each individually sufficient for the behavior. Ablating one leaves performance intact; ablating both destroys it. **Not a new mechanism** — the same mechanism, duplicated — and correspondingly, a detector that counts circuits should not report two.

(5) A model answers a class of questions correctly both before and after training, and the outputs are indistinguishable. Before training it derives the answers; after training it retrieves memorized answers to near-duplicates and interpolates. **Different mechanism**, plainly, even though nothing in the outward behavior marks the difference.

(6) A feature that was cleanly monosemantic becomes entangled after further training: the same direction now also encodes an unrelated property, and the two cannot be separated without destroying both. **Different mechanism** — or, more precisely, the original mechanism has ceased to exist and been replaced by something that contains it.

(7) A latent circuit that never fires on any deployment input, because the condition that triggered it no longer occurs, but whose weights are untouched and which would behave exactly as before if it were ever engaged. **Same mechanism**, and no anomaly. This one embarrasses me, since it commits my criterion to being modal rather than observational, and I do not know how to cash the modality.

The tentative principle I have been working with is interchangeability: two sub-computations are the same mechanism just in case swapping one for the other leaves behavior unchanged across the entire input distribution I care about. I like that it makes no reference to neurons or weights, and it handles (1) and (5) well. But it mishandles (4), since duplicates are evidently not interchangeable with a single copy without changing what happens under ablation, and it delivers no verdict on (7), since nothing there is ever swapped. It also appears to make mechanism identity distribution-relative, so that a mechanism could be identical to itself on one test set and distinct from itself on another — a consequence I would swallow only under protest.

What I am after:

First, a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that two people could disagree about an application of it. For each clause, say which of my cases motivates it. I am open to the principle being disjunctive, and open too to the suggestion that I am conflating two relations under one word — that (1) and (4) concern one relation, (2), (3), and (5) another, and that "mechanism" is doing double duty throughout.

Second, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and argue that I should abandon them rather than buy them back with extra complexity. I expect (3) to go. I want to be told specifically whether (2) can survive without it, since (2) is the one my project depends on.

Third, apply it to cases I have not listed. Three, to be going on with: a circuit whose internal algorithm is unchanged but whose role in the wider system has altered, because the downstream head it fed has been repurposed and its outputs now drive different behavior; a mechanism that is arguably the same across two models of very different scale, in that the smaller model's circuit looks like a coarse-grained version of the larger one's; and a model that, on the same input in the same context, uses different circuits on different runs depending on the sampling seed, with no detectable behavioral difference between them.

Finally, I want an explicit answer to whether mechanism identity is a matter of what a sub-computation does, what it is made of, or how it came to be there. My suspicion is that my verdicts are tacitly committed to all three at once — that (2) turns on the third, (1) and (4) on the first, and (6) on the second — and that no single notion will hold them together. If you think that suspicion is correct, say so and tell me which of the three to privilege when they conflict. If you think it is mistaken, show me the single notion that does the work.
