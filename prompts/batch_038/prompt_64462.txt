You're a professional philosopher who works on value theory and practical reasoning — ideally the kind a novelist would find deeply annoying at a dinner party. So. I'm deep in a novel and I've hit a wall that is secretly a philosophy problem wearing a craft problem's clothes. The book's engine is that my protagonist keeps landing in choices where it feels like there is no fact of the matter about which option is better — not that she's ignorant, not that it's close, but that "better" runs out. I want to know whether that feeling tracks one thing or two or three. To find out I've been logging my own gut verdicts, some hers, some mine, all of them real to me.

The cases:

1. Two grad programs, one in philosophy, one in composition. Neither better overall. Now sweeten the philosophy one with a bigger stipend. Still neither better. My verdict: HARD, and hard in the interesting sense.
2. Two identical apartments except one costs 3% less. One is better. She might agonise for a week, but that's fear, not hardness. Verdict: not hard, just close.
3. Two apartments again, one much better light, one much better commute. Give the light one a dishwasher. Still doesn't tip. Verdict: HARD, same species as 1.
4. Save your own kid, or two strangers' kids. Horrendous, but not hard — she'd know what she was doing before she finished the sentence. Verdict: awful, not hard.
5. Marry A or marry B, both wonderful, no ranking available. But here's the thing: the hardness evaporates the morning after the wedding and never comes back, not once, for forty years. Verdict: I want to say this one WASN'T really hard, it was just scary. This is the case that breaks my working theory.
6. My own, not hers. At 27 I turned down a job on another continent for a person. I genuinely could not rank them, then or now. But there has been zero leak in fifteen years — never bought the little compensatory thing, never revisited it, never had the drink where I say "sometimes I wonder." By my own rule that should mean it wasn't hard. It was the hardest week of my life.

Three bits of jargon I made up for my own notes, because I got tired of long phrases. Please use them back at me if they're useful and tell me if they're smuggling something in.

- Leak: after the choice, the person keeps making small compensatory gestures toward the forgone option. Buys the violin anyway. Names the dog after the road not taken.
- The coin-flip test: you flip for it. Relief at the outcome means it wasn't hard. Wanting two out of three means it was.
- Retroactive reordering: years later, sincerely and not as self-comfort, saying "obviously that one was better."

What I want:

Is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts, or does it take a disjunction? State it precisely enough that I could hand it to a smart reader and get the same calls out of it.

Are leak, the coin-flip test, and retroactive reordering part of the analysis or downstream symptoms of it? My hunch is that leak is the real marker and the coin-flip thing is theatre, but I can't defend that and case 6 is exactly what's strangling it.

Run my list past the standard machinery and tell me where each one lands: Raz's test for incommensurability, the small improvement argument, Chang's idea that some options are on a par rather than incomparable, Berlin's pluralism about values, and the transformative-experience stuff about choices where you can't know what it'll be like to be the person who chose. Which of my six does each handle cleanly, and on which does each break? I especially want to know whether any of them can separate 1 from 5 without also separating 1 from 6, because that's the exact distinction my book needs and I don't think it's a distinction anyone has a name for.

Then apply whatever principle you land on to three cases I have no verdicts for and tell me what it says: a concert pianist choosing between performing and teaching; someone deciding whether to undergo a treatment they've been warned will change their personality; and a parent with one school place and two children who'd each benefit exactly as much.

Finally, be honest about the cost. Which of my verdicts can the principle not save — say 5 or 6 or both — and which should I drop, the verdict or the principle? If the answer is that I need two notions where I wanted one, tell me what the seam between them is and what a novel loses by not being able to name it.
