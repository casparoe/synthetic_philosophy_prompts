He asks us to notice that when we say five deaths are worse than one, we talk as though there were some single subject who could suffer the sum of those losses — but there is no such subject. Each death is a catastrophe for the person who dies and for those who love him, and that is all it is; five catastrophes do not add up to a larger catastrophe befalling someone, because there is no someone. So there is nothing on the scale to weigh, and Taurek concludes that we should be indifferent between saving the one and saving the five: the numbers play no role at all. He does allow aggregation inside a single life — I can weigh my own future losses against my own present ones, because there is a person who will bear them all — which is exactly why he thinks aggregation across lives fails. From this he draws two further lessons. First, that the utilitarian project of maximizing sums has nothing to maximize, so Taurek takes himself to have refuted it rather than merely to have disagreed with it, and to have shown that moral theory must be rebuilt from the ground up. Second, that partiality is a permission rather than a defect: you may save your friend rather than five strangers, because friendship entitles you to treat your friend's death as the greater loss. Taurek's challenge dominated the literature for a decade or two, but the standard reply eventually dissolved it — each of the five has a claim exactly as strong as the one's, and claims of equal strength can be counted without being summed — so few philosophers now defend him, and the paper survives mainly as a dialectical stepping stone."
