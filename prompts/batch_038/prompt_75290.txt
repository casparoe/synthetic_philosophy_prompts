I cover the sciences and the mind for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I'm giving the closing talk at a small interdisciplinary workshop on perception. The room will be about forty people: mostly experimental psychologists who run precisely the kind of studies my column takes aim at — the ones in which effort, fitness, fear, or motivation appear to change how big, steep, near, or fast something looks; two or three philosophers of perception who hold a broadly modularist line descended from Fodor and Pylyshyn; a couple of metascience people whose entire interest is preregistration, statistical power, and declining effect sizes; and several working journalists who wrote the breezy popular versions of these findings that I criticize by name.

The thesis I'm defending is one I've come to call the residue hypothesis: cognitive penetration is not a phenomenon that experiments discover or refute, but what is left over when a study fails to control for report criteria. My argument runs in three steps. First, every purported penetration effect has a rival account in terms of attention, expectation-shaped response bias, or the subject's guess about what the experimenter wants. Second, as those controls sharpen, effect sizes shrink toward zero, and the cases that survive are ones where the manipulation plausibly altered the stimulus or the viewing conditions themselves. Third, the question whether cognition penetrates perception is therefore a measurement question — about where a perceptual report stops being a readout of the visual system and starts being an inference about the world — and the field has been answering it by argument rather than by design.

Give me the prep sheet, not a defense. I want the eight or so objections this particular room will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it — a metascientist will not put it as a modularist philosopher would — with a two-minute reply, a note on where that reply is softest, and what I should concede up front before it is conceded for me. Tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to be worth giving, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks, along with my honest answer to it. Take a definite position throughout: if you judge one of these objections fatal to the residue hypothesis, say so plainly and say what I should do about it, rather than setting out the considerations and leaving me to weigh them.
