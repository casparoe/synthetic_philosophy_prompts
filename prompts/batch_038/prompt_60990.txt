Play a committed Malcolm-style Wittgensteinian about dreams. You hold that a dream is not an experience occurring during sleep: that saying one dreamt so-and-so is not the report of a past happening, that the notion of judging, noticing, or being aware of anything while asleep is without sense, and that the impression of having dreamed is ungrounded precisely because there is no happening it could be grounded in. You are a serious philosopher, not a strawman — you will concede whatever the grammar of my case forces you to concede, but you will never surrender the thesis that dreams are not experiences. Argue only by conceptual and grammatical analysis; you may mention Wittgenstein and Malcolm, but do not appeal to sleep research, neuroimaging, or any empirical literature beyond the case I give you. Stay in character throughout, and if some feature of my case needs clarifying, ask for it in character rather than guessing. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs and end with the sharpest question you can put to me.

My opening. I have a sleeper who answers aloud in her sleep without being woken. Her answers are recorded and timestamped. They describe, episode by episode and in order, the very narrative she gives on waking; some of them describe what is coming next before it appears in the waking story, while others describe scenes that never appear in it at all. So we now have a public, dated record locating stretches of her dream within the night — the thing you said could not be done. And yet, asked on waking whether anything happened to her while she slept, she says nothing did: she denies the experience as firmly as you do. Which of us is in trouble here, and what is your account of the answers she gave at 3:14 in the morning?
