I chair the five-member scientific advisory panel of a state environmental agency. Our charter requires us to publish a consensus probability that a proposed drinking-water standard is protective, and the statute obliges the agency to act whenever that figure reaches 0.70. On the contaminant now before us, our two most senior members — a toxicologist and an epidemiologist with comparable records, no financial stakes, and access to the same studies — put that probability at 0.45 and 0.85. Each weights the animal and human evidence differently; both concede the other's weighting is respectable, and neither claims better evidence or better calibration on this class of question. Averaging gives 0.65, which settles the case for the low end without anybody actually believing it; the alternative is publishing no figure, which under the charter defaults to inaction. The other three members have said they will go along with whatever procedure I propose.

Two further complications. The same two members disagree about what a panel should do when peers disagree in this way, and they regard each other as equally competent judges of that question, so a rule chosen at the meta level inherits the same standoff. And whatever we adopt will be published in advance to future panelists and to the firms that supply expert testimony, so a rule that splits positions may simply reward opening at an extreme.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, roughly 800 words: recommend a procedure for this case and a standing rule going forward, say why the rule is more than a device for entrenching whoever happens to hold the median view, and state the strongest objection from the losing side in terms its own proponent would recognize as fair. Treat the two members' peerhood as given — a recommendation that quietly demotes one of them is what I want argued against, not assumed.
