I'm prepping a talk that stress-tests Strawson's reactive-attitudes framework, and the move I want to make is to feed his own taxonomy a case it was never built for. Help me figure out what he'd say.

Here's the track record I've been working from, my own summary of how he rules on the standard cases in the essay (paraphrased, don't try to match his wording):

- The guy who hurt you but didn't mean to — accident, or he was deceived about the relevant facts. Resentment is out of place, but he hasn't left the moral community at all; he's still a normal participant and still acknowledges the demand for reasonable goodwill.
- Coercion, "I couldn't help it in that situation." Same bucket as above.
- The very young child, the severely psychotic patient, the person under extraordinary strain. Different bucket: here we don't just excuse the act, we stop regarding him as a normally apt target of the reactive attitudes at all, and we slide toward treating him as something to be handled, cured, trained, managed.
- Someone who does wrong and feels genuine guilt and makes amends. Guilt and remorse are appropriate in him even though resentment in me would be misplaced in the accident case.
- Determinism being true, if it is, does not license the objective attitude across the board — we couldn't take it up toward everyone even if we chose to, and the choice isn't the kind of thing a metaphysical thesis can settle.
- The colleague I've deliberately decided to stop engaging with and just manage. That's the objective attitude, adopted locally and on purpose, and Strawson seems fine with it.

Now the new case. Call him Ray. Ray is a perfectly ordinary, competent adult — not a child, not psychotic, not under unusual strain, and he'd fail none of the tests for normal agency. At work he is flatly unresponsive to moral appeal: he has no indignation on anyone's behalf, feels nothing like guilt when he exploits a coworker, and would keep doing it if the incentives changed. At home it's completely different. He has real, vivid resentment when his daughter is treated badly by someone else, he's genuinely and specifically grateful to his wife, he feels shame and apologizes and does the work of repair. And for distant strangers he reports nothing at all — no more pull than for characters in a novel.

So: what's Strawson's verdict on Ray? Specifically —

1. Is Ray a proper target of resentment from the coworker he used? From his wife? From his daughter? From someone in another country he's never met and who's never met him? I want the answers separately, because I suspect they won't line up.
2. What rule is he actually running that generates his verdicts on the cases above? I want at least three or four candidate rules that all fit that track record, then the divergence: show me where they come apart on Ray. Obvious candidates I can think of are something like "capacity for reactive attitudes in general" versus "capacity indexed to the relationship in question," plus something keyed to whether the person recognizes the demand for goodwill at all, plus something keyed to abnormality as Strawson uses it. There are probably others I'm missing — tell me about them.
3. Give me your best guess, with reasons, and be honest about which of the candidate rules is actually supported by what he says versus which one I'd be smuggling in to make the framework look tidier than it is.
4. Separate two things I've been conflating: whether resentment toward Ray is *appropriate*, versus whether it's *non-optional* for us. Does Strawson's own insistence that we can't just switch off the reactive attitudes change the answer here, and does it change it differently for the coworker than for the stranger abroad?
5. Tell me one further case — a variant on Ray, small change — that would discriminate between your top two candidate rules better than anything in my list does.

No citations and no bibliography, I'll add those myself; I just need the reasoning. Don't strain to make Strawson come out consistent, and if his framework genuinely runs out of road on this case, say so and say exactly where.
