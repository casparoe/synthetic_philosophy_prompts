I'm about halfway through a novel and there's a character I've gotten stuck on — a woman in her late sixties who used to run a psychiatric ward, then quit medicine altogether under circumstances the book doesn't explain, and has ended up writing the weekly advice column for a mid-sized regional paper. Nobody at the paper has quite noticed that she no longer believes in the thing they think she's an expert in. The chapter I can't get moving is basically the column itself: I want a reader to come away with her entire worldview from the letters and her answers, with no narration doing any explaining. So — write me the column as it would actually be printed, masthead and all, somewhere around 700 to 800 words.

Her position is a strict Szasz-style one, but I don't want her name-checking him or any book or article, because that's not how people like her talk in print; the arguments should carry it on their own. Concretely, she holds that illness means something gone wrong in a body that can be found, so without a lesion there is no disease, only conduct; that what gets called symptoms are problems in living, and the diagnostic labels are moral judgments wearing a lab coat; that being unbearable, confused, or terrifying doesn't compromise anyone's capacity to choose, so nobody gets excused and nobody gets locked up for the convenience of the people around them; that a physician who commits you against your will has become a jailer with a better vocabulary; and that the whole apparatus of mental health law is a secular church with the state holding the keys. She can't slip on any of it, not for one letter, and she can't soften it into well, of course you should also see someone about it — that's the constraint that makes the piece work, because she still has to give these people an answer using only what her view lets her say.

Three letters I want in there. One from a mother whose adult son has stopped eating, answers people who aren't in the room, and has been locked in his bedroom since March; she wants to know whether to have him taken somewhere, and the answer my columnist gives should be the best thing in the piece and the worst thing in the piece at the same time, without either register being flagged. One from a woman whose husband gambles, who is fishing for the word disease so she can stop being furious with him, and watch what happens when she's handed responsibility instead — she should take the answer considerably further than my columnist intended. And one short, sharp letter from a reader who has noticed that the hospital calls his disagreement a symptom and the columnist calls his diagnosis a metaphor, and asks how he is supposed to tell which of the two is just relabeling his refusal; she answers that one too, and her answer has to be perfectly consistent and completely unsatisfying. Keep her deadpan and utterly sincere throughout — the humor comes from the doctrine being applied honestly, not from her being dim, and a psychiatrist reading it should wince at how much of it is right. Throw in the genre furniture as well: a mailbag line at the end, a small-print ad for her own book at the foot of the page, and an editor's note explaining that the Dr. before her name stays whether she likes it or not, which I think is a decent little joke in itself since she's written in about it four times. Don't tell the reader what to think about any of it, and don't let her break character even for a sentence.
