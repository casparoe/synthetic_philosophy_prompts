I am a doctoral candidate in conservation ecology, and I hold the graduate-student seat on my county's vector control advisory committee. At next month's meeting we vote on a petition from an animal-advocacy organization asking the district to (i) end all truck-mounted ultra-low-volume adulticiding and (ii) shift entirely to larviciding with *Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis*, on the grounds that mosquito control is a large-scale, publicly funded source of invertebrate suffering.

The petition's appendix attempts to quantify this. It estimates "insect-days of net negative experience" per treated hectare per season from three assumed parameters: a probability that adult mosquitoes and non-target Diptera are sentient, a hedonic weight per insect-day of larval life in crowded, food-limited habitats, and a fraction of naturally occurring deaths that are slow rather than rapid. Under the petition's central values, larviciding scores roughly welfare-neutral while adulticiding scores catastrophic; under its pessimistic values the magnitudes shrink by two orders of magnitude but the ranking between the two options does not change anywhere in the range they test.

The district's staff entomologist objects on three grounds, which I take seriously. First, our West Nile surveillance data show adult populations exceeding action thresholds at some treated sites, so ending adulticiding carries an expected human-health cost that the petition never prices. Second, Bti may not be the humane alternative the petition assumes: it kills larvae by gut disruption over a day or more after they stop feeding, whereas pyrethroid knockdown is close to instantaneous, and Bti also affects non-target midges in the wetlands we treat. Third, if the larval habitats we manage are already food-limited, larval survival is density-capped, so larviciding may not reduce total insect-days much at all — it may simply change which individuals die and how.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee in advance of the vote. It should recommend a concrete option — approve the petition, reject it, or approve it with specific modifications and a stated time frame — and it should separately tell the committee whether the petition's metric is fit to enter our decision procedure at all: adopt it, adopt a stripped-down version, or set it aside, with criteria a future board could actually apply to the next such petition. I want the strongest consideration on the losing side engaged rather than waved off. My own working suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than assume, is that the metric's outputs are fixed almost entirely by parameters nobody can estimate; but that rejecting it outright would leave the committee with no articulated way of weighing invertebrate suffering, which is in practice what we have done for a decade.
