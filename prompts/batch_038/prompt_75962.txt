You are a philosopher of mind who follows the experimental literature on perception closely, and you are setting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on modularity and the cognitive penetrability of perception. I want one essay question, answerable in about fifty minutes, that cannot be handled by reciting Fodor's characterisation of informational encapsulation or by listing the familiar pro- and con-examples.

Build the question around a stipulated study that you yourself write. Present it explicitly as a constructed vignette rather than a real published experiment, so that students are not rewarded for recognising it. In the vignette, participants view a briefly presented ambiguous figure and are told beforehand that it depicts either a sailboat or a church steeple; they then adjust a colour patch to match a specified region of the figure, and the two groups' settings differ reliably. State that the experimenters have already run the obvious controls: a precueing condition that equalises spatial attention across groups, a demand-characteristics check combining a cover task with post-experimental interview, and a condition in which the label is delivered only after the figure has been masked.

The question should require students to settle two things: first, whether those controls suffice to establish that cognition penetrated perception rather than that cognition changed what was attended to, retained in memory, or reported; second, whether the verdict would differ if the same effect emerged only after weeks of training rather than from a single label, and what that difference would imply both for whether the visual system is modular and for whether perception can still function as an independent check on a subject's beliefs.

Before writing the question, spend a short paragraph brainstorming two or three candidate cruxes and say which you chose and why. Then supply the question itself with its vignette; a model answer of roughly 650 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — including at least two moves that a well-informed student who has merely memorised the terminology would omit — together with three characteristic errors and the marks each should cost.

Think carefully about the difference between a synchronic top-down effect on perceptual processing and a diachronic change to the encapsulated system itself; that is where the question ought to bite.

End your response with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing one sentence that names the distinction on which the question is engineered to turn.
