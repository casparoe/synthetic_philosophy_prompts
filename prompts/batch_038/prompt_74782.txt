I have been working through the disagreement inside accuracy-first epistemology about whether the accuracy measure that drives the whole apparatus must be immodest, and I cannot see a way through it. Let me set out how I understand the two positions, in case my framing is already distorted.

The framework: epistemic value is measured by a proper scoring rule, and epistemic norms are supposed to be derived by showing that an agent who violates them is accuracy-dominated, or fails to maximize expected accuracy, relative to that rule. Everything therefore turns on the rule itself. Call a measure immodest, or self-certifying, when on any agent's own assessment her current credences score strictly better than any alternative she could adopt in their place.

The immodestists argue that a modest measure is normatively unusable. If you thought some rival credence function would do better than your own, you would be committed to planning to abandon your own view in favor of something you expect to be worse, which looks like a species of self-undermining akrasia. And the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalizing on new evidence goes through only under immodest measures, so abandoning immodesty means abandoning the diachronic results the framework was built to deliver.

The modestists reply that immodesty buys those results too dearly. It makes entrenched disagreement permanently rational, it licenses a dogmatism that looks plainly wrong for agents who know their own limitations — someone who knows she is poor at estimating a certain quantity should not certify her own estimates as the best available — and, which seems to me the most damaging charge, it makes the selection of the scoring rule look like reverse engineering: the desiderata are chosen so that the intended norms fall out, and the argument for conditionalization then inherits its force from a stipulation rather than earning it.

There is a further strand that strikes me as the same dispute wearing different clothes. It concerns whether to evaluate a policy for responding to evidence from the perspective of the agent who currently holds it, or whether to evaluate each credence-plus-evidence pair from no particular perspective at all. The second option appears to imply that an agent who expects herself to mishandle a piece of evidence should avoid gathering it, which is an unwelcome consequence; the first option appears to presuppose the immodesty it was meant to establish.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as something I am confident of: the two sides are not disagreeing about a single question. The immodestist is asking what an agent reasoning from inside her own perspective should expect of herself and plan to do; the modestist is asking what an external evaluator, with no privileged access to any one agent's credences, should count as accurate and should recommend. If one technical term is being made to play both roles — a scoring device for dominance arguments and a constraint on legitimate self-appraisal — then the impasse may be a symptom of a single word doing two jobs that need not coincide.

Please tell me whether that diagnosis holds up. Specifically:

(a) Is this a substantive disagreement with a determinate answer, in which case one side is simply wrong about which measure is correct? Or is it a disagreement about formal facts that a theorem could settle — for instance, whether the desiderata each side insists on are jointly satisfiable at all? Or a disagreement about standards of justification, concerning whether a stipulated desideratum can bear normative weight? Or a disagreement about what the term rational means, procedural coherence against accuracy-maximization? Or two groups answering different questions under one heading?

(b) Test my hypothesis against what each side actually says. If the two roles really are separable, why has nobody in the debate framed it that way — and is there something structural in the framework that makes them hard to separate, such as the impossibility of running a dominance argument without adopting some perspective or other?

(c) Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A new impossibility or representation result, a distinction between two things currently conflated, a re-specification of the question, or an empirical constraint drawn from what we know about how reliably people assess their own judgment? Or should we expect no progress whatsoever, and if so, what makes this dispute recalcitrant in a way that, say, the debate over coherentism and foundationalism is not?

I am after a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, argued for on the basis of the content of the positions themselves. I do not want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations to particular papers, and you should not guess at attributions. Please write in American English.
