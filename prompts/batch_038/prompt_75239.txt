I help run a small consciousness workshop, and two of our regulars have finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration after three years of talking past each other. They want the document they could both put their names to, and I'd rather you did the first pass than me.

The two camps, as they've stated them to me:

**Camp A** holds that phenomenal consciousness is a user illusion. The brain runs a drastically simplified self-model, and introspection is that model reading its own dashboard. On this view, talk about "what it's like" to see red is a story the system tells itself about states that were never the sort of thing that could be peered at in the first place — there's no rich inner show that the story is a bad report *of*.

**Camp B** holds that this is incoherent, or at least self-undermining. An illusion has to be an illusion *for* someone. If it seems to me that there is a rich inner show, then something is doing the seeming, and describing the machinery that produces the seeming doesn't make the seeming disappear. B will happily grant that the brain constructs, misrepresents, and confabulates — they just deny that construction is all there is.

Both say they'll sign anything true, so don't soften either position into something its holder would disown. Each disagreement has to be worded the way that side would word it if they were writing the doc themselves.

What I need:

1. **A numbered list of claims both can sign.** Anti-vacuity rule: every entry must be something some third party could plausibly deny. No "consciousness is real," no "the brain matters," no "this is a hard problem." And if a claim only *looks* agreed because each camp would read the words differently, don't paper over it — split it into two claims and say which camp owns which.

2. **A disagreements table**: the issue, A's formulation, B's formulation, and whether the difference is substantive or partly terminological. I'm fairly sure at least one row is really about what "illusion" is permitted to mean (illusion of what, and appearing to whom), and another is about whether "seems" is doing transparent or opaque work in B's argument. Handle those head-on instead of letting them fester, and say explicitly which parts of the fight you think are verbal and why.

3. **The cruxes** — the sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one side. For each, give the test or argument both agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance.

   For the first crux, use this design, which they've both already conceded is relevant. Subjects do a hard attentional task in a dim room. On a screen beside them runs a "neural decoder" that supposedly reads out the contents of their visual cortex in real time. It's prerecorded and hooked up to nothing. At one point it reports, with a confidence bar, that it is detecting vivid visual imagery in the subject right now — at a moment when nothing was presented and when, on A's own account of the task, not much was happening either. Seconds later the subject is asked to describe what they were experiencing just then. A predicts a measurable bump in reports of visual phenomenolgy relative to a control group that saw no readout. Write B's prediction in the strongest form B would actually accept, then say what B owes us if A's prediction comes true: can B write the whole thing off as demand characteristics and confabulated retrospective report, or is there a specific version of the result that genuinely costs B something? Spell out the controls needed for the two predictions to actually come apart — including the case where the fake readout is contradicted by the subject's own concurrent report.

4. **A second crux, about machines.** Both camps have to say what they make of the fact that people attribute inner experience to chatbots almost effortlessly, and chatbots self-attribute it almost effortlessly too. A will say that's precisely what you'd expect if self-attribution is a cheap output of any self-modeling system, and that the ease of it in both directions is the point. Give B's reply in B's own best terms, then state whether this crux can be settled by anything short of building the thing — and if not, what each side would want to see in the built thing.

5. **A closing section on disconfirming evidence**: what each side would count as a result or an argument against their own view. If you judge that one of them can't answer honestly — that their position is compatible with every possible outcome — say so and explain why, rather than inventing a concession they'd never make.

One housekeeping request from them: don't use the word "qualia" anywhere except inside quotation marks attributed to whichever camp is using it. They think it poisons the well.
