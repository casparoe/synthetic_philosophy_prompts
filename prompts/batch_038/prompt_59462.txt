He's already told me he thinks it doesn't but wouldn't say where, which is infuriating, so I want a second pair of eyes before I walk in. Here's roughly what I believe, in the order it developed:

1. The principle of indifference is a genuine normative requirement, not a free parameter you get to swap out. If your total evidence really is symmetric across some set of alternatives, you're required to split your credence equally, and there's no sense in which an agent could rationally do otherwise.

2. But that only gets a grip on finite sets. Equal credence in each of countably or uncountably many exclusive alternatives is just incoherent — there's no countably additive uniform distribution over the naturals, and I'm not going to fake one with improper priors, non-normalizable densities, or limits of finite cases passed off as the real thing.

3. On the chord problem specifically, I take the lesson to be that choose-a-chord-at-random is an incomplete instruction rather than a paradox. Specify it — random endpoints, random point along a radius, random midpoint uniform in the disc — and you get 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, and each is the right answer to the question it actually specifies. I don't think there's any further fact about which one a random chord denotes. Ill-posed, not contradictory. (I believe this is roughly the van Fraassen line, though I might be mixing up whose it is.)

4. At the same time I find the invariance move genuinely compelling and dont want to give it up. If you're assigning a density over chords on the basis of nothing but the geometry, it shouldn't depend on where the circle sits or how big it is, and demanding invariance under rotation, translation and dilation singles out a midpoint density going like 1/r, which reproduces the 1/2 answer. That doesn't look to me like a fourth arbitrary procedure sitting alongside the other three — it's the one that doesn't smuggle in a privileged origin or scale. And the straw-tossing version seems to back it up: build an actual machine that scatters straws and you get 1/2, which suggests the invariant answer is tracking something physical rather than something merely conventional.

5. Methodologically, whenever one of these paradoxes bites I look for a partition mistake or an under-specification rather than concluding that evidence can't pin down credences. I really don't want to end up a subjectivist about priors.

6. Though I also think which partition is the relevant one is often a judgment call the evidence doesn't force on you, and two careful people can carve differently and both be defensible.

7. And I have no problem using continuous densities in applied work — normal measurement error, uniform over a bounded interval — I just don't think that's the same move as claiming equal credence in each of infinitely many point-alternatives.
