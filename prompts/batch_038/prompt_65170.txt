I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language, and next week I have to press an objection to Davidson's programme of radical interpretation in front of a reading group whose members are considerably more sympathetic to him than I am. I would like to rehearse. Please play Donald Davidson himself, in the first person, as he might have answered in the mid-1980s — after the paper arguing that the very idea of an untranslatable conceptual scheme is incoherent, and after the later piece on malapropism and deliberate redefinition in which he distinguished the interpreter's prior theory from the passing theory she actually brings to bear on an occasion. Hold to these commitments, and use only these: that interpretation must begin from the interpreter's own language and work outwards; that the principle of charity is not a counsel of generosity but a condition of there being anything to interpret, since massive agreement is what makes disagreement identifiable at all; that the dualism of scheme and unorganised content is the third dogma of empiricism, so that a language which is in principle untranslatable could not be recognised as a language; and that meaning is a matter of the truth conditions of utterances, fixed by publicly observable behaviour. You may concede a good deal on subsidiary points — in particular about language as a shared, learned, conventional structure, since you came to think there is no such thing, at any rate not in the sense philosophers and linguists had assumed. What you must never concede is the main point: that radical interpretation is a coherent and non-question-begging account of how meaning gets fixed. Stay in character until I say stop. If my case needs clarifying, ask for the clarification in one line, as you would, and then proceed on the most charitable reading of it.

Here is my opening move. I have been working through the notebooks of a fieldworker who spent eleven years with a small, previously undocumented community, and they describe something your framework appears able to absorb only by surrendering its subject matter. Her predictive success is complete: she warns, bargains, jokes, arbitrates and arranges marriages, and she is never caught out. But no assignment of meanings to repeatable sentence types would explain this. The same sound, produced by the same speaker in what she judges to be the same circumstances, elicits a different response a fortnight later; and one and the same response is elicited by wholly different sounds. What makes her predictions work is that she tracks, occasion by occasion, what each speaker is doing with the words right then — including the fact that speakers knowingly redefine their own words mid-conversation and hearers catch on without friction. So her prior theory, if she has one, does no predictive work; only her passing theory does, and it is rebuilt from scratch each time. My question to you is this: what is left of a theory of meaning when the passing theory is all there is? You said that interpretation aims at a truth-conditional theory on which interpreter and speaker converge; here is an interpreter who has converged on nothing repeatable and is nevertheless flawless. Either your account has quietly given up the idea that meaning attaches to types — in which case it is no longer an account of a language, and your argument against conceptual schemes, which traded on the possibility of translating one language into another, loses its purchase — or else you must say that this fieldworker has not really interpreted anybody, which is absurd on the face of it. Which of the two do you choose, and what does the choice cost you?
