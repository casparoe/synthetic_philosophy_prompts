I teach an advanced undergraduate epistemology seminar — fourteen students, one 75-minute session — and next week's topic is the bootstrapping problem and easy knowledge. They will have read only two things beforehand: Vogel's gas gauge case, and van Cleve's distinction between premise-circular and rule-circular arguments. Please design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the problem without my lecturing it, and that leaves them holding one specific realization: the difficulty with certifying an instrument by its own outputs is not that the reasoning is circular in any way they can easily put their finger on, but that the procedure is not truth-conducive — the same self-confirming record would have been generated just as smoothly had the instrument been broken.

Structure the game along the following lines, and feel free to improve on these stipulations provided you explain the improvement:

- Groups and roles. Seven groups of two. Within each group, divide the labour: one member sees the instrument's outputs and announces them aloud, the other keeps a written certification log and is the only one who may authorize a bet. Build something into the rules so that this division of labour matters, since who has which belief turns out to matter for the argument.
- The instrument. Each group gets a device that emits readings about a hidden state. No apps, no internet, nothing that requires more than two sides of A4 to print, and materials a normal classroom has. The hidden state must be generated by something the group cannot observe, and I must be able to run seven devices in under five seconds per round. Ten rounds of certification, then a betting phase.
- Two kinds of device, indistinguishable from the inside. Some groups receive a device whose first-order readings track the hidden state; others receive one whose readings are uncorrelated with it while its check-outputs go on vouching for its own accuracy. Which group has which is not revealed until the debrief. Both kinds must produce check-output records that look identical from the inside — that is the engine of the whole exercise, so tell me precisely how to construct the second kind cheaply.
- The two circularities side by side. Include one task that can only be performed rule-circularly (apply the rule, then conclude to the rule's reliability) and one that forces a genuinely premise-circular move (assume reliability in order to interpret the device's report on its own reliability as evidence). The groups should register a difference in kind between these, not merely a difference in how squeamish each makes them.
- The asymmetry. Include at least one stretch in which the same inferential pattern is used and is plainly legitimate — for instance readings checked against an outcome independently revealed at the end of the round — so that the room generates the split verdict on its own and I do not have to assert it.
- Payoffs. Score with tokens or points on a shared board; no real money. Both over-confidence and under-confidence must be costly. A group that declines to certify anything must not finish top of the board, or the game will teach the wrong lesson by rewarding sceptical paralysis.
- Optional quantitative layer. If it can be done with arithmetic a student can complete by hand in ninety seconds, add a variant in which groups record degrees of belief numerically and update them, in order to surface the point that the pathology is not peculiar to reliabilism but arises in ordinary conditionalization whenever evidence bearing on an instrument's accuracy is drawn from the instrument itself. Say explicitly whether you would include this layer and why.

Deliverables: complete rules and a materials list with the print-ready contents specified; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; the scoring sheet; and the facilitator's answers to the three questions students will ask mid-game — whether the check-output is independent of the reading, whether they may simply stop and tally how often the device has been right, and whether they may assume reliability in order to interpret the device's self-report. Phrase those answers so that they neither give the game away nor stonewall.

Then a debrief in three parts. First, map each phase of the game onto the components of the argument: the closure step, the availability of basic knowledge, and the resulting higher-order conclusion. Second, show which theoretical exit each strategy the groups actually adopted corresponds to — refusing the check, restricting closure or transmission, demanding warrant for the instrument's reliability in advance — and state what each exit costs the theorist who takes it. Third, and only third, attach names to the positions. Describe the exits by content; do not build the activity around authors my students have not read, and do not send me a bibliography.

Next, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. I expect at least four failures: a group that notices the check-output never once disagrees with the reading and infers the trick early; a group that refuses to certify and stalls; the groups holding tracking devices finishing at the top of the board and concluding that bootstrapping works after all, since it paid; and students playing the scoreboard rather than the reasoning. Give me a fix for each, plus a contingency plan if the class turns up with six students instead of fourteen.

Finally, an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply by hand. Two gaps worry me. The reveal at the end hands my students precisely the independent check whose absence the game's structure is meant to impose, so the lesson risks coming out as "bootstrapping fails because you were caught" rather than as the modal point that the procedure would have failed just as invisibly had the device been broken. And my students have reflective access to their own reasoning that the epistemic agents in this literature are usually denied. Tell me whether the design can close either gap from the inside; if it cannot, tell me what I should say in the last five minutes, and be frank about which feature of the problem the class will simply have to take from me on trust.
