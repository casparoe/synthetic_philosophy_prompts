I have been reading the rationalist blogs for close to twenty years, but I have spent my working life as an applied Bayesian statistician: hierarchical models, partially pooled treatment effects across dozens of clinical trial sites, weakly informative hyperpriors, shrinkage, exchangeability, identifiability, posterior predictive checks, and the occasional argument with a regulator about whether a prior was informative in a way that mattered. That is the vocabulary in which I actually think. The material I read on those blogs about rational disagreement kept pointing back to the result that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, and to the assumption sitting underneath it — that agents share a single prior — together with the case made for that assumption (that something of the kind is needed to get the impossibility results off the ground, that impartiality or the absence of arbitrary disagreement is otherwise undefined, and that the personalist tradition licenses probabilities as subjective while leaving open whether they must coincide) and the case made against it (that a prior is only a coding of an agent's information, so that two agents having the same prior is either a difference of information mislabeled as a difference of rationality, or a substantive empirical hypothesis smuggled in as a definition of rationality). I can follow all of this, but only in a language that is not mine, and I suspect the translation will change how much of it I ought to believe.

So: restate that debate in my vocabulary, claim by claim, as faithfully as you can. Take the assumption itself, the strongest arguments for it, and the strongest arguments against it, and render each in the terms of hierarchical modeling — what would it mean for two analysts to share a hyperprior, to differ only in data, to differ in model, to be exchangeable with respect to one another. I would like the translation to cover the structural pieces as well: state spaces and partitions as I would recognize them, common knowledge, and the impossibility of mutually acceptable bets between agents with different information.

Then give me the residue report. For each claim, say whether it translates cleanly, whether it translates only with distortion (and name the distortion), or whether it has no counterpart at all in how I build models. Where the original claim is normative — about what an agent is obliged to believe rather than about what a procedure does — flag that explicitly, since my vocabulary can describe the second and not obviously the first. Finish with a verdict on the residue: is what fails to translate a genuine poverty in the statistical framework, or evidence that the debate was less substantive than it looked once priors stopped being treated as exogenous commitments?

Two specifics I cannot resolve myself. First, in my own work, two analysts with quite different hyperpriors will converge to any precision you like once I give them enough data, and I have sometimes thought this was a statistical version of the no-disagreement result. Tell me whether it is, and what exactly my analysts do and do not share that makes my convergence weaker or stronger than the theorem's. Second, is the shared-prior assumption best read as exchangeability between agents' models, or does it demand something my framework cannot even state?

Keep the translation honest. Do not settle the dispute by fiat with empirical Bayes, or by declaring the question ill-posed because my discipline stopped worrying about it; if you think the assumption simply drops out of a well-specified hierarchical model, that is a claim you have to argue for rather than assume. And work from your understanding of the arguments rather than pointing me at the literature — I am reconstructing this offline and want your reconstruction, not a list of places to look it up.
