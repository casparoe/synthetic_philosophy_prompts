I am a second-year law student working through a legal ethics reader whose opening chapter on confidentiality carries a boxed digression on Kant's 1797 essay replying to Benjamin Constant about a supposed right to lie. The box reads as follows:

"Kant's argument in the essay is an application of the universal law formula: he takes up the maxim of lying to a murderer in order to save a friend, tests it, and finds that it cannot be universalized, since if lies of this sort became common, declarations would cease to be believed and the whole institution of promising would collapse. The conclusion is that truthfulness admits of no exception whatever, and that Kant therefore requires the householder to tell the pursuer where the fugitive is hiding. Kant is unmoved by this result. For him the moral quality of an act depends on its maxim and not on what follows from it, so he accepts that the friend may be killed and holds that the person who told the truth bears no responsibility, consequences being outside anyone's control. Constant had proposed that we owe the truth only to those who have a right to it; Kant's reply is simply that such a principle is useless, because it leaves each person to decide who deserves the truth. The dispute is thus between a philosopher who refuses to look at outcomes and one who refuses to look at anything else — and because Kant's side cannot accommodate emergencies, the murderer at the door has become the standard refutation of Kantian ethics, a refutation Kant anticipated and absorbed."

I have read enough of the essay to suspect that several of these sentences are unfair to it, but I cannot yet say which, or on what grounds. Please audit the passage sentence by sentence, distinguishing for each one what a Kant scholar would accept, what is a defensible simplification that survives scrutiny, and what is wrong; where something is wrong, say what the essay argues instead. Three points in particular I would like settled. First, does the argument of that essay really run through the universal law formula, or is it doing something else — and if something else, what exactly is the work being done by the notions of a declaration, of a duty owed to humanity in one's own person, and of the conditions of right? Second, is Kant genuinely indifferent to outcomes in the manner described, or do consequences re-enter his argument somewhere, and if so where, and with what asymmetry between the person who lies and the person who tells the truth? Third, does the position commit the householder to answering the question and disclosing the fugitive's whereabouts at all, or is the duty of truthfulness in play narrower than the box assumes — for instance, as bearing only on declarations one actually makes, or only on those one cannot avoid? Finally, tell me whether the last sentence's conclusion survives the corrections: once the misattributions are stripped away, is there still a strong objection to Kant's position in this essay, and if so, what is it, and how does it differ from the objection the box actually presses? I am not after recommendations for further reading — I want this passage dissected and the boundaries between accurate gloss, simplification, and distortion made explicit.
