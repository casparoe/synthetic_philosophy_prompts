I've been following the risk-weighted expected utility stuff since Buchak's book and I've read most of the back and forth — the LessWrong threads on Allais, the Mind pieces, Easwaran's generalizations — and my feeling is nobody has moved a milimeter in ten years. Roughly: Buchak says independence and continuity aren't requirements of instrumental rationality, only wide-scope ones are, so someone who passes on the Allais bets, buys the insurance, and generally overweights bad outcomes is perfectly rational, and her representation theorem even hands them a unique risk function r. Critics say fine, but watch what happens when the decisions come one at a time — such an agent can be Dutch booked through non-additive betting quotients, and can refuse a package of bets she'd happily take separately; and anyway a curved utility function already handles ordinary risk aversion without any of this machinery. Buchak's answer is that those multi-decision arguments smuggle in a narrow-scope picture of rationality, which is exactly what's in dispute. And there it sits.

My guess is that the real disagreement isn't in decision theory at all. It's about whether the EU axioms are partly constitutive of having credences and desires in the first place — the Davidson/Lewis interpretivist picture, where violating independence means you've mis-described the agent rather than found a rational rival — versus whether they're substantive norms an agent can fail while remaining perfectly describable. If constitutive, the Dutch books are decisive by construction; if substantive, they're a cost and not a refutation, and REU stays alive. On that reading the whole thing is a philosophy-of-mind fight wearing decision theory clothing and no theorem ends it.

Is that right, or is something more tractable doing the work — a hidden formal question about uniqueness in the representation theorems, or about whether the agent is a planner or a pile of timeslices? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress here?
