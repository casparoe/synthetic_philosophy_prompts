I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, so please forgive the amateur framing. A good deal of my time at work goes into incident response, and I am now the person who writes and reviews postmortems after production outages. One of our practices is that the Root Cause field of the template is left blank: the standing line from the people who run the practice is that root cause is a myth, that a serious outage is always several individually survivable contributing factors arriving at once, and that insisting on one cause is just a socially acceptable way of finding someone to blame. I find this genuinely clarifying when I'm staring at a timeline with a deploy, a misconfigured retry policy, and a dependency that was already degraded.

A friend of mine is finishing a history doctorate, and I recently argued at her that the same discipline must apply to her field: historical causation is exactly like a distributed-systems outage. There is no such thing as the cause of a war or a revolution; there is a tinderbox of standing conditions, one of which happens to be the trigger, and any account that ranks causes is either doing politics or doing bad engineering. She said the analogy was carrying much more weight than I realized, but I could not say where.

Could you audit it for me?

- Give me the correspondence table. What in a historical event plays the role of the page that wakes the on-call engineer, the blast radius, the latent condition, the contributing factor, the trigger, the remediation item, and the rollback? If some of those slots have no occupant at all, say so explicitly; I suspect that is where the information is.
- Separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the conclusion I want to draw — that ranking causes is illegitimate — from the ones that are merely decorative.
- Then tell me whether my own home domain actually supports that conclusion. My understanding is that we still rank contributing factors by counterfactual leverage and still decide what to fix first, even while refusing to name a root cause. If so, what is the no-root-cause claim really denying, and is it the same claim as saying a historian cannot tell me one cause mattered more than another?
- Finally: does my conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and is there a better source domain for the analogy?
