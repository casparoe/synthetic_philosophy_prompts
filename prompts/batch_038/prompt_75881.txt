I am the teaching assistant for an upper-division course on post-Kantian German philosophy (sixty-one enrolled), and this week's section covered the aesthetics of Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Representation*: Book III of volume I, with the discussion of music as the centerpiece, plus the corresponding supplement in volume II. Students worked from the Payne translation, and the Haldane–Kemp was on reserve for anyone who wanted a second rendering. The graded item was a take-home response of about 500 words to a single question, worth 20 points, and students were told they could refer to books and sections rather than page numbers. I have marked roughly half the batch and want an independent audit of this particular script before I calibrate it against the rest; I already have a provisional score in my head from the first pass, but I would rather not tell you what it is, so please do not try to infer it or to guess at how the other answers went.

The question as it was given to the students:

> In *The World as Will and Representation*, Schopenhauer assigns music a position fundamentally different from that of every other art. Explain what that difference consists in, and show how it accounts for the distinctive power music has over its listener. Your answer must say explicitly what role the Platonic Ideas play in the case of the other arts, and must state whether the experience of music is, for Schopenhauer, still an instance of will-less contemplation — and if it is not, why not.

The student's response, in full:

> For Schopenhauer the world has two sides. It is representation, a world of objects articulated by the forms of space, time and causality; and it is will, the thing-in-itself that each of us also encounters from within as our own endless striving. The will objectifies itself at ascending grades — gravity and rigid mass, then the chemical and physical forces, then the plant kingdom, then the animal, and finally the human being — and each grade is what Schopenhauer calls a Platonic Idea: a permanent and adequate objectification of the will, prior to the plurality of the individuals that fall under it. Every art except music works through these Ideas. Its business is to lift us out of our ordinary concern with this or that particular thing and hold us in the contemplation of the Idea instead, so that a portrait lets us see the Idea of humanity rather than one more face, and a landscape lets us see the Ideas of the natural forces rather than one more valley. On this account art is a kind of knowing, and its value is that knowing quiets us: while we are absorbed in the Idea, the will that ordinarily drives us falls silent and we become the pure, will-less subject of knowing.
>
> Music differs because it does not proceed by way of the Ideas at all. In the Book III discussion, and again in the longer supplement in the second volume, Schopenhauer says that music is a copy of the will itself rather than a copy of the Ideas, and this is why it can bypass representation altogether and why it acts on us more immediately and more powerfully than any painting or poem: the other arts speak only of shadows, music speaks of the essence. He builds this into a structural parallel. The deep bass stands for the lowest grades of the will's objectification, crude matter and mass; the middle voices stand for the plant and animal kingdoms; and the melody — the high, singing voice that carries the tune and unfolds freely above everything else — stands for the intellectual life and endeavour of the human being. Harmony and dissonance carry meaning on the same scheme, since a dissonance demands resolution just as every satisfaction of desire is temporary and gives way to a fresh desire. Schopenhauer's elevation of music simply carries through the ranking Kant had already proposed in the third *Critique*, where music is placed at the head of the fine arts.
>
> This explains why music needs no text, and why Schopenhauer would reject the view that a piece is valuable because it expresses what its composer happened to feel. The composer is not describing his own sadness; he gives us sadness as such, the universal form of it, stripped of the particular occasion and the particular motive that produced it.
>
> The consequence for the listener is that musical experience is not will-less in the way the experience of a painting is. Because music presents striving itself rather than the Ideas, it stirs and agitates us; it acts on our own will directly instead of suspending it, and it is precisely for this reason that Schopenhauer thinks music moves us more than anything else. It follows that the deepest music is essentially sad, since what it discloses is that the inner nature of everything is insatiable wanting, and the listener is made to participate in that wanting. The stillness the other arts produce is therefore, on Schopenhauer's own premises, unavailable in the case of music.
