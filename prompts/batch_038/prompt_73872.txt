I'm writing a design doc for a multi-agent system where the agents never get a public announcement — each one only ever has noisy private signals about some shared state, plus a message channel that delivers with reliability 0.999. A philosopher friend read a draft and said its doomed: coordination needs common knowledge, common knowledge can only be generated by a public event everyone witnesses together, and reliable-but-imperfect messaging can't get you there no matter how many rounds you run (he mentioned something about an email game). I think he's right about the formal results and wrong about my system.

Can you build me a decision tree that starts from that setup and sorts out the positions in this debate? Each branch node should be a concrete question I could answer about my own system or about a small case — how deep the iterated knowledge actually needs to go for the coordination I care about, whether a breakdown would show up in payoffs or only in an epistemic standard that nobody's actions turn on, whether I'm allowed to redesign things so a genuinely public event does occur — and not "do you accept result X?". Leaves get named positions, including at minimum: the view that some finite level of iteration already does everything "common knowledge" gets invoked for; the view that high-probability common belief substitutes for it in most games; the pessimist who takes the email-game discontinuity at face value; the view that only being in a public situation together generates it; and the deflationist who says finite agents can't have it at all. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate, say which cell of the space looks empty — nobody defends it — and whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unfashionable, and finish with a paragraph telling me where my design doc lands.
