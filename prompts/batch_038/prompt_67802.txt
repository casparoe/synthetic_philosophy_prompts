I write a long-form column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a feature about why two competent, well-briefed, mutually transparent experts can go on disagreeing for years. Every illustration I have tried so far is either a metaphor a specialist would dismantle in one sentence or a formalism my readers would skip. I want a case built to order that I can print, and I want it verified before it reaches a reviewer who knows the literature.

The assignment: construct a scenario in which two agents share a genuine common prior, update perfectly, are each certain of the other's perfect Bayesian rationality, and exchange their posterior probabilities over the same question repeatedly and as publicly as their circumstances allow — and yet end up with materially different posteriors that do not close, no matter how long they keep talking. The condition on Aumann's agreement theorem that fails must not be the common prior and must not be rationality or knowledge of rationality; those are to be established in the story rather than assumed away. The failure has to come from the knowledge condition, and specifically from the way the two of them communicate, and it has to be the sort of failure a reader would insist on first hearing cannot possibly matter.

The constraints:

1. The setting must be ordinary and concrete — a field team and a headquarters, a monitoring station and a duty officer, something with stakes a general reader recognizes. Give the two people names and give the disputed question a number attached to it.
2. The communication channel between them degrades messages silently, at a rate both of them know approximately. Neither is a fool, and neither conceals anything. They take the obvious countermeasures — repetition, confirmation requests, a check on the received message — and your case has to explain why those countermeasures do not restore what is missing, or else concede that they do and quantify what is left over.
3. No infinite state spaces, no unmeasurable partitions, no failures of arithmetic, no forgetting, no motivated reasoning, no private evidence one of them sits on. The case must fail for a structural reason, not because the agents are cheaply non-ideal.
4. Make the common prior a real feature of the story rather than a modelling convenience: explain in one or two sentences why these two people plausibly start from the same distribution.
5. The scenario itself runs no longer than three hundred words, in plain English, with no notation and no technical vocabulary of any kind. The phrase "common knowledge" does not appear in the part I print.

Then a separate analysis section, up to eight hundred words, in which notation is allowed:

- State which condition of the theorem is unmet, in the theorem's own terms, and identify the step in the standard argument for agreement at which my case stops going through.
- Lay out the knowledge structure: what each agent knows about the other's posterior, what each knows about what the other knows, and the exact depth at which the chain terminates.
- Derive, rather than guess, how large the residual gap between the two posteriors can be as a function of the channel's failure rate. Say whether a small failure rate licenses a large disagreement or only a proportionally small one, and construct your numbers so that the actual gap sits at the bound and not well below it. If the honest answer is undramatic, give me the undramatic answer; I would rather print a true bound than an exciting one. Then say whether the gap shrinks as the number of exchange rounds grows, and how fast.
- Run the removal test: put the same two people in the same situation with a perfect channel and let them re-announce. Do they converge, in how many rounds, and what does that reveal about which single feature of my case was doing the work?
- Answer whether the difference between your noisy channel and an ordinary face-to-face conversation between the same two people is one of kind or one of degree, and defend the answer rather than asserting it.
- Write the rebuttal I will get from the economist I send this to. I want the best version, not a straw man: I expect it to run by enlarging the state space to include what the channel actually did, observing that a common prior over the enlarged space still exists, and pointing out that the theorem never made a claim about real conversations. Then tell me how much of my case survives that rebuttal and what it costs me to concede.
- Finish with one print-ready sentence stating the moral of the case in language a non-specialist will follow.

Two house rules. First, no citations, no reading list, no named papers, authors, or dates — I cannot check them and my readers will not care; if a step in your construction depends on a result you are not fully certain of, flag it in plain words instead of dressing it up. Second, do not let the scenario or the analysis stipulate the verdict: the disagreement has to emerge from the structure you have specified, and if you find that some constraint above cannot be satisfied alongside another, say which one, and show me the closest case you can build.
