I'm a philosophy PhD student and this term I'm marking for an advanced philosophy-of-maths option — mostly fourth-years and a couple of MPhils. The essay question was:

'Gödel and Cohen together showed that the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC. Does it follow that CH is neither true nor false? Discuss.'

Here's one of the scripts:

Gödel showed that if ZFC is consistent then so is ZFC together with CH, by constructing the inner model L of constructible sets, in which CH holds. Cohen showed the same for the negation: starting from a countable model of ZFC, forcing adds ℵ₂-many new reals and gives a bigger model in which the continuum is at least that large. So there are models of ZFC in which CH is true and models in which it is false, and it follows that CH is neither true nor false — the question how many reals there are simply has no answer, and anyone who thinks otherwise hasn't really absorbed the independence results.

This is best seen as a special case of Gödel's incompleteness theorem. Any consistent theory strong enough for arithmetic leaves sentences undecided, and CH is one of them; the only difference is that here we have two very different methods for proving it.

If anything, forcing tips the scales towards the negation. Any model can be extended so as to add more reals, and from outside its own reals are only countably many, so no one universe gets the size of the continuum right. Gödel seems to have accepted something along these lines, since he took his own consistency proof to show that CH is independent and therefore neither true nor false. For the same reason the programme of settling CH by new axioms has been pretty much abandoned: large cardinals are compatible with both CH and its negation, so no amount of extra consistency strength can decide it.
