I work on evaluation infrastructure at an AI lab, and I'm trying to understand the assumptions baked into one of our standing artifacts. Each release cycle we publish an internal ledger that records the capability cost of safety work: for every successive safety fine-tune, how many points the model gives up on our coding and reasoning benchmarks, with the stated goal of driving that number toward zero over successive models. Separately, our scheduling decisions are governed by what I'd call a competitiveness constraint — the reasoning that a slower, more careful lab simply loses, and that whoever is in front gets to set the standards everyone else inherits. The two sit oddly together. The ledger treats safety as a pure cost to be engineered away, while the race argument treats safety as the entire reason to want to be in front.

What theory of value does the ledger presuppose? Does reporting the cost in benchmark points already commit us to a single scale on which safety and capability get traded off against each other, and which rival views does that unit choice quietly rule out? And what does the competitiveness constraint presuppose about agency — does "the race makes us ship" function as a structural excuse that relocates responsibility from people to market conditions, and is that move available to an organization that also claims it wants to be first precisely so it can be more careful?

Please reconstruct the implicit theories, separate what our practices strictly require from what merely happens to fit them, and flag where they're philosophically underdetermined — places where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same ledger. Finally, mark clearly which parts of your answer are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested position as though it were the consensus.
