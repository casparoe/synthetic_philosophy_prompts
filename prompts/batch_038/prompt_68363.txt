ok context first: I'm a junior and for the last year I've been one of three student members on my university's residential conduct board. We hear about six to eight cases a month, all inside the housing system, and our sanction menu is fixed — written warning, some kind of educational/reflection assignment, restitution, probation, removal from housing. We can't suspend or expel, that goes to a different office entirely. Right now our actual "reasoning" is whoever on the panel talks the most plus a general vibe about whether the person seemed sorry, and I'm in a philosophy of law seminar at the same time so it's kind of wrecking my head, because every case turns out to be a pile of retributivist and consequentialist instincts in no particular order. Someone always says we have to send a message to the floor, someone else says the sanction has to fit what this person actually did rather than what it deters, and we never resolve it, we just split the difference and hope nobody appeals.

What I want is an actual decision procedure a panel of three students + one staff advisor could run in about forty minutes, with the steps in order, what counts as an adequate answer at each step, which steps are answerable by facts we can go find and which ones are value calls the panel just has to own out loud, and explicit places where the procedure stops at "no sanction available / not ours to decide." The thing I most want it built on is a distinction I keep half-remembering from seminar — something like: the question of why a punishing institution exists at all, the question of who may be punished, and the question of how much, and the claim that a lot of the mess comes from answering one of those with a principle meant for another one. I'm pretty sure that's Hart? If I've got that wrong just tell me and use whatever the real distinction is. Related: one of the other board members keeps pushing this idea about credibility, roughly that if students see the sanctions as tracking what people actually deserve then they comply more, so you should let desert drive even in the individual cases where sending-a-message points somewhere else. I want the procedure to say plainly whether that's a legitimate input at the how-much stage or whether it's a smuggled desert claim with a social-science costume on, and if it is legitimate, how it gets capped so it can't override the proportionality step.

Then run it on three of our recurring cases, in enough detail that I could see where each step lands. (1) a second-time party/noise complaint in the same hall, the person is polite and cooperative and there's no damage and no individual victim who wants anything, but the floor has signed a petition and two people are threatening to break their lease. (2) a student who forged a roommate's signature on a damage-report form during what everyone agrees was an acute mental health crisis, and has been in treatment since — so responsibility and future risk are pointing in opposite directions. (3) a theft where the person is genuinely remorseful and has already made full restitution, our instinct as a panel would be a written warning, except the person who reported it lives on the same floor for another year and that's the part everyone avoids talking about. I'd rather you tell me straight if the procedure comes out somewhere I won't like on one of these, and if it does, say why the panel's instinct is wrong rather than quietly adjusting the steps to match it. Also: flag every point where the thing is really judgment pretending to be a checklist, tell me what it structurally can't decide at all (I suspect the big one is whether the university should be sanctioning this stuff in the first place, or whether our whole grid is just desert dressed up as community standards), and write the whole thing in plain language — no citations, no reading list, I need to be able to hand it to a sophomore on the board who's never taken a philosophy class and have them follow it without me.
