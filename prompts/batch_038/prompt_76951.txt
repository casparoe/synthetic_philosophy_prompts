Last spring I sat on a civil jury in a state trial court, and this autumn I was one of roughly forty residents drawn by stratified lot for a city-sponsored citizens' assembly on zoning. The two experiences kept rhyming in ways that now bother me, and I would like help working out what each institution is actually committed to, philosophically, rather than what its advocates say about it. Here is what I observed. Both bodies are filled by lot from lists compiled for entirely other purposes — voter registration, motor vehicle records — and in both cases the summons is compulsory, with a nominal stipend and a threat of contempt or a fine behind it. The jury, however, was then whittled down: each side could strike venire members for cause without limit, and could also strike a fixed number peremptorily with no reason given at all, subject only to the bar on striking people because of their race or sex. The judge told us the pool had to be drawn from a fair cross-section of the community, and in the same breath instructed us to set aside everything we already knew, to decide only on what had been admitted into evidence, and not to look anything up. During selection, one woman who mentioned she worked as an emergency-room nurse was questioned at length about whether she could refrain from relying on her medical knowledge; she was not excused, but the exchange made clear that her expertise was being treated as a contaminant while her presence on the panel counted as a virtue. We were told we had to agree unanimously or the case would be tried again; our deliberations were secret, and as I understand it no juror may later testify in court about what was said in the room. Judges, by contrast, are chosen for credentials and serve for decades, and nobody seriously proposes selecting them by lot; legislators are elected, vote by bare majority, and cast those votes in public. The citizens' assembly was openly designed to be a statistical microcosm of the city, was briefed by experts picked by staff, deliberated over four weekends, and issued a report to the council with no binding force whatever.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory behind the jury practice, and then a comparison. Specifically: what account of the relation between a decision procedure and the correctness or legitimacy of its output does trial by randomly drawn lay jurors presuppose — an epistemic one, on which twelve unselected minds are likelier to land on the truth than one expert mind; a political one, on which state coercion is legitimate only if imposed by a sample of the coerced; or a representational one, on which the panel is a microcosm and so its verdict just is the community's verdict? Which of these can the practice be shown to require, and which merely happen to fit it comfortably? Then the tension I cannot dissolve: the institution demands a fair cross-section of the community and afterwards filters out precisely the things that make people members of that community — prior knowledge, occupational expertise, settled opinions, personal stakes. Some single theory must be doing double duty here, or the two halves are incoherent. I would find an account most persuasive if it could explain why the nurse's knowledge is excluded while her presence is required, and what it says about which parts of a person are permitted to enter the room. Alongside that: why is unanimity demanded of twelve strangers resolving one dispute, while a bare majority suffices for several hundred elected officials resolving the law of the land; and why is secrecy the rule in the jury room and publicity the rule on the legislative floor, given that jurors are unaccountable by design (one term, no reappointment prospect, immune from later questioning) and legislators are accountable by design? If the citizens' assembly movement inherits the jury's justification, I want to know which parts carry over and which break — in particular whether an advisory body reporting to a council needs the same theory of legitimacy as a body whose verdict binds, and whether "microcosm" means the same thing when the microcosm is told what the questions are and who the experts will be. Finally, tell me where all of this is underdetermined: which features of these practices are licensed equally well by rival theories, so that pointing at the practice cannot settle the philosophy; and what would concretely have to change in courtroom or assembly procedure — composition, instructions, voting rules, publication of reasoning — if we replaced the presupposed theory with an explicitly epistemic one on which the panel is an instrument for tracking the truth and nothing more.

I am not looking for case citations, doctrine names, or a reading list; take my descriptions of the procedures as accurate and treat this as a conceptual exercise. Please organize the answer as follows. First, a short prose reconstruction of the jury's implicit theory and an explicit verdict on whether the cross-section requirement and the bias-purging requirement can be made consistent under a single theory. Then a table with one row per practice feature — compulsion and lot, peremptory and for-cause strikes, the fair-cross-section requirement, the instruction to disregard outside knowledge, unanimity, secrecy of deliberation, single non-renewable service, majority rule in legislatures, publicity of legislative votes, expert briefing of the assembly, the assembly's advisory status — and five columns: Feature (as described above), Presupposed commitment (the view the practice requires, stated without jargon), Rivals excluded (which competing accounts of legitimacy this practice rules out or strains against), Status (one of: strictly presupposed / merely compatible / underdetermined), and Change if replaced (what would have to be altered in procedure if we adopted an openly epistemic, truth-tracking account instead). After the table, give me a bottom line of two or three sentences: which internal tension is genuinely unresolvable, which is only apparent, and whether the jury and the citizens' assembly turn out to rest on the same theory or on two different theories that happen to share a selection mechanism.
