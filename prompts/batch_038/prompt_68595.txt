I write literary fiction. My fourth novel concerns a woman who loses, inside a persistent virtual world, something she cannot afford to lose, and I accepted a seat on a platform's community-standards appeals panel partly as research. I am the panel's only non-employee member, and the case we hear Thursday has split it down the middle. I would like a decision memo to circulate before we convene.

The stipulated record:

- The world is called Aldermoot. It runs on headsets and haptic gloves, is persistent, and supports a secondary market on which in-world items sell for real currency.
- Zone 4 is designated a full-contact roleplay zone. Community Code §12 provides that in-zone conduct "performed in character is governed by the norms of the Zone." §3 prohibits "conduct, in or out of character, that causes harm to another user."
- The Appellant, playing a brigand, used a grapple mechanic to pin the Complainant's avatar, triggered a scripted search of the person that stripped the avatar's clothing layer, and took a signet ring the Complainant had worn continuously for eleven months. She had paid a real-money minting fee for it; comparable rings were then listed on the market at roughly three hundred dollars.
- The Complainant states that she survived a sexual assault years ago, that the encounter produced a physical panic response she felt through the gloves, and that she has not logged in since. Two other users attest that she was shaking and unable to speak in the voice channel afterwards.
- The Appellant states that he did not know her, that the zone requires no per-encounter consent exchange (opt-in is world-level), that every mechanic he used is documented and available to any player in Zone 4, and that he sold the ring to a third party within the hour for cryptocurrency he has since spent.

One faction of the panel holds that §12 is precisely the kind of consent framework that makes rough roleplay legitimate, and that ruling against the Appellant amounts to declaring that nothing done inside a game can be part of the game. The other holds that a real woman's body responded to a real event and that property of hers, with an owner and a price, was taken and converted, and that we are sheltering behind the word virtual.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: a formal opinion to the chair, roughly nine hundred words, under headings, in prose rather than bullet points, with a disposition the panel can vote on. It must do four things. First, distinguish what is actually at issue here — harm to a person delivered through a virtual object; harm to a virtual object that is nonetheless a real thing of some kind, owned, priced, and convertible; or harm occurring only within a shared pretense and therefore no harm at all — and argue, rather than assert, which of these the incident instantiates, allowing that more than one may be present at once and that they may not rise and fall together. Second, determine whether §3 or §12 controls, and state what would have to be true for the observation that she was inside a game to function as a defense at all. Third, take the Appellant's strongest point seriously — that the mechanics were documented, the zone was advertised, and world-level opt-in is the only consent structure this world has — because he will be represented and I do not want a memo a competent advocate could shred in one paragraph. Fourth, propose a rule the panel can apply to the next forty appeals, given that we cannot audit anyone's trauma history, intentions, or in-character commitments. State a remedy as well: reinstatement or not, restoration of the ring or its market value, any zone-level change.

I want no citations, no bibliography, and no named authorities; you will not have the sources at hand and I will not trust reconstructed ones. Give me the reasoning laid bare instead.

Close with a single final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: [one of: Ban sustained / Ban overturned / Ban overturned with conditions / Remand for further findings]
