so this started as a fight about a dishwasher and turned into four nights of 1am reading. my partner does policy evaluation for a living and is constitutionally incapable of letting any decision through without saying "we just add up the utils," and last week I finally made him say where that comes from instead of treating it like long division. It's Harsanyi's aggrgate theorem — the aggregation one. I'm a software person, not a philosopher, so I may be mangling it, but here's what I think I've got: if each person's preferences over prospects are vNM-rational (expected utility, the whole independence-axiom package), and the social ranking over those same prospects is also vNM-rational, and you respect Pareto, then the social ranking has to be a positive weighted sum of the individual utilities, unique up to an affine transform. That's the whole thing. The theorem says nothing at all about what the weights are — equal weights come from a separate move, an impartial observer who takes themselves to have an equal chance of being anybody, and that move is doing all the moral work while wearing a lab coat. So "it's a theorem, utilitarianism is just math" is really "the math gets you a weighted sum, and then you buy the weights at a shop nobody mentioned." That's what I want to satirize: not Harsanyi, who I think is fine, but the discourse of people who say "well, the theorem settles it" and then stop talking.

The piece: a customer support ticket thread, about 750 words, on the help portal of a company that sells a device which literally implements that social observer. You onboard a group of people, it emits one number, it's marketed as ending arguments. The customer is a small-town planning commission that bought one to settle a zoning dispute and is now regretting it. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and the company never once admits anything is broken — every complaint gets answered in support-desk register (licensing tiers, SLAs, known-issue IDs, "by design," escalation to Tier 2, a sunset notice). Include ticket numbers, timestamps, and agent names. The jokes have to come from the formal content being right, so somebody who has actually read the theorem laughs instead of wincing. Beats I want hit, roughly in this order: the opening complaint is that the device ranked a proposal where one developer gains enormously and nine households each lose a little as strictly better, and the customer wants a worst-off mode or any kind of fairness setting; the reveal that equal weights are not in the base product at all, that the base product is silent on weights, and that equal weighting is a separately licensed module built on an assumption about an observer who thinks they might be anybody, which the agent describes as an "onboarding stance" rather than a moral claim; one exchange where Tier 2 explains, correctly and without apology, that a mode that cares about the shape of the distribution rather than the sum breaks the independence axiom and the device would start contradicting itself — I want this to be the moment where a reader who knows the literature ends up siding with the company; onboarding stalling because every member of the commission has to agree on the probabilities of the states before the machine will compute anything, and two of them don't; a commissioner whose own choices violate the axioms (something Allais-shaped, or maybe he just hates ambiguity), and support's answer being that he is out of domain and will be handled as noise; a known-issues list in which a real philosophical problem is filed as a bug — e.g. shifting one person's utility zero-point changes no ranking, by design while the population is fixed, expected to get much worse once the variable-population release ships, because the device has no idea where zero goes for people who do not exist yet; a long-running neighbor feud in which one man's utility is visibly sourced from another man's misery and enters the sum with a positive weight, and the only remedy on offer is lowering his weight, which the license forbids because weights are strictly positive and nobody can be zeroed out; and a closing message offering a refund conditional on the requester's own preferences passing a coherence check that he fails.

Two constraints. Don't name any real philosopher anywhere — invented customers and agents only — and don't put anything in quotation marks as though it came from a real text, because I can't verify it and I'm not interested in spreading invented quotes; paraphrase if you need to gesture at something someone once said. And never state an objection in the abstract: if the reader is supposed to learn that the device can't see distribution, it should be because a settings page is greyed out, not because anybody utters a sentence like "this raises questions of distributive justice." Finally, please make the customer's asks get steadily more sympathetic and the company's refusals steadily more airtight, so that by the end I genuinely can't tell whether the machine is broken or the customer wanted something incoherent. That ambiguity is the point — I still don't know which side of the dishwasher fight I'm on, and I'd rather the piece not resolve it for me.
