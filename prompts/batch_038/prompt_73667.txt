I was at a friend's place last Thursday for her monthly metaphysics reading group — six of us around a kitchen table, mostly people who did philosophy as undergrads and now have day jobs, plus one guy who's actually in a PhD program somewhere. We'd all been assigned something on persistence beforehand and the discussion collapsed within about ten minutes into a two-person argument between Amara (four-dimensionalist, quite militant) and Ben (endurantist, equally stubborn). I mostly made tea and took notes. They went at it for the better part of an hour and each walked away convinced they'd won. I honestly don't have a horse in this race — I've never been able to feel the force of either intuition, which is probably why I was useless in the room — so I'd like a real referee. Here's the exchange as best I've reconstructed it from my notes:

1. Amara opened with the poker. Straight on Monday, bent on Tuesday. If it's wholly present on both days, then one and the same object has incompatible properties, which is a contradiction. Her view avoids it cleanly: the Monday-part is straight, the Tuesday-part is bent. So endurantism is either flatly inconsistent or forced to deny that the poker really changes.
2. Ben said that's not an argument for parts, it's just relativization. "Straight on Monday, bent on Tuesday" is every bit as coherent as "the Monday-part is straight, the Tuesday-part is bent" — both accounts locate properties relative to times. If relativizing to a time kills the contradiction, he's already killed it, and he gets nothing by then positing temporal parts. So the temporary-intrinsics argument is neutral between the two views, not a reason to prefer hers.
3. Amara said the relativization isn't free. On his view nothing has a shape simpliciter, everything is had-shape-relative-to-a-time, so he quietly gives up intrinsic properties altogether, which is a much bigger cost than he's admitting; her parts have their shapes genuinely intrinsically. And once you allow objects to bear properties relative to temporal locations, you're describing something spread out across a temporal region — he has the worm view and is just refusing the name.
4. Ben turned it around: on her view nothing ever changes, either. Change is a thing persisting and being different at different times, and she has no single thing that is both straight and bent — just a succession of parts that differ from one another. Difference between parts isn't change, any more than the difference between the handle and the blade makes the knife change. So the "nothing changes" charge lands harder on her.
5. Amara said that's begging the question: he's built into the definition of change a requirement only his ontology can meet. "The poker changed shape" is made true by the Monday-part and the Tuesday-part differing, and that's all the claim ever amounted to; he has no independent grip on change apart from the intuition he's leaning on.
6. Ben said then it's a verbal dispute and they should stop. They agree about every fact there is to agree about — which shapes get instantiated where and when, what exists at each time, how the world is arranged — and disagree only about how to carve up descriptions of it. And if that's all the disagreement is, there's no argument left for temporal parts at all.
7. Amara said it isn't verbal, because the views diverge on things you actually have to make calls about: gradual replacement, fission, whether there can be vague or indeterminate facts about which object survived, whether objects have determinate spacetime boundaries. Plus a physics point — simultaneity is frame-relative, so "wholly present at a time" has no privileged physical meaning, and relativity doesn't single out the three-dimensional slices his view needs.
8. Ben said the physics point doesn't discriminate. Spacetime is what it is on both views and her worm occupies exactly the same region his enduring object does; if the slices are frame-relative, then "the object exists at this time" is frame-relative for her too, and she doesn't get a preferred foliation for free. And on the cases, he claimed that in every replacement or fission scenario he can think of they issue the same verdicts about what survives and what we'd observe — so she's described a disagreement with no consequences, which is exactly what verbal means.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step? For each numbered move I want a rating (roughly: clearly mistaken / partly right but oversold / solid) and a short justification — I care especially about whether move 2 really does neutralize move 1 or whether Amara's cost objection in move 3 buys her back the advantage, and about whether move 4's "nothing changes" charge is a genuine objection or just a restatement of endurantist prejudice. Then answer the question move 6 and move 7 are actually fighting over: is this dispute verbal, and if only partly, which parts are substantive and which parts are just two ways of talking? Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — is there a single crux that would actually move one of them if it were settled, and what would count as settling it? No bibliography or reading list please, I'm not going to chase down papers; I just want your judgment on these moves as they were actually made.
