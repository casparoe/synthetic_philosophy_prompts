the setup, as i remember it from a book i read years ago: a jar with 90 balls in it. 30 of them are red, guaranteed. the other 60 are black or yellow, but nobody will tell you how they're split — could be 60 black and no yellow, could be the reverse, could be anything between. first you're offered two bets: win 100 if you draw red, or win 100 if you draw black. nearly everybody takes the red bet. then you're offered: win 100 if it's red, versus win 100 if it's black or yellow. nearly everybody takes black-or-yellow.

i'd make both those choices without thinking about it. but when i tried to write down why, it went sideways on me:

(1) the reason to take red over black is that i know the odds on red — one in three — while the odds on black could be anywhere from nothing to two in three.

(2) except i don't really know the odds on red either. all i've got is the word of whoever filled the jar. they might have miscounted, or been joking, or slipped a few extra reds in after telling me, or "red" might cover a couple of orange ones.

(3) and if i take (2) seriously, then my odds on red aren't a clean one in three either. they're a spread, just a narrower spread that i'm not in the habit of writing out.

(4) whatever it is that makes me swallow the jar-filler's story is the same kind of trust i put in a weather forecast, a supplier's lead time, an industry mortality table, a customer survey — and i couldn't put an honest number on any of those either.

(5) so there's no such thing as known odds versus unknown odds. there are only descriptions i trust more or less. the jar isn't a special puzzle at all, it's just the normal human situation with the trusted part written up as a number and the untrusted part written up as a shrug, and the puzzle is an artifact of the bookkeeping.

(6) but if that's right, then every time i pick the known-odds bet i'm kidding myself, and i also can't see why i insure anything or diversify my suppliers, since the actuary and the freight broker are just more jar-fillers.
