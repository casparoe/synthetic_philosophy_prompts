For the better part of a year I have been reporting on invertebrate welfare — insect farming, municipal mosquito control, the ethics of field collecting — and somewhere along the way I began keeping a private ledger of the practices I would personally sign off on. I would like to know what principle, if any, the entries add up to.

The entries, in the order I wrote them down. A museum's ethyl acetate killing jar for collecting beetles: not fine. Letting the same beetles desiccate in a dry vial: fine, though I cannot tell you why that is the better outcome. Freezing a vial of Drosophila at the end of an experiment: fine. Boiling a lobster alive: not fine. Sedating crayfish in a clove-oil bath before killing them: fine, and better than freezing them. A municipal larvicide program that destroys what I will generously call a trillion mosquito larvae to prevent a dozen human cases of West Nile virus: fine. Swapping one sodium streetlight for a warmer LED so that a few thousand fewer moths are drawn into it each season: a genuine moral improvement, not a rounding error. A gene drive built to collapse a regional mosquito population, with a body count on the same order as the larvicide's: not fine. A cricket-flour protein bar: fine. Eating a handful of live crickets on camera as a stunt: not fine. A survey protocol that photographs and releases specimens instead of collecting them: fine, even though it requires roughly ten times the site visits and so, through trampling and repeated netting, probably kills more insects than the collecting it replaces.

My own tentative rule is that I should minimize expected harm, computed as my credence that the animal is sentient, multiplied by the number of animals, multiplied by something standing for the intensity and duration of what they undergo. That rule does most of the work, but it renders the larvicide and the gene drive morally identical, which I decline to accept, and it dissolves the streetlight into insignificance, which I also decline to accept. I have begun to suspect that my ledger tracks how easily I can picture an animal's death rather than anything about the animal: the trillion larvae are unfilmable, and that may be the whole of my acquittal.

So I want the principle, or the short list of candidates, that my entries actually support — stated precisely, with the entry that motivates each clause, and with its verdicts on two or three cases I have not thought to list. Then apply it to the question I keep circling: whether a city should treat mosquito abatement as a welfare problem at all, given that it would have to weigh a trillion unfilmable deaths against a dozen human cases, and given that my streetlight verdict appears to commit me to counting the former. Finally, be explicit about which entries a coherent principle is forced to overturn, and whether it can overturn them without overturning the ones I care most about. If the price of saving every entry is a rule no one could actually apply, tell me so and give me the bill.
