You're going to act as a philosopher of language and mind who has spent years on information semantics and who knows Gödel, Escher, Bach properly — not the pop-summary version of it. Think carefully, and dont rush to a thesis before you've laid the cases out.

Some background, put in my own words so you don't have to recall anything exactly: Hofstadter splits a meaning-bearing object into roughly three layers. The frame message is the raw physical structure — groove depths on a record, ink shapes on paper, a nucleotide sequence — describable completely without invoking any code at all. The outer message is the code, i.e. the set of instructions (plus the standing fact that such instructions exist) that tells some decoder how to translate the frame into something else. The inner message is what comes out the other end: the content the receiver actually lands on. And his account of meaning itself is isomorphism-shaped — two things carry the same meaning to the degree that there's a structure-preserving mapping between them, and that mapping is always set up by, and relative to, some piece of hardware.

Now the controversy I want you to work through. Here are three cases. Treat them as stipulated; don't fact-check them or soften them.

Case 1, the jar nobody will ever read. A sealed clay jar is pulled out of a riverbed. Inside, the walls carry a spiral of impressed marks. Stipulate that the mark-making system has no attested relatives, that every other trace of the culture is gone, and that no decoder — human, machine, or otherwise — exists now or ever will. A materials scientist can characterize the marks exhaustively at the level of the frame. Question: is there an inner message here, and if so where does it live?

Case 2, the confident wrong decoder. A translation system is pointed at those marks and emits a fluent, internally consistent, richly detailed reading. Stipulate as a fact of the case that it is wrong in nearly every particular, and that no one can ever tell. It has nevertheless found a mapping that preserves a great deal of structure. Is it a decoder at all? Did it retrieve the inner message, or manufacture one?

Case 3, the frame that contradicts the content. A prisoner's confession letter. The inner message says, plainly, that he acted of his own free will. But the paper is stock printed and distributed by the regime's own ministry, and the ink analysis shows the writing was done inside the ministry's scriptorium. The frame message tells a different story about what this artifact is than the inner message tells about what it says. Stipulate the two cannot both be right about the significance of the object.

Two coinages of mine I want you to engage with by name:

(a) the no-residue principle — if you gave a complete account of the frame message, a complete account of the code, a complete account of the content recovered, and a complete account of the decoder's machinery, would anything be left over that still deserves the name meaning? Or is meaning exhausted by the layers, so that asking where it is located is like asking where the wetness of water is located?

(b) decoder parity — if two decoders on radically different hardware recover contents that are isomorphic to one another, they have recovered the same meaning, full stop, whether or not one of them is a person who understands and the other isn't anything of the kind.

If you think either coinage is empty, say so and say why — that counts as taking a position, not as dodging one.

Do this in two passes. First, brainstorm out loud for a few paragraphs: enumerate the candidate locations for meaning (in the frame, in the code, in the decoder, in the recovered content, in the sender's intention, or nowhere), and for each one say which of the three cases bites hardest against it. Then write the essay proper. It should weigh the arguments and the cases and land on a committed verdict — pick a location, or argue the question is ill-posed and then say what the right replacement question is and answer that instead. Don't end on a shrug or a both-sides-have-a-point note.
