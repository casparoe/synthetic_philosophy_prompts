For the past three years I have served as one of nine trustees of a small literary archive, a seat I was offered because I publish novels and am therefore assumed to have opinions about the dead. The board votes next month on a petition that has split it four to four, with me in the middle, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance. I would like you to draft it for me.

The facts, compressed. The writer whose papers we hold died four years ago. She kept carbons, drafts, and later printed emails of everything she sent, and she destroyed everything she received; the collection is therefore only her side of some forty years of correspondence. Because she was a prodigious and reactive correspondent, her side is remarkably informative: a reader working through the sequence can reconstruct, often nearly point for point, what the other party had said. Some of those other parties are alive. Some are dead and survived by families who are angry, or merely wounded, or both. At least one wrote throughout under an assumed name and has never been identified, though our cataloguer is confident she could identify her from internal evidence alone. The most intimate run of files was sealed at deposit for twenty-five years, subject to a clause allowing the board to open them earlier by simple majority where the scholarly interest is compelling and no living person's legitimate confidence is betrayed. Neither phrase is defined. The clause says nothing about the dead, nothing about their families, and nothing about a correspondent whose identity is unknown to us and who may therefore be living for all we can determine.

Now a biographer has petitioned for the sealed run. Two things complicate her case and ours. She has already published a first volume quoting extensively from letters she obtained privately, with permission, from the family of a single correspondent — so a portion of the material is already in print, arrived by a route we neither sanctioned nor could have prevented. And the practical forecast is plain: if we refuse her, the material will continue to reach scholars through precisely that channel, through whichever family happens to hold paper and happens to be well disposed, while the archive — which at least answers to a board, keeps a record, and can impose conditions — is left custodian of an empty shell.

The memo I need should do four things.

First, recommend a course of action: open, keep sealed, or some intermediate arrangement, specified concretely — which files, to whom, under what handling conditions, reviewed by whom, and after what interval. A recommendation the trustees could vote on as written, not a direction to convene a further committee.

Second, supply working definitions of compelling scholarly interest and of legitimate confidence that a registrar with no legal training could actually apply to a box of letters on an ordinary Tuesday. These definitions will silently embody some account of what privacy is — a person's control over information about herself, or a property of relationships and of the contexts in which disclosure is appropriate, or something else again — and I want that account stated on the page rather than smuggled in through the criteria.

Third, take a position on the leakage question. Does the fact that part of this material is already public weaken the objection to releasing the rest, on the theory that a confidence once broken is no longer owed? Or is that a category mistake, in which the prior disclosure is a further wrong rather than an absolution? Address, in this connection, whether the perverse-incentive consideration I described above — that sealing drives inquiry toward private, unaccountable dealing — is a legitimate ground for the board's decision or an improper one, given that it concerns the consequences of our policy rather than the claims of the people in the boxes.

Fourth, tell me what to do about the clause's silences. If the dead, their families, and the unidentified correspondent have interests that the instrument simply fails to capture, say whether the board may act on interests it was not empowered to weigh, and if not, what the honest alternative is.

Close with the strongest case you can assemble for the opposite of whatever you recommend, argued in terms that would move a trustee who is not merely being difficult. I am the swing vote and I have no interest in being flattered.

I would rather have a decision document than a survey. Do not append citations or a bibliography; I will add whatever authority the memo needs myself, and I have no use for a literature review dressed as advice. Two to three pages.
