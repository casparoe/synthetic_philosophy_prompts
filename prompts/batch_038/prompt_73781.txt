I am the director of evidence and impact at a private foundation that makes roughly 120 grants a year in global health and agricultural development. Every grant memo ends with a field our charter requires: "net expected benefit," defined in the charter as the grant's effects on wellbeing over fifty years, discounted at three percent annually, with anything beyond year fifty entered as zero. My predecessor filled the field with a figure extrapolated from the applicant's own monitoring data. I have come to think that the horizon, the discount rate, and the extrapolation habit are doing the real work of making an unsolvable problem look solved: for a meaningful minority of our grants, the long-run consequences are not merely hard to estimate but of unknown sign, and some of them — releasing a technology, changing who holds paid work in a district, altering an ecosystem — cannot be undone once done. At the same time, declining to fund is itself a choice with consequences I can trace no better, and I have no interest in a framework whose practical upshot is paralysis.

Please write me a decision procedure my program officers can actually run: one page, about two hours per grant, usable by someone who has never studied decision theory, with every term defined in a clause at first use. Build it on whatever the debate over consequentialist cluelessness has genuinely established rather than on a taxonomy invented for convenience, but give me no bibliography, no citations, and no reading list — refer to positions by name only where naming them changes what an officer does.

The procedure needs the following.

First, a sorting step that classifies proposals by the kind of uncertainty they present. I would expect at least three classes: effects that are hard to estimate but bounded and stable in sign; effects whose sign is unknown but whose magnitude is bounded; and effects that are irreversible or unbounded, where the causal trace leaves any region we can reason about at all. Tell me whether that is the right cut, and if it is not, replace it and say why. Note that our own charter already contains a materiality cutoff — anything smaller than two percent of projected direct effect is not entered — and tell me whether a cutoff like that is a legitimate simplification or a way of smuggling in a verdict.

Second, for each class, the questions the officer asks, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure terminates. Permitted terminations: fund; do not fund; fund at reduced scale; fund in reversible stages with a pre-committed rule governing expansion; or, this cannot be settled on expected-benefit grounds and must be escalated to the board as a values decision. I want to know which termination is the modal outcome — if most proposals do not clear quickly, the procedure will be ignored within a year.

Third, what goes in the "net expected benefit" field for each class, given that the charter requires an entry. Propose what an honest entry looks like when no number can be defended, and say how we keep a board member skimming the memo from reading that honest entry as an implicit endorsement.

Fourth, an explicit treatment of the omission asymmetry. The procedure must not treat only the intervention's consequences as requiring justification while letting the counterfactual of inaction pass unexamined. Show me where that discipline lives in the steps, or concede that it does not.

Fifth, a statement of which error the procedure is calibrated to commit more often — refusing defensible work or approving indefensible work — and why that is the right error for an institution with our endowment and our grantees' dependence on us.

Sixth, an anti-gaming section. Officers learn the procedure and want their proposals funded. Name the two or three moves by which a proposal gets pushed into the permissive class, and the checks that catch each.

Seventh, at every step, a mark indicating whether the step is settled by evidence or is a value choice the board must own. Do not let discount rates, horizons, or materiality thresholds present themselves as technical.

Then run the procedure on three proposals.

One: contracting with regional mills to fortify salt with iodine and iron, with strong trial evidence on anemia and child cognitive outcomes, low cost per beneficiary, and plausible but unmeasured effects on household schooling and fertility decisions.

Two: our flagship, funded ten years running — a district-wide cadre of salaried, trained, and equipped community health workers drawn from the villages they serve. Mortality effects are well documented. The knock-on effects are not: on local labor markets, on the standing of the young women who hold these posts, on age at marriage, on trust in public clinics, on who later stands for local office. Several of these are sign-unknown; all are bounded by the size of one district.

Three: underwriting the development of a drought-tolerant staple variety and its release as unrestricted public germplasm — no intellectual property, available to any breeder or seed company, not retrievable once in farmers' fields. Potentially very large benefits; potentially irreversible harms through genetic narrowing, displacement of landraces, and pest adaptation.

I expect at least one of these to come back with a verdict the board will not like, and I expect it to be the flagship. If your procedure does not produce an unwelcome result for proposal two, tell me whether that is because the case genuinely clears or because the procedure has been built to clear it. For the unwelcome verdict, lay out the options honestly: reduce scale, restructure into reversible stages, or re-describe the justification in terms the charter does not currently recognize — fairness, capability, institutional duty — and tell me whether that last move is intellectually respectable or a dignified surrender.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do. Which of the things I have asked for is it quietly not delivering? What would a critic who holds that all of this is preference dressed as method say about my specific design, and what is the strongest reply available to me?
