I'm a third-year econ PhD student working on choice under ambiguity — states, acts, consequences, preference orderings, choice functions, feasible sets, and basically nothing else. A friend in the philosophy department roped me into a reading group on the ethics of nuclear weapons, and we've just spent two weeks on Anscombe: the campaign she ran in 1956 against Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree, the pamphlet she put out for it, and then the 1958 paper where she coins the word consequentialism and argues the whole post-Sidgwick tradition amounts to one and the same thing. I can follow her, but I can't hold the argument in my head in my own terms, which means I keep nodding along in seminar without actually knowing what I'd be committing to. So: translate her position into my vocabulary, as literally as you can manage, and then give me the residue report.

Here's the position as I understand it, so you don't have to guess what I'm pointing at. (i) Choosing to kill the innocent is never right, whatever good it produces — no amount of weighing gets you across that line, because the line isn't a weight. (ii) In Hiroshima and Nagasaki the civilian deaths weren't a regrettable side-effect of destroying a city; killing the population was the mechanism by which the intended end (surrender, and so avoiding an invasion) was supposed to be produced, so the intended/merely-foreseen distinction doesn't rescue the decision — it convicts it. (iii) Therefore Truman committed murder, and honouring him is not neutral: conferring the degree is itself a kind of participation in it, which is why she thought the vote mattered and not just the bombing. (iv) Consequentialism isn't a rival decision procedure that happens to reach different verdicts; it's a rationalisation that gets deployed precisely when the act can't be defended any other way, and the appeal to the balance of consequences is itself a symptom. (v) Doing and allowing aren't morally equivalent, so the "but if you don't bomb, far more people die in the invasion" move doesn't have the shape she thinks its proponents assume it has. Underwrite all of that with her account of action: acts are individuated by the descriptions under which they're intentional, not by the physical events they cause.

Now the asks. Go claim by claim, and for each say whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all in my framework. Specifically: which of these become constraints on the feasible set rather than terms in the objective function, and does that move already cheat, given that a constraint on a choice set is behaviourally indistinguishable from a sufficiently strong preference and revealed preference flattens the two together? Does the intended/foreseen distinction have any purchase on act-individuation — my tools individuate acts by their state-contingent outcome maps, so two courses of action with the same outcome map are the same act for evaluative purposes, whereas she needs the identical physical outcome to be murder under one description and not under another; is there anything in the toolbox (Sen's rational fools move, commitment as against sympathy, or writing the choice problem with acts described causally rather than consequentially) that gives her a slot, or is the framework structurally blind here? How should I render the absoluteness — lexicographic ordering, infinite disutility, hard constraint — and what does each of those quietly smuggle in? Can her point about consequentialism-as-rationalisation become anything about motivated reasoning, endogenous preference formation, or the fact that the analyst picks which choice problem to write down, or is it simply inexpressible? And where does the complicity claim about the degree go, given that conferring it changes no one's outcome? Finally give me your verdict, ranked: which parts of the residue are a genuine expressive limitation of my vocabulary, and which are evidence that her position is doing something other than what it looks like it's doing. Please don't smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call it translation — above all, don't render 'never permissible' as 'very costly' and then congratulate yourself on a clean mapping.
