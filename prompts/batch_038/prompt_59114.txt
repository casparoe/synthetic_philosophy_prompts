Consider the hitch-hiker case Parfit uses to raise the possibility of rational irrationality: stranded and needing a ride, you get one only because you promise to send the driver money once you reach town, and afterwards keeping that promise serves no interest of yours at all. Now add two stipulations. The driver can tell with near certainty whether you intend to keep your word. And the encounter is provably one-off — you will never meet him again, no one will ever learn what you did, and nothing in your future turns on your being known as a promise-keeper.

Give me your credence that, so described, it is rational all things considered for you to send the money. Then decompose it: the probability that intentions can be rationally assessed independently of the acts they issue in; the probability that an appeal to dispositions, or to what kind of agent you are, does genuine explanatory work here rather than smuggling in a covert appeal to other cases like this one; the probability that stipulating transparency plus a one-off encounter actually blocks that smuggling; the probability that all-things-considered rationality includes promise-keeping reasons that are not interest-relative to begin with; and any further terms you think belong. A number and one line of justification for each, then how they combine.

Finally, say which term the total is most sensitive to and what argument would move it — and whether your decomposition must deliver a different verdict in the toxin-style variant, where the reward attaches only to having the intention and nothing whatever turns on acting on it.
