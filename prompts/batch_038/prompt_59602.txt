Bertrand Russell's illustration of the multiplicative axiom, in his Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy, has become the standard way I encounter the axiom of choice being introduced: faced with infinitely many pairs of shoes, you can specify a selection rule, namely take the left shoe from each pair; faced with infinitely many pairs of socks, you cannot, since nothing distinguishes the two members of a pair, and so some further principle is needed to guarantee that a selection exists at all. In my department this picture is put to work in two opposite directions. One lecturer deploys it to argue that the axiom is wholly unobjectionable, on the ground that anyone who denies that you may take one sock from each of infinitely many pairs has simply lost touch with what choosing amounts to. A philosophically inclined classmate deploys the very same picture to argue the reverse: that the socks case is precisely where the axiom shows its teeth, because it asserts the existence of a completed infinite collection of selections for which no rule can be given, while the shoe case is a comforting special case that trades on our habits with finite collections.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the axiom. Specifically:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly. What in the socks-and-shoes story plays the role of the family of nonempty sets, of the choice function, of the union, and of the absence of a defining property? Mark which parts of the mapping are exact and which are merely suggestive.

2. Identify what the picture quietly imports from its source that the target lacks: an agent, a succession of acts ordered in time, physical objects whose mutual indistinguishability is an empirical rather than a structural fact, and above all the finitary intuition that a selection carried out pair by pair can be finished. Say which of these imports actually damages the argument the analogy is being used to support, and which are harmless decoration.

3. Take up the two places where I suspect the analogy misleads most. First, the shoe case is usually presented as showing that a choice principle is sometimes dispensable; is the relevant difference genuinely a matter of whether we can state a rule, given that in ordinary set theory the sets whose existence is asserted are not required to be definable at all? Second, the socks case is treated by one side as obviously legitimate and by the other as the crux of the difficulty; is the claim that one sock may be taken from each of countably many pairs in fact demonstrable without any form of the choice principle, and what does the answer imply about which of my two partisans is reading the illustration correctly?

4. Explain whether the analogy equips anyone to anticipate the consequences that actually make the axiom contentious — the well-ordering of arbitrary sets, and the decomposition of a sphere that underlies the Banach–Tarski paradox — or whether those consequences are simply invisible from inside the picture, and if so, what feature of the picture hides them.

5. Conclude by saying which use of the analogy survives the audit, if either; what the socks-and-shoes comparison can and cannot be made to carry about the truth or the acceptability of the axiom; and, if the picture is doing more harm than good, what a better illustration would be — or why no such illustration is available.
