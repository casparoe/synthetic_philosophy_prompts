For thirty-nine years I was a corrosion chemist at a national laboratory, where my working life consisted of proposing mechanisms, finding that several fitted the data equally well, and then designing the one experiment that would separate them. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my family finds comfortable, and I have been reading in the literature on political obligation — on whether there is a general duty to obey the law, and on when the breach of it can be anything other than wrongful. I did not expect that reading to become practically urgent. It has, because for six years I have chaired the board of a small private foundation, endowed in 2003 by a labor lawyer whose deed of trust directs us to make one award each year for "an act of civic conscience undertaken at personal cost" and leaves that phrase entirely undefined. The award is a medal, seven thousand five hundred dollars, and reimbursement of the recipient's fines, restitution and legal costs. There are seven of us: two lawyers, a journalist, a retired headteacher, a hospital chaplain, an architect, and me. Two members hold that because the award is institutional and public it necessarily commends lawbreaking as such, and that a general obligation to obey the law forbids us ever to make it; the other five hold that the obligation is conditional on the state's own conduct and that the deed anticipates exactly such cases. We meet twice a year, we have never agreed on a written standard, and I have come to suspect that we have been applying one without knowing which.

Our record, so far as it bears on this year's decision, is six cases. (1) 2007, awarded 6–1: nine residents, average age sixty-eight, occupied the hearing room of the county commissioners during the scheduled vote to close the district's only public clinic, after two years of petitions; the vote could not be taken; they were arrested, pleaded guilty and paid their fines. The minute records only that the action "was addressed to those responsible for the grievance." (2) 2010, declined 7–0: a lone protester drove steel spikes into marked trees on public land at night, intending that the blades of whoever felled them be ruined; the harvest was suspended; he was never identified and afterwards described what he had done to a magazine. The minute reads that the board "could not commend an act whose author declined to answer for it." (3) 2012, awarded 5–2: a technician at a state environmental laboratory who documented that her supervisors were altering assay results copied the records, gave them to a newspaper, identified herself in writing, was prosecuted under the statute she had broken, convicted, fined, stripped of her certification, and paid rather than appealed. (4) 2016, declined 4–3: a coalition blocked a state highway for eleven consecutive days to protest a pipeline route that had already survived two judicial reviews, announcing each day's action in advance and accepting arrest each morning; commuters, freight operators and two ambulances were delayed, and an administrative appeal that was available to the coalition had never been filed. The minute says only that the board found the means "out of proportion to the end sought" — four of us understood that to concern who was made to bear the cost, three of us understood it to concern the choice of a target that was not the pipeline at all. (5) 2019, awarded 4–3: three activists contesting a drilling permit dismantled and carried away a valve assembly at a wellhead owned by the permit holder, having notified the company and the state police the previous afternoon; they remained at the site, were charged with criminal mischief, accepted suspended sentences and paid eleven thousand dollars in restitution. The majority's minute says the act "was aimed at the instrument of the contested decision and at no one else," which I now notice does not distinguish two things that might be distinguished. (6) 2023, awarded 4–3: a one-day occupation of the lobby of the bank that held the mortgage on a contested development site, which turned away perhaps two hundred customers; the occupiers announced it, stayed past closing, accepted citation. The three dissenters objected precisely that the customers were strangers to the quarrel; the majority recorded that the inconvenience was "slight, brief, and unavoidable in any action visible enough to matter." The divisions have not been stable across these cases, and I cannot tell whether that is because my colleagues are inconsistent or because the cases differ in ways I have failed to isolate.

This year's nomination is before us next month. A county has been under a federal consent decree for four years requiring it to remedy sewage, overcrowding and the denial of medical care at its jail; a state-appointed monitor has twice reported missed deadlines; the county's response has been to approve a thirty-four million dollar expansion of jail capacity while deferring the decree work, and a contempt hearing is set eleven months from now. A group of five — a retired schoolteacher, two graduate students, a plumber and a man who until recently inspected pressure vessels for a living — testified at hearings, filed comments, and worked on two failed ballot measures. On a Saturday night in April they entered the expansion's construction site, which was empty, and poured a quick-setting compound into the engines of two tower cranes and a concrete pump. They left a signed statement taped to the site office door setting out the decree and the monitor's findings, telephoned the local newspaper at six the next morning, and presented themselves at the sheriff's office on Monday. They are charged with burglary and criminal mischief; three have pleaded guilty and received suspended sentences, restitution and three hundred hours of community service, which they have performed; two await trial and say they will not contest the facts. The equipment belonged not to the county but to the general contractor, a local firm whose project manager has stated that the company holds no view on the jail controversy; the damage is assessed at one hundred eighty-six thousand dollars, work stopped for nine days, forty-one workers lost hours, and the county says the delay will cost it one point nine million dollars. Nobody was injured or placed at risk of injury; the group chose the weekend for that reason and says so.

I should like a memorandum, and I should like it to reason from our record rather than from the literature. First: set out as precisely as you can the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with all six decisions, distinguishing those the record actually requires from those it merely permits, and identifying any pair of principles that have been travelling together in our cases and would come apart in a new one — I suspect the 2019 minute fuses at least two. Second: for each candidate principle, state what it implies about this year's nomination; a table of the six decisions against the candidate principles, with the nomination as the final row, would suit me well. Third: tell me which of our six decisions is the closest analog and exactly where the analogy gives out. Fourth: give me your single best recommendation on what we should decide if we wish to be consistent with ourselves, and — since I expect the record will not settle it — state in a sentence or two of language fit for the minutes what principle we would be adopting by awarding and what we would be adopting by declining. Fifth: if no single set of principles in fact fits all six decisions, say so plainly, identify the outlier, and tell me what it would cost us to keep it. Sixth, and this is the part my old habits make me most eager for: describe the hypothetical future nomination, with its facts specified closely enough to be decidable, that would most cleanly separate the two leading candidate principles, and record in advance what each principle implies about it. Finally, a short paragraph on the disagreement between our two camps about the general duty to obey the law: whether an institutional award commends the breach of law as such, or only this breach on these conditions, and whether that dispute is substantive or a dispute about what the word "commend" covers. Please do not supply citations, references or a reading list — I have read enough of it, and a model or a scholar guessing at my bibliography is of no use to me. Equally, do not invent precedents we did not have, and do not tell me what a reasonable board might do in general; tell me what this board, with these six decisions behind it, should do next month.
