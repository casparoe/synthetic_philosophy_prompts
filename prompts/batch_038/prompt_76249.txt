I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, drafting the second chapter of my dissertation on whether the provenance of an object can bear on its aesthetic properties. Before I commit my own framing to paper, I would like the underlying conceptual issue explained to me at a level of precision suitable for a specialist reader. I know the literature well, so please do not supply a bibliography, page references, or quotations; what I need is the architecture of the arguments, laid out so that I can see which premises are load-bearing.

The knot is this. In his discussion of forgery, Goodman argues that a forged painting which nobody can visually distinguish from the original may nonetheless differ from it aesthetically, and that the inability to tell them apart therefore does not show that the difference between original and forgery is aesthetically irrelevant. Against this stands a family of objections holding that aesthetic properties supervene on the perceptible properties of an object, so that two objects which are perceptually indiscernible cannot differ aesthetically, whatever else differs between them — their histories, their makers, our beliefs about them. I want to understand precisely where these two positions make contact and where they merely talk past one another.

For the chapter I have been working with a three-way distinction among the reasons authenticity might be said to matter aesthetically:

(1) **Discriminability in principle.** Even if we cannot now tell original from forgery, it does not follow that nothing is there to be told; with training, comparison, or better instruments the difference might become perceptible, and the mere possibility of such a difference is itself a reason to look differently at the two objects now.

(2) **Trace or evidential value.** The perceptible features of the original — the handling of paint, the sequence of decisions they register — are causal traces of a particular artistic performance, and seeing them as traces rather than as copies of traces alters what we attend to and how we organize our attention.

(3) **Achievement.** Value resides in the artist's having solved a problem, and this may not show up in the perceptible surface at all; the forgery, however indistinguishable, is the product of a different and lesser kind of achievement.

My suspicion is that these are not independent, that (1) may covertly depend on (2), and that Goodman's argument is only as strong as whichever of them it is really leaning on. Please address the following, in roughly this order:

1. Reconstruct the argument and the supervenience objection so that the point of conflict is explicit. Identify what has to be meant by "perceptible" on each side — perceptible by a given viewer at a given time, or perceptible by someone under some achievable conditions — and show whether the objection and the reply are genuinely incompatible once that ambiguity is resolved, or whether one side wins by stipulation.

2. Say what Goodman's position is on whether aesthetic properties supervene on perceptible properties at all, and whether the forgery argument requires him to reject that supervenience or only to reject a particular way of specifying its base. This is where I suspect the standard presentation of the debate is vaguer than it should be.

3. Assess each of my three reasons in turn for what premises it needs, and then answer the harder question: what survives of the argument if I strike out (1) and (2) and keep only (3)? Is an argument built solely on achievement still an argument about *aesthetic* properties, or has it silently shifted to a claim about artistic or historical value — and what would it take to hold that line?

4. Consider a case of my own devising, and tell me whether it helps or hurts. Suppose the forgery is produced by a process that replicates the original not merely in appearance but in its physical microstructure — the layering, the cracking, the chemical composition — so that no instrument, however refined, could ever discriminate it from the original. Nothing discoverable remains, so the "in principle discriminable" move is unavailable, and the two objects differ only in a causal history that leaves no trace in either. What resources remain for holding that they differ aesthetically? Is this case a *reductio* of the Goodman-style argument, or is it a case the argument was never meant to cover, and if the latter, how should the scope of the claim be stated so that the restriction is principled rather than ad hoc?

5. Explain the work being done by the distinction between autographic and allographic art forms, and whether that distinction is derived from the forgery argument or presupposed by it. Then take a form that seems to sit badly with the criterion: works realized by other hands from the artist's written instructions, in which authorized executions are distinguished from unauthorized ones not by any property of what ends up on the wall but by a historical relation of authorization. What does a case of that kind show about whether the criterion is about art *forms* or about individual *works*, and what revision, if any, does it force?

6. Finally, turn the analysis on my own apparatus: say where the three-way distinction is unstable, whether it carves the problem at anything like its joints, and whether case (4) is smuggling in an assumption that makes the conclusion look more surprising than it is. I would rather find this out now than from a referee.

One further request about how you frame the answer. Please distinguish clearly throughout between what is settled or near-universally granted among people who work on this and what remains genuinely contested — for instance, whether aesthetic properties supervene on perceptual ones at all, whether relational or historical properties can be aesthetic properties, and whether the argument I have sketched is generally regarded as having succeeded. Where a position I might otherwise take for granted as background is in fact a minority or disputed view, flag it as such rather than presenting it as the consensus, and say which side of the dispute you are implicitly taking whenever your explanation has to assume something.
