Been going down a rabbit hole on Zhuangzi's butterfly thing, mostly because someone on a forum I lurk on claimed it's basically the oldest surviving statement of the underdetermination problem, and I couldn't quite tell whether that's right or whether he was just pattern-matching.

Here's the bit as I've got it in my head from some translation I read years ago: he dreams he's a butterfly, flitting about, completely absorbed in being a butterfly, with no awareness that he's Zhuangzi. Then he wakes and he's unmistakably Zhuangzi again. Then the worry: was it Zhuangzi dreaming he was a butterfly, or is it now a butterfly dreaming it's Zhuangzi? But he adds that between Zhuangzi and a butterfly there must be some distinction, and calls that the transformation of things.

My read has been that the skeptical part does all the work — the two hypotheses are evidentially symmetrical, so there's no fact you could appeal to, and the transformation-of-things line is a poetic shrug tacked on at the end, basically conceding the skepticism while saying it doesn't matter. Then later in the same chapter he says something to the effect that you only realize you were dreaming once you wake, and there's a greater awakening after which you see this whole life as one big dream, except fools think they're already awake — and then he undercuts it by saying that he himself, in telling you you're dreaming, is also dreaming. I've been calling that move the ladder-kicking problem, since it seems to saw off the branch he's sitting on.

So: is my model right that this is a global skeptical argument with a self-refuting escape clause, i.e. brain-in-a-vat with better prose? And if the transformation-of-things line isn't just decoration, what is it actually doing — supplying the missing premise that breaks the symmetry, or making a separate claim about identity that doesn't touch the epistemology at all?
