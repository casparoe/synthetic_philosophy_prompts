In a seminar paper I'm revising, an objector argues that Moore's proof of an external world is structurally identical to the bootstrapping pattern that epistemologists now widely reject. That pattern: you consult a fuel gauge, take its indication at face value, infer that the tank is full and hence that the gauge got it right on that occasion, iterate enough times, and thereby conclude that the gauge is reliable — apparently generating knowledge of its trustworthiness out of nothing but its own outputs. On the objector's reading, Moore's holding up of his hands plays the role of the gauge readings, and the conclusion that external objects exist plays the role of the bootstrapped reliability claim, so the proof fails for exactly the reason the gauge reasoning does.

Please audit the analogy rather than the proof. First, the correspondence map: what plays the gauge, the readings, the reliability conclusion, and the independent check that is missing in each case. Second, sort the disanalogies into those that actually undercut the objector's inference and those that are merely decorative — in particular, whether the vice bootstrapping is accused of (warrant failing to transmit, or knowledge arriving too easily) is the same vice as begging the question against a sceptic who disputes the premises. Third, whether the verdict of failure survives the audit, and whether the analogy licenses anything about Moorean anti-sceptical reasoning in general; if it misleads, say what comparison I should be teaching with instead and how much weight that one can bear.
