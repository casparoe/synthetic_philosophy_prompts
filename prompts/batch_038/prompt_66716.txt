Consider the airworthiness certification regime for large transport aircraft: the U.S. rule at 14 CFR 25.1309 and its European counterpart, together with the industry safety-assessment guidance used to demonstrate compliance with them. The rule is graded rather than absolute. Each "catastrophic failure condition" must be "extremely improbable" and must not result from a single failure; each "hazardous failure condition" must be "extremely remote"; each "major failure condition" must be "remote." The regulatory vocabulary is qualitative, but the engineering guidance that operationalizes it attaches numerical ceilings per flight hour — on the order of 10⁻⁹ for catastrophic conditions, 10⁻⁷ for hazardous ones, 10⁻⁵ for major ones. A substantial share of the compliance findings are made not by the authority but by personnel employed by the manufacturer under delegation. The regime then issues a certificate for each type on that basis, and maintains a parallel machinery of continued airworthiness: mandatory service reporting, airworthiness directives issued after the fact when a design turns out to have been mis-assessed, and instructions for continued airworthiness written into the certificate itself.

Two features of this arrangement strike me as philosophically loaded, and I want them pulled apart. First, it is a lottery structure. No individual ceiling is zero, the ceilings apply condition by condition across a large catalogue of hazards, and the regime does not govern many of the actual causes of accidents at all — weather, crew error, maintenance error, unlawful interference. Aggregated across a global fleet flying tens of millions of flights a year, the expected number of fatal outcomes is a known, published, non-zero quantity, and it is met year after year. Second, it is a preface structure. Every individual finding in a safety assessment is defensible on its own evidence, while the assessors plainly do not take the conjunction of all their findings across all certified types to be true — which is why the directive machinery exists at all.

I am writing about the epistemology of regulatory practice and I want a careful reconstruction of what this regime presupposes, not a summary of the aviation-safety literature. Please address the following.

What account of the relation between high probability and outright belief does the practice presuppose? A threshold view on which sufficiently high probability warrants belief, plus closure of rational belief under conjunction, is jointly inconsistent here in the familiar way; say which of the two the regime gives up, or whether it gives up neither because it is not in the business of belief at all. That last possibility deserves sustained treatment: does a certificate assert that the aircraft is safe, or does it merely license an operation? If the operative attitude is pragmatic acceptance rather than belief, what is left of the puzzle — and what in the regime (the public assurance that a type is safe, the legal liability that attaches to a certification finding, the passengers' reliance) forces the doxastic reading back in?

What does the graduated scale presuppose about value? The ceilings are indexed to a severity class and to the flight hour, not to the number of lives exposed; the same 10⁻⁹ governs a condition aboard a four-engine aircraft and aboard a narrow-body. Say whether that indexing is committed to an aggregative calculus of expected harm, to a per-instance constraint on how much risk may be imposed on any one set of occupants, or to something else, and whether the practice distinguishes in any way between those who board and those who are underneath the flight path.

Does the continued-airworthiness machinery resolve the coherence problem or aggravate it? Read one way, the regime never asserts the conjunction: it asserts each finding separately and builds in a correction channel, which is exactly the move that dissolves the preface paradox. Read another way, the correction channel is itself probabilistically budgeted and depends on failures actually occurring and being correctly diagnosed. Which reading does the institutional design support?

Then tell me which rival views the practice rules out rather than merely sits uneasily with: an infallibilist or knowledge-first norm on what may be certified; a strict consistency requirement on rational belief; a zero-risk requirement; and the denial of conjunction closure as a general solution. Be disciplined about the distinction between what the regime strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories of the probability-belief relation would license exactly the same engineering behavior and the same documents. Do not force the regime to hold a theory it does not hold.

Finally, run the analysis back against the paradoxes themselves. This is a large, long-running institution that has operated for decades under rules that look Lockean and behave inconsistently in the textbook sense, and it works. Is that evidence that the lottery and preface paradoxes are technical curiosities with no practical traction, or evidence that a systematically incoherent doxastic practice can nonetheless produce reliable outcomes — and if the latter, what does that imply for the argument from such paradoxes to the conclusion that rational belief cannot be governed by a probability threshold at all? Say also what the regime would have to become under a strictly aggregate conception of its aim, and whether that version would be more coherent, more defensible, or simply unadoptable.
