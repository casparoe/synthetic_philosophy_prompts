For eleven years I have sat on the panel that awards our trust's bursaries: about forty a year from roughly twelve hundred applications, decided by six volunteers with no training in economics and ten minutes per file. We rank applicants on parental household income, and I have become convinced this measures the wrong thing. Much of what a family hands on never appears in a death or in an income figure — a deposit on a flat, school and university fees, rent covered during an unpaid internship, a car, a business introduced rather than bequeathed. My own way of putting it, which may be crude, is that advantage should be counted in capital-years: the sum handed over multiplied by the number of years the recipient has had the use of it. On that measure a £100,000 deposit given at twenty-two has done far more work by the time someone is sixty than the same £100,000 arriving when a parent dies at eighty-five, which is nevertheless the transfer most of the debate about taxing estates is focused on. (I know a gift made within seven years of death can still be caught, but the money that matters is usually spent decades earlier.)

Please turn this into a procedure my panel could actually run: the questions to ask of each file in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to estimate capital-years from what an applicant can honestly report without demanding intrusive documentation, and where the procedure must stop and hand the case to a human being because what remains is a question of values. Flag every step that is a judgement call dressed up as a rule. Then apply it to three files of your own construction, including one where it recommends a candidate the panel will resist, and one where a parent gave up their earnings to care for a disabled child, so that no money moved but a great deal was handed on. Say plainly what the procedure cannot do, and whether ranking people by what their families gave them is coherent in a scheme whose whole point is to help those who received least. No references or reading list — I want the reasoning, not the names. Please write in British English.
