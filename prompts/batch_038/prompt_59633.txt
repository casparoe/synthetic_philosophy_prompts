Okay so here's my problem. I'm in a philosophy-of-mind reading group and we've spent the last month on pain, and every single week somebody reached for pain asymbolia as the knockout case, and by the end I'd stopped believing the case was doing the work people think it's doing. The standard picture we all have in mind: patient with a lesion, reports the stimulus, localises it fine, rates its intensity on a scale, describes it in perfectly ordinary sensory terms — but doesn't mind it. Won't withdraw, doesn't guard the limb, may grin or even laugh while it's happening, no avoidance of the source afterwards, no distress on the face. Grahek's framing of it as feeling pain without being in pain is the one our group leaned on hardest. What bugged me is that everybody treats "asymbolia" as one datum, and then the theory of what makes pain bad that you already like gets to explain it however it wants.

So I stayed up too late and made up a taxonomy, splitting it into three stipulative profiles. Don't go looking for these in the literature, they're mine, and part of what I want to know is whether they're actually coherent. Profile A: the sensory stuff is fully intact *and* the patient says the sensation is bothering them right now, but there is zero avoidance, zero facial distress, zero tendency to prefer that it stop when you force a live choice — felt unpleasantness that is causally severed from anything. Profile B: the patient sincerely insists the sensation is not unpleasant at all, no felt badness whatsoever, and yet when you offer them a button that ends it versus a button that keeps it going they reliably press the first one — a bare revealed preference with nothing it feels like to have. Profile C: the patient says the present sensation isn't bad, but becomes visibly distressed as soon as you make them imagine it continuing for a month, or attend closely to its quality for a few minutes — the badness shows up only under prospection and sustained attention.

Given that, rank the main accounts of what makes pain bad by how well they cope with all three profiles at once, in tiers (S down to C or whatever scale you like). The accounts I want ranked: intrinsic hedonism (the raw feel is bad in itself, full stop); evaluativism (unpleasantness is the experience representing a bodily state as bad or damaging); imperativism (the experience has imperative content, roughly "stop this!"); desire/dislike accounts (unpleasantness is the experience's being disliked or motivationally opposed by the subject); the two-component view that splits sensory-discriminative from affective-motivational processing and puts the badness exclusively in the second; and the deflationary line that asymbolics don't have the pain experience proper at all, only a sensory remnant of it. Add any position you think I've left out that would actually move up the table. For each placement give a sentence or two saying which of the three profiles does the damage — I care less about general elegance and more about which account survives which profile. Then give each account a number from 0 to 1 for how much of the total asymbolia evidence it can absorb without straining, where 1 means the profiles are just what you'd predict anyway. Finally tell me straight: is one of my three profiles something no existing account was designed to handle, and does that mean the taxonomy is exposing a real blind spot or just that I've invented a case that's neurologically impossible. No citations needed, I'll fill those in myself, and don't worry about hedging on the empirical side — I'd rather have a committed ordering than a survey.

Lead with the bottom line, meaning the tier list plus the six numbers, in your first sentence or two, and only then give the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
