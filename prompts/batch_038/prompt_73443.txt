You are a philosopher of medicine with substantial experience of sitting on clinical ethics committees, and I would like you to approach what follows as a design problem rather than as an essay. I am a second-year DPhil student, and I co-run a termly seminar on end-of-life law with two consultants from our hospital's palliative care team. Our next session concerns the line that a great many jurisdictions, ours included, have drawn for most of the modern period: withdrawing or refusing treatment, and sedating for symptom relief even where death is foreseen, are permissible, whereas administering a lethal drug at the patient's request is not. The standard philosophical defences of that line are the acts and omissions distinction and the principle of double effect, and the standard attack upon it is Rachels's 1975 argument that the distinction between killing and letting die has no rational basis and that, if anything, the active route is the more humane. My seminar keeps collapsing into a rerun of that old exchange, so what I want is a new case, built to a specification, that forces the defenders of the line to say something they have not said before.

Here is the specification. Construct a single concrete clinical scenario — a named patient on an ordinary ward, no science fiction, no exotic technology, no experimental drugs — in which a regime permitting only the passive options (withdrawal, refusal, palliative sedation) leaves that patient strictly worse off, in total suffering across the whole of their remaining life, than a regime that also permits a lethal injection on request; and in which the double effect reasoning the passive regime invokes to justify its sedation cannot, without patent inconsistency, be extended to justify the injection. The following must be held fixed across the two regimes, so that the difference in verdict cannot be attributed to them: identical diagnosis and prognosis; identical and undisputed decision-making capacity, with no depression, no delirium, no ambivalence and no fluctuation; an identical, sincere and repeatedly expressed request; no coercion, no family pressure, no financial or bed-capacity incentives, no resource scarcity; physicians of identical skill, identical conscientiousness and identical motives, who in both regimes intend precisely the same thing, foresee precisely the same consequences with precisely the same confidence, and work to the same institutional protocol. The extra suffering must fall on the patient rather than on the family or the staff, and it must not come from a botched procedure, administrative delay, a change of heart, a diagnostic error, or a reluctant or incompetent practitioner of the passive route — none of the usual escape hatches. The scenario itself should run to no more than two tight paragraphs.

Before you write it, show me your brainstorming: name three or four candidate clinical vehicles (ventilator withdrawal, deactivation of an implanted cardiac device, voluntarily stopping eating and drinking, continuous deep sedation, and so forth) and say for each which of the constraints above it fails, and why. Only then commit to one and write the case. Afterwards, demonstrate that the design works — state what the acts and omissions distinction says about the choice the passive regime makes, what double effect says about the sedation and why it cannot be stretched to cover the injection, and what a straightforward reckoning of total suffering says — and then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work. Finally, run the two tests I care about most. First, delete that feature and tell me whether the case collapses back into an unremarkable instance of the old Rachels argument. Second, give me the strongest available reply from a defender of the distinction: not a straw man, but the reply that would actually be made in a law journal, together with your assessment of whether it succeeds. I should add what the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I want a scenario whose verdicts are generated by the theories as they actually stand, not one whose verdicts are smuggled in by stipulation. If you conclude that some part of the specification cannot be met, please do not quietly relax it — say which constraint is unsatisfiable, explain why, and describe the nearest workable case instead.
