The exam question was:

> Distinguish the limiting relative frequency theory of probability from the personalist (subjective Bayesian) theory. For each: state its central claim, say how it handles the probability of a single case — one particular coin toss, one particular atom decaying at noon — and identify what you take to be its most serious problem.

Here's the student's answer:

---

The limiting relative frequency theory says that the probability of an attribute A within a reference class B is the limit, as the number of members of B goes to infinity, of the proportion of members of B that are also A. Probability on this view isn't some extra ingredient in the world; it's just a fact about how often things happen, and it's objective in the sense that it doesn't depend on anyone's opinion. Because it's defined as a limit over an infinite sequence, it can't be confirmed by any finite run of experiments, but that's less an objection than a feature: the theory tells us what our finite data are estimates of.

On single cases, the frequentist says there is never really a genuinely single case. A particular coin toss belongs to many classes — tosses of that coin, tosses by that hand, tosses in that room — and the probability of the toss relative to a class is the limit in that class. Von Mises dealt with the arbitrariness this creates by requiring randomness: no gambling system, meaning no rule for selecting a subsequence that doesn't peek at the outcomes, can pick out a subcollective with a different limit. So what you do with a single case is locate it in the maximally specific reference class for which there's a stable limit. That solves the problem: the single case gets a determinate probability once you've found the narrowest class containing it. This is also how the frequentist handles the atom decaying at noon — you look at the class of atoms prepared identically to it, and the decay probability is the limiting relative frequency of decay in that class.

The subjective theory says probabilities are degrees of belief: properties of agents rather than of the world. My credence that the atom decays at noon is just where I stand, and two agents can rationally assign different probabilities to the same event as long as they have different evidence. The formal core of the view is coherence: an agent's degrees of belief have to obey the probability axioms and be updated by conditionalization, because anyone who violates these constraints is vulnerable to a Dutch book — a package of bets that loses money no matter how the world turns out. That shows incoherent credences are simply mistaken, in the same way a belief that violates the law of non-contradiction is mistaken. Single cases are no problem for the subjectivist at all, since there was never any need for an infinite sequence: the probability of the single case just is the agent's degree of belief in it.

Most serious problems. For frequentism, that it makes probabilities permanently untestable and that it looks unable to handle genuinely unique events, like the probability that a particular candidate wins a particular election — though as above the reference-class move blunts this. For subjectivism, the biggest problem is that it makes probability too cheap: coherence constrains only the shape of your beliefs, not their content, so an agent with wildly false views about coins can be perfectly rational by this standard. Relatedly, subjectivism can't account for the probabilities physics gives us. Quantum mechanics says the chance of a given outcome is 0.5 whether or not anyone is around to have a credence about it, so if probabilities are just degrees of belief, quantum mechanics is literally false about the world. That seems decisive, and it's why I think frequentism is the better theory despite its difficulties.
