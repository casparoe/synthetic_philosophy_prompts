You're a moral philosopher who specializes in the ethics of war, but one who's also spent real time talking to service members instead of only to other academics. Take your time on this — I want careful reasoning, not a diplomatic summary that splits the difference.

At a dinner last month two of the guests got into a fight and I've been turning it over ever since. One is my uncle, twenty years in the Army, most of it as a maintenance and fuel NCO. The other is a woman from my wife's philosophy reading group. The trigger was a guy we both know — call him D — who did two tours providing security for supply convoys in a war that most people now think got started on claims that didn't hold up. D is applying to be a school resource officer back home and somebody in town said he ought to be ruled out because of what he took part in.

Here's roughly how it went:

(1) My uncle said the justice of a war is way above a soldier's pay grade. Politicians start wars, soldiers fight them, and once you're in uniform both sides have the same permission to fight and the same exposure to being killed — a man who fights by the rules does nothing wrong even if his side's cause was rotten. He compared it to a boxing match, or a duel: two people who've agreed to the contest, so neither one gets to complain about being hit.

(2) She said that's a rule of law wearing a philosophy costume. Actual morality looks at what you're doing to whom: the getaway driver for a bank robber has no right to shoot the teller, and the teller is entirely within his rights to shoot the driver. So a soldier on the unjust side is doing something wrong, and the men shooting at him aren't committing murder when they do it. She added that my uncle's picture is worse than confused, because it makes serving an aggressor cost-free, which is precisely the incentive you don't want to hand people.

(3) My uncle said she's describing a world where every side privately knows it's the good guy. Tell each soldier that the men across from him are criminals and you've just deleted the last restraint in the field. It's the equal standing of soldiers that makes surrender work, that makes medics and prisoners get treated, that makes people stop shooting when the flag comes down. So even if her story is true in some abstract sense, the convention is doing serious moral work and she wants to rip it out. He also said D was nineteen, had a recruiter, a paycheck and a sick mom, and had no access to whatever intelligence would have told him if the war was justified.

(4) She allowed that might justify the laws of war, but said that's a different question from what's morally true, and that the whole trouble begins when people mistake a useful legal rule for a justification. On the nineteen-year-old she said he was conflating two seperate things: someone who couldn't have known is excusable, but being excused isn't the same as having done nothing wrong, and it sure doesn't make him someone you're forbidden to defend yourself against. She also doubts the history — armies have had norms about prisoners and medics for centuries without needing his theory.

Then my uncle said something like "so you'd have D stand up and say he was in the wrong," and she said no, she'd have him say he fought in a war that was wrong and that he isn't to blame for it, and he said that's a distinction without a difference, and it spiralled from there.

What I want from you:

- Before any verdicts, work out what this disagreement is actually about. My sense is there are at least two distinct questions tangled together and each of them is answering a different one. Say what they are and who's talking past whom.
- Then adjudicate move by move. Rate each of the four numbered moves, plus that final exchange, on a scale from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a couple of sentences justifying each rating. Be willing to say a move is partly good and partly bad, and say which part is which.
- Give me the strongest possible version of move (3) — one where my uncle isn't just defending a convenient fiction but is onto something real about how the moral rule has to be shaped for creatures like us, given what they know and what they're like under fire. Does such a version exist, and does it survive her reply in (4)?
- Is she right that "excused" and "permitted" come apart the way she claims? Test it on a sensor operator who flew the drone from Nevada and never deployed, on a conscript fighting on the defensive side, and on somebody who enlisted *because* he thought the cause was just and turned out to be wrong. Does your answer shift across those three, and if so, what's the variable doing the work?
- Who's more right overall, and what are they both missing? And does either position actually tell the town anything about whether D should get the school job?

Please don't give me a reading list or a bibliography or a bunch of "see so-and-so's book" — I'm not looking anything up and I don't want to have to. Just reason it out. Naming a view or coining a term is fine when it earns its keep, but explain it in plain English the first time.
