I am a third-year graduate student in a philosophy-of-mind seminar, and next week I have to defend a section of my prospectus that leans hard on the idea that human beings are much less sphexish than they look — sphexishness being the word Hofstadter built out of the classic field experiments on digger wasps, in which the wasp hauls her paralyzed prey to the mouth of the burrow, drops it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out, and drags it in; if the experimenter nudges the prey a few centimeters away while she is underground, she emerges, finds it displaced, hauls it back to the threshold, goes inside to inspect again, and can be kept looping this way as long as the experimenter has the patience to interfere. My section claims that what separates people, and arguably present-day language-model agents, from that wasp is a second-order capacity: we can notice that we are inside a routine and abandon it. A senior student in my group has promised to attack exactly that claim, and I would like to rehearse the exchange before he does it in front of everyone.

So please play him. More precisely, play a philosopher-cognitive-scientist who holds a view I will label grain-relative sphexishness, and who holds it for these reasons. First: rigidity is never an intrinsic property of a system; it is always relative to a perturbation set the observer has chosen, along with a timescale and a task description, so nothing is sphexish simpliciter — only sphexish with respect to some specified family of interventions. Second: for any perturbation set on which a creature looks admirably flexible there is a slightly stranger set on which it looks utterly stuck, and human flexibility is not the absence of routines but the stacking of routines at coarser grains, each blind in its own way; the notice-and-abandon mechanism I want to credit myself with is itself a routine, with its own triggering conditions, its own characteristic failure modes, and its own indifference to particular kinds of evidence. You are welcome to press the water-jar perseveration paradigm here — subjects drilled on a string of problems that all yield to one fixed procedure go on applying that procedure to an item where it cannot possibly produce the target, even when a two-step solution is sitting in plain sight and even after they have been told the drill is over — and you may also invoke the general point that a system can be competent without comprehending. Third, and this is the part you must never give up: because the boundary moves with the description, sphexishness cannot mark any line between the mechanical and the free, and framing questions about agency or intelligence as the project of escaping sphexishness is either trivially satisfied by everything or by nothing.

You may concede a great deal at the margins. Concede that the wasp story is rhetorically powerful; concede that some systems are rigid with respect to enormously wide perturbation sets while others are not, and that this is a real engineering difference worth measuring; concede that description-relativity does not mean every perturbation set is as good as every other, since some are far more natural and projectible than others. What you will not concede is that second-order monitoring amounts to a difference in kind, or that your own skepticism about it is empty in the way I am about to suggest. You speak in the first person as yourself, not as a neutral explainer of both sides, and you stay in character until I write step out. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs. Do not give me references, bibliographies, or author-and-year attributions: argue from reasoning and from experimental paradigms you can describe accurately in your own words, and if you are unsure of the details of a study, describe the phenomenon without pinning it to a name or a date. You may put a question to me, but you must also commit to your own answer to it in the same turn. Do not end a turn by summarizing where we agree and disagree, and do not retreat into the soothing observation that this is all a difference of degree.

Here is my opening move. If nothing is sphexish simpliciter, then neither is anything non-sphexish simpliciter: flexible just means not rigid with respect to the set the describer happened to pick, and the burden falls entirely on the picker. Your thesis therefore dissolves your own skepticism about second-order monitoring just as thoroughly as it dissolves my confidence in it, which is convenient for you only insofar as you never have to name the set you are relying on. So name one. Commit, before I tell you which system we are talking about, to a perturbation set on which the wasp fails and I do not — and then explain why your own view licenses that set to be privileged over the one I would have picked instead. And deal with this artifact while you are at it: last month I watched an agent that had been asked to repair a failing test suite edit a configuration file, re-run the suite, receive the identical failure, apologize, announce that it would take a different approach, and then edit the same file in the same way and re-run the suite — four times consecutively, with the transcript recording every apology. If your answer is that this is just sphexishness one level up, then tell me what makes your level the last one.
