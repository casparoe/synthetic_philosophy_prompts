I retired four years ago after thirty-five years as a colloid chemist. My working life consisted, in the main, of measuring how fast particles settle out of suspension, which is to say of using a law that assumes the particles are perfect spheres, that they are far enough apart not to interfere with one another, that the walls of the vessel are infinitely distant, that the flow around each grain is smooth and slow, and that nothing flocculates. Everyone in that building knew every one of those assumptions to be false. We corrected for two of them, ignored the rest, and produced numbers good enough to design equipment with. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science, more or less seriously, and I have assembled the argument below. I believe most of it is my own, though a graduate student in the family tells me that the reasoning from (4) through (5) is a theorem proved in the 1970s against a definition of this notion offered by a well-known philosopher of science, and that the theorem was taken to be fatal to that definition. She could not tell me why the damage stops there. Neither can I.

(1) To say that one theory is closer to the truth than another is to say something about what the theories claim. Theory A is closer to the truth than theory B exactly when every true statement that follows from B also follows from A, and no false statement follows from A unless it already follows from B, with at least one of these holding strictly.

(2) Nothing else could be meant by it. If closeness to the truth were not a matter of which true claims a theory carries and which false claims it carries, then it would not be a relation between a theory and the world; it would be a report about us — about how well a theory suits our instruments, our questions, our appetite for simplicity. I want the phrase to be about the world.

(3) Every theory that has ever done work in the natural sciences is false. To be applied at all, a theory must idealize, and to idealize is to entail falsehoods; and in any case each theory is eventually pushed past the range in which it held.

(4) So take any two theories of real interest, A and B. By (3) both are false, and A is itself among the false statements that follow from A. If A were closer to the truth than B, then by (1) A would also have to follow from B: B entails A, and B is the logically stronger of the two.

(5) But if B entails A, then everything that follows from A follows from B, so A has no true consequences that B lacks — while (1) requires that A have all of B's true consequences, which can now happen only if the two have exactly the same true consequences. And suppose B entails A without A entailing B. Consider the statement that either A does not hold or B does. Since A is false, that disjunction is true. It follows from B, because B follows from itself. Yet it does not follow from A, for A would entail it only by entailing B, which we have just supposed it does not. So here is a true statement that follows from B and not from A, and (1) is violated again. Therefore, for two false theories, A is closer to the truth than B only if A and B are logically equivalent — and equivalent theories cannot be ranked strictly at all.

(6) By (3), every theory of scientific interest is false. So no scientific theory is closer to the truth than any other. Newton's mechanics is not closer to the truth than the physics it displaced, and the picture of the atom I was taught as an undergraduate is not closer to the truth than the one that replaced it. The claim that science approaches the truth is not false so much as empty: no arrangement of the world could make it come out true.

(7) I can see one retreat — say that what improved was accuracy within a domain rather than closeness to the truth. But accuracy is always accuracy over a domain somebody chose, measured with an instrument somebody chose, carried to a number of decimal places somebody chose, and there is no fact of the matter about which domain is the right one to be accurate about. So the retreat does not restore a relation between theory and world. It substitutes a record of our decisions.

I cannot accept (6), and I cannot see where the argument gives way. My instinct is that (5) must be the weak link, since I do not follow the logic of it and since a theorem about arbitrary statements seems to me too strong to be true; but I may simply be flinching, and if it is a theorem I suppose it is not. Of the steps, (3) is the one I would fight hardest to keep, having spent a career on it, and (6) is the one I cannot live with. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please take the argument apart step by step before you commit to a diagnosis — ask of each step what it would take to deny it, and what the denial would leave you holding — and only then tell me where you would put your money. If a step has a name or a history, mention that in passing, but do not send me a reading list; I have no library access and I am not asking for one. And if you conclude instead that the argument is sound and that my reluctance is a survivor of my profession's self-image, say so plainly, and tell me exactly what I am giving up when I say that one of my models was better than my predecessor's.

Answer me as a professional philosopher of science, someone who has actually worked on truthlikeness and on idealization rather than only on the broad shape of scientific change. Write in continuous prose, as one writes a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no boldface, no tables. My steps are numbered for my own convenience; you need not reply in kind. I would much rather have one careful long letter than a tidy outline.
