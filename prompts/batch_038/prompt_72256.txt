I work the claims desk at a fact-checking site and our senior genetics editor signs off on every ruling. Over the past year her verdicts on heritability claims have gone like this:

- "Height is about 80% heritable, so roughly 80% of your height comes from your genes and 20% from what you ate." — false
- "PKU is about as heritable as a trait gets, and it's still almost completely preventable just by changing what a child eats." — true
- "We can take the heritability of educational attainment measured in the Nordic countries and use it to work out how much schooling matters somewhere with wildly unequal schools." — false
- "If every kid got equally good schooling, the heritability of test scores would go up, not down." — true, though she flagged that the piece reporting it implied schools had stopped mattering
- "Two groups each show a heritability of about 0.6 for the same trait and one group averages higher, so most of the gap has to be genetic." — false
- "Twin studies probably come out high because identical twins get treated more alike than fraternal ones." — true but unsettled, she let the hedge stand
- "Numbers built from common DNA variants across the genome usually come in below the twin-study numbers for the same trait." — true
- "The trait is highly heritable, so there's no point funding a program to change it." — false
- "My niece and her identical twin were raised the same way and turned out very different, so heritability for that has to be low." — false
- "Two populations can have identical heritabilities for a trait and still differ in their averages, and two populations with the same average can have different heritabilities." — true

Now on my desk is a magazine piece about adoption. The sentence we have to rule on is basically: you were adopted at birth and never met your biological mother, and because schizophrenia is about 80% heritable in the population you come from, your own odds of developing it track the genes she passed on much more than anything your adoptive parents did.

Where I'm stuck is that its the first claim in the pile that applies a heritability number to one named person while still being, as far as I can tell, statistically defensible — adoption studies really do find risk tracking biological relatives rather than adoptive ones. So:

What rules could she be running that fit all ten verdicts above? For each candidate rule, what does it predict here — false, true, or true-but-misleading — and is there any verdict in the pile that leans toward one rule over another? Give me your best guess at her label plus the reasoning you'd actually put in the note that goes with it. Then tell me whether there's a rewrite of that sentence that every one of those rules would pass, and what seperate claim, if it landed on her desk next, would settle which rule she's really on.

Last bit: if the desk wanted to adopt a house principle explicitly instead of inheriting hers by accident, which of these rules would you argue for, and what would it cost us — i.e. what kinds of accurate claims would we start failing, or bad claims start passing?
