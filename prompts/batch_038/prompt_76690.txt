You are to work as a specialist in Bentham's moral and legal theory — in particular his doctrine of the proper ends and limits of punishment, his critique of the principle of asceticism and of what he calls the principle of sympathy and antipathy, and the extensive posthumous manuscripts in which he argued that same-sex sexual acts should not be criminal offences. The evaluative dimension is **portability**. By Bentham's machinery I mean two distinguishable components: (i) hedonism about value, the thesis that pleasure and exemption from pain are the only intrinsic goods and evils; and (ii) the felicific calculus, the thesis that legislative choice is a cardinal summation of pleasures and pains across persons, weighted by intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity and extent. Portability asks: if the argument is transplanted into a framework that keeps the broadly consequentialist architecture but abandons (i) and (ii) — say a pluralist account of well-being on which knowledge, friendship, autonomy and achievement are non-derivative goods, and on which harms are not reducible to felt pain — does it still do its work, does it do its work only after you substitute a different account of what the harm consists in, or does it collapse?

Here are my paraphrases of the considerations in play. Treat them as mine, not Bentham's words; nothing below is a quotation.

A. Interrogated, disapproval of these acts yields no assignable mischief. The only reason the disapprover can give is that the practice is odious to him, which is a report of an antipathy rather than a justification, so the punishment has no end it can be said to serve.

B. The charge of "unnatural" does no work. Whatever occurs occurs in nature; the accusation is a way of dressing up "this disgusts me" as a claim about the order of things.

C. Where the parties consent and no third party is affected, the conduct falls on the self-regarding side of the ledger, and the parties are ordinarily the best judges of their own interests.

D. The prohibition is inefficacious. A propensity of this strength, and of this early formation, is not extinguished by threats, so the law buys no prevention and pays the full price in suffering.

E. The mischief of the punishment exceeds the mischief of the offence, and much of the harm popularly attributed to the practice — secrecy, blackmail, ruined reputations, despair — is manufactured by the law rather than by the acts.

F. Such an offence can be proved only through an apparatus of informers, entrapment and intrusion into private rooms, which is a standing threat to everyone's security; the law's evidentiary requirements therefore generate a diffuse mischief exceeding that of the conduct they target.

G. The practice has been tolerated and in some societies honoured, and those societies did not dissolve; the near-universality of the aversion is better explained by custom and religious inheritance than by any perception of harm.

H. To the objection that these acts defraud the species: the number of persons so inclined is small, and the inclined are not thereby withdrawn from marriage or reproduction in any quantity a legislator could detect, so the demographic objection fails on its own arithmetic.
