My roommate and I burned most of last night on this and neither of us budged, so I'm outsourcing it.

We were arguing about the Rawls/Nozick-style complaint that utilitarianism doesn't take the distinction between persons seriously — the idea being that you can't justify taking from A for B's benefit by pointing at a bigger social total, because there's no social super-person who receives the compensation. A just loses.

I said that either does real work or it's a slogan. He insisted it does real work, and then, to prove it, he walked me through what happens when you push it hard — and I think he wrecked his own case, but I couldn't say where, and now I've started to suspect he's right and I'm the one who's confused. His argument:

1. All the weight of the objection comes from one claim: a benefit can justify a harm only if there's a single subject in whom both land, someone who ends up better off on balance rather than merely a component of a bigger aggregate.
2. So what's doing the work isn't the boundary between persons at all. It's whether there's such a subject.
3. But on the reductionist picture of persons — the Parfit-style one, where you're a bundle of psychologically continuous states that hold together by degrees and thin out over time — there's no sharp difference in kind between my relation to myself in forty years and my relation to a stranger. Both are matters of degree, and mine fades.
4. So for the me of forty years hence, there's no single subject in whom my present sacrifice and his later benefit both land. He's, in the sense that matters here, someone else.
5. Therefore the objection, applied consistently, condemns saving for retirement, going to the dentist, grinding through a bad year of grad school for the sake of the career after it, and every other trade of present cost for future gain.
6. That's absurd. Nobody thinks prudence is the same kind of thing as exploiting strangers.
7. So (1) isn't the criterion. And whatever criterion actually does the work — consent, or something about whose life it is — will license some interpersonal aggregation too, like cases where the person who bears the cost agreed or would have agreed.
8. Hence separateness of persons doesn't refute utilitarianism's structure. At most it knocks out a few extreme cases and leaves the rest as a matter of degree, which is roughly where the utilitarians already were.

He thinks 8 is where I should land and that the objection is basically aesthetic — a preference for tidiness dressed up as metaphysics. I don't buy that, but I also can't tell him which number to cross out. (3) feels like a premise he's smuggling in on Rawls's behalf, and (5) feels like it's missing something obvious about the dentist case that I can't articulate.

So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost the objector? If it's (3), explain why the objector gets to just refuse reductionism rather than argue against it, and whether that refusal is really available to someone who also wants the intrapersonal case to matter as much as common sense says it does. If it's (5) or (7), tell me what the criterion really is — compensation, justification-to-the-person, agency, something else — and whether that criterion is stable or just a patch applied where the argument got uncomfortable. I want you to commit: pick the step, defend the pick, and tell me how confident you are in it. Don't hedge into a tour of the five possible replies.

And genuinely — if you think my framing is broken somewhere, if the objection isn't really an argument with premises so that I'm asking you to grade a slogan, or if I've mangled what Rawls and Nozick were actually doing, say that plainly rather than playing along politely.
