I run the quantitative risk assessment group at a national nuclear regulator — twenty-two years in the job, most of it spent defending probabilistic safety assessments and backfit cost-benefit cases to review panels. Our published criteria come in two forms. The first is an individual risk criterion: a per-person annual frequency of death, above which risk counts as intolerable, below a much lower level as broadly acceptable, and in between as requiring demonstration that further reduction is not reasonably practicable — adjudicated in practice by a gross-disproportion test weighing the sacrifice against the risk reduction. The second is a societal risk criterion expressed as an F-N curve, where a slope of minus one on log-log axes corresponds to constant expected fatalities and anything steeper is meant to register an aversion to catastrophe as such. When a licensee proposes a modification, we compute the change in expected fatalities per year, monetize it at the value of a statistical life, and compare it with cost. In a recent case — a small but essentially certain non-fatal injury to one identified maintenance worker, traded against a diffuse reduction in collective public dose across a few hundred thousand people — I discovered that our forms have no field in which anyone could record an objection of the kind I wanted to make, and my panel had no vocabulary for it either.

That case sent me to Scanlon's contractualism, and I want his position translated into the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can manage. The position to translate has roughly four parts: that an act is wrong when it would be forbidden by principles that no one could reasonably reject, where the justification is owed to each person individually rather than to the population as a whole; the restriction that a principle can only be rejected on grounds that speak to how it bears on the person doing the rejecting, never on grounds about totals or averages; his example, as I recall it, of someone who could stop a stranger's agonizing electric shocks only by switching off the transmitter and missing a match he has been looking forward to, which he takes to show that large numbers of small benefits do not outweigh one serious harm; and his attempt to justify saving the five over the one by comparing complaints pair by pair rather than by summing them. For each of these four, tell me what its counterpart is in my toolkit — the individual risk criterion as a floor, the de minimis level, the steepness of the F-N curve, the gross-disproportion test, the distinction between identifiable and statistical lives, the choice between evaluating a proposal on expected rather than realized outcomes — or whether there is no counterpart at all.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the real deliverable. Sort the four claims into those that map onto risk-regulatory machinery cleanly, those that map only if I bend a term (say so explicitly, and mark the bend rather than quietly redefining an engineering word to carry a moral load — tolerable and acceptable already do more work in our documents than they can bear), and those for which my framework has no expression whatsoever, no matter how generously it is stretched. I especially want your view on three questions. First: is a steep F-N curve the operational content of the anti-aggregation intuition, in which case Scanlon and I are closer than we look and part of his disagreement with expected-value reasoning is rhetorical — or is it a category mistake to say so, because the curve still aggregates, just nonlinearly? Second: does the inexpressible residue indicate a genuine hole in risk regulation, namely that our instruments evaluate distributions rather than addressing anyone, so that the second-personal character of justification is exactly what our forms cannot hold? Third: is there anything in my vocabulary — ex ante versus ex post evaluation, de minimis levels, the identifiable-victim distinction, risk equity as a separate criterion from risk efficiency — that contractualism has no way of stating and would be worse off without? Please do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in under the name of translation and then congratulate Scanlon for holding them, and do not grade his view by assuming that expected fatalities are the only thing worth counting; and keep it self-contained, since I want an argument I can bring to a panel meeting rather than a list of further reading.
