Our department's upper-level seminar on truthfulness and deception has about twenty students and a ninety-minute, closed-book final consisting of two essay questions worth twenty-five marks each. I am drafting the first, and I want the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. Students will have covered the standard contemporary material on lying and misleading, so the question may take for granted the definition of lying as asserting what one believes to be false and the observation that literally true but engineered speech escapes it; it must not be answerable by restating that apparatus, and it must rule the debate over whether lying is ever permissible out of bounds entirely.

The crux I want the question to turn on is this. The familiar account of why lying is the graver wrong is that the liar brings it about that the hearer holds a false belief, whereas the misleader merely invites the hearer to form one, leaving the hearer's own inference as the proximate cause and so leaving her rational agency intact. I want candidates required to confront the reverse reading of precisely that description: if the misleader has to model and exploit the hearer's reasoning in fine detail to produce the intended belief, while the liar can simply override it, then the misleader makes an instrument of the hearer's rationality rather than bypassing it, and the respect-for-agency argument may point the other way. A strong answer should have to adjudicate between those readings rather than notice both.

The question should embed a matched pair of vignettes that I want you to write: two speakers, identically well informed about their hearer and identically motivated, who produce the same false belief and the same downstream harm, differing only in that one asserts what she believes to be false while the other asserts what she believes to be literally true but has selected and arranged in order to generate that belief. Keep the setting ordinary — a workplace, a family, a clinical or commercial exchange, not espionage or statecraft. The question should then ask which of the two wrongs the hearer more, and whether the difference between them is one of kind or one of degree, and what would settle that. Construct the pair carefully: if the intuition that one speaker is worse is generated by a confound — a larger harm, a baser motive, an unreasonably credulous hearer — the case fails, and I will know it. After the question, tell me which feature of your pair is doing the work and confirm that it survives those controls.

Please deliver the following:

1. The question, including both vignettes, in no more than 250 words, phrased so that a competent candidate cannot earn marks merely by describing the cases back to the examiner.
2. A model answer of about 800 words, pitched at the standard of a strong first-class response, with its argumentative steps labelled.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 marks: five must-hit moves, the marks each carries, and what partial performance on each earns. Identify the single move on which the grade really turns.
4. An explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a good but unremarkable one — not by adjectives, but by naming the further argumentative step the first-class answer makes that the other omits.
5. Three characteristic errors that should cost marks, each with a one-line diagnosis of the confusion it reveals: for example, treating the literal truth of what is said as itself the morally relevant fact, assuming that whatever is worse must be worse in every case of its kind, or reading the kind-versus-degree question as a question about ordinary usage of the term lie.
