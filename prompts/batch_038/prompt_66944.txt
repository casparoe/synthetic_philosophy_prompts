My roommate's in a health policy master's and she's been ranting at me for two weeks about a requirement in a real-ish insurer's surgical pre-authorization pilot, and I want to turn it into a short story instead of hearing about it again over dinner. Here's the setup as she described it. Before an elective surgery is approved, the member has to complete two paper forms about the "post-operative complication rider" — the part of the plan that pays out only if a listed complication actually occurs. Form 1 asks you to initial one of two boxes: box (a), the rider pays your estate a flat $100,000 if any listed complication occurs; box (b), the rider pays $500,000 if a listed complication occurs *and* an independent panel draws your case in a 1-in-10 lottery, otherwise it pays nothing. Form 2 is a smaller version of the same thing: box (c), a flat $5,000; box (d), $25,000 subject to the same 1-in-10 panel draw. Cordic Mutual's internal adjudication rule — the part my roommate can't stop bringing up — says a member whose two forms don't line up in the expected way (certain pick on one, lottery pick on the other) has not "expressed a preference" at all, and gets assigned the default rider by whatever standard cost model the plan already uses. The forms don't tell you what the aligned pattern is. And apparently there's a woman in one of the clinic's waiting rooms who has figured out the shape of the rule and makes money filling out Form 1 for other people — clients who want the flat payout but want to be recorded as having wanted the lottery, or some variant of that — so she's basically selling her own consistency, in person, one clipboard at a time.

Write it as a literary short story, somewhere around 1,500–2,200 words, mostly scene and dialogue rather than summary. Ground rules: never name or explain the decision-theory result the forms encode — no character says the words axiom, paradox, expected value, or rationality-as-consistency, and there's no narrator aside that spells it out for the reader. The two forms should get filled out on the page, at least one of them in real time, and the fact that Form 2 is structurally Form 1 shrunk down should surface through what characters notice, not through exposition. The woman who trades picks can't be a villain or a punchline — she needs a line of reasoning good enough that it actually lands with at least one other character in the story, even if it doesn't convince the reader. And the ending should cost somebody something and quietly commit to a view about whether Cordic's rule is defensible, or whether what she's doing is defensible, without announcing which view it took.

Use whatever names and place names you like; mine if it helps: the member is Renata Ostrowski, the clinic sits in a rust-belt city I'm calling Merriam Falls, the plan is Cordic Mutual's Accord Pilot, and the person who has to decide what to do about the traded forms is a claims auditor named Dale. Please don't put in footnotes, an afterword, or a closing paragraph that translates the story back into the argument — I'd rather it just be a story and let me argue with my roommate about it afterward.
