I am assembling a seminar on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and before I commit to a framing I want to know what my own judgments about putative counterexamples add up to. Here they are, in the order I have them.

An accelerating machine — one that takes half a minute for its first step, a quarter for its second, and so on, finishing infinitely many steps within the hour and then printing whether a given machine halts — strikes me as a real counterexample, though not to the thesis Church actually meant.

A quantum computer: not a counterexample in any sense. It answers faster, not more.

A trial-and-error machine that emits a running sequence of guesses converging on the correct value of f(n) but never signals when it has settled: not a counterexample, even though such machines can converge on the halting predicate and so outrun every recursive function.

A device that reads a voltage to unbounded precision and outputs its binary expansion: not a counterexample, since the voltage may well be a computable real and no measurement could ever tell us otherwise. The same device stipulated to be reading a Chaitin omega: a counterexample.

An oracle machine supplied with the halting problem as its oracle: not a counterexample, because the oracle is no part of any procedure.

A human clerk working through rules with paper and pencil: precisely what the thesis is about. A mathematician who sees that a Gödel sentence for a sound system is true: not executing a procedure at all, so no threat from that direction either.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the second and third items. Both turn on a process whose completion is not finitely accessible from inside the process, yet I accept the accelerating machine's infinite run and reject the trial-and-error machine's. Is there a principle — about finitude of steps versus finitude of time, about what a finite agent can come to be entitled to, or about which of the several theses on offer (a conceptual analysis of effective calculability, an empirical hypothesis about nature, a methodological norm for a discipline) is the one in play — that delivers all of these verdicts? Please state it clause by clause, indicating which case motivates each clause; give its verdicts on three cases I have not raised, namely a machine that halts only at the first infinite ordinal, an arrangement of spacetime in which one observer can receive a signal from another who has completed infinitely many steps, and a proof-checker that verifies each step of a demonstration too long for any human being to survey; and say whether the principle makes the thesis knowable a priori or leaves it hostage to physics. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and what the cheapest revision of my set would be.
