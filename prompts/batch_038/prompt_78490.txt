our perception reading group finishes this week and the last two sessions have been a stalemate — half the room thinks the argument from illusion forces there to be one kind of experience common to the good case and the bad case, and the rest of us are disjunctivists who can't get them to see what exactly we're denying. i want to close with a poem instead of another argument.

Around 26 lines, no fixed meter, in two halves that are as close to interchangeable as you can make them: same imagery, same syntax, same line count, so nothing in the words lets a reader tell which half is someone actually looking at a lemon on a kitchen table and which half is a subjectively perfect halluncination of that lemon. Then a coda of two or three lines in which the voice refuses to name anything the halves have in common. The conceit is that the mirrored halves stage the highest common factor move — everything reportable is the same, so there must be a shared element — and the coda takes it away, holding that the only thing true of both cases is that the subject can't tell them apart, which isn't a positive ingredient of experience at all. Use illusion if you like (the stick that looks bent in water), but the pressure should come from the hallucination, since that's the case that's supposed to do the work. I want to be able to point at the couplet where the common factor argument gets its grip and the line where it gets refused.

After the poem, tell me how confident you are that the coda states the disjunctivist denial accurately and hasn't quietly slid into an adverbial or sense-datum view, single out the line you're least sure about, and flag anywhere the symmetry cheats — any difference between the two halves that would give the game away to someone who knew which was which.
