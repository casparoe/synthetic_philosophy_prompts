I am a senior in a political philosophy seminar writing my paper on public commemoration, and I have hit a wall: my judgments about the monuments in my own city are firm, and I cannot state the principle that generates them. Every case below is a real place where I grew up, but nothing beyond these descriptions is needed — all the philosophically relevant features are stipulated here.

1. A bronze statue of the city's founder, raised in 1911 by a heritage society during the height of segregation. He built the first hospital and held eleven people in slavery. My verdict: it comes down.
2. A street named for the same man, part of the original 1840s grid, now carrying the addresses of four thousand households. My verdict: it stays.
3. A street named in 1968 for a councilman who cast the deciding votes against fair housing and left no other mark on the city. My verdict: rename it.
4. In 1988 the founder's descendants set a bronze plaque into the courthouse wall that names him, credits the hospital, and states in the same sentence that he held people in slavery. My verdict: the plaque stays — but the identical words carved on the statue's pedestal would not save the statue.
5. A 1926 obelisk to "the pioneers of the valley," naming no individual and celebrating no wrongdoing. My verdict: it should go, because it is the only marker of the valley's settlement that says nothing about who was already living here.
6. A hospital wing named for the benefactor whose gift built it; his fortune came from lobbying for compulsory sterilization. My verdict: the name goes, even though the wing's patients will testify that the money saved lives — and nothing would change if he had given anonymously and the wing had been named for a physician instead.
7. A park bench dedicated in 2005 to a slumlord who spent his last thirty years building affordable housing. My verdict: fine. But a statue raised in 1901 to an officer honored for his postwar reconciliation work: it comes down, though the shape of the repentance looks the same.
8. A stadium bearing its corporate sponsor's name under a naming-rights contract. If the sponsor turns out to be a predator, I treat this as a contract question with no honor in it at all — yet a donor-named bench on campus feels categorically different, and money bought both.

Cases 2 and 3 are the pair I cannot reconcile. My provisional principle was that street names designate while statues honor; but the founders' streets were named honorifically too, and case 3 shows that a street name can be the only honor a person ever received.

What I want from you: two or three candidate principles, each broken into numbered clauses, with the case above that motivates each clause; a statement of which of my eight verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and would force me to abandon; and, for the candidate you judge best, a defense of that choice over the more complex alternative that saves everything. Then give me that principle's verdict on two cases of your own choosing, designed to be as awkward for it as possible. Finally, settle the case I keep returning to: melting the founder's statue down and casting the bronze into a monument to the families his city displaced — better or worse than storing it in a museum basement, and what my preference implies about whether the object itself carries the honor.

Please present the comparison of candidates as a table with these columns: case number, my verdict, candidate A's verdict, candidate B's verdict, clause that produces the verdict, and cost of that clause (what it wrongly predicts elsewhere). Prose may precede the table, but the table should cover all eight cases plus your two additions. Write in American English.
