I am in my fifth year of a philosophy PhD, writing a dissertation on introspection and self-knowledge, and my supervisor has asked me to draft the section of a handbook chapter that surveys what he calls the "incoherence arguments" against qualia — the family of arguments that claim the concept is not merely false but unusable, rather than arguments that merely deny that any state has the property. The paradigm case for my section is Dennett's 1988 "Quining Qualia," and in particular the Chase and Sanborn passage, which I have now read several times without being entirely sure what its conclusion is supposed to be. My uncertainty has been compounded by a chapter in a recently published critical anthology on consciousness that I was sent as a PDF; its author is a well-known defender of phenomenal realism, and I would rather not name him in anything I circulate, but his opening characterization of the argument is the sort of thing that gets repeated in seminar rooms and then hardens into the received account.

Here is the relevant stretch of his summary, more or less verbatim in structure: "Dennett's case against qualia rests on two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, whose reports come apart: one maintains that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did although he can no longer stand it, while the other maintains that its taste has changed for him without being able to say how, and insists that his own standards of judgment have not. Because no test, whether introspective or third-personal, could reveal whether the underlying sensory properties shifted or only the taster's reactions to them, Dennett concludes that the question whether the qualia changed has a determinate answer that is in principle inaccessible, and hence that the notion is idle. His definition of qualia is of properties that are private, intrinsic, and known with certainty by their subject, and he takes each of these marks to be unsustainable. The verdict is therefore eliminativist in the strict sense: there is no phenomenal character to tasting coffee, only a nexus of discriminatory and verbal dispositions that we misdescribe in the vocabulary of inner experience. Whatever one thinks of that conclusion, the argument's engine is verificationist — it assumes that a property capable of making no difference to any possible report is no property at all — and so it cannot embarrass a realist who cheerfully concedes that qualia make no difference to anything observable."

Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me what to trust? Specifically: which sentences would Dennett happily endorse, which are defensible simplifications, and which are misrepresentations that change the argument's shape? I am especially interested in whether the author has correctly identified the conclusion — that is, whether the argument really issues in a determinate-but-unknowable fact about qualia shifts, or in something stronger about whether the distinction between a change in qualia and a change in reactive dispositions can be coherently drawn at all — and in whether his list of defining properties is complete, since I have a vague memory of a fourth mark that the chapter omits and of a different gloss on at least one of the three he names. I would also like your judgment on two further points: whether it is fair to describe Dennett as an eliminativist about phenomenal character tout court, or whether he denies the technical posit while preserving something he takes to be the real phenomenon; and whether the verificationist diagnosis is accurate as an account of the argument's structure, and if so, whether the escape route it proposes actually works — that is, whether a realist who grants the verificationist premise has thereby defused the argument or merely relocated the problem it was meant to expose. I have the paper and the anthology chapter in front of me, so I do not need a bibliography or any pointers to secondary literature; what I need is a verdict on the summary, with the reasoning behind it, so that I know which of his formulations I can safely borrow for my own section and which I must rewrite. For what it is worth, I am inclined to think the taster cases are best read as showing that the concept cannot be applied rather than that its application is hidden from us, but I cannot yet articulate why, and I would rather not build a section of the chapter on an intuition I cannot defend.
