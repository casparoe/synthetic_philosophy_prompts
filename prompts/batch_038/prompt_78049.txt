so I've been going around in circles for two weeks after a forum thread about Szasz and there's one analogy doing basically all the heavy lifting that I want pulled apart properly.

setup: my cousin is an ER nurse and she's the one who made the argument, and she made it well, which is why it's stuck. her version goes — nobody thinks the guy who comes in unconscious from diabetic ketoacidosis should be left to "exercise his autonomy." you push dextrose, you treat, you don't hold a consent seminar while his glucose is 40 or 600. and psychosis and severe depression are the same deal: brain chemistry gone wrong, the disease itself is what's compromising the judgment that's refusing treatment, so overriding the refusal isn't a rights violation, it's just medicine. then she adds the anti-stigma leg: calling it an illness is the *compassionate* move, and Szasz's insistence that these are problems in living — moral and interpersonal conflicts dressed up in lab coats — re-stigmatizes people by turning their suffering back into a character flaw.

so the analogy is: psych patient refusing treatment : involuntary psychiatric intervention :: unconscious diabetic : emergency glucose. and as far as I can tell it's being made to carry three conclusions at once, which is what bugs me:

(1) mental illness is a disease in the same sense as diabetes, so the whole "it's a myth" move fails at the threshold before it gets anywhere interesting
(2) non-consensual psychiatric treatment can be legitimate, at least in the acute cases
(3) the disease framing is what removes blame, so dropping it necessarily makes things worse for the people it's applied to

I'm sympathetic to Szasz but not a fanboy, and my gut says (1) and (3) are doing sneaky work while (2) might partly survive on its own. I want the audit, not a side winning.

what I actually want out of this:

- the correspondence table. who's the patient, what's the glucose, what plays the role of the unconsciousness, what plays the role of the blood draw, what's the timescale, who's the bystander. and be explicit about which items in the source have no counterpart at all in the target — I suspect there are a couple that matter and everyone skips them.
- where the mapping breaks, sorted into breaks that actually bite on (1)/(2)/(3) versus ones that are just noise. two I already suspect and want checked: first, that "the disease is compromising the judgment" step is circular on the psych side in a way the DKA side isn't, because in the ER there's an independent measure — the meter reading — that doesn't route through the patient's self-report or their behavior, whereas the psychiatric evidence for impaired judgment is mostly the behavior itself. is that a real asymmetry or am I overreading it? second, the difference in duration and reversibility: the nurse's coercion lasts hours and is aimed at getting the person back to a state where they'd obviously consent, and she's confident about that. does that gap do real work on (2), or is it a difference of degree?
- what the source smuggles in. the diabetic case comes pre-loaded with a known mechanism, a lab threshold everyone agrees on, near-universal prior wishes (basically everyone has said beforehand "of course treat me if I'm out"), and no live dispute about whether the thing is a disease. tell me how much of that pre-loading the psychiatric side actually has, for which conditions, and what's left of the analogy once you subtract it.
- whether it holds uniformly across the range, because my cousin slides freely between "manic, hasn't slept in four days, thinks the FBI is in the drywall" and "grad student with moderate depression who'd rather not take an SSRI," and I think those are wildly different targets being matched to the same source. if the analogy only licenses anything at one end, say which end and why the sliding happens.
- and then the verdict on (1), (2), (3) separately — I bet they come apart and only one of them is actually being carried by the analogy rather than by something else (prior wishes? danger to others? plain paternalism about acute self-harm?). if you think a better analogy exists for whatever survives, offer one and say what it can carry that diabetes can't. my own candidates feel like the same move with new clothes: the hypothermic hiker who insists he's fine and wants to lie down in the snow, the drowning swimmer who panics and fights the lifeguard, the person mid-seizure you restrain for their own safety. do any of those earn their keep, or are they all just diabetes again with the lab value hidden?

one more thing and I mean it: no both-sides shrug at the end. give me your actual call on each of the three, and then tell me what would change it — which specific argument or piece of evidence would flip you on (2) especially, and where in everything above your own reasoning is most likely to be broken. I assume the soft spot is the phrase "disease in the same sense," which feels like it can be gerrymandered to make either side win, so if you catch yourself doing that, flag it instead of doing it quietly.
