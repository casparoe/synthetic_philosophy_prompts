I trained as a philosopher of science and eighteen months ago took a postdoc embedded in an archaeology group, partly to get out of a stalled project and partly because I wanted to see whether my methodological worries had any purchase on real work. My first assignment was to draft the interpretive synthesis chapter of a site report. The chapter turns on deposits like the following — a composite of several we excavated, stripped of anything identifying: a shallow pit cut into the plastered floor of a room that shows no sign of domestic use, containing thirty-seven fired-clay figurines, every one of them snapped at the same place, the neck or the left shoulder, none of them burned or worn, no refuse, no fill from later disturbance, and a sealed capping layer above. The draft my colleagues handed me says that this records a single episode of deliberate destruction, that the snapping was intentional and patterned rather than accidental or taphonomic, and that the episode is best understood as the ritual termination of objects bound to the household that made them. I could not bring myself to defend the last clause, and in trying to explain why to my PI I ended up writing out the following argument, which has since become a problem for me rather than for her.

(1) Our only access to the beliefs and intentions of a past society is its surviving material record. (2) Any inference from a material pattern to a mental state requires a bridging principle connecting patterns of that kind to states of that kind. (3) Every bridging principle is either analogical — drawn from societies whose practices we know independently, through ethnography, documents, or our own case — or general, asserted of humans as such. (4) A general principle is compatible with any material pattern whatsoever, because a given belief admits of indefinitely many material expressions and a given expression of indefinitely many beliefs; so no pattern can confirm or disconfirm it. (5) An analogical principle takes whatever warrant it has from its source, and nothing in the target's record can raise or lower that warrant, since a pattern in the target becomes evidence only once it has been read through some bridging principle or other, and any apparent failure of the analogy can always be absorbed as a disanalogy. (6) The record has additionally passed through three filters — what people chose to make, use, and discard; what physically survived decay, disturbance, and reuse; what we happened to find — and each filter can be characterized only by inference from the filtered output itself. (7) So no inference from the archaeological record to anyone's beliefs is warranted, and the interpretive sections of site reports are unfounded. (8) But nothing in (2) through (6) uses any feature peculiar to the distant past: the same reasoning shows that surviving texts do not warrant belief ascriptions, since they too reach us through copying, selection, and accident and are read through the same analogies, and it shows the same of museum collections, and — because all I ever have of a living person's beliefs is behavior and its products, filtered by what they chose to express and what I happened to notice — it shows that I have no warranted beliefs about anyone. (9) (8) is false: I know, well enough to argue about it, what my colleagues believe about this pit. (10) So something above is wrong, and I have been unable to find it.

Here is what I need from you. Act as a philosopher of archaeology and of scientific inference who has thought seriously about reasoning under systematic, non-random sampling bias, and think the problem through carefully before you commit — including working through at least two rival diagnoses and testing each against the requirement in my next sentence — but show me only the finished result. Name the step you reject, explain precisely why it looked unobjectionable when I wrote it, and then price the alternatives: what a defender of the chapter owes if they give up (4), if they give up (5), if they give up (6), and if instead they bite the bullet on (8). If it matters, I would rather surrender (7) or the inference in (8) than (4) and (5), which feel to me like the actual content of the argument, though I recognize that preference is motivated and may be the thing leading me astray. There is one test your diagnosis has to pass. My colleagues will stake the report on claims about the same pit that look structurally identical to the ones I called unfounded — that it was dug and filled in a single episode, that the capping layer postdates the fill, that the breakage occurred before deposition and not after — while I think any claim about what the snapping meant is hopeless. If your answer cannot tell me what makes the first sort of inference work and the second sort fail, or cannot tell me why that distinction does not reduce to my saying so, then it has restated my problem rather than solved it. Begin with your bottom line in the very first sentence, naming the step you reject and the reason in a single claim, and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my premises back to me, no summary of the stakes.
