I'm a second-year PhD student working on decision-making under ambiguity, and next week I'm supposed to give my department's reading group a defence of imprecise probabilities — credal sets rather than single credence functions. To make sure the defence was worth giving, I tried to build the strongest argument I could against my own view, and I ended up with something that disturbs me, because I can't find a false step in it and yet I can't accept what it concludes. It is entirely my own construction, with my own numbers, and I'm not asking you to reconstruct or compare it with anyone else's version; I want it taken apart on its own terms.

Here is the setup. I bought a coin at an estate sale from the estate of a mint that closed in 1911. It is physically ordinary — I know it is not two-headed and not two-tailed — the mint's records are gone, nobody alive has seen it tossed, and tomorrow a machine will toss it exactly once. Let H be the proposition that it lands heads. My doxastic state toward H is a credal set C consisting of all probability functions p with p(H) in [a, b], where in my case a = 0.01 and b = 0.99. Now the argument:

(1) Immunity. Your doxastic state toward a proposition is rationally permissible only if there is no sequence of offers you could foresee facing such that choices each of which your state permits, and none of which it rules out, would leave you a payoff you know in advance to be strictly worse than the payoff of declining every one of them.

(2) At stage 1 you may buy bet X for $6; X pays $10 if H and $0 otherwise. At stage 2 you may buy bet Y for $6; Y pays $10 if not-H and $0 otherwise. Both offers are fully described to you at the outset, neither conveys any information about the toss, and you may accept or decline each independently.

(3) Under E-admissibility — an act is permitted iff some p in C assigns it expected value at least as high as that of its alternatives at the stage where it is offered — buying X is permitted at stage 1, since p(H) = 0.99 is in C and gives expected value $9.90 − $6 = +$3.90.

(4) By the same reasoning, buying Y is permitted at stage 2, since p(H) = 0.01 is in C and gives expected value +$3.90.

(5) Buying both costs $12 and returns exactly $10 whichever way the coin falls: $2 worse than buying neither, as you knew before stage 1.

(6) So C violates Immunity, and by (1) C is not rationally permissible.

(7) Nothing in (2)–(6) depended on how wide C is, only on its having more than one member. For any a < b, price the two bets just below 10b and 10(1 − a) dollars respectively; then each is E-admissible while the pair costs more than the $10 it guarantees. So every non-singleton credal set about H violates (1).

(8) Hence whenever a body of evidence fails to determine a unique probability for a proposition, no doxastic state toward it is rationally permissible. But some state is permissible — I have one right now, and I am not irrational merely for having been handed a coin I have never seen. So evidence always determines a unique probability, and imprecision is never required, never even allowed.

(9) In my case, if the evidence "ordinary coin, no records, never observed, one machine toss" determines a unique probability, the only candidate is 1/2, by indifference.

(10) So (1) commits me to indifference in full generality — even though indifference prescribes different values for the same evidential situation depending on how the parameter is described (uniform over the bias, uniform over the log-odds, uniform over how many of a hundred balls are red), and (8) allows only one permissible state.

The conclusions I cannot accept are (8) and (10) jointly: either indifference is mandatory in exactly the cases where it gives description-relative answers, or there is no permissible attitude at all toward a coin nobody has ever tossed. Both seem worse than anything I gave up to get there. Two things I have already tried, neither of which satisfies me. First, I tried substituting Γ-maximin for E-admissibility at (3)–(4): it buys neither X nor Y, so Immunity is satisfied — but it also refuses every bet on H whose price exceeds what the worst case in C supports, so I seem to have traded a pump for paralysis, and my choices now respond only to the lower bound of my set, which throws away everything the rest of the set encodes. Second, I tried denying (1) on the ground that it makes my beliefs' rationality depend on what offers exist out there: on that principle my state toward H would be permissible in a world without bookmakers and impermissible in a world with them, which seems to make doxastic rationality hostage to the market. But that reply appears to sever credence from choice altogether, which is the one link I was using imprecision to explain.

So: which step do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I want a committed diagnosis rather than a menu of the available moves — if two steps are independently rejectable, rank them and say which one you would actually give up. Please address in particular whether (1) is even the right form for a diachronic requirement, and whether the foreseeability built into (2) and (5) is doing something the argument needs and cannot have; whether (6) illicitly transfers a defect from a decision rule to the doxastic state it is applied to; whether (7)'s generalization is as harmless as it looks to me; and whether (9)–(10) is a genuine consequence of the argument or an over-reach I bolted on, since if I simply delete those steps I am still left with (8), which is the part I most want to resist. Finally, if you think the argument is sound and I should abandon imprecise credences, say so plainly and tell me what the honest costs of that are; and if you think I have mis-stated my own position, or that the question as I have posed it cannot be answered well without settling something larger first, tell me that rather than humouring me.
