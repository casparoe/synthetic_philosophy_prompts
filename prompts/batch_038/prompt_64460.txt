I sit on my town's capital projects committee — unpaid, mostly retirees plus a couple of us who actually read the appendices — and last week we were scoring a levee upgrade. Big cost now, flood protection that mostly pays off forty to seventy years out. One of the other members waved the whole thing away with "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" and used it to argue we should be discounting those future benefits at something like 7% a year. Two of us pushed back but it sort of landed, because the proverb sounds like plain common sense and our objection sounded like pedantry.

My problem is I can't actually say what claim he was making, and I suspect that sentence is doing several different jobs at once. Can you pull apart the distinct precise theses it could be standing for? I'm thinking along the lines of: is it a claim about risk (the bush birds might never be caught) or a claim about time as such (an identical good is worth less just for being later, even when it's certain to arrive)? Is it about probability, or about the size of the ratio — why two-for-one specifically, and over what interval? Is it about liquidity and option value, about who the worth is worth *to*, about the possibility that you'll be dead or moved away before the bush birds show up? Sort the readings out cleanly and for each one tell me whether it's a truism nobody would deny, whether it's a substantive but defensible principle, or whether it's simply false. Then say which of the readings would actually license a 7% social discount rate on a public flood levee, and which wouldn't get him anywhere near that number even if its true.

For context on why I'm bothered: I've also come across the standing argument that discounting the future *purely* because it's later — no uncertainty, no changing needs, just later-ness — isn't a reason at all but a failure of the imagination. That seems flatly at odds with the proverb, and I'd like to understand how both things can be in circulation at the same time without one of them being nonsense.

Please flag which readings you think are genuinely in the sentence versus which ones you're reaching for, and give me some feel for your confidence on the verdicts — I'd rather you hedge where you're unsure than hand me a tidy list that's quietly wrong in two places. And if you think I've set this up badly, say so instead of playing along: if the proverb was never about time discounting in any interesting sense and the whole exercise is me building a strawman out of something a tired 78-year-old said at a Tuesday meeting, that's a fine answer too.
