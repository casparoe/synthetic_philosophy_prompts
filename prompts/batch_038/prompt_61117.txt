So — I'm about seventy thousand words into my third novel and I've got a problem I made for myself eight months ago and have been politely ignoring until now.

The book is set in an invented valley with a reincarnation-flavoured religion where karma is basically a literal system of moral accounting. Villagers bring cases to an old laywoman everyone calls the Tallywoman — she's not a priest, more like a magistrate of desert — and she tells them what their account looks like. I wrote eight of these little scenes across the first half, mostly following my nose, and chapter 26 now depends on her being consistent, and I honestly can't remember what rule I was running.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A man puts nightshade in his brother's well intending to kill him; the brother left the valley a week earlier and nobody drinks from it. She says the account stands at full weight, same as if he'd died.
2. A midwife running to a breech birth knocks over a lamp and the house burns to the ground, everyone gets out alive. She says no fruit at all — "the fire was already sitting in the lamp."
3. A merchant gives generously his whole life but only where it can be seen, and keeps a private list of who owes him. She says he's been paid in full already, his account is closed, nothing further is owed to him.
4. A girl steals loaves from a rich baker who never notices, to feed her brother. She says the theft bears fruit, small, ripening in this life; the feeding of the brother bears its own fruit, larger; they don't cancel, they're entered seperately.
5. A man beats his wife for twenty years and after she dies fasts and tends strangers' sick until his own death. She says the beating still ripens, the tending still ripens, and the two never meet.
6. A hunter kills only what he eats and says a prayer over each body. She says light fruit, and it will come back to him as a death of his own that arrives quickly and without fright.
7. A son dedicates the merit of a long pilgrimage to his dead mother. She says it reaches her if she's somewhere she can be glad of it; if she's somewhere she can't, it stays with the son.
8. A boy sets a snare for birds; a magistrate signs a removal order without reading it. She says the snare is the heavier of the two that day, and the signature is the heavier across the years.

Now chapter 26. Wren, my protagonist, knows her brother has intended for a year to kill the steward who conscripted the valley's sons, and half the valley knows it, and he's going to do it on the night of the festival. She gets there two days early and kills the steward herself — planned, calm, no rage, and she tells people afterwards exactly why. Then she walks to the Tallywoman and announces the whole thing, asking for the killing to be entered on her account instead of his, on the theory that he's the one who was going to be ruined by it and she's the one who's already ruined.

What I need from you:

- Which rules actually fit all eight verdicts. I can see at least an intention-at-the-moment rule, a character-formation rule, a mirror-the-form-of-what-you-did rule, and something about accounts not being commensurable, but I can't tell which of those I've actually committed to and which are just compatible with what I wrote.
- What each of them says she tells Wren. Does the substitution work, half-work, or fail outright — and under which rule does her brother still carry a full-weight intended killing he never carried out?
- Your best single guess at what I've already signed up for, with the reasoning, plus which one or two of the eight scenes is doing most of the work and which is the loosest.
- If there's a reading consistent with all eight that lets the sacrifice partly land without her just lying to Wren, I'd genuinely rather have that.
- A ninth case I could slip into chapter 9 as a two-page scene that would discriminate between the top contenders. It has to be the kind of thing a villager would actually bring her, and her answer has to be short.
- What theory of moral desert I'm endorsing with each option — is desert transferable at all, is it retributive mirroring or self-formation, do acts net against each other. I want to know the philosophical cost of each ending, not which is nicer.

No need to point me at the real Buddhist or Jain sources, I've read enough of them badly. I don't need the ending fixed either. I need to know what the ledger already says.
