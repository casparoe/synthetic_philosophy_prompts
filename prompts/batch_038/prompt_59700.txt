I'm writing a short piece about credentialism in hiring for a general-audience site, and the guy editing it keeps leaning on an analogy I want to check before I let it stand. His line: the reason a peacock's tail is called a handicap is that it works like the extra weight a good racehorse carries — the better you are, the more you can lug around — and that's exactly why an expensive degree or a 100-hour workweek screens for talent honestly. Only the able can carry the weight, so the weight is evidence of ability. He reckons the biologists and the economists are making the same point about degrees, so the conclusion just follows and I shouldn't water it down.

I don't think it carries that much and I don't know enough biology to say why. Can you audit the thing for me?

First: I always assumed the name came from horse-racing handicaps — tell me whether that's right, and what a handicap is actually *for* in that setting and who sets it, because I suspect that's where it starts going wrong. Then map it properly: what plays the weight, the handicapper, the better horse, the race itself, and the punters watching — and for each, what it corresponds to in the peacock case and what it corresponds to in the hiring case, and whether the correspondence holds or buckles. Then separate the disanalogies that actually do damage to his argument from the ones that are just decoration. The one I most want pinned down: does the whole thing depend on the cost being *cheaper* for the talented to pay than for the untalented, and if so, does that hold when the cost is four years of tuition and unpaid internships rather than a tail? Its the load-bearing part as far as I can tell. Also worth saying: who benefits from the signal being read, and does that stay the same across the three cases? And is a degree even the same kind of thing as a tail, or is it closer to something unfakeable that you don't choose?

Finally, does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — and if it doesn't, what should I be reaching for instead, if anything.
