I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and last month I sat in on a closed workshop where two participants got into a sustained argument about how the alignment problem ought to be stated. I'm reconstructing the exchange as faithfully as I can; neither of them was performing for the room, and both were good. What I want is an adjudication of the argument as it actually ran, not a survey of the field. Call them R., who builds these systems at a lab, and S., a philosopher seconded to the same group.

1. R. opened by saying the problem is routinely overstated because it is stated as a problem about values. On his telling, the narrow and correct statement concerns intentions: a system is aligned when it does what its principal intended it to do. He argued this is the tractable version, since intentions are the sort of thing that can be elicited and checked — you can ask the principal what she meant, and you can afterward find out whether the output was the one she would have asked for. Talk of values, he said, imports a different and much larger question — which values, whose, settled by what procedure — that has nothing to do with getting an assistant to carry out instructions.

2. S. said this only looks narrow, because ascribing an intention is never a matter of reading it off behavior, or even off the principal's own after-the-fact report. Her example: a field linguist trying to work out what a speaker intends by a word, armed with nothing but the speaker's dispositions to use it in the presence of things and her assent to sentences. The linguist can construct several mutually incompatible manuals of ascription, each consistent with everything the speaker ever does or says, and there is no further evidence to appeal to. Our situation with a principal is the same in kind, S. said: any assignment of intentions must hold most of the principal's beliefs true, and which beliefs you hold true is settled by what you already take to be reasonable. The values sit inside the ascription, not downstream of it.

3. R. answered that this trades on the artificial setting. A deployed assistant does not face a linguist's poverty of evidence; it sees thousands of interactions, it receives corrections, and the corrections compound, so whatever underdetermination remains gets squeezed by data. He added that the principal's angry or pleased reaction is a checkable fact rather than a metaphysical one, and that a theory unable to distinguish a system that did what its user wanted from one that did not has stopped being a theory about anything.

4. S. said the corrections cannot do the work he needs, because they establish what the principal now endorses, and the whole difficulty is that nothing independent settles what she meant beforehand. Her case: the principal asks an assistant to shorten a document. The assistant cuts the qualifying clause, and the shortened version is now misleading. The principal sees it and objects. R. said the assistant plainly failed — she wanted it short and not misleading. S. said that verdict is available only because he has already assumed the principal is reasonable and means her words to serve her purposes; a rival norm — take each term strictly as she would gloss it if asked to define it — delivers the opposite verdict, and it is compatible with everything she has ever done, including her objection, which can be described either as her changing her mind about what she wants or as her discovering what she had meant all along. There is no third description to appeal to.

5. R. conceded the tie at the level of the whole web of ascriptions but said engineering does not need the whole web, only a decision procedure, and there is one: choose the output that the principal, better informed and having reflected, would be least likely to reject. He said this is normative but thin — a procedural value about deferring to the principal's considered judgment, not a substantive ethics — and that framing the problem this way is more honest than calling it value alignment, because it exposes how little normative content is actually required.

6. S. said the thin clause is precisely where the controversy has been hidden. Whose considered judgment counts when the principal is a committee or a company; what "better informed" means, given that more information changes what a person endorses; and whether a system that predicts reflective endorsement has tracked the principal's intention or displaced it with a standard of its own devising. On her account R. had not narrowed the problem at all — he had relocated it into a single subordinate clause and given the clause a modest name.

Neither budged, and I left without knowing whose side I was on. So: take the six moves in order and grade each one — decisive, telling, weak, or a miss — with a sentence or two justifying the grade. Then tell me what each of them gets right that the other misses; whether move 5 genuinely rescues the narrow statement or whether move 6 lands; and whether either of them is re-staging a dispute that has already been argued at length in some other discipline, where it may have gone better than it did in that room. I am not after references — I could not check them, and they are not what I need. I want the reasoning.

Close with a final line in exactly this form, so I can log the result: `Verdict: ` followed by R, S, or Split, then an em dash and no more than twelve words naming the decisive point.
