I'm the site supervisor on a developer-funded rescue excavation — a Bronze Age settlement with about six weeks left in the season — and the client's press officer has already drafted a release describing our Building 4 as a prehistoric shrine. I have to decide what goes into the interim report before that release is issued, and I can't get a straight answer from anyone on site.

Here is what we actually have. Building 4 is a rectangular timber structure, roughly six by nine metres, standing about forty metres apart from the main cluster of houses on a slight rise. Inside: a plastered bench along one wall; a stone-lined pit in the corner containing burnt grain and ash but no cooking debris and no butchered bone; and a concentration of 41 complete or near-complete pots along the eastern wall, most crushed in place. Two of those pots have holes drilled through them after firing, so they could not have held anything. Immediately outside the doorway is a pit with nine cattle mandibles, mostly from young animals, deposited together. Inside the building there is almost no domestic refuse: no loom weights, no quern fragments, no hearth with cooking residue, no midden. The yard outside has a normal scatter of all of those.

My director calls it a cult building and wants the report to say so plainly. My finds specialist says the pot concentration is a collapse deposit — vessels stacked on a shelf, roof failure — that the perforated ones are simply broken or misfired pots that were reused as something else, and that a bench and a stone-lined pit are exactly what you would expect in a granary or storeroom. She also notes that Building 4 was swept clean before it was abandoned, so the absence of rubbish may be evidence about a broom rather than about a god. Meanwhile one of the PhD students sent me a checklist she copied out of a book: a special natural setting; a building set apart from ordinary life; attention-focusing features in the architecture; a cult image or a stand-in for one; special facilities such as altars, benches, basins or libation pits; food and drink brought in and consumed, burnt off or poured away; the same symbols recurring over and over. She has ticked six boxes for Building 4. I ticked the same six and still feel as though I know nothing more than I did before.

Three things I can't work out. First, does ticking that checklist constitute evidence at all, or is it built so that any structure whose everyday use isn't immediately obvious to us will pass it? Second, if the collapse-and-storeroom story accounts for every single thing we recovered, is there anything left over for the shrine story to account for, or am I simply preferring the more interesting option? Third — and this is the part nobody on site wants to raise — I'm not convinced the two stories are even rivals. It is entirely possible that the same building held the harvest and was also where something was done with it, and that the people who built it would not have recognised the line I'm being asked to draw down the middle of it. If that's right, what should the report say? "Ritual and storage" reads like a fudge that lets everyone off, and "we found a building with a bench and a lot of pots" is true and useless.

I also owe the press officer a yes-or-no answer by Friday on whether we can call it a shrine. Tell me what a defensible answer to her looks like, and what the interim report should say, and where the line runs between honest qualification and hiding behind it. And be explicit throughout about which parts of what you tell me would be treated as settled by people who do this for a living and which are genuinely argued about among them — I don't want to walk into Friday's meeting asserting as standard practice something that turns out to be one school's view.
