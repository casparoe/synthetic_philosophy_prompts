Context, then the ask. I'm one of two people drafting the methodology annex for a public inquiry into how a regional child-protection service failed over roughly thirty years — composite situation, I'd rather not name a real one, but you know the shape: homes got licensed that should never have been licensed, warnings went into files nobody opened, and each death on its own looked like a 'tragic accident' while the whole set of them did not. The annex has to say up front what the inquiry will count as an explanation of how this happened. Not what it will conclude. What will qualify as an explanation at all.

The table is badly mixed. Half the commissioners are ex-judges who want something that looks like a derivation; the other half ran NGOs and think in stories about particular people; the lawyers want whatever we write to survive cross-examination; and the report eventually has to be read by the families, who are not going to read a philosophy of social science.

So before anyone starts arguing about Hempel or narrativism in the abstract, give me the requirements list. What does an account of 'how this came about' have to be able to do to be adequate for us — where adequacy is fixed by our purposes, which are: usable in a public report, capable of underwriting statements about who could have acted otherwise, defensible clause by clause under hostile questioning, and followable by non-specialists without a diagram.

For every requirement, give the situation behind it — a concrete thing an inquiry like ours will run into that the requirement exists to handle. I mean cases like: a minister who signed nothing and can't be shown to have decided anything; a report that was commissioned, delivered, and never opened; a pattern that only exists across forty seperate individually-unremarkable decisions; the awkward fact that a comparable region with the same budget and the same statute didn't collapse; and the fact that the people who ran the service mostly thought they were doing their jobs competently.

Then sort the list, and be blunt about it. Which requirements are non-negotiable for our purpose. Which are really just the house style of one of the camps at the table — the ex-judges' insistence that every explanatory step be backed by a generalisation that would carry over to a comparable case, the NGO half's insistence that it read as one continuous sequence with a through-line — and should be named as preferences rather than smuggled in as criteria. And which ones aren't about explanation at all but about presentation, and belong in the writing brief rather than the annex.

The part I most want: tell me which subsets of these requirements cant be jointly satisfied, and where exactly the break is. My own suspicion is that 'every step must be backed by a transferable generalisation' and 'the whole must be holdable in one head as a single sequence' pull apart once you're covering three decades, and that 'it must show the outcome was avoidable' fights with 'it must show the outcome was what this system produced, not what villains did' — the more you secure the second, the less anyone is left to blame. Say whether that's right or whether I've got the wrong pairing. And say whether anything in the middle gets both — a mechanisms-flavoured account, or Dray's move of explaining an action by showing it was the reasonable thing for that agent in that situation — and what such a middle position has to give up to fit through our constraints.

Last: which single requirement, if we adopt it, knocks out the most candidates? If one clause in the annex is going to do nearly all the work, I want to know which clause and what it excludes.

Table or list, whichever is clearer. No reading list and no bibliography — I wouldn't get to it, and the annex certainly can't cite a stack of philosophy books.
