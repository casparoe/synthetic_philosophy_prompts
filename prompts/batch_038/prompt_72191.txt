I want a case analysis of the beating of Rodney King and the 1992 California state trial of the officers, read through Daniel Dennett's contrast between the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model. Let me supply the facts I am relying on, so the analysis does not turn on my recollection of contested details. On March 3, 1991, Los Angeles police officers beat Rodney King after a high-speed pursuit; a bystander, George Holliday, filmed part of it from his balcony with a camcorder, and the footage was broadcast around the world within days. Four officers were prosecuted in state court, the trial was moved out of Los Angeles to Simi Valley, and in April 1992 the jury acquitted most of the defendants on most of the charges, deadlocking on a single remaining count against one officer — or something close to that; please do not let anything important hang on the precise disposition. The Los Angeles riots began within hours of the verdicts. Two of the officers were later convicted in a federal civil rights prosecution. What matters for my purposes is that both sides worked from the same tape. The prosecution's posture was that the tape speaks for itself: watch it and you see a man being beaten. The defense slowed it down and disassembled it moment by moment, arguing that each individual strike had to be judged against what an officer could have perceived in the instant preceding it, that what an ordinary viewer takes to be one continuous assault is really a sequence of separate responses to King's movements, and that the footage underdetermines what any officer saw or knew.

As I understand Dennett, the Cartesian theater is the picture on which the brain's parallel processing converges at one place and one time, where the show is presented to a witness, so that there is a determinate answer to when a given content became conscious and to what it was like at that moment. The multiple drafts model denies this: there are many parallel streams of editorial revision, and which version counts as the experience depends on when and how the system is probed, so there is no fact of the matter about the single moment a content arrived. Relatedly, what I take to be his Orwellian/Stalinesque point is that for many timing phenomena there is no experiment that could decide whether the experience was veridical and the report revised afterward, or whether the revision happened before experience was settled — and that this is because the question is empty, not because the answer is merely unknown. Dennett's book came out in 1991, the same year as the beating, which is part of what put the pairing in my head.
