---
**(a)** The problem is that (i) and (ii) have the same antecedent and the same consequent, differing only in tense and mood, yet anyone who accepts (i) will refuse to assert (ii). Since Oswald did shoot Kennedy, a material reading makes both of them vacuously true, so the material reading can't explain the difference — it gets (i) right for the wrong reason and gets (ii) right too, when (ii) is exactly what we won't say. Adams's version of the point is probabilistic. My degree of belief in (i) is just my conditional probability that someone else shot, given that Oswald didn't, and that's very high, because I believe Kennedy was shot and I believe a shooter that well-placed would have been one person. My degree of belief in (ii) is nowhere near that high: I have no view at all about what would have happened. And notice that A ⊃ C has probability P(¬A) + P(A ∧ C), which is always at least as big as P(C|A), so material readings systematically make conditionals with improbable antecedents look more probable than they should be — which is why I shouldn't be prepared to assert "if this match is wet it will still light" just because I'm confident the match isn't wet.

**(b)** Lewis's triviality result is a seperate argument and it's worth keeping it apart from the one in (a). Lewis supposes that an indicative conditional expresses a proposition, call it A→C, and that the identity P(A→C) = P(C|A) holds not just for my probability function but for every probability function defined on the language. He then shows this can only happen if the language is trivial: the identity forces conditional probabilities to collapse into unconditional ones, so no two sentences can differ in probability except between 0 and 1. So there is no proposition for (i) to express with the probability Adams's thesis demands, and Adams's thesis is false — not just unhelpful, false. That's a stronger conclusion than (a) gives you, since (a) only showed the material reading assigns the wrong probabilities, while Lewis shows no reading assigns them at all. It also means you can't rescue indicatives by finding a cleverer truth condition: indicatives just aren't the sort of thing that has one. Nothing follows either way about counterfactuals, though, since Adams only ever advanced the thesis for indicatives — so Lewis's result is perfectly compatible with counterfactuals having genuine truth conditions while indicatives don't.

**(c)** Stalnaker's reply keeps one semantics for both moods. A conditional is true iff its consequent holds in the closest antecedent-world, where closeness is fixed by a selection function that respects the actual laws and the overall similarity of worlds. What separates (i) from (ii) is the indicative constraint, which is a condition on assertion: you may assert "if A, C" indicatively only when A is compatible with what the conversants are taking for granted, and in evaluating it you're only allowed to select among worlds compatible with those presuppositions. Since I take it for granted that somebody shot Kennedy, the closest world compatible with my beliefs in which Oswald didn't shoot is one where someone else did, so (i) comes out true. Counterfactuals carry no such restriction, so the selection function is free to pick worlds I'd count as epistemically impossible: the closest world where Oswald doesn't shoot is one where the security arrangements differ in ways I know nothing about, and there's no reason to think anybody else fires. So the moods really do have different truth conditions after all — (i) is true and (ii) is false — and the difference is semantic, not pragmatic. One further wrinkle: Adams's thesis can't even be stated when P(A) = 0, so the whole debate only gets going for antecedents with non-zero probability, which Lewis's theorem assumes too.
