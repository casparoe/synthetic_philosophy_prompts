For four years our magazine ran a column called "Small Parts," in which readers described a personal decision — what to eat, whether to fly, whether to join a boycott — and the columnist issued a ruling, usually in a paragraph. She has left the magazine, and we want the column's first reply under a new writer to be continuous with that record rather than a fresh start. Here are the rulings I have extracted, together with her stated reasoning where she gave any:

1. A reader eats one factory-farmed chicken breast about once a month, alone, at a supermarket where nobody she knows shops. Ruling: not wrong.
2. A household of four eats factory-farmed poultry three times a week. Ruling: wrong. Her reason invoked the mechanics of restocking: orders are triggered by sampled sales, so a steady household volume moves purchasing even though any single purchase moves nothing.
3. A reader lives on a street where every household watered its lawn only before six in the morning for two summers under a voluntary drought appeal. The appeal has lapsed, the reservoir is at record capacity, the water board confirmed usage is unmetered and billed flat, and the reader knows his lawn cannot affect anything. He resumed evening watering. Ruling: wrong, with the remark that the reservoir was beside the point.
4. A reader asks whether to join a consumer boycott of a brand that everyone agrees will fail, where joining costs him nothing and will not change the outcome. Ruling: join. "You are not there to change the outcome."
5. A reader keeps her job in the strategy office of an oil major, gives a fifth of her salary to climate litigation, and asks whether staying is wrong. Ruling: not wrong, and probably better than leaving. "Somebody has to be in the room when the question is asked."
6. A reader asks whether to vote in a constituency where the result is not in doubt. Ruling: vote. "You are part of the electorate, not part of the result."
7. A reader flies one long-haul return a year to sit with a dying parent and asks whether the trip is wrong. Ruling: not wrong; a life, she wrote, is not a rounding error.

The letter we now have to answer:

The reader is a doctoral student in marine policy. Her research group has spent two years assembling a regional coalition to reform sourcing in a fishery whose stock has collapsed, and the coalition is in final negotiation for a three-year grant from a foundation whose annual dinner is next week. The dinner menu features that species. The fish were landed two weeks ago by the industrial fleet; the inshore fleet that shares the stock has observed a voluntary moratorium for three seasons at considerable cost to itself. The caterer has confirmed in writing that portions are contracted by headcount, that refusals are not reported to the sourcing team, and that anything left on a plate is discarded. The reader is seated at the host's table, opposite the foundation's director of programs, who has separately emailed her asking for candid feedback about the menu after the dinner. Her supervisor has told her that refusing the course in front of that table would end the grant negotiation. Her options are: eat it; decline quietly on a pretext and leave the plate; decline openly and explain at the table; eat it and use the feedback channel afterwards; or decline openly and use the channel as well.

What I need from you:

(a) Which principles are jointly consistent with all seven rulings? I can see at least a marginal-expected-harm principle, a fair-play or benefiting-from-others'-burdens principle, and a principle on which what matters is that an act visibly takes sides in a contested practice — but I am not confident that these are the right three, or that they are three rather than two or four. State each candidate precisely enough that it yields verdicts rather than moods, and show how each handles rulings 2, 3, and 5, which I suspect are doing most of the constraining.

(b) For each candidate principle, which of the five options does it recommend in the new case? I expect at least two of them to part company here; say exactly where they diverge and what feature of the case drives the divergence.

(c) What should we print? Give a single recommendation, identify which of the seven precedents is the closest analog, and explain how that analogy might mislead us. If the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say what principle the column would be committing itself to by printing each option, and which commitment it can least afford given rulings 3, 4, and 5 taken together.

(d) Describe one further reader letter — a case we could plausibly receive — that would discriminate between your leading candidates, and state what each candidate would rule on it.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about (c): which reading of which precedent, or which consideration I have not mentioned, would push you to a different recommendation, and at which step your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
