You are a decision theorist who has taught the absent-minded driver problem many times, and I want your help designing a session, not an explanation of the problem. Do not explain it to me; plan how I should explain it. I do not want references or a reading list — nothing to assign, just the plan.

**The setting.** Fifty minutes, one whiteboard, no slides, no handouts. Twelve people, in two halves. Six are second-year philosophy graduate students who have had one term of expected-utility theory and one term of probability, and no game theory whatsoever: no extensive-form diagrams, no information sets, no strategy concepts. Six are machine-learning researchers who know POMDPs and policy optimization cold, and who have met this puzzle, if at all, in blog-post form. Three of the ML half have read about the Sleeping Beauty problem and are fully prepared to announce within four minutes that the driver is the same thing wearing a different hat. I have decided not to draw a decision tree, both because half the room cannot read one and because the tree is the observer's representation of the situation rather than the agent's, and the entire difficulty lives in the gap between those two.

**The case I will use.** I state the parameters explicitly so that nothing depends on your remembering a particular published version. A man leaves a bar and drives home along a highway with exactly two exits. At an exit he can get off or keep driving. He has drunk enough that whenever he finds himself at an exit he cannot tell which exit it is, and cannot tell whether he has already passed one. Getting off at the first exit pays 0 — wrong side of town, he walks. Getting off at the second pays 4 — home. Driving past both pays 1 — he ends up in an all-night diner, better than walking, worse than home.

My arithmetic, which I want you to verify before you build anything on it, since the session collapses if it is wrong: ex ante, the best plan is to get off with probability one-third whenever he reaches an exit, and that is worth four-thirds; always getting off is worth 0; always driving on is worth 1. Separately, if he plans to drive on and then finds himself at an exit, his own picture really is fifty-fifty between the first exit and the second, and given that picture getting off is worth 2 while driving on is worth 1 — so the move that looks best from where he stands, applied at every exit, is worth nothing at all.

**Two things I want to do that I have not seen done, and on which I want your judgment rather than your compliance.**

First, I want to carry the whole session on what I have been calling the two-coins ambiguity: the difference between the coin the man flips once at the bar and then obeys at every exit, and the coin he carries in his pocket and flips afresh each time he reaches one. My hunch is that the real payload of the problem is here — that the comfortable equivalence between committing to a plan in advance and behaving stochastically in the moment silently assumes the agent can remember which decisions he has already made, and that once that assumption goes, the two procedures come apart and the word "randomize" stops naming a single thing. Tell me whether this framing can bear that weight or whether it will mislead, and where in the sequence it has to arrive.

Second, I want the session to end on a claim about a malformed question rather than on an answer: that "what should the driver do at the exit?" has no determinate answer until you say whether you mean the man at the bar or the man at the wheel, because those two have different information and, worse, the choice he makes at one exit is simultaneously the choice he makes at the other. I want the room to leave holding that, not holding a number.

**What I need from you.**

1. The order of introduction, with rough minute allocations, and for each piece an explicit statement of what it depends on and why it therefore cannot come earlier. Where you think a natural-looking ordering is actually impossible, say so and say what blocks it.
2. The one case that carries the session, and what it must have and must lack — how many exits, whether the payoffs must be ordered as mine are, whether any external cue may exist, whether the drinking has to be literal. If my version is not the best vehicle, propose the smallest modification that is.
3. The misconception map. I expect at least: "he should just condition on his belief about where he is and act"; "this is Sleeping Beauty"; "randomize" treated as though it were unambiguous; "just commit ex ante, obviously"; "the paradox shows expected-utility theory is broken." Add the ones the ML half will bring, including whatever version of "a belief-state MDP settles this" they will reach for, and say what is right in that instinct before you say what it misses. For each misconception, give the precise point in the sequence at which to head it off, the sentence or move that does it, and which ones I should let the room raise on its own rather than pre-empt.
4. One closing question that separates the people who grasped the structure from the people who memorized the answer. Make it a variant case, not a restatement, and tell me what a good answer and a bad answer to it sound like.
5. What to leave out at this level, and where I should say out loud that I am leaving it out.

Think carefully before answering. Begin by brainstorming rather than drafting: work through the structure of the problem yourself, check my numbers and tell me plainly if any of them are wrong, enumerate every way this particular audience can fail to get the point, and test several orderings against what depends on what. Only then commit to a plan. If you think either of my two ambitions is mistaken, or that they pull against each other, say so and tell me which one to sacrifice.
