I'm taking a metaphysics sequence this term on material constitution, and over the winter break I did several weeks of filing in a museum's registration office. The experience has made the seminar material look strange to me, because the registration desk runs on a set of rules that nobody there would recognise as metaphysics but that seem to encode a very definite one.

The rules, roughly as I saw them applied:

1. Every collection object is issued an accession number on acquisition and keeps it for life. A marble sculpture that loses a hand has a new hand carved from freshly quarried stone and toned to match; the object is still catalogued under the same number, still attributed to the same artist, still exhibited as the same work.
2. The museum was offered a cast produced from a high-resolution scan of one of its bronzes — shape-for-shape identical, visually indistinguishable. It was declined as a work, would at most be catalogued as a study copy, and would be insured at a small fraction of the original's value.
3. When the museum buys a bronze from an authorised edition of eight, each cast is a work in its own right with its own number and catalogue entry, and a damaged cast would not be made good by substituting another cast from the same edition.
4. When a painting is re-attributed from a master to his workshop, nothing physical changes — same canvas, same number, same shelf — but the record and the valuation change dramatically, and the staff speak as though the object had turned out to be a different thing all along.
5. When a metal object is stolen and melted down, it is recorded as destroyed rather than altered or relocated; yet the insurance schedule for the metalwork galleries lists, as best I could tell, a weight for each object alongside its value.
6. In the case of the gilded bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery, the originals were removed to the museum across the square and casts installed in the doorway. The museum's account is that the work moved and the building received a substitute — not that the doorway kept the work while the museum acquired the parts.

My difficulty is that these do not look as though they issue from a single view. Rule 1 treats an artwork's matter as replaceable part by part. Rule 2 treats what makes something the artwork as a history of production that a perfect duplicate lacks. But the new hand in Rule 1 was not made by the artist either, so origin cannot be what Rule 1 tracks in every part; and Rule 6 has the work travelling with the object rather than with the history or the site, which sits oddly beside both. Rule 5 meanwhile has the institution treating one thing as both a lump of metal with a bullion figure attached and a work that no lump can be reduced to.

So: reconstruct, as explicitly as you can, the account of what an artwork is and of the identity conditions of material objects that this bundle presupposes. Then separate what genuinely follows from that account from what merely happens to sit comfortably with it — I want to know where the practice is underdetermined, such that a four-dimensionalist, someone who thinks the statue and the lump are two coinciding objects, and a mereological essentialist could all staff the same desk and issue the same numbers. Is the bundle coherent as it stands, or is one of the rules doing double duty? And a suspicion I cannot shake: the operative criterion appears to be that an object is the work if and only if it bears the accession number, which is either circular or else not a criterion of identity at all but a bookkeeping device standing in for a real one. Which is it? Finally, what would concretely have to change — labels, insurance schedules, storage arrangements, the vocabulary of condition reports — if the museum adopted the coincidence view?

I'm not after a survey of who holds which position; I want the reconstruction and your assessment of where it holds together.
