Two years ago my brother was in a bad wreck and he's been in one of those minimally conscious states ever since — some days he tracks with his eyes, some days nothing. The neuro team here keeps using that TMS-EEG measure where they zap the cortex and compress the echo into a complexity number, and it comes out of the integrated information crowd, so I went and read way more IIT than any layperson should. Somewhere in there I built this argument that seems to prove my brother isn't conscious, and neither am I, and I can't find the exit.

(1) IIT says consciousness just is Φ — the integrated information of the major complex, the subset of elements with maximum Φ at a given time. Note it doesn't have to be the whole system: exclusion picks out the maximum, and the maximum is often a proper part of the system, which is part of why the cerebellum is supposed to contribute almost nothing to experience despite having most of the neurons.

(2) IIT also insists Φ is a matter of a mechanism's actual, intrinsic cause-effect power, not of what it looks like from the outside. Hence the familiar IIT line that a computer running a perfect simulation of a brain isn't conscious: the simulator's real causal structure is that of a serial, mostly feedforward machine, and feedforward structures have Φ around zero. Functionally equivalent, phenomenally empty.

(3) So consciousness lives at the grain where the cause-effect power actually is.

(4) But there's no principled place to stop at that grain. From the point of view of ion channels a neuron is a black box; from the point of view of proteins a channel is a black box; from the point of view of quarks all of it is. Each level down is at least as real and at least as concretely causal as the one above — and by (2), the higher level is exactly the sort of thing that a mere simulation can have without being conscious.

(5) Maximizing Φ across grains doesn't rescue this, because nothing in the axioms forbids the lower grain from winning all the way down. If the tie-break is the most fundamental description that still has causal power, the tie-break is particle physics.

(6) So the major complex of a person sits at the fundamental physical grain.

(7) But at that grain a brain is a vast pile of mostly-local interactions — each quark talking mainly to its immediate neighbors — and integration of that kind is right down near the floor set by the minimum information partition. Φ there is negligible.

(8) Therefore the integrated information of the thing that literally is my brother is negligible, and by (1) there's nothing it's like to be him. Same for me. And it would mean the number the clinicians get off their EEG electrodes is measuring a grain that the theory itself says doesn't exist.

I want to keep (2), honestly. If simulation didn't matter the theory wouldn't have any teeth and wouldn't say anything interesting at all about a brother whose cortex fires but whose behavior doesn't. And obviously I want (8) to be false — I'm not prepared to accept it, I just can't see which step is bad.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine to me when I wrote it, and what does giving it up cost the theory? If the repair has a price — a weakened claim, an added principle, a commitment nobody could measure — state the price rather than just the verdict. And tell me whether the same move generalizes, because if it does I've got a problem with the whole reason I started reading this stuff.
