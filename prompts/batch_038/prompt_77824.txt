A friend and I argued for two hours last night about whether belief-talk legitimately applies to large language models, and we kept circling back to one move neither of us could resolve, so I'd like to see it argued properly. Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 words between two interlocutors defending the two positions we held.

The first is a strict instrumentalist about the intentional stance: ascribing beliefs to a system is nothing more than a predictive strategy, licensed exactly when it is the best strategy available and answerable to nothing beyond the success of its predictions. On this view there is no further question whether the ascribed states are really there. The second is a realist about representational states in the very same systems, holding that a system has beliefs only if the states explaining its behavior are the sort that can be false, that can be recombined into novel conclusions the system was never trained to produce, and that can be identified independently of the questions we happen to ask it.

The case they should fight over is one I keep returning to. The same model, given the same factual question under two different system prompts, returns flatly contradictory answers; and an account that works directly with the internal activations — a direction that an experimenter can flip in order to change the answer — predicts both outputs and the whole family of related outputs better than any belief ascription does. The realist concludes that there is no unified subject here for beliefs to belong to. The instrumentalist replies that the realist has misidentified the system: beliefs belong not to the bare model but to the model conditioned on its prompt, and the fact that a mechanistic account wins on this particular task is beside the point, since the stance was never the claim that intentional ascription predicts best in every case.

Press that re-indexing reply as hard as it can be pressed, and give the realist a genuine answer to the charge that it is unfalsifiable — that if the "system" can always be redescribed until the ascriptions come out stable, the stance has no content left. Have both of them confront the holism objection as well: the instrumentalist will insist that no belief is ever ascribed in isolation, so no single anomalous answer refutes anything, and the realist should say what would count as evidence against that. They must respond to each other's actual moves rather than restate their positions, and the dialogue should end unresolved — but not with a shrug: each side should be left with one concession and one objection it has failed to answer. No references to the literature; just the argument.
