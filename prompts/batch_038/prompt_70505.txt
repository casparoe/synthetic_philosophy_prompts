I serve as one of three lay members on the medical and rules commission of my country's national governing body for rowing. I have no medical, legal, or philosophical training; I was appointed because I am a former competitor and because the board wanted a parent's perspective. I have spent the last month reading around a problem I cannot solve, and it comes to us as two files that will be decided at the same sitting in three weeks.

File one. A rower, now nineteen, was born with a congenital condition that left the musculature of her right leg underdeveloped and weak. At age seven she received a one-time cell-based therapy that was licensed, at that time, only for treating that condition in children. It worked, and she regained ordinary function. But the treated limb has since been found to have a muscle-fibre composition that now measures well above the average for her event — above what most of the field she races against has in both legs combined. She has taken nothing since. She is on the national squad and in contention for a world championship seat.

File two. A second rower has the same condition, but was not diagnosed until she was seventeen, after four years of competing. She received the same therapy with a less complete result: she retains a measurable asymmetry, and she has asked for permission to continue an approved maintenance medication that is otherwise prohibited in competition.

Our rulebook tracks the international standard on therapeutic exemptions. Paraphrasing one of its conditions, the treatment must not be expected to improve performance beyond what would follow from restoring the athlete to normal health. Both files run into that clause, from opposite directions. The first rower was never at any prior level of health to be restored to — her condition was present from birth, so the phrase has nothing to attach to — and hers was a single childhood event rather than a current use, which our exemption process was never built to reach. The second rower does have a documented pre-illness baseline, and her doctors say the best achievable outcome still leaves her short of it, so nothing about her request looks enhancing; yet she is asking for a standing permission we would have to renew indefinitely.

Two further things trouble me. First, nobody else in her crew could obtain the first rower's childhood therapy, because none of them has the condition it was licensed for; if we clear her, it is hard to see what we are doing other than permitting an advantage that arose from medicine no rival could have had. Second, if we do not clear her, it is hard to see what we are doing other than disqualifying a young woman for having been ill as a child — and doing so to a field that has never been asked to give anything up. My own instinct, which I cannot defend when pressed, is that what separates treatment from enhancement should turn on what was wrong with the person and what the intervention was for, not on where they end up standing afterwards. But that instinct gives me no answer at all about an athlete who is now, as a matter of measurable fact, better equipped than everyone she races against.

Draft the commission's decision memo for circulation to the board and to both athletes' representatives. It should: recommend a definite outcome for each file, with the date by which each step would be completed; state whether the commission should adopt a written standard for treatments of congenital or early-onset conditions that leave residual capacity above the statistical norm, and if so give that standard in a form the board could adopt as written, along with criteria a future panel could apply to a new file without re-litigating the principle; explain the reasoning in terms a nineteen-year-old athlete and her parents could follow and reply to; and set out honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side of each recommendation, saying why it does not prevail rather than waving it away. If the two files cannot be decided consistently with one another, say so plainly and explain what distinguishes them.

Begin with the memo itself. No preface, no summary of what I have told you, and no commentary after it.
