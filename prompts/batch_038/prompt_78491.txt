So I have zero formal philosophy background — I'm a software engineer — but there's a small reading group I go to on Thursdays, and last week we did Hobbes. Mostly Leviathan, but the guy running it also brought in material about Hobbes's decades-long public fight with John Wallis over squaring the circle, which is the part I actually cared about, because I have a weakness for watching someone be confidently wrong in public for twenty years.

Anyway, after it wrapped up two people from the group (I'll call them Ruth and Amir) got into a genuinely heated arguement in the parking lot that then continued over text, and I got dragged in as tiebreaker, which was a bad idea because I followed maybe half of it. Roughly how it went:

1. Ruth said Hobbes's whole business about geometry — that we can be certain of it because we draw the figures ourselves and know how they get generated — is basically a 17th-century version of constructivism in mathematics: you only genuinely know an object if you know how to build it. Her claim was he got there centuries early.

2. Amir said that's pure projection. His version: Hobbes's point is about definitions, not construction. Names and definitions are ours to stipulate, so reasoning from them is certain because there's no external thing the reasoning has to match up with. That's the axiomatic point — you're free to posit your primitives — and it has nothing to do with building anything.

3. Ruth said if it's only about stipulated definitions, Hobbes can't explain why geometry is special. Arithmetic and algebra would get the same certainty, and he clearly didn't grant them that: he sneered at algebraic symbolism and treated Wallis's infinitesimal methods as unintelligible gibberish. Her read is that he wanted proofs to track something you can picture coming into being by motion, which is exactly why symbol-shuffling bothered him.

4. Amir said sure, but the reason geometry is special has nothing to do with pictures. For Hobbes, to know a thing is to know its cause, and the only causes we actually know are ones we bring about ourselves. Geometry is certain because the figures are ours; physics can only ever be hypothetical — we observe effects and invent causes that could have produced them, never knowing which one did. So it's a claim about causation, not about proof.

5. Ruth said that's her point restated — knowing the cause just is knowing the construction. Amir said no, because on his version Hobbes can extend it to politics (we build the commonwealth ourselves, so we can know it a priori — that's how Ruth remembered him putting it), and math-constructivism would never license that jump while cause-knowledge would.

6. A third person who'd been listening said they were both doing charity work on a crank, and the honest summary is: Hobbes had an epistemology he liked, he was bad at math, and he insisted for decades he'd squared the circle, so asking which reading is right is just asking what a stubborn man's private jargon really meant. Amir said that dodges the only interesting question, which is whether the philosophy and the blunder are connected or seperate — i.e. whether the construction view is precisely what convinced him a quadrature had to be reachable by the right kind of generation.
