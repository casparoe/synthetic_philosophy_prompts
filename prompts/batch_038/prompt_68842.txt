I'm a staff software engineer, and once a month I run a 60-minute lunch session for about 18 engineers at my company on decision-making and tradeoffs. Whiteboard, no slides, laptops open but nominally discouraged. I have no formal training in philosophy or economics. I've read the material on the Kelly criterion and on the difference between averaging a gamble across many parallel copies of it and averaging it across many repeated plays of the same one, and I think that distinction is the most practically important thing I've encountered in years — but I do not want to spend the hour explaining it. I want the room to get hit by it and then have me attach the vocabulary.

**What I want them to walk out seeing:** that a bet can be unambiguously favorable in expectation and still be ruinous to the person who has to live through it in sequence; that the correct amount to risk depends on the odds and not merely on whether the expected value is positive; that once zero is reached the game stops, which changes the arithmetic in a way their instinct about averages ignores; and that "the best strategy" is not one thing but depends on which of two different objectives you're actually playing for.

**Design constraints:**

1. Everyone starts with the same endowment of tokens. No real money: tell me what the tokens are made of, and what they're worth at the end — I want the stakes genuinely felt and my cost near zero.
2. Between 8 and 12 rounds, your call. Each round offers the same bet at stated, stable odds, each participant wagers whatever fraction of their current holdings they like, all wagers resolve simultaneously, and the results are recorded publicly.
3. No formula is given and the growth-optimal fraction is never disclosed. The odds must be attractive enough in expected-value terms that an all-in strategy is available and tempting rather than obviously silly.
4. The numbers must reliably produce a divergence between the mean final holding and the median final holding within that small number of rounds: the leaderboard headed by someone whose risk-taking was, on your own analysis, wrong, while most of the room is near zero. I want this to be a predictable consequence of the parameters, not luck. Show me the arithmetic, and give me the probability that the effect fails to appear with 18 participants.
5. At a designated mid-point, introduce a second objective — reaching a stated target by the final round, rather than growing as much as possible — and require each participant to commit to one objective before they can see how the two come apart. They should discover that the wager which is best under one is not best under the other, and that the target-seekers are licensed to be bolder than the growers.
6. Partway through, permit participants to form pools and share outcomes. Let them work out unaided what pooling does to the gap between the mean and the median, and don't intervene unless they miss it entirely.

**Then give me:**

- A minute-by-minute run sheet for the full 60 minutes, including what I say, what they do, and how the ledger is kept so that resolving 18 simultaneous bets per round doesn't eat the session.
- A debrief that maps specific moments they just lived through onto the concepts: the time average versus the ensemble average, the geometric mean versus the arithmetic mean, the absorbing state at zero, why the optimal fraction is a function of the odds, and why a target-seeking objective permits overbetting relative to a growth objective. Every item should point back at something that happened in the room, not at a definition.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a mitigation for each. At minimum, solve for: the participant who wagers nothing every round and finishes ahead of most of the room, and then asks what the point was; someone quietly computing the optimal fraction on their laptop in round one and short-circuiting the discovery; the four or five people who are effectively bankrupt by round three and have forty minutes to disengage; and the possibility that the one lucky aggressive player becomes a hero in the room's eyes rather than a warning.
- An honest account of what this game cannot reproduce, which I will have to supply by saying so out loud. I already suspect the finite horizon and the fact that real odds are estimated rather than printed on a card; tell me what else, and how much of the lesson survives.

One thing I want you to handle explicitly, because someone in the room will raise it: I've read that the whole growth-rate business is criticized as nothing more than a log-shaped preference for outcomes smuggled in and rebranded as a structural discovery about repeated gambles, and that defenders say it's a real fact about dynamics that holds regardless of anyone's preferences. I can't adjudicate that argument myself. Tell me what my game does and does not establish about it, which side the pooling rule in constraint 6 tends to support, and give me two sentences I can actually say when the objection lands.

**Format.** Write the design in prose, then give me a JSON object using exactly this schema so I can script the scoreboard and the ledger:

```
{
  "setup": { "endowment_tokens": int, "materials": [string], "what_tokens_are_worth": string },
  "bet": {
    "probability_of_win": float,
    "net_payout_multiple_on_win": float,
    "expected_value_multiplier_per_token_wagered": float,
    "growth_optimal_fraction": float,
    "target": int,
    "target_seeking_policy": string
  },
  "rounds": [ { "round": int, "minutes": int, "what_i_say": string, "what_they_do": string } ],
  "projected_distribution_final_round": {
    "mean_tokens": float,
    "median_tokens": float,
    "probability_median_participant_below_endowment": float,
    "probability_at_least_one_participant_above_target": float,
    "probability_effect_fails_to_appear": float
  },
  "failure_modes": [ { "symptom": string, "cause": string, "mitigation": string } ],
  "unrepresentable": [ { "feature_of_the_real_idea": string, "why_the_game_misses_it": string, "what_i_say_instead": string } ]
```

Follow that with one markdown table projecting the shape of the field round by round, columns: `round`, `median_tokens`, `mean_tokens`, `share_of_room_at_or_below_25_percent_of_endowment`, `share_of_room_above_target`, `what_the_room_notices`.

Every numeric field must be a value you actually derived or simulated under the rules you specified — no placeholders and no round numbers chosen for effect — and every number in the JSON and the table must agree with the prose. Compute everything for 18 participants and note where the result is sensitive to that headcount.
