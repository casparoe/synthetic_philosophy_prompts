I teach one section of a second-year interdisciplinary seminar on the explanation of atrocity — eighteen students drawn from history, psychology, and politics — and in week six I have seventy-five minutes at a whiteboard, since the course bans slides, to handle the disagreement between Christopher Browning and Daniel Goldhagen over why the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 did what they did. They will have read Browning's account of the Józefów action of July 1942, including Major Trapp's offer to let any man who did not feel up to the task step out and the fact that only about a dozen of roughly five hundred took it, together with a précis of Goldhagen's 1996 claim that the killers were moved by a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism rather than by pressure from above or from beside them. About half the section has also completed an introductory psychology course in which Milgram was taught as settled business: sixty-five percent went to the maximum voltage, therefore the situation does it, full stop. What I want from you is a plan for how I should explain the disagreement, not an explanation of it and not a summary of either book, both of which I know.

The part I actually need help with is the dependency structure. I want to organize the session around three levels of explanation — situation, culture, and character — but I cannot decide whether to name that triad at the outset and then sort the evidence into it, or to keep it latent and let it crystallize only after the Józefów material has done its work. My worry about naming it early is that it degenerates into box-ticking and the students stop reading Browning as a historian doing anything other than applied social psychology; my worry about naming it late is that they never see that culture and character are different kinds of answer rather than two words for the same thing, which is precisely the confusion the debate turns on. Tell me which order you would choose and what turns on that choice. Then give me the sequence: which piece has to be in place before which and why; what to do with Milgram, given that they arrive with it half-digested and given that someone will invoke the Stanford Prison Experiment within five minutes as the obvious precedent; and which single case should carry the whole session, whether drawn from what they have read or from something else entirely, along with a statement of what that case must contain and what it must conspicuously lack in order to do the work I need it to do. My own instinct is to begin with Milgram because it is familiar ground, move to the opt-out at Józefów, and only then bring Goldhagen in as the objection; a colleague insists this is backwards, and I would like to know who is right and on what grounds.

I also want a proper misconception map, specifying the point in the sequence at which each misapprehension must be headed off rather than answered after the fact. I can predict several: that ordinary means harmless, and therefore that Browning is quietly exculpating the policemen; that if the situation explains the conduct then the men were not really agents and the question of guilt simply dissolves; that Goldhagen's cultural explanation is either circular or a claim about national character wearing a historian's coat; that refusal was impossible, which Browning's evidence about men being reassigned without sanction cuts directly against; and — the one that ruined my session last year — the confident announcement that of course they themselves would have stepped out, which hardens into a smugness I could not dislodge in the time remaining. That session slid into an argument about whether Germans were different, someone remarked that if anyone can do this then nobody is to blame, and I never recovered the room, so please design explicitly against that failure rather than merely noting that it is a risk. Close with a single question I can put to the section in the final five minutes whose answers would tell me whether they have grasped that these two books offer competing explanations rather than competing moral verdicts, and with what I should leave out at this level and say openly that I am leaving out — the historiography of the controversy, the archival troubles with the prison study, the methodological quarrels about how much weight the policemen's postwar interrogations can bear — since I would rather flag those limits myself than have them surface as gotchas. Please write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables. I will be reading it on the train on the morning of the class and I want it to run as one sustained argument about how to teach this, not as a worksheet. And please do not recommend readings or propose additions to the syllabus; it is fixed and the students already have the material.
