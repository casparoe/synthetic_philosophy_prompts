So, full disclosure: no philosophy background whatsoever. I run a little coffee-and-wine tasting group out of my garage, maybe eight of us, and over a few years of arguing about cups I've talked myself into a position I'll call palate expressivism. When somebody in the group says "this shot is under-extracted" or "that wine is unbalanced," they aren't describing a property sitting out there in the cup that the claim either matches or fails to match. They're doing something closer to taking a stance — roughly, expressing a disposition to rank this cup below others, plus an expectation that the ranking would survive if anyone else tasted it carefully and in the same conditions. So the sentence isn't true or false in the way "the water was 94 degrees" is; it's a preference wearing the clothes of a claim that everyone should share it. I know that sounds like I'm just redescribing subjectivism, but the expectation part matters to me: the whole practice only works because we treat these rankings as something other people can be gotten to agree with.

I also know about the Frege–Geach problem, i.e. that expressivism comes apart the moment the sentence gets embedded in a conditional or negated, because the same words show up both asserted and unasserted and my attitude story only seems to cover the asserted case, and I've read that people have tried to patch this with attitudes about attitudes or with some kind of conditional acceptance. My own patch, which I haven't seen anybody else propose and which might be terrible, is that in my group these judgments almost never show up bare. They come attached to a comparison — "under-extracted relative to the Ethiopian we had last week," "unbalanced for a wine with this much new oak" — so what's expressed is already a ranking between options rather than a free-floating report. That means embedding is just re-ranking: "if this shot is under-extracted, then we ground too fine" is expressing acceptance of a ranking that carries over from one comparison to another, not describing a state of affairs that licenses a prediction. Negation is then just a demotion in the ranking, and disagreement is two people refusing to converge on the same ordering.

What I want is a map of where this thing is exposed. What would count against it — not the generic "but taste claims feel objective" pushback, but specific observations about how people actually argue about flavor, arguments, formal results, intuitions about cases, and places where the view might just collapse into a rival (response-dependent realism, fictionalism, plain old subjectivism). I want to know what it predicts that those rivals don't, and whether it risks anything at all or whether I've quietly insulated it with the "relative to" clause until it can't lose — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind, since a lot of these positions seem to survive fine on reinterpretation. Also tell me straight whether my re-ranking patch explains the inference or just relabels it, and whether there's an embedding my story genuinely can't handle: questions, "it's not the case that," probability talk, and especially reports about other people — "she thinks the wine is unbalanced" — which is the one I'm already scared of. For what it's worth, the thing that would personally make me drop the whole view is if it can't make room for a real arguement where one of us is simply wrong, not just differently disposed; if all it can say is "our rankings clash," I think I'd rather be a realist about extraction.

Please answer in American English, and don't hand me a bibliography or citations — I can't chase sources and I'd rather you explain everything in your own words. Start with a bottom line of two or three sentences: is this position salvageable as stated, salvageable only with a specific repair, or dead.
