So my psych teacher mentioned this idea called sphexishness once and it's been rattling around in my head ever since. The story goes that there's a digger wasp that stings a cricket to paralyze it, drags it to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to check that everything's fine, then comes back out and drags it in. But if a scientist moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is underground, the wasp comes out, finds it, drags it back to the entrance, and then goes inside to check AGAIN, and it will keep looping like that basically forever instead of just picking the thing up and carrying it in, which would obviously solve the problem. Supposedly Hofstadter named that sphexishness and used it as the picture of what it means to lack free will: not that your behavior is caused, but that you're trapped in a routine you can't step outside of even when stepping outside would be trivially easy.

Anyway, I watched the AlphaGo documentary and I keep thinking about the Lee Sedol match in Seoul in March 2016 (AlphaGo won 4-1) as the perfect test case for this idea, and I want someone to actually work through it with me instead of just telling me machines aren't conscious or don't have feelings, because that's not what I'm asking about. I'm asking about rigidity versus flexibility.

Here's the facts I'm thinking of, correct me if I've got them wrong. Game 2, move 37: AlphaGo plays this bizarre shoulder hit on the fifth line that every pro commentator said no human would ever play, and DeepMind said their own network had given something like a one in ten thousand chance of a human playing it — and it turns out to be brilliant, arguably the move that won the game. Game 4, move 78 I think: Lee plays a wedge right in the center that everyone lost their minds over, people called it God's touch afterwards, also a move nobody expected, and after that AlphaGo's own estimate of its winning chances collapsed and it played a run of moves that looked like flailing before it resigned.

So map it out for me properly. Which of those two moments, if either, counts as anti-sphexishness, and is that even a difference in kind from what the wasp does or just a difference in how many branches the loop has? Was AlphaGo being sphexish in game 4 when it kept going through motions after its evaluation fell apart, and is resigning flexible or is it just another routine firing? And what about Lee — pros spend their entire childhoods internalizing standard patterns and opening shapes, so was his wedge an escape from his own sphexishness, or the only reason he found it is that he'd drilled the patterns so hard?

The part that bugs me most: AlphaGo can play any legal move on the board, break every human convention, sacrifice huge groups, do things twenty generations of pros thought were mistakes — but it cannot decide it doesn't care about winning. Does the sphexishness framework have any way to talk about that, or does it only ever look at flexibility of moves and never notice that the goal itself is nailed down? If flexibility is the test for the free will worth wanting, who comes out better here, and does that mean the test is wrong?

And tell me straight where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to answer. Does this episode confirm sphexishness as a useful concept, break it, or show it was never about cases like this? Also I vaguely remember reading that the wasp story is overblown — some wasps do eventually break the loop, and re-checking a nest that's been disturbed isn't automatically stupid — so does the entomology being sloppy undercut the whole argument, or does the concept survive without it?
