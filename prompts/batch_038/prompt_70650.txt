My father died eleven days ago at the age of eighty-three, and I am delivering his eulogy on Saturday morning in a chapel that will hold about a hundred and twenty people. I am not asking for advice on how to write it, and I am not asking for an outline of what a eulogy should contain. I would like the speech itself, in finished sentences, for reasons that will become clear.

The governing fact is this. From the age of nineteen, my father kept a moral ledger on the model of the one Benjamin Franklin describes in his autobiography: the thirteen virtues, each with its brief precept, one virtue under particular watch each week, thirteen weeks to a cycle and four cycles to a year, with a small ruled book containing a page per virtue and a column per day in which a fault was entered as a black spot. My father copied the scheme closely enough to be recognizable. He was a tool-and-die maker by trade, and he kept the book for fifty-one years, in nine volumes. It is on my kitchen table now, and I have read all of it.

What it shows, and what I want the speech to be built out of, is the following. Order defeated him in every cycle of every volume, exactly as it defeated Franklin. Humility does not appear in the early volumes at all; it enters in 1971, the year after my mother told him he was insufferable at other people's dinner tables. Then there is the part I have not yet decided how to handle. For roughly a decade, in the middle volumes, there are columns in the book that are not his own virtues, and one of those columns carries my name. Those columns then stop — I can date the change to the month — and for the remaining thirty years the book contains no faults but his own. In the last volume the thirteen have become five. And beside one mark near the end he has written in the margin that this one cannot be corrected, using the printer's word for an error already set in type; Franklin used the same word of his own misdeeds, and I cannot tell which of them my father was echoing.

The reason I want the speech written rather than planned is that Franklin's own account ends not in success but in a decision, and I need the eulogy to end in the same place without turning that ending into a lesson. Franklin tells a story about a man who wants his ax bright and free of speckles, takes it to the grindstone, watches the smith work and work, tires of the labor, and consents in the end to carry home a speckled ax; and Franklin says of his own project that it never arrived at the perfection he had been so ambitious of, but left him a better and happier man than he would otherwise have been. My father's nine volumes are the record of a man who kept the wheel turning for fifty-one years and never once claimed a bright ax. I want the speech to hold those two facts together and refuse both of the easy readings of them: that he got there, and that the attempt was a sickness. Neither is true, and my brother will know instantly if I say either.

My brother is the difficulty inside the audience. He regards the ledger as the worst thing about our father and has said so to me twice this week, standing in that kitchen with the volumes between us. Roughly half the room will be men who worked with him at the plant and who will recognize the columns as a shop practice; the other half are family and neighbors who never saw the book and must not be made to feel they are being given a lecture on an eighteenth-century American. So: do not explain the scheme to them. Name Franklin at most once, and if you name him, do not gloss him. Take up the resonance between his trade and his book only once — he could hold a tenth of a thousandth of an inch on a surface grinder and could not hold Order for thirteen weeks, and the difference between finishing steel, which has a specification, and finishing a self, which does not, is the whole of what I want the middle of the speech to turn on.

Three things are private and may not be used. The columns bearing my name may be alluded to at most once, in a sentence my brother will hear and no one else will notice; they must not be described. My mother's remark about dinner tables may be used, because she would have wanted it. The margin note may be used, but its wording may not be reproduced. Beyond those, you may draw on at most three concrete details from the book; it is not an exhibit.

I would also ask that you observe a rule I feel strongly about: put no words in Franklin's mouth and none in my father's. I have not been able to check a single quotation this week and I will not read aloud an aphorism I cannot verify, or a line of homespun wisdom of the sort that gets attached to both men. There are to be no quotations from the autobiography, nothing from the almanacs, and no invented entries in my father's hand. Paraphrase or nothing. Equally, no better place, no he is at peace, no we are all a little like that, and no describing the ledger as endearing, eccentric, or quaint; it was none of those things.

There must be one moment, and only one, where the room is permitted to laugh — at his expense, of a kind he would have entered against himself as a fault. Without it the speech will not survive its fourth minute, and I would rather the laugh come from the men who knew his hands than from me.

Five to six minutes when spoken, which I take to be eight hundred to nine hundred words. The final lines should turn on the ax and should not explain the image: plain enough that the chapel hears a sentence about metal, and the shape of it recognizable only to anyone who has read the passage.

After the speech, and separated from it, give me no more than three sentences of note telling me which line is meant for my brother and precisely where the laugh falls. Nothing else.
