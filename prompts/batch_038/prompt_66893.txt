I have spent most of my reading life on rationalist blogs and forums, which means that what I know about collective decision-making comes from mechanism design, governance engineering, and the coordination literature rather than from political philosophy. My working vocabulary is permissionless versus permissioned systems, Sybil resistance, stake-weighted and quadratic voting, quorums and veto players, delegation and liquid voting, cost-of-exit, capture, and the general question of how to build a decision procedure that does not get taken over by the people it is supposed to constrain. I have run governance for two mid-sized online communities and done design work on a token-based DAO, so I am fluent in the failure modes and not merely in the slogans.

Recently I have been reading around what I gather is called the boundary problem in democratic theory, and I would like it translated into my vocabulary rather than explained to me. My rough understanding of the debate: the membership of the demos cannot be settled democratically, because any vote about who gets to vote already presupposes a body of voters with settled membership; so theorists look for a criterion from outside the procedure — enfranchise everyone whose interests are affected by the decision, or everyone who is subject to the rules it produces, or everyone capable of being moved by reasons — and then argue about which criterion is correct, and about whether any criterion can be applied without already knowing the answer it is supposed to deliver.

First, restate the circularity charge in my terms as faithfully as you can. What is the governance-engineering analogue of the claim that the procedure which fixes membership cannot have its own membership fixed by that procedure? I suspect it has something to do with the genesis block, the initial token allocation, the founding team that writes the first version of the rules, and the fact that every system I have worked on has an openly undemocratic founding moment followed by a claim that everything afterward is legitimate. Tell me whether that is the same structure wearing different clothes or only a superficial resemblance, and where the two come apart.

Second, take each candidate criterion and ask what becomes of it on my side. What does an affected interest look like when the only things you can actually operationalize are a stake, a wallet address, a contribution history, a moderation record, or an estimate of what leaving would cost someone? What does being subject to the rules become when the rules are a smart contract or a community policy that binds only those who have chosen to stay, and when the standard answer to discontent is exit rather than voice? Where the criterion demands something unobservable — that a person's interests be at stake whether or not they hold any token, whether or not they know about the community, whether or not they will ever interact with it — say plainly that my toolkit cannot see it, rather than quietly substituting a proxy and calling the proxy a translation.

Third, and this is the part I actually want: a residue report. For each element of the debate, classify it as (a) translating cleanly into governance-engineering terms, (b) translating only with distortion — in which case say which side got bent and how, or (c) having no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. For the (c) cases, give me your view on which side is at fault. Is my framework missing something real — for instance, that there are people who bear the costs of a community's decisions and cannot be enfranchised because any credential cheap enough to reach them is also cheap enough to forge — or is the philosophers' disagreement less substantive than it appears, dissolving once you notice that each position is stipulating a different scope for what counts as the system and then dressing the stipulation up as a principle? I am genuinely uncertain which of these it is, and I would rather have the question settled than flattered.

Finally, please flag the seams. Wherever you notice yourself tempted to import an assumption from my side — that boundaries are drawn by a designer who stands outside them, that membership is observable, that exit is always available and meaningful, that legitimacy is a property of procedures rather than of scopes — say so explicitly instead of letting the assumption do silent work. A rendering that is visibly strained at two or three joints is more useful to me than one that is smooth everywhere and has secretly changed the subject.
