I'm three weeks into a seminar on diachronic rationality and I've talked myself into something embarrassing. My term paper is supposed to run a money-pump argument against intransitive preferences — the completely standard one, where Dana ranks A > B > C > A at a single moment, a dealer swaps her C for B for a penny, then B for A for a penny, then A for C for a penny, and she ends up holding exactly what she started with minus three cents. Nobody in the seminar disputes that one. But I wanted to check how far the principle behind it reaches, so I wrote down a general version, and the general version seems to prove that it's irrational to expect to ever be a different person than the one you are now. Which I can't accept. Here's what I've got.

Same Dana, but now the cycle is spread out in time instead of held at one moment.

(1) If a series of offers, each of which an agent accepts because it improves her situation by her lights at the moment it's made, leaves her worse off by her own lights than declining them all would have, her preferences are rationally defective.

(2) Today Dana holds A and ranks A > B > C. She takes a job that, as she puts it, changes what she's able to want. In year two she ranks B > C > A. In year three she ranks C > A > B.

(3) Her sister, who has watched her do this twice already and can price the pattern better than Dana can, makes three offers. End of year one: pay a penny to swap A for B — Dana takes it, B is on top now. End of year two: a penny to swap B for C — takes it. End of year three: a penny to swap C for A — takes it.

(4) So Dana ends up holding A, minus three pennies, and each of the three trades was an improvement when she made it.

(5) By (1), that pattern of preferences is rationally defective.

(6) Nothing in the argument needed the changes to be trivial, bad for her, or avoidable. It only needed that the changes be real and that somebody be able to anticipate them. So any agent whose preferences will shift in a way a third party can predict is rationally defective.

(7) And notice the usual escape isn't open in the cases that matter. If Dana could see the whole sequence from where she stands, she'd just refuse the first swap. But the changes that actually matter — having a kid, getting sober, losing a faith, grieving somebody — are ones you can't anticipate the content of from the inside, since you don't yet know what the person who'll be doing the wanting will want. So she can't be sophisticated about it, and the pump goes through anyway.

(8) Therefore it's rationally defective to expect your preferences to change, and the only fully rational agent is one who's finished changing.

I don't believe (8) and I don't want to. I keep wanting to blame (6) or (7), since those are the ones doing all the generalizing, but I can't get a clean reason to reject either that doesn't also blow up (5), and (5) is just the ordinary pump with the dates spread out. Every individual step reads fine to me on a first pass, which is exactly why I'm asking.

So: which step do I give up, why did it look innocent, and what does giving it up cost? Two things I'd really like to keep if it's at all possible. First, (7) — I want to be able to say that walking into an experience that remakes what you care about can be the rational thing to do, not a failure of diachronic coherence. Second, the single-moment pump from the opening, since my whole paper leans on it; if the fix I need here also disarms that one, I want to know now rather than in April. I'm not asking for a bibliography, I've got the readings, but I do want the diagnosis to be specific — name the move, don't just tell me the argument is subtle. Also, if the honest answer is that (1) is doing something different in the two cases, say what the difference is and whether it's a principled difference or just a place to draw a line when you need one.
