I've been sitting in a museum conservation lab for about three weeks reporting a piece on how institutions decide when an object has stopped being itself. Two things I watched: a Baroque cabinet getting something like a third of its marquetry replaced with new veneer, cut to match; and a fire-damaged modern sculpture that the estate has re-milled from a laser scan and reassembled with a handful of surviving original fragments. Nobody in the building wants to say the word Theseus, but everyone has a working theory, and none of them line up. The head registrar wants a one-line test she can put in the accession policy — some threshold of replacement after which the object number changes and the thing goes on the replica schedule. The insurers want the same test, for a total-loss claim on the sculpture. The conservators have their own criterion nobody's articulated properly, which is roughly that a reversible intervention can't destroy the object but an irreversible one can, no matter how small.

So: I want the spec before I write any of this up. What does an account of material constitution — of what an artifact is and what makes it the thing it is over time — actually have to deliver to be any use? Give me the requirements list: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (the object and its matter; the work and the physical thing in the crate; being numerically the same object and being an acceptable stand-in for it; surviving and surviving-to-a-degree), the ordinary beliefs and the practical results it can't contradict, and the institutional constraint that a non-philosopher has to be able to apply it to a real borderline case and get an answer. For each requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it, because that's the part my editor will want and it's the part I can't fake.

Then take a position on the list itself, and don't hedge it: which of these demands are genuine requirements of adequacy and which are just one camp's hobbyhorse smuggled in as a criterion. Which pair can't both be met — I suspect the demand for a threshold a registrar can apply and the demand that the account not offend ordinary counting are in direct conflict, and if you think so, show me exactly where they break. And tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the most popular views. Close with at most three lines scoring the main contenders — the one-object identity view, the constitution view, the artifactual-kind view, and the eliminativist/nihilist option — against the list, and say which one you'd actually recommend to the museum, with reasons. Hard cap of 800 words; if you have to cut, cut the scorecard, not the reasoning. I don't want a survey or a both-sides tour — I want you committed, and I want to be able to quote you.
