I direct a small institute that funds mixed-methods research on institutions, and for two years my senior seminar has contained a standoff I can no longer referee. On one side is an agent-based modeller; on the other an institutional historian who works from archival case studies. Their empirical results have repeatedly converged — on the collapse of one shared irrigation system, for instance, the modeller reproduced the historian's chronology by simulating conditional cooperators whose monitoring effort falls below a threshold, and the historian accepted the simulation as a faithful account of the mechanism. Both also agree that only individuals act, that social facts depend on individuals, and that a good explanation must be anchored in real people's reasons and situations.

They cannot agree on a single question: whether a claim such as "the electoral rule produced the two-party system" or "the erosion of monitoring norms caused the commons to fail" is an explanation at all, or at best a placeholder awaiting cash-out. The modeller treats structural talk as dispensable shorthand and is willing to concede almost anything about its convenience. The historian says the modeller's account omits the explanans itself, because the relevant property — a rule, a distribution of tenure, a monitoring norm — belongs to no individual and cannot be recovered by aggregating individual properties. Neither has moved. When I press them for what observation or argument would settle it, each retreats to a general philosophical commitment.

I have tried the obvious repairs. I made them separate the theses — that only individuals exist and have causal powers; that every social explanation must ultimately be stated in individual-level terms; that a research programme should aim at such explanations — and the quarrel survived the separation intact. I then pointed them to the truce that is now fairly standard in the philosophy of social science, on which causal mechanisms must run through individuals while the supervenience base of a social fact may include relational and structural properties. The modeller accepted this as trivially true and said it changes nothing about what he owes; the historian accepted it and said it proved her point. I have also suggested that this may be the exclusion problem for mental causation wearing a sociologist's clothes, which produced a week of unproductive analogy-mongering.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion: the residual disagreement is no longer about social ontology but about a modal claim — whether macro-level generalizations are projectible and stable under intervention, that is, whether they survive being multiply realized — compounded by a disagreement about the norms of explanation that neither side can ground. It may also be partly verbal, turning on 'cause', 'explain', and 'individual'.

Please diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it. Using what each side actually says, tell me which distinct questions are being run together under one heading; which of these are verbal, which substantive, which are two people answering different questions, and which may be ill-posed; whether a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the real work, and if so whether it is the sort of thing that could be settled domain by domain; whether the truce positions are stable or collapse under pressure, and how; and whether my hypothesis is right, partly right, or wrong. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a reframing, a case study of a particular kind — or argue that none should be expected and say why.
