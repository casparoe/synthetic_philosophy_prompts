I live in a 9-unit housing co-op and we settle basically everything by having each household score the options 0 to 100, then averaging the scores. My brother-in-law says we didn't really choose that method — that it's forced on us, that once a group rule clears a handful of obvious-sounding fairness conditions it has to be an average of the individual scores, up to each person rescaling their own numbers. Apparently there's a real theorem about it. He listed the conditions and I only half followed, but here's my reconstruction:

(a) if every household gives two options the same score, the group has to treat them as tied;
(b) nobody's scores are weighted more than anybody else's, so if you swapped two households' score sheets the group result wouldn't move;
(c) each household's numbers only matter up to their own units and zero point — someone scoring everything out of 5 instead of 100 can't change the outcome;
(d) the group score moves smoothly with small changes to individual scores, no cliff edges;
(e) everybody scores using the same view of the odds — e.g. we all agree on how likely the roof is to fail next winter.

Rank these five by how much we'd actually lose by dropping each one, most painful to give up first, with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does. Don't hedge and don't hand me a list of considerations pointing both ways — I want your ordering and your reasons. And if you think one of them is quietly doing all the work, or that dropping one changes what the theorem is even claiming, say that plainly.
