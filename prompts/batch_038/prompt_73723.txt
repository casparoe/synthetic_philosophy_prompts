I am a professor of philosophy and the faculty director of an undergraduate program that maintains a shared library of biographical material — ancient lives, medieval vitae, modern authorized biographies, memoirs, published correspondence, obituaries, and a few films. The library feeds a dozen courses across the university and a summer institute for secondary-school teachers, so what we admit gets taught by people who did not choose it and who will not have the background to handle it critically. Twice a term colleagues nominate additions; my committee consists of myself and two graduate students, neither of whom has training in ancient philosophy or church history; we allow ourselves roughly thirty minutes per nomination; and the decision I sign determines not only whether a text is admitted but, in a short written entry, how it may be taught. At present we decide by taste. I want to stop, and I want to stop by means of a procedure rather than a slogan.

What I need is a decision procedure my committee can run on a nomination, ordered and operational, whose content is derived from what the relevant traditions actually say about learning from lives rather than from generic media literacy. Three bodies of material should do the work. First, Plutarch's own methodological remarks: his insistence at the opening of the Life of Alexander that he is writing lives and not histories, so that slight matters — a remark, a jest, a trivial incident — carry more evidential weight about character than the greatest battles; the image at the opening of the Life of Aemilius Paulus of reading the deeds of the good as one looks into a mirror and trying to adorn one's own character with their likeness; and his treatment, in the prologue to the paired Lives of Timoleon and Nicias, of the biographer's obligation toward the faults of a subject, where he argues for indulgence on the ground that human nature does not produce unmixed character, alongside his complaint in the essay on Herodotus that a writer who dwells on vice without necessity is not honest but malicious. Second, the conventions of hagiography and what they do to a text's evidential value: the recurring topoi of the saintly childhood, the distribution of goods, the deathbed, and the posthumous miracle; the fact that the genre's purpose is edification and liturgical commemoration rather than record; and the fact that some vitae concern figures whose existence cannot be corroborated at all. Third, the contemporary literature on moral exemplars, in particular the role of admiration in picking out exemplars, the difficulty of correcting an admiration once it has been formed, and the objection that a wholly virtuous life is not a life anyone should want.

The procedure must do the following. It must put its questions in a fixed order and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, so that my graduate students can run it without me in the room. It must distinguish at least four modes in which a life may be used in teaching — as a mirror against which students compare themselves, as a template whose features may be copied, as a cautionary case, and as an object of criticism, where the point of assigning it is to interrogate the admiration it solicits — and it must assign each admitted text to one or more of these modes while expressly forbidding the others, since the central failure we are trying to prevent is a text admitted for one use and taught in another. It must preserve, as a live distinction and not as decoration, Plutarch's separation of recording a deed from imitating it, so that a life can be admitted where its subject's conduct is condemned without the subject being dismissed. It must have a rule for silence: when a text's suppression of a fault is itself evidence about the fault, and when passing over a fault out of regard for our common nature is legitimate. It must stop with a rejection at identified points, rather than always issuing a qualified admission. It must include a withdrawal rule for a text already in the library, since we have admitted things on one understanding of the sources and later learned otherwise. It must specify the two or three sentences the committee writes down for the entry that travels with the text. And wherever a step is in truth a matter of values or of my own judgment rather than something the graduate students can settle from the materials, the procedure must say so on its face instead of disguising the judgment as a test.

Then run the procedure on three nominations. One: Plutarch's Life of Alcibiades, nominated by a colleague in classics for the unit on ambition and civic loyalty — I should tell you now that this is my own favourite life in the collection and that I expect you to reach a verdict I will dislike. Two: a ninth-century vita of a desert ascetic, nominated by religious studies for the unit on self-denial, in which the acts are plainly legendary and the subject's existence cannot be corroborated. Three: an authorized biography of a twentieth-century statesman written by a member of his family under an agreement granting exclusive access to the papers, nominated by political science for the unit on responsibility in office.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of criteria, and what it leaves out that matters. I want you to be explicit about what would change your verdicts — which single consideration, if I supplied it, would move any of the three nominations to a different mode or a different outcome — and to identify the point in your own reasoning where you think it is most likely to fail. If you believe that the enterprise of vetting lives for moral teaching cannot be proceduralized at all, say so and argue it rather than complying with my brief.
