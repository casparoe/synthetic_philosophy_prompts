I am a final-year undergraduate in statistics, and this term I have been permitted to audit a philosophy seminar on the epistemology of collective judgment. We have just spent two weeks on the Condorcet jury theorem, which I understand as follows: given a body of voters facing a binary question with a determinate right answer, each voter independently right with probability p, and a majority decision rule, then if p exceeds one half the probability that the majority is right increases with the size of the body and approaches certainty in the limit, while if p falls below one half the majority converges just as reliably on the falsehood. The seminar has mostly discussed what the assumption of independence amounts to, whether p is a property of individuals or of a question-and-population pair, and why correlation among votes seems to ruin everything. My own training points in an unfamiliar direction: I have taken two courses in psychometrics, spent last summer fitting two-parameter logistic models to a professional licensing examination, and am fluent in the Rasch and 2PL families, item characteristic curves, item information functions, local independence, differential item functioning, person-fit statistics, and the Spearman–Brown treatment of how reliability grows with test length. My seminar partner, a philosophy major, thinks this background makes me incurably reductive, and I want to find out whether he is right.

What I would like is a systematic translation of the jury theorem into the psychometric vocabulary I actually think in, followed by an honest report of what the translation fails to carry. For the translation itself, please work through the theorem's components one at a time rather than gesturing at a general analogy: voters, the binary question, the competence parameter p, the independence assumption, the aggregation rule, the jury's size, and each of the two asymptotic halves of the result. I already have a partial mapping, and I would like you to test it rather than simply endorse it — voters as items, competence as a function of item difficulty and discrimination, the majority verdict as a total score, independence as local independence, jury size as test length, and asymptotic infallibility as reliability tending to one in the Spearman–Brown sense. I suspect at least two of these will not hold up, and I would like to know which and why. Then three specific knots I cannot untie. First, in item response theory the latent trait is continuous and the item response is probabilistic because of where a person sits on that continuum; the jury theorem has a truth that is a single proposition and a competence that does not obviously depend on any continuum at all — what, if anything, plays the role of theta, and does the theorem quietly presuppose a distribution over voter positions that the standard statement leaves out? Second, positive correlation among item responses is, in psychometrics, evidence that a scale is coherent and is precisely what reliability measures; positive correlation among votes is, in the seminar's discussion, a fatal defect. I can see that both claims cannot be naive, but I cannot state where the difference lies — whether it turns on the correlation being explained by the thing being measured rather than by something else, and if so, what the psychometric analogue of a correlation that runs through the truth rather than around it would be. Third, psychometricians can calibrate items against a criterion, run contrasted-groups validations, and detect misfitting respondents; a jury has no validation sample, because the right answer is unavailable to anyone at the time of voting — tell me whether this absence has a name and a consequence in my own framework or whether it is genuinely outside it.

Please write about thirteen hundred words, organised into a mapping section (a table is welcome, provided it has a column recording where each correspondence is strained rather than merely asserting that it holds), a residue section that sorts every claim into translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all, and a closing judgement on whether that residue indicts psychometrics for lacking resources or indicts the jury-theorem literature for being less determinate than it sounds. Do not recommend readings, cite literature, or name papers — argue from the concepts themselves, and do not supply references you cannot stand behind. Assume my psychometrics and gloss the philosophy: I do not need a definition of differential item functioning, but I do need any philosophical term of art explained when you use it. Above all, flag every place where you find yourself quietly importing one of my framework's commitments — a single continuous latent dimension, an operational definition of correctness, a population from which parameters could in principle be estimated — in order to make the theorem look tidier than it is, since those smuggled assumptions are exactly what I am trying to catch. And please engage my seminar partner's objection directly: he maintains that the theorem is about a fact of the matter that is not constituted by anyone's disposition to answer correctly, and that psychometrics can only ever measure the latter. Can my vocabulary express that distinction at all, and if it cannot, is that a limitation I should accept or a reason to distrust the distinction?
