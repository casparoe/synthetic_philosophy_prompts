Right, bit of an odd request from me. I spent forty years doing stable isotopes in sediment cores, and since I retired I've fallen in with a philosophy reading group; we're finishing a run of sessions on the whole 'collapse' industry — the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui — and I've been asked to close the last one with a poem.

So: act as a working poet who has also read up on archaeological method and palaeoclimate records, and think carefully before you start. Sketch me a short plan first — one line per stanza on what's happening and where each end-word lands — then the poem itself.

I want a sestina: six stanzas of six lines and a three-line envoi, with the end-words in this order for stanza one — rain, tribute, count, ash, gone, resolution — and then rotated the proper way thereafter. No new end-words anywhere, obviously.

The form is the argument. A sestina never introduces fresh material, it just keeps permuting the same six words, which is exactly how these collapse narratives strike me: drought, overextended elites, a destruction layer, disappearance — a small fixed vocabulary reshuffled onto whichever society is being diagnosed. The rotation should carry the further thought that what reads as abrupt failure is often a change in the resolving power of the record: cities leave rubble, the dispersed villages people actually moved into leave almost nothing, and two centuries of drift can look like one bad decade once the error bars are folded in. Let 'count' do double duty — counting years, and a people counting as one thing capable of dying at all. Don't settle whether collapse is real or merely a change; the envoi should leave that open.

Someone who knows Tainter on diminishing returns to complexity should find it right rather than decorative, and it must still stand up as a poem for the ones who don't. No glossary, seperate explanation, keep it tight — the group meets at half past seven and I dont want to be reading out footnotes.
