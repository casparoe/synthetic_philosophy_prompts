I have been trying to pin down what I actually believe about when an idea, a form, or a pattern comes to belong to somebody, and I want to do it from my verdicts upward rather than from a theory down. Here they are, in the order I wrote them.

1. A comedian's joke, re-performed word for word by another comic: not fine.
2. The same comedian's premise — the observation the joke is built on — independently rebuilt by another comic in his own words, landing on a different punchline: fine, no wrong done.
3. A list of ingredients and proportions printed in a cookbook, reproduced in another book: fine.
4. The headnote and the photograph facing that recipe: not fine.
5. A researcher who isolates and patents a naturally occurring genetic sequence: not fine. A team that designs and synthesizes a molecule that did not previously exist: fine.
6. A model trained on a large body of photographs that produces new images in the recognizable manner of a living photographer: fine, though uneasily so.
7. The same model emitting one particular photograph closely enough that it could be identified: not fine.
8. A novelist who reads an unpublished synopsis of mine and publishes a book containing my argument in her own prose: not fine, and not merely impolite.
9. A firm that screens plants a community has used medicinally for generations, isolates the active compound, and patents it without acknowledgment or compensation: not fine.
10. A firm that runs the identical screen without ever having encountered that tradition: fine, or at any rate not the same wrong.
11. A slang word coined by teenagers in one city, later registered as a trademark by a corporation that sells it back to them: something wrong has been done, and I cannot say to whom.
12. An ethnomusicologist's field recording made in the 1930s, with the collector's estate still drawing royalties: not fine. But substituting the singer's descendants as claimants does not obviously repair it, and I cannot articulate why not.

The provisional hypothesis I keep reaching for is this: what a person owns is not an abstraction level but a derivation — a claim reaches only as far as what others could not have arrived at without going through you. Cases 1, 8, and 10 are what make me say it. Cases 2, 5, and 6 are what make me doubt it can be the whole story, since a causal chain runs from those photographers, and from that prior knowledge of the sequence, to the model and the synthesizing team just as plainly as it runs to the novelist in case 8.

Three things I would like from you. First, a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Second, that principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed: a database of facts assembled at enormous expense and copied wholesale; a distinctive dance step, invented by one performer and now used by everyone; a liturgy; and a system that produces a joke indistinguishable from a particular living comic's. Third, an honest account of which of my twelve verdicts the best candidate cannot accommodate. The one I would most resist surrendering is 6. The case I keep returning to is one I left off the list: a cook who spends two years in a chef's kitchen, then opens across the street serving that chef's signature dish exactly, having written nothing down. My other verdicts pull in both directions on it, and I want to know which way the principle pushes.

Please indicate how confident you are in each claim you make about what the principle should be, and single out the one point on which you are least sure — particularly if that point is a place where you suspect my verdicts are tracking two distinct notions of ownership that I have failed to separate.
