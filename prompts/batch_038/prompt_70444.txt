I am assembling a set of verdicts about when a biological trait has a function, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I have been told there are two standard answers — one that looks to a trait's history, one that looks to what the trait currently does — and neither seems to fit everything below.

1. A healthy mammalian heart: pumping blood is its function. The thumping sound it makes is a reliable effect of its pumping, and the heat it generates genuinely helps keep a small animal warm in a cold climate. Neither the sound nor the heat is a function of the heart.

2. A newborn's heart with a congenital defect, which never pumped blood and never will: it had the function of pumping blood, and it failed at it. This verdict feels as firm as any I have.

3. A lineage of cave-dwelling fish whose eyes have been shrinking for many generations and are now little more than light-sensitive tissue beneath the skin: seeing is no longer a function of them, even though they descend from eyes that were selected for seeing and still respond crudely to light.

4. A moth population whose pale, speckled wings made it hard to pick out against lichen-covered bark. The lichen has died off, the bark is dark, the pattern now makes the moths conspicuous, and the population is collapsing. Camouflage is still the function of the wing pattern. I also refuse to say these moths' wings are malfunctioning: they are doing exactly what they always did, in a world that has stopped rewarding it.

5. Ground-nesting birds blown onto a hot, treeless island within the last handful of generations. They now hold their wings over their eggs to shade them, and the chicks that survive are the ones whose parents do this. Wing shape has not changed at all. Shading is not yet a function of the wings; it is something the birds happen to do with them.

6. The human appendix. Suppose the proposal that it acts as a reservoir for gut bacteria, repopulating the gut after a severe bout of diarrhea, turns out to be correct, and suppose there is no reason to think the structure was ever selected for this. I want to say the appendix does have this function, and has it in the sense that matters when a surgeon is deciding whether the organ is dispensable. As far as I can tell this verdict and the one in case 5 have the same shape: current benefit, no selection history. My only half-formed thought is that the reservoir role depends on what the appendix is — a narrow blind-ended tube carrying lymphoid tissue — whereas the shading depends on nothing about the wings beyond their being present. I do not know whether that can be made precise, or whether it is just my gut reaction restated.

7. A bacterium that picked up an antibiotic-resistance gene from an unrelated species a few generations ago. The enzyme it now produces breaks down the antibiotic, and I want to say that breakdown is a function of the enzyme in this bacterium, even though nothing in this bacterium's own lineage has any history with it. I am unsure whose history I am appealing to here: the organism's, the gene's, or the trait token's.

8. Two wolves that are physically and behaviorally identical, one assembled atom by atom by an improbably lucky accident, one built to the same blueprint by engineers who intended every part. I want to say the engineered wolf's heart has the function of pumping blood and the accidental wolf's heart has no functions at all. What troubles me is that I also expect function claims to explain how an organism is put together and keeps itself alive, and the accidental wolf is explained in exactly that respect just as well as the engineered one.

9. The case I keep going back and forth on. A rail-like bird that lost flight long ago and whose reduced wings are now, within the last few hundred generations, used to brace and balance as it scrambles up cliff faces. Compare a penguin, where I say without hesitation that swimming has replaced flying as the flipper's function. For the rail I cannot decide whether flight lingers as a function of the wing, or was extinguished the moment the lineage stopped flying, or whether the wing now has two functions of different kinds. If the answer turns on how far back one looks, I want to know what could fix that distance without arbitrariness.

What I would like from you:

- A principle, or a small number of competing principles, stated precisely enough to be applied: conditions on a trait T having function F in organism O at time t, rather than a label. If the correct answer is two-tiered or disjunctive, say so, and say either what unifies the tiers or why nothing does.
- For each clause of the principle, the case above that motivates it and the verdicts it delivers.
- An honest accounting of the verdicts you cannot deliver. Where a principle saves all nine only by growing baroque, prefer the simpler principle, tell me which verdict it overturns, and argue that the overturned verdict was mistaken rather than merely outvoted.
- Three further cases of your own choosing that would discriminate between the leading candidates, with the verdicts your principle assigns. Please include an organelle that was once a free-living organism, and a trait whose current benefit accrues to the colony rather than to the individual bearing it; I expect at least one of those to break something.

Names and dates are not what I am after. I want the principle stated so that I could apply it myself to a case neither of us has thought of.

Finally, tell me where you are least sure. Which clause of your principle would you bet against? What argument or case would move you to a different one? And if you think my verdicts are jointly satisfiable only on an assumption I have not noticed, name the assumption and say what would show it false.
