I am drafting a novel whose spine is a quarrel I cannot afford to render cheaply. Two of my characters — a philosopher of science in the Popperian tradition and an economic historian — argue about Malthus for the better part of two decades, and then, in the last section, sit down and co-author an adversarial-collaboration statement. I need that document to be good enough that neither of them emerges as a strawman; the reader is meant to be unable to tell which one the book favours.

Write the statement. The philosopher's side: the principle of population was a claim about the world — that population, when unchecked, outruns the means of subsistence, so that some check must eventually bite — and the industrial era tested it and it failed; every later defence (the check did operate, only through falling birth rates rather than famine; only over a horizon nobody specified; only in places we were not watching) is precisely the kind of rescue that drains a theory of content. The historian's side: the preventive check was not bolted on afterwards but belongs to Malthus's own vocabulary, and the demographic transition is what a preventive check looks like once children cease to be an asset and become a cost; the theory was right and merely early, and calling it unfalsifiable confuses a long lag with an absence of structure.

Give me the claims both can sign, about what the argument actually asserts and what the historical record actually shows; the residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; and the cruxes, where settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether a prediction carrying no date has a truth condition at all, and another is whether a mechanism confirmed in the direction Malthus specified but by means he condemned as vice counts as confirmation of the mechanism or as a substitution for it. For each crux, give the case, dataset, or line of argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Set it out as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a table of disagreements.

One request as a craftsman rather than a partisan: tell me how confident you are in the agreed claims, since those are the ones my characters stake their credibility on, and single out the point in the whole document where you are least sure of yourself — the place where a real philosopher or a real historian would tell me I have misdescribed them.
