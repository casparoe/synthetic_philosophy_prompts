So I run a little philosophy-of-biology reading group out of my apartment — four of us: a behavioral ecology PhD student, a philosophy grad student, and two self-taught types (me included) who got into all this from an old Slate Star Codex post about spandrels and evo-psych. We've spent the last eight months going around the adaptationism fight that kicked off with Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper in 1979, and it's kind of wrecked my brain, because after four and a half decades neither camp has moved an inch and I genuinely can't tell whether that's because the question is hard or because nobody is disagreeing about the same thing. Both sides are on the same facts about how development works, both accept natural selection, both accept that some traits are byproducts — and yet the rhetoric reads like a holy war.

Here's the debate as I've got it, correct me where I've garbled it. The structuralist side says: build a dome on arches and you get leftover triangular spaces whether you want them or not, so a big chunk of any organism's form is architecture rather than design; organisms aren't assembled part by part, they're grown out of constrained developmental systems; and the adaptationist procedure is basically unfalsifiable, because when one story about function fails you try another, and when they all fail you retreat to a different kind of selection or to drift. The adaptationist side says: selection is the only known process that builds complex functional design, so you literally cannot do biology without asking what a trait is for; the critique is aimed at sloppy practice, not at the program; optimality and engineering models make risky predictions that keep paying off; and "it's a spandrel" is its own just-so story unless you say a byproduct of *what*. Plus the architectural example itself is bad — those San Marco supports are pendentives, there were other ways to hold a dome up, so they were chosen, which is design thinking of exactly the kind Gould says you can't do. Then Gould and Vrba introduce exaptation in the early 80s, which one camp reads as a concession and the other as a clarification, and then evo-devo people spend two decades saying the structuralists were right all along while working adaptationists keep publishing successful function studies and shrugging.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to break if it's wrong: this is mostly a fight about burden of proof and default priors wearing the costume of a fight about how much of the phenotype selection built. Related to that, here's a three-question split I've been using on my friends, and I'd like you to test it against what the two camps actually say rather than just accept it — (1) origin: did selection for the current effect cause this trait to be there; (2) design: is the current form well-matched to that effect; (3) necessity: could development and architecture have produced this form anyway. My claim is that Gould and Lewontin are almost entirely attacking the inference from (2) to (1), while the adaptationists are defending (2) as the best evidence available for (1) and insisting (3) usually has an answer — so they're not really arguing about (1) at all, they're arguing about whether good design is proof of design-for-a-purpose. If that's right, is there any disagreement left once you accept the thing Gould himself kept restating later, that causes of origin have to be kept separate from current utility? Second thing I want: is there a quantifiable empirical core hiding under all this philosophy? If you operationalized it as "take 100 well-studied traits in one well-studied organism, how many turn out on further work to be better explained as byproducts than as adaptations," would the two camps actually give different numbers, and would that bet be resolvable in practice? Third: run one concrete contested case — the human chin, the female orgasm, the appendix, male nipples, whatever you think is cleanest — through the three questions and tell me what evidence would move each side, or say honestly that nothing would. I don't want a lit review or a reading list (I have the papers), and don't bother quoting anyone; I want the diagnosis, argued from what each side is actually committed to.
