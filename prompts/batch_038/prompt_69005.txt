write me an essay on a material-constitution puzzle. not the stock one though — i've got a variant and i want you to actually adjudicate it, not survey the options and shrug.

the setup: a sculptor takes one lump of clay and makes a bust out of it. call the lump CLAY and the bust FIGURE. so far this is just the familiar coincidence case — same matter, same place, plausibly different modal properties (CLAY can survive being squashed into a ball, FIGURE can't).

now the twist. a month in she decides the nose is wrong, so she tears off a walnut-sized bit from a *different* lump and kneads it onto the face until there's one continuous mass with no seam. two questions bite immediately:

1. does CLAY survive the addition? if yes, CLAY has gained a part it didn't have — and notice there's still around, occupying exactly the bust-minus-nose region, a thing made entirely of the original clay. call it CLAY-minus. it isn't identical to CLAY (different parts), isn't identical to FIGURE, yet it's sitting right there, properly inside both of them, doing all the ordinary work of being a lump of clay. are you now committed to a pile of partially overlapping clay objects wherever any lump gains or loses a bit of clay? that's the old cat-loses-its-tail structure, but here the leftover is *inside* the other candidate rather than beside it, which seems worse.

2. if instead CLAY doesn't survive — if the arrival of the nose-clay destroys it — you need a principled line. "only maximal continuous masses are lumps" is available, but then explain why the nose-clay, which was a proper part of a perfectly ordinary lump a minute earlier, stopped being part of any lump at all the moment it got kneaded in.

i want the essay to (a) say what exists on the table after the nose goes on and what's identical to what, (b) defend one answer rather than listing them, (c) be explicit about which principle it's giving up or restricting — mereological essentialism, the no-two-things-of-the-same-kind-in-the-same-place principle, the assumption that every statue is constituted by exactly one lump, the idea that "the lump" picks out something determinate — and pay that cost openly instead of hiding it, and (d) test the answer on the slow version: same bust, but a museum conservator replaces a thin outer layer of clay every year for forty years, and all the removed clay is kept in labelled boxes in the basement, and at the end it's pressed back together into a single lump. does your verdict on the nose case carry over to that one, or does it come apart — and if it comes apart, say where the difference is doing the work.

use whatever machinery you like: four-dimensional worms vs stages, sortals and dominant kinds, vague or partial composition, the problem of the many, constitution as a time-indexed or modal-profile-indexed relation, conventionalism about identity. but end on a verdict, the kind someone could reasonably reject. if you think one of my two questions is malformed, say so and say why, but you still owe me a list of what's there afterwards.

no reading list, no citations, no "philosophers have long argued" throat-clearing — just the argument. around 1500 words, longer is fine if the case needs it, and don't tell me it depends on intuitions unless you also tell me which intuition is the load-bearing one and why i should trust it.
