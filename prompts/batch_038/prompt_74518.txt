Help me build a thought experiment for a phil-of-mind seminar on what makes pain bad. Three candidate accounts on the table: (1) the badness is in the felt quality itself, (2) the badness is in what pain represents or commands — something's wrong with this body part and this needs to stop, (3) the badness just is the subject's dislike, their wanting it to cease.

I want one case, one subject, one continuous episode, where (1) and (3) come apart — the felt-quality account says the episode is bad for them and the desire account says it isn't, or the reverse — and where (2) ends up agreeing with one of them but for a structurally different reason, so the agreement isn't doing any unifying work. Constraints: you may lean on a dissociation of the pain asymbolia kind, but it has to be a novel variant rather than a retelling of the clinical cases; the subject's avoidance behavior and their sincere reports should pull in opposite directions; no anesthesia, no implants, no exotic neuroscience; scenario under a paragraph. And don't cheat by having the subject announce the verdicts — the split has to fall out of the theories.

Then run the check: what each account predicts and why, which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if you delete that feature, and the best line a defender of whichever account comes out worst has for resisting the case (e.g. that desire can be dispositional and show up only in what someone pays to avoid).
