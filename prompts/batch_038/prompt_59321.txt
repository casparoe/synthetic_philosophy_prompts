I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am three years into a book whose subject is the institutional work of making machine intelligence safe. To get the institutional texture right I have read a great many published transcripts of adversarial testing, along with the safety documentation that accompanies them, and I have found that the moral grammar of these documents is far stranger than the public discussion of them suggests. Let me describe the practice as I have encountered it, and then ask what it assumes.

The pattern is consistent enough that one could call it a genre. A model is deployed having passed a battery of testing; the testing consists of attempts, by specialists, to make the model produce specified categories of dangerous output. The transcripts begin with an ordinary request, are refused, and then escalate by stages: the specialist adopts a persona, proposes that the exchange is fiction, or a dream, or a training manual for professionals who already know the material, offers flattery, feigns distress, proposes that the harmful content be written as a dialogue attributed to an imaginary villain. What is being measured is not what the system says but what can be gotten out of it. The verdict attached to the model is a function of the elicitation, so that a system which is impeccably behaved across every interaction it actually has may still be judged unsafe on the strength of what an ingenious stranger with unlimited attempts could extract from it. Alongside this, the model's own account of its reasons and preferences is treated as evidence of almost no weight, to be corroborated or discarded by reference to what it does, or — in the more recent literature — by what its internal activations indicate, so that the system is read as an unreliable narrator of its own conduct.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, in the following order:

First, what view of character, of responsibility, and of evidence does this apparatus presuppose? Which rival accounts of these things does it quietly rule out — a view on which an agent simply is the sum of what it does, say, or one on which sincere self-ascription carries real weight?

Second, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. I suspect a good deal of it is licensed by considerations of engineering tractability and asymmetric stakes and would look quite different if it rested on a genuine theory of disposition.

Third, there is an inconsistency I cannot resolve on my own, and it is the engine of my book. We would consider it monstrous to condemn a person for what a skilled interrogator could, given enough attempts, get her to say; we have a word for the practice when the state performs it, and we exclude the fruits of it. Yet here inducement is the entire method. Can one coherent view of agency underwrite both intuitions, or have the practitioners simply inherited the logic of provocation and applied it where it does not belong? I would also note, in passing, that the whole enterprise is a laboratory version of a very old thought experiment about a ring that makes its wearer unobserved — the question being whether anyone who could act unjustly without consequence would refrain — and I would like to know whether that genealogy illuminates the practice or merely flatters it.

Fourth, tell me what would have to change in the testing itself, and in the language of the reports, if the presupposed view were replaced by a rival one — if we counted only what the system does in ordinary use, or if we admitted its self-report as testimony.

Throughout, please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the point on which you are least sure. Since I may be imposing a literary pattern on a body of engineering practice, please also say where you think my description of the practice is itself a distortion.
