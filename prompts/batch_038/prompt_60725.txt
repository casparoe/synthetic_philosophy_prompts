I've gotten stuck in a rut arguing with my brother about whether it's actually wrong to eat meat, drive everywhere, or skip voting. His whole thing is "one person changes nothing, so you're off the hook," and I keep having intuitions pulling the other way but I can't say what they're tracking. Before I read anything else on this, write out the spec: what would a theory of individual responsibility in collective harm have to deliver to count as adequate — adequate for deciding what I should do, not just for winning the argument. I've been making up my own test cases, so use them and tell me which are genuine requirements and which are just me being squeamish:

- a machine with 1000 buttons where yours is guarenteed to be one of 999 that do nothing, and one of them fires it, but nobody can tell which — you don't even know whether you triggered it
- the landslide: your vote would have mattered only in a tie that didn't happen, and you couldn't have known that beforehand
- the doomed pond: everyone else is definitely going to poison it anyway, so your not dumping changes nothing
- the one-in-a-million: your act raises the odds someone gets hurt from 1 in 1,000,000 to 1 in 999,999
- buying the last unit that tips an order vs. buying one in the middle of a huge order

Also, which distinctions does it have to keep apart that people keep smushing together — causing vs contributing vs benefiting vs just being part of it, and whether "what if everybody did that" is a separate question or a cheat. Say for each requirement why it's a requirement. And most of all: is there some subset of these that can't all be satisfied at once, and which single requirement knocks out the most standard answers?
