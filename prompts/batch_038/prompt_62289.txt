ok so I keep seeing Aumann's agreement theorem waved around in arguments about peer disagreement — "rational Bayesians with a common prior who know each other's posteriors can't disagree" — and I don't think I actually understand what the common prior assumption is doing inside it. Can you explain the difference between reading the CPA as a claim about how real agents' beliefs are structured, as a normative rule for how you're permitted to build a model of a multi-agent situation, and as a coherence constraint on the possibility space itself (something like "the prior is the expectation of each agent's posteriors")? Which of those readings actually does work in the argument, and which one is what lets people slide from the theorem to conclusions about disagreement between flesh-and-blood people?

Two specific things I want you to wrestle with. First, the Harsanyi-style line that a difference in priors isn't a genuine rational difference at all, just a difference in information, so insisting on a common prior isn't a substantive restriction — versus the opposite complaint that the theorem is then near-vacuous, because any interesting agreement result should get its content from the updating rather than from a constraint on the starting point. Where do those two positions actually disagree, and is one of them talking past the other? Second: the agents never occupy the ex ante moment the prior is supposed to describe. If the prior is a representation of a state of ignorance nobody was ever in, is the CPA a claim about the world, or just a bookkeeping device for saying the model hangs together?

and if it's relevant, say whether things change when the state space is infinite — I have a vague sense that unbounded disagreement becomes possible there, but I might be thinking of a different result, so tell me if I am.
