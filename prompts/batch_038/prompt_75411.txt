You are a writer who works equally in analytic ethics and literary speculative fiction. Begin by brainstorming in your own words before drafting any prose: identify which specific claim inside the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism your story will put under pressure — the denial that any social entity is a subject of experience, the charge that interpersonal aggregation is a category mistake, or the claim that what makes a cost bearable when imposed on one's own future does not carry across the boundary between lives. Keep this to a few sentences.

Then write a story of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words set in a colony where the objection's central premise is false as a matter of biology: the population constitutes a single subject with its own experiences, memory, and good. Two further facts must be dramatized through events and records rather than explained: the collective subject's good can rise while every member's falls, and members cannot introspect the collective's states directly but must infer them from measurements kept by an administrator. The plot should turn on a decision to impose real costs on some members for the sake of the collective's good, and on what an objecting member can and cannot say given the facts of this world.

Do not name philosophers, do not let characters state the objection in theoretical language, and do not settle the dispute in the closing lines. Aim for concrete institutional and bodily detail, and for an ending that leaves the reader unsure whether the objection has been answered or merely relocated to a new boundary.
