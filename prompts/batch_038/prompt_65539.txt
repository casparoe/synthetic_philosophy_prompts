I've been going back and forth on the argument about Lakatos's research programmes for a while now and I can't tell whether it's actually unresolved, actually settled, or just badly posed. Here's the version I keep running into. One side says the progressive/degenerating distinction does real work: a programme that keeps patching its protective belt to swallow anomalies is objectively worse off than one that predicts novel facts, and that's a substantive difference rather than a matter of taste. The other side — Feyerabend-ish people plus a lot of historians who actually run case studies — says the distinction only ever gets applied in hindsight, that you can't tell a temporarily degenerating programme from a dead one, and that "novel" is doing all the work while nobody agrees on what counts as novel: is it a fact the theory wasn't constructed with (the predictivist reading) or a fact lying outside the domain of the background assumptions it was built from (the heuristic reading)? On that view the whole thing collapses into we-know-it-when-we-see-it.

To get a handle on it I did something probably dumb. I took a live controversy I know reasonably well — the argument over whether current AI systems might have morally relevant experience — and tried to code it as a research programme: hard core, protective belt, problemshifts, year by year for the last decade or so. Two results. First, almost every modification to the belt looks like a defensive patch when it's made and like a bold extension five years later, so my coding flips depending on the window I pick; I got "degenerating" for stretches everybody would call the field's best years just by sliding the window. Second, whether a claim counted as a novel prediction turned out to depend entirely on which auxilary assumptions I decided were background, and I couldn't find a principled way to fix that set.

So here's my hypothesis, which I'd like you to attack rather than flatter: the debate stalled not because people disagree about the history of science and not because of a taste difference, but because "progressive" is being treated as a property a programme has at a time when it's really a property of a window or a sequence — and once you say that out loud a chunk of the disagreement evaporates, since the critics are right that synchronic appraisal is impossible and the Lakatosians are right that diachronic appraisal isn't merely aesthetic. Is that the actual structure of the impasse, or am I missing something more interesting — a hidden disagreement about what a methodology is for (guidance to working scientists vs. retrospective rational reconstruction), or about whether appraisals are supposed to issue in abandon-this-now advice at all, or a genuine substantive split I'm smoothing over? I want the diagnosis argued for from what each side actually claims, not a survey of Lakatos's views.

Then two follow-ups. Given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here — something formal, like a scoring rule over sequences of theories or a precise novelty relation defined relative to a stated background set; a change of question; a new kind of case study; or should we expect none, and why? And does the reflexive move have any teeth: can you appraise the methodology of research programmes by its own lights, and would doing that settle anything or just relocate the problem? No references needed, I'm not going to chase them down.
