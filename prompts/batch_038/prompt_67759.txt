I joined the colour QA laboratory of a ceramics manufacturer three months ago as a junior analyst. I was in the room for the exchange below and have reproduced it as faithfully as I can from my notes.

The background, as far as I understand it. Our client contract specifies a glaze colour by reference to a master tile kept in a vault. The master is fragile and fades slightly each time it is taken out and exposed to light, so it has not left the vault in seventeen years. In its place the laboratory keeps a working tile, and production batches are measured against the working tile. A batch passes if its colour-difference reading against the working tile is at or below 1.0 on the standard metric we use, a scale calibrated so that readings around 1.0 sit at roughly the smallest difference an unaided eye can detect. Working tiles chip and age, and when one does it is replaced by a newly fired tile made to match it; a replacement is accepted if it reads within the same 1.0 of the tile it replaces. There have been twelve replacements in twenty years. The handheld spectrophotometer we use has a quoted repeatability of plus or minus 0.3 on that metric.

Last month, tile twelve was taken down to the vault and held against the master. The difference between them is obvious at a glance, with no instrument at all. This provoked the following argument at our Monday meeting, between Elena, our colour chemist, and Rahul, the QA manager.

1. Rahul: There is no problem here. Every renewal was within tolerance of the tile it replaced, and every batch was within tolerance of the tile in service at the time. Twelve steps of at most one unit each is a worst case that never occurred; the errors were random, so they largely cancelled. And no one can see any difference between tile twelve and tile eleven, or between eleven and ten. Every single judgement this laboratory has ever made was correct.

2. Elena: Then one of your two claims has to go. Either some renewal was misjudged, or "within tolerance" does not mean what the specification says it means. If a chain of steps, each of which is imperceptible, can take you from the master to something visibly a different colour, then being the same colour as the master is not a real property the tiles have at all. It looks like a property and behaves like one only because we never look at the ends of the chain together.

3. Rahul: It is a real property and it is sharp. There is a number in the contract: 1.0. Tile twelve either is or is not within 1.0 of tile eleven, and there is a fact about that. What is not sharp is imperceptibility. You are sliding from the vagueness of the eye to the vagueness of the specification, and the specification is the only thing anybody is being judged against.

4. Elena: Then your sharpness is borrowed. The instrument's repeatability is plus or minus 0.3, so a tile reading 0.9 might really be 1.2, and you cannot apply a razor-edged cutoff with a blunt instrument. And you still have to decide what happens at 1.05. So the boundary is vague, just one level up from where you put it.

5. Rahul: That is what guard-banding is for. We pass at 0.9, we reject at 1.1, and anything between goes to escalation. The escalation rule is sharp as well.

6. Elena: So you now have three cutoffs where you had one, and every one of them was fixed by a committee rather than found in the tiles. If the boundaries are stipulated, then the answer to whether tile twelve is the same colour as the master depends on decisions we happened to make. That is not realism about a property; that is us drawing lines and then reporting them as discoveries. And it leaves the question that actually matters with no answer at all: what colour is the client's tile supposed to be?

7. Rahul: The master is the answer. Tile twelve's colour is whatever the master's colour is, and the fact that our working copies have wandered off is a failure of process, not a discovery about the nature of colour. You are using an administrative screw-up to prove a metaphysical thesis.
