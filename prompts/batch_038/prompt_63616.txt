I write literary fiction and I've got a scene problem, plus a favor to ask that's maybe a little odd.

My novel has a man who gets his freedom handed back — actually, concretely, the door is open — and he stays, and he's relieved. Readers are going to file that under cowardice or Stockholm syndrome, and I can't fix it with better sentences. I've decided the only way I'll understand it is to make a room full of people build the argument for staying before they know what they're doing. A friend who runs a community reading group out of a bookshop back room has offered me a slot: 14ish adults, two hours, Saturday morning, index cards and a whiteboard and nothing else, no budget, no projector, nobody's a philosopher, and exactly three of them have read the Grand Inquisitor chapter and they will absolutely spoil it for everyone if I let them.

Design me the activity.

What I want them to run into on their own, without being told: the case against freedom as a mercy. Bread first, virtue later. That people don't want a god they choose, they want something to bow to. That somebody has to know this, and that knowing it is a punishment, and the few who take it on are doing it for everybody else's sake. The whole thing has to feel like a favor being granted and not a scam being pulled — that's the part I cant write, and the part I most need the room to feel in its body.

Hard constraints:

- No preamble, no lecture, Dostoevsky's name doesn't get said until the debrief. They discover the shape of it; nobody hands it to them.
- Real choosing with real small consequences, not a discussion with prompts. I want roles, some information asymmetry, and a scoring mechanism that isn't points-for-points-sake — ideally the score measures something the participants would themselves call peace or happiness, so that at the end I can turn their own numbers around and show them what they were optimizing.
- Somebody has to end up in the Inquisitor's chair without having volunteered for it. Somebody else has to be the person who is asked a direct question and says nothing, and that silence has to be an actual move in the game with an actual effect on the outcome — not theatre, not a prop.
- The three who've read it: give them a job that keeps them useful and keeps their mouths shut. Don't sideline them, that's boring and they'll sulk and leak the ending anyway.
- The two hours includes the debrief. Build the timings realistically and assume people take twice as long to decide anything as you'd think.

Give me the run sheet as a table with exactly these columns: Time | What the room is doing | What participants are told | What they're allowed to do | The one thing I must not say or do here.

Then the debrief. Map each beat onto the specific claim in the chapter it's standing in for. Tell me bluntly what will go wrong the first time I run it, because something will, and how to improvise around it. And be honest about which part of the idea a room with index cards simply cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself in the last ten minutes.

Finally, because my real motive is theft: end with a JSON array, one object per beat of the activity, each with these fields — "beat" (short name), "felt_force" (what it makes a person in the room feel, in ordinary words), "as_prose" (one or two sentences showing how you'd try to get that force onto a page), "transfers" (true or false), "why_or_why_not" (does the force survive the trip from a room with other people watching you to a reader alone at 11pm). I genuinely want to know which of these forces are artifacts of having a body in a room, because those are the ones I can't steal.
