Ok so I did a year abroad in Strasbourg and I've been stuck in an eight-month group chat arguement about whether France actually has separation of church and state, and I've become convinced the problem is that "separation of church and state" is being used like it names one claim when it obviously names several incompatible ones. Help me take it apart.

The facts I brought to the fight, mostly from living there: in Alsace-Moselle the 1905 separation law was never applied, the pre-1905 concordat regime still runs things, so the state pays the salaries of Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed and Jewish clergy, the President of the Republic signs off on Catholic bishops after the Pope proposes them, and public schools teach those faiths with a parental opt-out. My friend's reply was that this proves France doesn't have separation at all, it just markets itself as having it. Then someone else said Germany is worse, because there the state tax office withholds the Kirchensteuer off your payslip on behalf of the churches, keeps a handling fee, holds the membership database at the federal level, and you have to de-register with a state authority (not a church one) to stop paying — so church and state are fused at the payroll. A third person said England is the only honest country: established church, bishops sitting in the upper house, a monarch who governs the church, so the slogan just doesn't apply there and everyone knows it. I'm the one who brought up the US, where churches pay no income tax, legislatures employ chaplains and the motto is printed on the money, but where everyone agrees there's no established church.

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making — no single church picked out as official; no public money to religious bodies; no state interference in doctrine or appointment, and equally no religious interference in legislation; no religious tests for office; religious instruction kept out of state schools; the state refusing to recognize any legal order but its own; and whatever else you think belongs on the list that I've missed. For each, say whether it's a near-platitude that all four countries satisfy, whether it's substantive and genuinely contestable, or whether it's just false or incoherent as a claim any real state could meet. Then run through it reading by reading, not country by country, and mark which of France, Germany, England and the US clears each one — I especially want the Alsatian case handled, since it seems to make separation fail inside a country that passes it everywhere else, which suggests the readings differ in scope as well as content.

Two more things. Which reading does my friend need for "France is only pretending" to come out true rather than rhetorical, and is that a reading anyone who isnt already a maximalist about the phrase would sign up to? And is there a version on which separation is scalar instead of binary — because that's the move he refuses to allow. My own suspicion, and tell me if it's a cop-out, is that the only claim all four countries actually share is that no religious body can make rules legally binding on people who don't belong to it, and that everything else is a matter of degree.

No need for sources or case names, just lay the readings out cleanly enough that I can paste the list straight into the chat and make everyone pick which one they're using.
