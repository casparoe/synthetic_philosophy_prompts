Second novel's done and the third one keeps turning into a book about dying, which is not what I signed up for, so partly I'm using you as a way of procrastinating with dignity. But I've got a real problem underneath the plot stuff. I want to know what I actually believe about whether death is bad for the person who dies, because right now my gut is pulling in two directions and the book can feel it.

Here's what I'd sign off on today, going purely by instinct:

- The old Epicurean move sounds great as a line of dialogue and hollow as a life. If it's nothing to me, since there's no me around to be deprived, then why does the death of a 27-year-old cellist read as a tragedy and the death of a 93-year-old who finished everything he'd set out to do read as... fine. Almost a good ending.
- I don't mind one bit that I didn't exist for the four billion years before I was born. Not a twinge. But I do mind the time after. And that bothers me, because I can't find anything in the two stretches that's different except which end of me they're on.
- My grandmother's friend wrote a novel for eleven years and died two months before it came out. It came out, it sank without a trace, nobody ever read it. I want to say something bad happened to her. She never knew. It can't have registered anywhere.
- Same woman, different version: the book comes out, gets a decent review, sells okay. Sixty years later her grandson Googles her and finds out most of it was lifted from some forgotten Hungarian. Everyone knew at the time except her. My instinct says this is worse for her than the sinking, and honestly I don't trust that instinct, because the two cases are the same shape and only the content differs.
- A man dies never knowing his wife had been seeing someone else for four years. I wince for him. But a man who's deceived and finds it out and is miserable for the last year of his life seems worse off still, and that pushes back on the wincing.
- Someone with advanced dementia, no recognition of family, no memory of ten minutes. Part of me says death is less of a harm to them now. Which seems to imply the harm is indexed to a person who isn't there anymore, and then I'm back with Epicurus through a side door.
- My grandmother knew for eleven months. Her sister got the same diagnosis and died in six weeks without ever being told. Same outcome, and I keep feeling the eleven months carried something extra, though I can't say what it was made of.
- And the one I keep circling: the finished book versus the one where the last chapter's missing. I suspect I'm letting my trade answer a metaphysics question for me, and I suspect that anyway.

The two candidate principles rattling around in my head are roughly: badness has to land somewhere in a life that's being lived, or it isn't badness at all; versus: a life is a story with a shape, and what's bad is the shape it ends up having, whether or not anyone was around to feel the ending. The betrayal stuff drags me toward the first one and the manuscript stuff drags me toward the second, and I can't get both.

So: can you state one principle, or a small number of competing ones, that delivers my verdicts here, and tie each clause of it to the case that forces that clause? I need it precise enough that I could argue with it. Then run it on three or four situations I didn't list — pick them yourself, ideally ones that would be useful to a novelist, since I'll want to test it dramatically. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save, and which ones you think are just sentiment I should drop before I build a whole book on them.
