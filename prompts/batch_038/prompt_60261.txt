I'm teaching a grad seminar on choice and self-change this term and there's an essay question I keep circling around. Before I put it on the syllabus I want to see a full worked-out answer to it, so write me the essay — around 2500 words, more if you need it. I want an argument with a verdict, not a survey.

The question: does reversibility rescue rational choice about transformative experience?

The background I'm assuming you already have. L.A. Paul's setup: a transformative choice is one where you can't know in advance what the experience will be like, and where going through it also rewrites the very preferences you'd use to evaluate it, so the expected-value machinery seems to have nothing to feed on. Becoming a vampire, having a child, that family of cases.

Now here's the wrinkle I want you to actually work with, and I should flag that I cooked it up for this question, so don't go looking for it in the literature. Call a transformation *round-trippable* when you can undergo it and later come back out: the one-year novitiate, the secondment into a completely different line of work, the emigration you can always reverse, the marriage with a no-fault exit already priced in, the high-fidelity simulation that hands you the phenomenal texture without the commitment. The natural thought is that round-trippability dissolves Paul's problem entirely. If you can always walk back out, you don't need to know what it's like beforehand — you adopt a try-it-and-see policy and let experience do the epistemic work for you. Choice stops being one-shot and becomes sequential, and sequential choice is exactly what standard decision theory is good at.

The counter-thought, and this is the one I want you to put real pressure on: for some class of experiences, irreversibility is *constitutive* rather than incidental to what it's like to have them. Part of the phenomenology of a genuine conversion, or of having a child, or of burning your boats, is precisely that there is no exit door. On that reading a reversible version isn't a cheap sample of the same thing — it's a different experience with a different character, and so it supplies knowledge of the wrong target. Round-trip options don't fill the epistemic gap, they relocate it.

So: take a side. Either reversibility genuinely rescues these choices, in which case tell me what's left of the original problem and why anyone should have thought it was deep; or it doesn't, in which case tell me whether sequential choice is still rational in some weaker sense, or whether we're just left making these decisions on faith, on character, or on some commitment to a future self we can't evaluate. Whichever way you go, treat the constitutive-irreversibility thesis as a substantive claim about what experiences are, not as a slogan — say what would have to be true of an experience for its irreversability to be built into what it feels like from inside, and whether there's a principle way to sort experiences into the ones where it is and the ones where it isn't. And handle the objection that the person who comes back isn't the person who left, so the sample is contaminated at both ends: you didn't get the real thing going in, and you don't bring clean testimony out.

Invent whatever further cases help. No bibliography, no citation dump, no "some philosophers have argued" hedging — I want the reasoning. And please don't end on a shrug.
