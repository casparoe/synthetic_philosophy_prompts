I am a second-year master's student in a seminar on the indeterminacy of translation, and the course is built around a recurring exercise that I would like help thinking through before Wednesday's session. Our professor introduced the exercise by way of Quine's jungle linguist and the gavagai problem, but the cases we now work on are constructions of our own. She maintains what she calls the ledger: for a stipulated toy language, Tarni, with a complete observational record — every utterance type, the stimulations that prompt assent or dissent, and the natives' dispositions toward those sentences that no stimulation prompts — students submit pairs of rival translation manuals, and she rules on each pair, sorting it into exactly one of three boxes: (i) a mere notational variant, where the manuals look different but are not really in disagreement; (ii) a genuine case of indeterminacy, where the manuals are empirically equivalent yet genuinely conflict and nothing could decide between them; or (iii) not a case of indeterminacy at all, because some further elicitation would settle which manual is right. We are permitted to contest a ruling, but only if we can name the criterion she would be departing from, so the seminar has quietly become an exercise in reverse-engineering her criterion from her verdicts.

The ledger currently has five entries. (1) The Tarni term sulo is assented to precisely when a cup is presented and dissented from otherwise; Manual A renders sulo as cup, Manual B as undetached cup part, with the remainder of Manual B adjusted so that every Tarni sentence receives the same stimulus meaning and the same truth conditions. Ruling: notational variant. (2) A second Manual B renders sulo as the spatial region exactly occupied by a cup and reconstrues every other Tarni term in the same way, objects going over to the regions they occupy, again sentence for sentence; a classmate objected that this mapping was far more contrived than the first, and she said the contrivance was beside the point. Ruling: notational variant. (3) Two manuals that agree on the stimulus meaning of every Tarni occasion sentence and on the natives' disposition toward every standing sentence, including all the elaborations Tarni can form, but that assign different truth conditions to a single sentence of Tarni cosmology — on one it says there are exactly seven rivers, on the other that there are exactly seven rivers and an eighth, which is hidden — and that are not related by any re-reading of the domain. Ruling: genuine indeterminacy. (4) Two manuals differing only over whether the natives would assent to sulo on being shown a lifelike wax cup. Ruling: not a case of indeterminacy; the record is incomplete and an elicitation would settle it. (5) The Tarni proper name orun: one manual takes it to name a distant mountain, the other to name that mountain's shadow; every sentence containing it receives the same stimulus meaning and the same truth conditions on both, and a one-to-one re-reading of the domain carries the first manual into the second. Ruling: notational variant.

This week's pair is the one I have to write up, and it is the first I cannot classify. Tarni has a term kesh. The record fixes assent to kesh when the natives are shown the clear stuff drawn from wells — a glass of it, a puddle, a lake — and dissent in the expected circumstances; the language also forms a numeral construction, kel kesh, assented to when exactly two delimited bodies of the stuff are presented, and a construction we would gloss as much kesh, and the record likewise fixes the natives' dispositions for every further construction Tarni can build out of kesh, including ones we would gloss as the same kesh and as kesh poured from one vessel into another. Manual A renders kesh as water, treats it as a mass term, and reads kel kesh as containing a covert measure unit, two units of kesh; Manual B renders kesh as portion of the watery stuff, treats it as a count term, and reads kel kesh as a plain plural, two portions. The two analyses make identical predictions across the entire record — the same stimulus meaning for every sentence, the same truth conditions for everything the natives say or would say — yet there is no assignment of objects to objects that carries one manual into the other, since they disagree about the logical grammar of the term rather than about what it denotes. Three of us have already staked out positions: one insists she will file it under (i), because nothing the natives say comes out differently and her notational rulings have never required more than that; another insists she will file it under (ii), because every notational ruling in the ledger has rested on a domain-wide re-reading and none is available here; a third thinks it belongs under (iii), on the grounds that some elicitation must expose the difference between a covert measure unit and a plain plural, though I have stipulated that the record closes that off and I would want to be told if you think that stipulation is doing dishonest work.

Please predict her ruling and, more importantly, lay out the candidate criteria that are jointly consistent with all five entries so far — I can think of the re-reading criterion, an agreement-about-truth-conditions criterion, a bare sentence-for-sentence intertranslatability criterion, and a criterion turning on whether the difference concerns reference rather than meaning, but I suspect there are others worth naming — and say what each predicts about kesh, which of them the existing ledger simply cannot distinguish, and why. Then give me your best guess as to which criterion she is actually running, with the reasons for preferring it over the rivals, and describe a sixth ledger entry we could propose to her that would separate the leading contenders as cleanly as possible. Finally, tell me what principle about what makes two manuals genuinely disagree we would be committing ourselves to if the seminar accepted her ruling on kesh, whichever way it goes. My undergraduate training was in linguistics, so I keep wanting to settle the case by hunting for a behavioral difference; please say explicitly whether that instinct is a category mistake here. I have the course packet and am not after references, sources, or further reading — I want the analysis. Please write your answer in American English.
