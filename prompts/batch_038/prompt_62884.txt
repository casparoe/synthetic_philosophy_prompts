You are a metaphysician who works on composition and mereology and who reads enough contemporary fiction to take a working novelist's craft problems seriously rather than translating them away into something more tractable. Please think this through carefully before you answer; I would rather have three well-argued candidates than a survey of everything anyone has ever said.

I am a novelist. My third book is a fragmentary thing — vignettes, interpolated letters, found documents, chapters of wildly uneven length — and for four years I have done little else but decide whether some set of pages belongs to it, belongs to another book, or belongs to nothing. I have accumulated a body of verdicts, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle, in the same sense in which the metaphysician's special composition question asks when some things compose a further thing. I am not asking for a workflow or an editorial checklist. I am asking for the principle, stated precisely, and for an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are my verdicts, as I actually hold them rather than as I think they ought to look.

1. Forty vignettes, no shared protagonist, all set on one street across a hundred years, each written for this book: one novel. Firmly.
2. The same forty vignettes with one word altered in each so that a stray cat reappears throughout: still one novel — but now unmistakably one, where the first version was only arguably one. I know composition is supposed to be all-or-nothing, and I know a single word per vignette cannot be doing that much metaphysical work. I cannot make the feeling go away.
3. Two finished novels, no shared characters, no cross-reference, issued together in one volume under one title: two novels, not one. The binding and the shared title do not make a whole.
4. Two unrelated drafts saved one after the other in the same file: two books and a housekeeping accident. But this is the pair I cannot reconcile — forty unrelated vignettes saved one after the other in the same file is case 1, and there I say one novel. In both cases the only thing the parts have in common is that they sit next to each other in the same container. I want a principle that either separates these two or tells me plainly that one of my verdicts is wrong.
5. A character assembled from three people — my grandmother's voice, an uncle's trade, a stranger's hands seen once on a train: one character. The same three sources distributed across three storylines and presented as three people who never meet: three characters, not one. Same material, opposite verdict.
6. An epigraph quoted at the front of the book: part of the novel. The acknowledgements, printed on the facing page inside the same boards: not part of the novel. Both are prose, both are mine, both sit between the covers.
7. Cut the last sentence: same novel. Cut ten per cent of the sentences, scattered: same novel. Cut the four-page chapter in which the brother dies — under four per cent of the total, smaller than cuts I have just allowed — not the same novel. So it is not quantity; and yet if those same four pages are broken up and redistributed as scattered sentences across the book, cutting them leaves the same novel.
8. A chapter by a collaborator I have never met, inserted unchanged, sharing nothing with my own chapters: not part of my novel. The same collaborator writing alternate paragraphs of a chapter with me, neither of us afterwards able to say who wrote which: one chapter, one novel. Intermingling seems to matter in a way that authorship does not.

My own tentative principle has been this: some pages compose one novel if and only if a reader can trace a single question through them that the whole is trying to answer. It handles 1, 3, and 5 well. It seems to mishandle 2, where the question traced is trivially the same either way; it seems to mishandle 7, where the question survives the loss of the brother's chapter and yet the novel does not; and I cannot tell what it says about 6, since an epigraph is not obviously the sort of thing that carries a question.

What I want from you, in this order.

First, brainstorm. Lay out the shape of the problem before proposing anything. Say what each of the standard answers to the special composition question — that composition is unrestricted, that it never occurs, that it occurs only under some restricted condition such as contact or fastening, and the view that there are only simples arranged novel-wise — would predict about my cases. Say where the argument from vagueness bites, given that verdict 2 appears to make composition a matter of degree, and given verdict 7, which resists every quantity-based formulation I can construct. And be candid about whether a novelist's one book is even the right analogue for the metaphysician's composite object, or whether the analogy breaks at some point I have not noticed — for instance over whether the parts exist independently of the whole, which I suspect they do not.

Second, give me two or three candidate principles, each precise enough that a stranger could apply it to a manuscript I have never seen. For each, go clause by clause and say which of my verdicts that clause exists to capture. If a clause does no work, cut it; if a clause is really a judgment call wearing the clothes of a rule, say so and mark it.

Third, run each candidate on four cases I have not settled: a book consisting of one sentence reprinted on four hundred pages, altered by a single word on each page; my completed novel plus a chapter found among my late father's papers, which I have inserted unchanged and which shares no character, place, or concern with anything else in the book; two of my novels, ten years apart, that share one minor character appearing once in each and are otherwise unconnected; and a novel I have planned in full, chapter by chapter, but never written — do the planned chapters compose an unwritten book?

Fourth, tell me plainly which of my eight verdicts each candidate forces me to surrender, and which of them you think I should surrender. I would rather lose a verdict I am fond of than be handed a principle with a hidden escape clause that quietly licenses whatever I already wanted to do.

Finally, say what the right treatment of the vagueness is: whether any precise principle must misdescribe the phenomenon by being precise at all, and whether the honest options are that there is a sharp boundary I cannot locate, that my judgments are indeterminate and admit of more than one equally good precisification, or that one-book-hood comes in degrees — and what follows for a working novelist if the last of these is true, since I have to send something to a printer.

Do not give me references, a bibliography, a reading list, or quoted passages; I cannot check any of it and do not want it. I want the reasoning laid out so that I could reconstruct the argument myself. Take whatever length the material genuinely requires and no more.
