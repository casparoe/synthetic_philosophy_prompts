A friend and I spent most of a Saturday arguing about advance directives and I still cannot settle it, so I would like your assessment of the disagreement rather than a summary of the terrain.

Begin with the practice, as I understand it. In many jurisdictions an adult may put in writing that, should she lose decision-making capacity, she is not to be resuscitated, or given antibiotics for a pneumonia, or fed by tube. While she retains capacity she may revoke or amend that document at any time and in any manner that communicates her intent; a sentence spoken to a clinician is enough. Once she loses capacity, the document governs, or a surrogate does, and the surrogate is instructed first to reconstruct what this patient would have wanted and only then, where there is no evidence of that, to decide in her best interests. Meanwhile, at the bedside, when the patient in advanced dementia now seems placid, enjoys her food and resists nothing, clinicians and families not infrequently decline to act on the directive in the chart.

My friend's position is that this apparatus embodies a substantive theory of idealized preference: it treats the calm, informed, reflective earlier self as better placed to speak for the person than the later self, and the institutions exist to let her idealized wishes bind her non-idealized future ones. My position is that it embodies no such theory, that the switch actually doing the work is capacity and not idealization at all, and that the practice is therefore internally incoherent — it permits a terrified, exhausted, poorly informed but still competent patient to overrule in one sentence the settled judgment her better-placed self reached over years, while refusing to let that same settled judgment be overruled by a contented patient who has lost the capacity to overrule anything.

Here is the apparatus I think we were missing, and I would like you to use it explicitly. Treat idealization as having separable parameters rather than as a single operation: how much information the agent has; how vivid her acquaintance is with the condition she is choosing for, as against mere knowledge that it exists — the gap between knowing about advanced dementia and having spent a week inside it; how long and how carefully she has reflected; how far the preference coheres with her other values once the inconsistencies are worked out; and from whose standpoint the idealization is performed, the person's own improved standpoint or an observer's reconstruction of it.

Please do three things. First, say which of those parameters the practice in fact keys on, where it keys on none, and whether the revocation rule, the surrogate hierarchy, and the bedside reluctance can be made to key on a single one without strain — or whether the strain is the whole point. Second, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Name rival accounts of authority across time — a purely evidential reading on which the document is only the best available proxy for present wishes, a reading grounded in the persistence of the person, a best-interests reading on which the earlier self is morally a stranger whose preferences count only as data about welfare — and tell me which of these would license exactly the same institutional behaviour, so that the practice cannot serve as evidence between them. Third, say what would have to change if the practice were rebuilt on a consistently idealization-based scheme: which of the three features would be abolished, which would be extended, and whether the result would be defensible or monstrous.

Close with a verdict on whose diagnosis was closer to right, and on what the argument was really about — a substantive disagreement about persons, or a disagreement about how much theory a legal institution needs in order to be coherent. I want the reconstruction and the reasoning, not a survey and not a list of anything to read.
