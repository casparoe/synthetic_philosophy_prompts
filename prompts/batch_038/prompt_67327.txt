My roommate Rae is three years into a dissertation on weakness of will and I've started keeping a log of her verdicts, mostly because she gives them over breakfast and I want to see if she's consistent. So far:

- Marco writes "cook dinner" on the fridge board every Sunday, calm and convinced; by Wednesday he's on the phone with the Thai place, muttering that he knows this is the worse option. Weak-willed, obviously.
- Priya has never made any rule about her phone one way or the other, and burns two hours on it most evenings because she feels like it. No regret at the time, none the next morning. Not weak-willed — "she just likes her phone, that's a different complaint."
- Tomas judged it better to catch the earlier train so he could finish a chapter, had decided to, and then had a panic attack on the platform and missed it. Not weak-willed. Her words: "that's fear, not weakness."
- A runner who'd committed to finishing the marathon steps off the course at mile 18 after a medic tells her the bone is cracked. Not weak-willed — "revising on evidence isn't weakness."
- Elias still honors a resolution he made in his twenties never to argue with his landlord in writing, even though he's calmly concluded it's costing him money, feels the pull to break it, and doesn't. Also not weak-willed, and she called it "the mirror image, and if anything worse."

She also said once that what she cares about is "whether you're being governed by your own settled view or by how things strike you in the moment," and I honestly can't tell whether by settled view she means the commitments you've laid down or the judgment you'd make from a cool hour, which turns out to be exactly where the new case bites.

Here's the new one I want to spring on her. Dana decided in January, calmly and for reasons she could spell out, that she wouldn't take freelance work after six in the evening while she was studying for a certification; she told her partner, put it on the same fridge board, and never revoked it. In April a client offers her a rush job at 7pm and she takes it. Nothing new has come in — same client, same money she'd already estimated, exam date hasn't moved. An hour before saying yes she thought it over coolly and concluded she should take it, not torn about it; a week later she still says it was the right call and feels no distance at all from the want that moved her. Her ranking never flipped as the moment got closer either — ask her about the job from a month out, before or after, and she ranks it the same way. And when you ask why she changed her mind since January she says "because I wanted it," and can't name a consideration.

So: what does she say about Dana? I want the candidate rules she could be running that fit all five earlier verdicts — there's the judgment line, the resolutions line, the preference-reversal/hyperbolic-discounting line, and I think at least one more hiding in the Elias and Priya calls — and I want to know what each of them predicts for Dana, because my sense is they don't all land the same way. Then give me your best guess at her actual verdict and why you'd bet on that rule over the others, given only the five data points and that one ambiguous remark. Finally, design the next case I should put to her: the one that would cleanly separate whatever rules are still tied, including separating the yes-camp readings from each other rather than just from the no-camp.

Tell me how confident you are in the verdict you land on, and single out the one claim in your whole answer you're least sure of.
