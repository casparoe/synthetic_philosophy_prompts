So I'm sitting in on a friend's early-modern seminar this term — not enrolled, I just like the free coffee and the arguments — and last week the reading was Malebranche, and afterwards three of us ended up in a diner going back and forth about the simulation argument. Somebody (I honestly cant remember who) said Malebranche is the one early modern philosopher who'd be completely unbothered by Bostrom's trilemma, because on his view bodies aren't doing any causal work anyway and we only ever perceive things in God. Another person said that's exactly backwards: he'd be appalled, because the third disjunct hands the world over to finite creatures instead of God, which is pretty much the thing he spent his whole career denying. We never settled it and I keep thinking about it.

Here's the setup I want you to run him through, with some specifics so this isn't just "what would Malebranch think about computers." Suppose the argument goes through and we learn the following. We are inside an ancestor simulation run by a posthuman research consortium. The substrate physics is coarse-grained: detail is only resolved where somebody is looking, and unobserved regions are stored as compressed summaries. The operators can intervene in the sim at will and occasionally do, but their standing policy is to intervene rarely and otherwise let the laws run. They built our branch in order to study how a particular plague spreads through a pre-industrial society, so a good deal of the suffering inside is data they wanted to collect. And the operators turn out to be simulations too, of unknown depth — nobody at the top of any stack has ever been identified.

Given all that, work out what he'd actually say. Specifically: (1) which disjunct of the trilemma does he take, and does his answer change once he learns the operators are themselves simulated? (2) On occasionalism and the claim that God is the only true cause, do the consortium's interventions have to count as genuine causation, or can he demote them — and if he demotes them, is there anything left to the discovery that we're in a computer? (3) His general/particular volitions distinction looks like it should do heavy lifting here: is a rarely-intervening operator just running its own general volitions, and does that multiply lawgivers in a way his system can't absorb? (4) He also holds that reason doesn't conclusively establish the existence of the material world and that we perceive bodies only through ideas in God — so is there any difference at all, for him, between "the physical universe" and "a sim"? And does finding out we're simulated touch his theodicy, or the idea that God acts by the simplest and most general ways for his own glory? (5) One of us claimed Malebranche would just agree with the modern line that a simulation is genuinely real and involves no downgrade from ordinary matter; tell me whether you think that's right or whether he'd reject it for reasons internal to his own system. (6) The plague-study detail: does a world built to harvest suffering make the evil in it more excusable or less on his account, and what would Arnauld have thrown back at him about it?

No reading list, no page refs, no citations — I can't look anything up and frankly you can't either, so don't pretend. Just argue it from the system as you understand it, and be explicit about which moves are grounded in something he actually wrote versus which are you extrapolating. I also want you to flag the one place where you think his framework has no resources at all to respond, the crack this scenario pries open. Finish with a couple hundred words in his own voice, addressed to the consortium researcher who has just told him that we're a study.
