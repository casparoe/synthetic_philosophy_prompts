I've read rationalist blogs and their comment sections for the better part of two decades, and one phrase surfaces in every thread that touches on coercion: "Your liberty to swing your fist ends just where my nose begins." The attribution is a mess — I've seen it handed to Holmes, to Mill, and to at least two other people — but the deployment is always identical: it arrives at the point where an argument is about to have to say something precise, and it ends the argument instead.

Last month our city council floated two measures at once, and the local forum ran a single long thread on both. Measure A: a ban on gas-powered leaf blowers, justified by noise and particulates. Measure B: a "quiet credit" scheme under which every household receives an annual allotment of decibel-hours above the ambient limit — for parties, remodels, whatever — and may sell, gift, or trade those credits to neighbors. One commenter, whom I'll call R, used the slogan twice in one post, to opposite effect. Against A, he argued that a ban on a machine nobody is obliged to own stops no one's fist: the law merely declines to hand you a leaf blower, and withholding a benefit takes nothing away. In favor of B, he argued that the credit market is the fist-and-nose principle done properly, since a purchased credit means the noise reaching my ear arrives with my nose's advance consent — no crossing at all.

I think at least one of those moves fails, but I can't say which, and I suspect the sentence is doing different work in each. Please unpack it for me.

1. Lay out the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making. For each, state it as a self-contained thesis and make explicit the three things the metaphor conceals: what counts as a fist (which actions), what counts as a nose (which interests or boundaries), and what counts as reaching it (physical contact? causal contribution? imposed risk? a shift in probability? one part of a diffuse aggregate?).
2. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive but defensible claim, or false — and if false, give the counterexample that kills it.
3. Say which reading, if any, supports R on Measure A, and which supports him on Measure B. If the two require different readings, say so plainly, and say whether any single reading gets him both conclusions.
4. Run the readings against two further cases I keep returning to. First: an HOA that fines me fifty dollars a day for painting my front door a color it disapproves of — nobody prevents me, they only make it expensive. Second: a noise-permit ordinance under which permits are granted on request and never refused, so no application has ever been denied. R says the permit requirement therefore changes nothing about anyone's freedom; I feel less free under it and cannot articulate why. I want the readings to explain that feeling or to tell me it's an illusion.
5. Tell me what the sentence would have to be amended into to survive scrutiny, and whether the amended version is still worth repeating — or whether it loses its force precisely because it must now say aloud what it was smuggling in.
6. I've used this line myself in older posts, usually against people arguing that regulation is theft. Given the readings, tell me which one I was most likely assuming and whether it's one I should keep.

I'd like the readings as a numbered menu I could paste into the thread, each with a one-line verdict, before any longer discussion. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want the disambiguation and the verdicts, not the scholarship.
