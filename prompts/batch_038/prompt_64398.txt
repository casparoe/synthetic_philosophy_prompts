I am the president of a small private college — call it K—, founded 1871, currently 480 students — and in nine days I will give the last commencement address in its history. The board voted in March to close at the end of the academic year, on my recommendation. In the first rows: the 104 graduating seniors and their families. Behind them: faculty and staff who are losing their jobs. Further back: trustees, and alumni who spent a decade telling me the college would survive if I had only tried harder. One reporter from the regional paper will be in the balcony.

Facts the speech has to work with, all true and all already known in the room. For twenty years our planning documents argued from the inside view: donor loyalty, the music program, our location, our niche in teacher preparation, the unusual strength of this senior class. In 2011 we commissioned a study of institutions in our region and enrollment band and learned that roughly a third of them had stopped enrolling students within twenty years; we filed the study and reasoned our way out of it. Enrollment fell anyway. The endowment never recovered from 2008. And the history we recite at every commencement since 1871 — we survived the 1930s, we survived the closures of the 1970s, we saw the demographic cliff coming — is a history told exclusively by survivors, and this year I am the one who has to say so.

Write the address: about twelve minutes, roughly 1,600 words, meant to be spoken, no podium jokes about the weather.

The engine is reference-class forecasting, and it must do structural work, not decorate. Four demands.

First, no inside-view consolation. "We beat the odds for 150 years," "our kind of thing endures," "you were the exception" — every version of those sentences is available to me and I have decided to use none of them. The address must still be bearable. Find the other way.

Second, no outside view as exoneration either. "We were a statistic all along," "the base rate did it" — I don't believe that, and it hands the room a determinism nobody in it actually holds. Say plainly how much of this closure is the general fate of institutions of this kind and how much is decisions that I and my predecessors made. Commit to a split, defend it in the speech's own idiom, and don't hover between the two framings to stay safe.

Third, handle the reference-class problem honestly at a level a mixed room can follow, without naming it or citing anyone. The one-third figure depends entirely on how the class was drawn — our state, our region, our enrollment band, founded before 1900, endowment below some threshold — and the number moves as the boundaries move. Worse, we know which class we belong to because we already know the outcome: the class was drawn around a body. If the speech uses numbers, it must be candid that the numbers were chosen; if it declines to, it must say why declining isn't a dodge. Pick one path and make the choice legible.

Fourth, the seniors. They have been told for four years that they were special to this place, and they will hold a degree from an institution that will not exist in ninety days: transcript requests handled by a state agency, the campus becoming something else, no future commencement that is theirs. Tell me — inside the speech, not in a note to me — whether a member of a reference class whose members mostly perish can be an exception in any sense worth wanting, and what these 104 people are actually being told. Do not resolve it with "your education belongs to you and no one can take it away," which belongs to the same dishonest family as the first demand.

Manner: no quotations from anyone living or dead, no Latin, no scripture, no mention of the reporter or of the litigation. It should sound like someone who has read the forecasting literature and will not show off about it; the only term from it that may be spoken aloud is "base rate," at most twice. A parent in the second row must be able to hear a farewell and a faculty member who teaches statistics an argument, with neither feeling addressed at the other's expense. Mention my own career only where the speech needs someone to be answerable for something.

Give me the address and nothing else: begin with its first sentence, with no preface, no title, and no explanation of your choices beforehand. Then, after a blank line and under the heading "Liberties," no more than five sentences on where you took liberties with the forecasting ideas, where you leaned on a contested view of what a base rate licenses you to say about a single case, and which of my four demands you found hardest and how far you bent it.
