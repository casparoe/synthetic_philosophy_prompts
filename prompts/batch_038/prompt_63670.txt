I've been teaching the analytic/synthetic fight to the same cohort for a few years now and I've become convinced the debate is a fossil: the texts haven't changed since 1951, everybody on both sides knows every move the other side is about to make, and nobody has ever budged anybody. That makes me suspect the disagreement isn't the one people think it is, and I'd like help figuring out what it actually is.

The two camps as I see them. Carnap-style side: inside a framework you've chosen, analyticity is just a matter of the meaning postulates you laid down, the notion is perfectly well defined there, and it earns its keep becuase it explains necessity and apriority and underwrites the difference between asking a question inside a framework and asking whether to adopt the framework at all. Quine side: the whole family of intensional notions -- analyticity, synonymy, necessity, self-contradictoriness, meaning postulate -- is a circle you can't get into from the outside; the only clean instances are artificial languages, and there the word just reports what the designers stipulated, so it does no explanatory work; plus holism, i.e. any sentence could be kept whatever the evidence turned out to be provided you make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system, and no sentence is unrevisable in principle, so the most the boundary could be is a measure of how centrally entrenched a belief is. Then the positions in between that I know about: Grice and Strawson argue that the systematic agreement in how people sort examples, and the surprise people show when someone proposes a counterexample, already establish that there's a real distinction there and that Quine never earned his rejection of it; and Boghossian separates a metaphysical notion, truth purely in virtue of meaning, from an epistemic one, being justified in believing something merely on the basis of understanding it, and says Quine's arguments at most kill the second.

Here's my own bit, which is why I'm asking. Last term I ran a stripped-down exercise: I took the word 'analytic' off the table entirely and asked people two questions about each sentence -- could a competent speaker who fully understands it rationally withhold assent, and if it turned out false would we have to say the speaker had been misusing a word rather than got something wrong about the world. The Carnapians and the Quineans gave the same answers on maybe nine cases out of ten: 'all bachelors are unmarried', '7 + 5 = 12', the axioms, the classical logical laws, everyone agreed. The splits came on 'whales are fish' as used before Linnaeus, 'all vixens are foxes' once you learn the actual biology, and 'every event has a cause'. And what struck me is that when people disagreed about the classification they usually agreed about all the facts and disagreed about which way the explanation runs: is a sentence hard to revise because its truth is settled by meaning, or does saying its truth is settled by meaning just describe the fact that it's so central we won't give it up? Same verdict on the cases, opposite explanatory order.

So my hypothesis is that the surviving dispute is neither verbal nor about extension but about direction of explanation -- about who owes what account to whom -- and that priority disputes of that shape may be unresolvable in principle, which would explain seventy years of nobody moving. I want you to take the alternatives seriously rather than wave them off: that it is verbal after all, a fight about what 'meaning' and truth-in-virtue-of mean, so the camps have never actually contradicted each other; that they're answering different questions under one label, one about the semantics of a constructed language and one about the epistemology of belief revision in science; that there's a hidden empirical question doing the work, e.g. about how word-learning actually goes -- could a child acquire 'bachelor' and 'unmarried' with no rule-governed synonymy at all, just distributionally -- with the impasse persisting because neither side lets the other's evidence count as evidence; or that the question is ill-posed becuase immunity to revision isn't a property sentences have, only whole theories do.

Diagnose it. Reasons that engage what each side actually says, not a tour of positions, and commit to one diagnosis rather than hedging across all of them. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: is there any conceivable formal result, discovery, thought experiment, or reformulation of the question that would break the tie, or should I tell my students honestly that this one is finished? Be explicit about whether direction-of-explanation disputes can ever be settled at all, since that's the load-bearing part of my hypothesis.

Don't give me a reading list, I've got one. Do tell me what would change your mind: which of my alternatives, or what consideration, would push you to a different diagnosis, and where you think my seminar data is most likely to be misleading you.
