I write a long-form column on the science of collective cognition, and I've hit a wall that seems to me more philosophical than empirical. In the course of reporting on how groups arrive at decisions, I kept coming back to the Anglo-American criminal jury, which appears to operate on a set of assumptions about group minds that no laboratory study of collective intelligence ever licenses. I'd like you to tell me what theory of collective agency the practice actually presupposes.

Here are the features I have in mind, all of them routine:

- The verdict is returned as a single unattributed act. The court records that the jury found the defendant guilty; no vote tally, no dissenting opinion, and no account of who persuaded whom ever becomes part of the record. The foreperson speaks, but speaks for a body.
- Afterward, jurors are broadly barred from testifying about what was said in the room, whose vote shifted and why, or their own mental processes in reaching the verdict. The exceptions are narrow and external: prejudicial information smuggled in from outside, improper outside pressure on a juror, a clerical error in entering the verdict on the form. The inner life of the deliberation is sealed.
- Courts have long treated a quotient verdict — jurors agreeing in advance to be bound by the arithmetic mean or some other tally of their individual votes — as defective, and likewise a frank compromise verdict in which each side trades down from what it actually believes. Yet the unanimity produced by hours of argument is treated not as a happy accident but as the expected outcome, to the point that judges may give a supplemental instruction urging a deadlocked minority to re-examine its position in light of the majority's reasoning while not surrendering convictions it honestly holds. A hung jury is a failure, not a data point.
- Meanwhile some jurisdictions are content, after prolonged deadlock, with a substantial majority verdict; and the same legal system that treats the verdict as indivisible also holds each juror individually to an oath, individually examines them for bias before empaneling, and individually punishes a juror who lied to get seated.

Against that background: what must be true about juries for these rules to make sense rather than to be mere administrative convenience? Is the verdict supposed to be a judgment of a single agent that is not reducible to twelve private judgments, and if so, in what does the irreducibility consist — the process by which it forms, the obligations the jurors incur toward one another, the fact that no aggregation rule over pre-deliberative attitudes would reproduce it? What exactly is wrong with a quotient verdict on this picture, and why would argument cure a defect that arithmetic cannot? What does the evidentiary seal imply about whether a group act can be decomposed into its members' contributions? Does the instruction to a deadlocked minority assume that a holdout can come genuinely to share the verdict, or only to acquiesce in it — and does the law care which?

Then please be skeptical on my behalf. Distinguish what these rules strictly require from what merely sits comfortably with them, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: how much of it could be explained by finality, the protection of jurors from harassment, and the economics of retrials, with no commitment whatsoever to group agency, and what in the practice would resist that deflationary reading. Note also whether the parts presuppose consistent things — indivisible group act and individually responsible jurors; unanimity as the norm and majority verdicts as the fallback.

Finally, run the counterfactual. If courts adopted a forthrightly aggregative conception, on which a verdict is nothing over and above a tally of individual beliefs, tell me which specific institutional arrangements would have to change, and whether the resulting practice could still deliver the one thing the present practice seems designed to produce: a verdict that can be addressed, and answered for, as the act of a single body.
