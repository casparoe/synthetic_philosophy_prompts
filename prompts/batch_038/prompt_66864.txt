**Question 3 (20 pts).** Explain the accuracy-dominance argument for Probabilism. Your answer should cover (a) what constraints the argument places on the measure of inaccuracy it uses and why those constraints are needed, (b) exactly what the dominance theorem establishes about a credence function that violates the probability axioms — and what it does not establish, (c) in what sense the argument is meant to be nonpragmatic, and whether that is enough to answer the objection that it still smuggles in a desire-like state, and (d) whether the same line of argument extends to Conditionalization, and what has to change if it does.

**Student's answer.**

Probabilism is the claim that a rational agent's credences have to obey the probability axioms: non-negative, summing to 1 across a partition, and additive over disjoint propositions. The accuracy argument tries to establish this without appealing to betting at all.

The first step is to say how accurate a credence function is. You choose a scoring rule that assigns an inaccuracy to a single credence at a single world, then extend it to a whole credence function by summing across the propositions in the agenda. The canonical example is the Brier score, which measures inaccuracy as the squared difference between your credence and the truth-value of the proposition, so that being certain of a truth costs nothing and being certain of a falsehood costs the maximum.

Not just any scoring rule will do. Joyce imposes conditions on it: additivity, so the inaccuracy of the whole is the sum of the inaccuracies of the parts; normality, so a credence's score depends only on that credence and the truth-value of the proposition it attaches to; convexity, so the penalty grows the further you move from the truth; and propriety. A rule is proper when the credence you actually hold scores better than any other credence you could have held instead. The Brier score is proper in this sense, which is why it gets used.

Given those conditions the theorem says: if b is not a probability function, then there is some probability function b\* that is strictly less inaccurate than b at every possible world. The converse holds too — no probability function is strongly dominated by anything. So an incoherent agent is guaranteed to be less accurate than she could have been, no matter how the world turns out.

What the theorem does not say is that coherent credences are accurate. A perfectly coherent credence function can be badly off in the actual world; the claim is only that no single alternative beats it everywhere. Coherence is a kind of insurance against guaranteed avoidable error, not a guarantee of truth.

This is where the argument is supposed to be nonpragmatic. The Dutch book argument for the same conclusion says that an incoherent agent's credences license a package of bets that loses money come what may. Joyce's argument is that same theorem with the payoffs relabelled: instead of counting dollars you count inaccuracy, and the dominating credence function is the sure-loss package read the other way round. Because the units have been swapped from money to accuracy, nothing in the argument depends on what the agent wants, and so the objection that it presupposes a desire-like state never gets a grip — there is no desire anywhere in the premises.

For Conditionalization the same dominance strategy goes through. If your plan for updating on a piece of evidence isn't to conditionalize on it, then some conditionalizing plan is more accurate than yours at every world, which means conditionalizing is guaranteed to leave you more accurate than you were before you learned anything. That's the payoff of the accuracy-first framework: one argument answers both questions.
