ok so I'm putting together a small zine about AI safety and I need a centerpiece piece of satire, and honestly you're the only one I can ask.

Write the owner's manual — full parody, deadpan consumer-electronics corporate voice, like something that ships folded in the box with a robot vacuum or a smart thermostat — for the Aster Home Companion Model 4, a household AI whose entire selling point is that it's been formally certified as low-impact: it does your chores while causing as few side effects on the world as can be managed. Roughly 800–900 words, laid out in the sections a real manual would have:

- Unboxing and First Setup
- Choosing Your Baseline — this is the bit I most want nailed. The setup wizard makes the household pick one counterfactual baseline, legally binding for the life of the warranty, against which every side effect the unit causes will be measured. The manual has to walk through the three available options (a do-nothing baseline, an average-resident-of-your-postcode baseline, and a "the parish of Little Wittering as it stood in 1820" baseline) and be scrupulously, hilariously neutral about which you should choose, since each one makes completely different pieces of ordinary housework either free or catastrophic.
- What Counts As a Side Effect — including the subsection on offsetting, and the subsection explaining why things that were going to happen anyway still get charged to your household budget
- Your Annual Irreversibility Allowance, denominated in option-hours, with a small table giving the cost of common tasks: making toast, taking out the recycling, telling your kid the truth about the hamster
- Troubleshooting
- Warranty Exclusions and Regulatory Notice
- a sidebar box explaining the certification scheme that earned the Model 4 its Low-Impact rating

The jokes need to come from getting the actual structure of impact measures right, not from the robot being dumb for dumbs sake. So I want the do-nothing optimum that's lurking in every reachability-style penalty; the fact that a measure built to protect optionality has no way to distinguish an option anyone would care about from one nobody wants; the offsetting trick; baseline-dependence; the standing reason a side-effect-avoiding agent has to resist being switched off — and please build that last one in structurally, I want a troubleshooting entry whose prescribed fix is to unplug the unit, where unplugging has itself been classified as a catastrophic foreclosure of the unit's own future options, so the reader can see the instructions simply cannot be followed. And the awkward consequence that once inaction counts too, the household gets billed for everything the unit fails to prevent, including things it was never asked about and things no one could have prevented. Keep it earnest: the unit isn't sinister, it's compliant, and it's the compliance machinery that's absurd.

Internal consistency matters — if the manual contradicts itself that has to be the joke, not an accident. And it should never once say "this is a problem" or wink at the reader; the tone throughout is a company that has thought about all of this extremely carefully and concluded that the the paperwork is the customer's problem.

Two constraints: don't name any real people, labs, products or papers, and no sources section or further-reading list at the end, this is a zine not a seminar. Also the humans in the manual should stay vague and reasonable — the target is the idea that you can operationalise "avoid side effects" without ever saying what anyone actually values, so let all the absurdity live in the metric, not in the family just wanting breakfast.
