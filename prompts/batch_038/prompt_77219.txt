I want to work out whether my judgments about first-person authority add up to anything coherent, so here they are, case by case.

**Pain.** If I say something hurts, no outside evidence can show I'm wrong — at most it can show I'm wrong about its cause or its location.

**Visual experience.** Not the same. I was utterly certain for years about the colour of a wall in the house I grew up in, and when I went back I found I had it wrong. My confidence then felt exactly like my confidence now about things I'd still bet on.

**Mental imagery.** Worse. I used to describe my images as clear and detailed, until I tried doing things with them — reading the small print on an imagined page, counting the windows of an imagined façade — and found I couldn't. My self-assessments of vividness turned out to be close to worthless.

**Belief.** I don't seem to look inward at all. Asked whether I think it will rain tomorrow, I don't consult a feeling; I consider the sky, the forecast, the season. Yet having done that I'm prepared to report my own belief, and I'm rarely wrong in a way that surprises me.

**Motive and preference.** Here I'm an unreliable witness. I've produced confident, articulate reasons for choices I now suspect were made for reasons I'd disown, and I've watched myself fail to notice a substitution in what I had just picked out as my favourite.

**Character.** My partner and my closest friends read my temperament better than I do. When they tell me I go evasive under criticism, I recognise it as true and had never thought of it myself.

**Anger.** Split. Sometimes it's undeniable from the inside. But sometimes someone else is right that I'm not angry, merely exhausted, and I'm the one who's wrong to insist.

So I'm inclined to say first-person authority is real for pain, real but differently sourced for belief, and largely illusory for imagery, motive, and character. What account delivers all of that? I want a principle, or a small set of candidates, each stated precisely, together with which of my cases motivates which clause. Then say what your preferred account predicts for cases I haven't listed — whether I can be wrong that I'm trying, or that I have just decided — and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot save. The one I'm most attached to and least sure how to keep is the split verdict on anger: I want avowals to be immune in the way pain reports are, but I don't want to say the other person is simply mistaken about my anger. Please argue it through rather than pointing me at the literature.
