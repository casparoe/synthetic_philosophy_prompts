I'm giving a 20-minute paper at an interdisciplinary methods workshop and I want the Q&A prep, not a defence of the paper.

The room: mostly classical and Near Eastern archaeologists of a processualist stripe who think "hunting for the historical kernel" is a category error dressed up as scholarship; two oral-tradition people in the Parry–Lord lineage who will insist that formulaic composition *generates* detail rather than transmitting it; a couple of ancient historians whose whole question is going to be whether my criterion is falsifiable or just a nicer-sounding way of saying "trust your gut"; and one Hebrew Bible scholar who has spent twenty years in the Exodus debate and will deeply resent Troy and Arthur being waved at her in the same breath as her material.

My paper's thesis, in short: the "is there a historical core here?" question is being asked at the wrong level. Don't ask whether a given detail is true — ask whether the society that produced the tradition had a *channel* with the capacity to carry that type of fact across the gap between the events and the earliest surviving text, where capacity means genre, institution, and incentive-structure, not merely "they had writing". The sketch I'll give is that the Linear B palace archives are exactly such a channel for some payloads and structurally incapable of others; that the post-palace centuries have no channel at all for palace-level political content; that in the Arthur case a channel does exist but it is dynastic/genealogical and gets conscripted to carry battle-topography, which is a different payload with different corruption properties; and that the Exodus case is genuinely different again because the payload in question is a mass movement rather than a named event, so the test bites from the other direction. I'm explicit that the test licenses no inference from "a channel existed" to "the content came through intact" — it only sorts traditions into candidates and non-candidates. I know that concession is where I'm exposed.

So: prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones a philosophy audience or a general-interest journalist would raise at the same paper — ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it.
