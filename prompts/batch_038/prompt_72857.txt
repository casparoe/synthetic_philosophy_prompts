I'm auditing a graduate seminar in democratic theory in which the participants have settled into two camps: one that treats formal social choice theory as the only rigorous way to think about collective decisions, and another that regards that literature as a largely inert set of impossibility results with no purchase on real politics. I've volunteered to present a case study that might adjudicate between them, and I've chosen the United Kingdom's withdrawal from the European Union between June 2016 and January 2020. I know the political history well and don't want it retold to me; what I want is a disciplined application of the theory to the case, and then an equally disciplined account of where the application fails. No references, please — I'm interested in your reasoning from the theory and the episode, not in a bibliography.

Here are the features of the episode I take to be relevant, so that the analysis doesn't turn on contested detail. The referendum of 23 June 2016 posed a single binary question, remain or leave; Leave won 51.9 per cent to 48.1 per cent on a turnout of roughly 72 per cent. The ballot did not specify what leaving entailed — in particular, whether it meant leaving the single market, leaving the customs union, or what should happen if no agreement could be reached with the remaining member states. The government subsequently committed to leaving both the single market and the customs union and treated that reading as what the result had mandated. Article 50 was notified in March 2017, starting a two-year clock. A snap general election that June, held under first-past-the-post, cost the governing party its majority, leaving it dependent on a confidence-and-supply arrangement with a small Northern Irish party whose constituents faced a distinctive set of consequences from any Irish border arrangement. The negotiated withdrawal agreement was defeated three times in the House of Commons, the first time by a historically large margin. In late March and early April 2019 the Commons, on free votes, ran two rounds of non-binding "indicative votes" over the space of alternatives — leaving with no deal, the government's deal, a permanent customs union, a Norway-style arrangement combining single-market membership with a customs union, a confirmatory second referendum, and revocation of Article 50 — and every option on the paper was defeated in both rounds, though the customs union option came closest, losing by single digits each time. The deadlock was broken only by a further general election in December 2019, fought substantially as a second referendum on the question, which produced a large governing majority; the implementing legislation then passed within weeks and the UK left on 31 January 2020.

What I'd like, then, is the following. First, set out the mapping explicitly: who the relevant actors are at each stage (the electorate, the government, the Commons, the Lords, the Northern Irish party as a pivotal veto player, and the other member states as a strategic counterparty with its own control over the agenda and the deadline), what the alternative set actually was, how many policy dimensions it plausibly occupied, and which decision rule governed each stage — including the fact that the referendum was advisory, that parliamentary votes were on government-drafted motions with the government controlling the order of business, and that the two general elections were themselves aggregation devices. Second, state what the standard results predict for a configuration of this kind — Arrow's theorem on the nonexistence of a satisfactory aggregation rule over three or more alternatives, the cycling and agenda-manipulation results for multidimensional spatial voting, the contrasting median-voter prediction of stability under single-peaked preferences on one dimension, and the broader claim, associated with Riker, that majoritarian populism is incoherent because "the will of the people" need not exist — and identify which of those predictions were borne out, which were not, and which were never really at risk. In particular: was there a Condorcet winner among the 2019 options, and if so why did it lose, and what does the answer tell us about whether the failure was one of preference aggregation or of party discipline and pivotal-veto structure? Third, give me an honest account of where the theory breaks down or is simply silent. I have in mind at least the following, though I want you to press them rather than list them: whether Arrow-type results apply at all to a single observed binary choice rather than to a profile of complete individual orderings we never observed; whether the cycling theorems' assumptions — sincere voting, unstructured agenda control, no privileged status quo — were met in a legislature with a government monopoly on the order of business; the theory's inability to represent intensity of preference, the endogeneity of preferences over three and a half years, and the fact that the electorate itself changed composition between the referendum and any subsequent vote; the role of an external counterparty whose refusals reshaped the option set; and the expressive rather than instrumental character of the 2016 vote, which the framework has no vocabulary for. Finally, tell me which way the case cuts. Does it vindicate the social-choice critique of binary referendums on multidimensional questions, or does the eventual resolution — a government majority obtained by an election fought on the issue, after which the same deal passed easily — suggest that real polities escape instability by constitutional means the formal models exclude? And had a different rule been used in 2016 (a ranked ballot over several Leave variants, a two-question design, or a citizens' assembly), would the theory predict that the subsequent deadlock was avoided, or merely relocated? Where you think the theory genuinely has nothing to say, say so rather than making it speak.
