I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for two terms now I have also been teaching a graduate seminar in a science-communication program on triage and the allocation of scarce resources. My students are not philosophy majors — they are heading into health, environment and science desks — but they have spent three weeks with Taurek's essay on whether the numbers count, and the final expected them to work with the argument rather than the labels. The exam was closed book, forty-five minutes, twenty-five marks for the question below.

I have marked the rest of the pile. I am deliberately telling you nothing about my own reaction to this particular script, because I want it marked to the same standard as everything else and I do not want to steer you. Please form your own judgement of it.

**The question as set**

An urban search-and-rescue team has shoring timber enough for one operation only. In collapse void A there is a single trapped survivor; in void B there are five. The team cannot reach both, and there is no third course of action that saves anyone.

(a) [6 marks] Explain why Taurek denies that the presence of five survivors in void B rather than one in void A by itself settles what the team ought to do. Your answer should say what the aggregation of lives would have to presuppose in order to work.

(b) [7 marks] Taurek suggests that in a case of this shape it may be appropriate for the rescuer to toss a coin to decide between the two voids. Say what the coin toss is supposed to achieve on his account, and explain why that rationale cannot simply be a way of getting the best expected outcome.

(c) [7 marks] A rival proposal says: do not give the two voids equal chances; give each of the six trapped individuals an equal chance of being rescued, which means the five in void B hold a five-sixths chance between them. Does this proposal accept or reject Taurek's central argument? Defend your answer either way.

(d) [5 marks] Does Taurek's view license the team to stand down and rescue no one at all? Explain.

**The script**

¶1. Taurek's starting point is that the numbers do not by themselves tell you what to do. If the five in void B die, that is a terrible loss — but it is five separate terrible losses, each of them a loss to the person who dies and to the people who love her. There is no further entity, "the six trapped survivors," that suffers a loss when five die rather than one. So there is no pool of harm sitting out there waiting to be minimized, and once you see that, the inference from "more people will die in void B" to "you must go to void B" loses its force. What aggregation presupposes is exactly this pool: harms summed into a single quantity that belongs to nobody in particular. Taurek's point is that the summing cannot be done, because we have no way of knowing how much each individual stands to lose and no access to anyone else's experience of loss. Comparing the five's losses with the one's is not merely difficult, it is impossible in principle, and so the aggregation step never gets off the ground.

¶2. The coin toss follows from this. It is fair because it gives each of the six trapped people the same chance of being rescued, one in six apiece. Nobody is written off in advance and nobody's life is treated as worth less than anyone else's. Crucially, the coin toss is not a piece of arithmetic about expected outcomes; it is not the rescuer working out which void contains more survival and going there. It is a way of showing equal concern for each of the six, and it works precisely because the rescuer is not trying to maximize anything. A rescuer who simply went to void B because five is more than one is treating the person in void A as though her death counted for less, and the coin toss is the alternative to that.

¶3. The rival proposal in (c) is therefore a straight rejection of Taurek. Giving the five a five-sixths chance is maximizing with an extra step: the proposal weights the outcome by how many people are in it, which is the aggregation Taurek denies, dressed up as fairness. It makes no difference that the proposal talks about chances rather than outcomes, because a five-sixths chance for void B is still a way of saying that five lives are worth more than one, and Taurek's whole argument is that no such claim is available. So the rival accepts nothing from him. It concedes only the trivial point that you cannot literally save everyone.

¶4. On (d), Taurek is often read as saying that we owe nothing to strangers, and that is too crude. He is not indifferent to the numbers: he thinks the rescuer should care about each of the six, and his objection to going straight to void B is an objection to a certain kind of justification, not to saving five people. Where a rescuer loves the person in void A, Taurek allows that she may go to void A out of that love and that we should not blame her for it. But it does follow from his denial that the numbers impose any requirement that the team is permitted to stand down. If nothing about the five gives the team a reason it must act on, and nothing about the one gives it a reason it must act on either, then the team is free to save its timber for another day and no criticism is available. That is the unattractive but honest upshot, and it makes Taurek a sceptic about rescue duties in general rather than a theorist about how to choose between them.
