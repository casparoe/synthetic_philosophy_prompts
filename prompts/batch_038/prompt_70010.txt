I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years as a plant virologist, and moral philosophy has become my occupation. I am presently working through the family of views loosely called suffering-focused ethics: negative utilitarianism proper, which holds that only the reduction of suffering carries moral weight, and its softer descendants, which grant suffering lexical or strongly prioritarian weight while allowing that happiness counts for something. I should like to test these views against a real decision that I followed at the time and now find philosophically peculiar.

The facts, briefly, since you cannot look them up. Smallpox was certified eradicated in 1980, the last naturally occurring case having been in Somalia in 1977. The final fatal case anywhere was a medical photographer at Birmingham in 1978, infected by an escape from a research laboratory in the same building; after that accident the world's variola holdings were consolidated into two World Health Organization reference laboratories, in Atlanta and at Koltsovo in Russia, and since 1984 only those two have been authorised to hold live virus. From 1986 the World Health Assembly repeatedly recommended destroying what remained: a date was set for the end of 1993, moved to mid-1999, then to mid-2002, and in 2002 the Assembly agreed instead to temporary retention for research purposes, reaffirming destruction thereafter but never fixing a date. A 2010 review by experts appointed by the WHO concluded that retention served no essential public health purpose. The case for retaining rests on three things: the possibility of undeclared or forgotten stocks — vials of viable variola dating from the 1950s were found in an unused storage room on the NIH campus in 2014; the fact that the genome sequence is public and orthopoxviruses can in principle be assembled from synthesised DNA, so destruction cannot remove the knowledge; and the argument that live virus is needed to test vaccines and antivirals.

I want the analysis run in both directions. First, the mapping. Under a suffering-focused view, who are the parties to this decision, what precisely are the options, and what quantity is being compared? Bear in mind that those who would suffer from an escape do not exist yet, so the comparison must be impersonal, and that retention trades a small annual probability of a very large amount of suffering against a small probability of averting one. State what the strong view recommends, what the lexical and prioritarian variants recommend, and whether any of them can register the achievement of eradication as such — whether the theory has the resources to distinguish a world with no suffering from a world with no sufferers, given that the standing objection to negative utilitarianism is precisely that it cannot.

Second, and more important, what does the episode do to the theory? Is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case these views were never built for, having been developed for questions of killing, procreation and the suffering of wild animals rather than for the stewardship of an eradicated pathogen by an intergovernmental body under uncertainty? Be explicit wherever the theory falls silent, and resist making it speak.

Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my question. Close by telling me what would change your mind: which argument or which step of the mapping would move you to a different verdict, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to have failed.
