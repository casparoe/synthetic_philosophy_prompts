I've assembled an argument to the effect that computationalism about the mind is not false but empty, and I can't locate the error — assuming there is one. First, a working notion: a physical system S implements a formal automaton A just in case there is an assignment of each of A's states to a physical state of S (possibly a disjunctive one), injectively, such that the causal transitions among the assigned physical states mirror the formal transitions among A's states.

(1) Physical states can be individuated as finely as we please; nothing in S's physics picks out a privileged granularity.
(2) To forestall the usual complaint about gerrymandered mappings, I further stipulate that the assigned states are individuated by their counterfactual profiles, so that the mirroring holds not only along the trajectory S actually traverses but across every input in A's finite alphabet.
(3) Given (1) and (2), for any S that is deterministic, long-lived, and rich in distinct physical states, and any finite A, such a mirroring assignment can be constructed.
(4) Hence S implements every finite automaton.
(5) The granite block in my garden is deterministic, long-lived, and physically rich, so it implements the automaton that is the best computational model of my visual system over the past ten seconds.
(6) If implementing the right automaton is what constitutes having a visual experience, the block is now having my visual experience — and, by the same route, every finite experience of every finite mind.
(7) A picture on which everything has every mentality is not a substantive theory but a collapse of the notion; and (1)–(3) are assumptions about mappings, not about minds.
(8) So implementation cannot constitute mentality; and since (4) applies equally to brains, nothing implements anything determinately, and computationalism says nothing at all.

Which step should I give up, why did it look unobjectionable, and what do I pay for giving it up? I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since together they seem to be what makes computational explanation objective rather than a matter of how we choose to describe things. I also want to know whether the move you recommend leaves intact the mundane claim that the machine I'm typing this on is running a word processor — if it doesn't, I suspect you've conceded too much to my argument rather than diagnosed it. And if you think there is no flaw and I should accept (8), tell me which other commitments that costs me.
