Working through Aquinas on tyrannicide for my thesis chapter and the secondary literature has hit a wall I can't see through. Roughly: one camp says he has a perfectly consistent doctrine — the young Sentences remark about the man who frees his country by killing a tyrant is about someone acting with (or at least in the name of) the community against a ruler with no title at all, On Kingship distinguishes a tyrant by usurpation from a tyrant who has a title but rules oppressively, and the Summa's rule that killing belongs only to public authority fits right in, since tyrannicide would be a public act, not a private one. The other camp says the same texts show he never endorses killing a ruler at all: the Summa allows it only for public authority or by some special divine command, On Kingship warns that private attacks on rulers usually leave the people worse off with a worse ruler and tells you to wait for God when there's no human remedy, and the bit about disturbing a tyrannical regime not being sedition stops well short of lethal force — so the Sentences passage is a youthful borrowing from John of Salisbury that he quietly dropped.

My hunch is that this isn't really a disagreement about what the texts say, since both sides quote the same ones at each other. So what is it — a fight over what "permits" means for a writer who never addresses the case head-on, over which genre or which period counts as his considered view, or a hidden conceptual gap nobody has noticed, like what "public authority" even is once the ruler is the tyrant? Diagnose it rather than take a side, and tell me what would count as progress and whether I should expect any. End with a last line in exactly this form: Diagnosis: {one short label}.
