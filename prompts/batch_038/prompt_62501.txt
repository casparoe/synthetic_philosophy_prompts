I came to the Letter Spirit material sideways, through type design rather than cognitive science: I spent a couple of years drawing letters, then read Hofstadter and McGraw's 1993 paper on the perception and creation of alphabetic style, and later the gridfont material in *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies*, and I found myself resisting the spirit of the project even while admiring it. The position I have settled into — call it the schema view — is this: for each letter of the roman lowercase there is a structural core that can be stated once and for all, independently of any particular style, as a specification of parts, their connectivity, and a small number of relational constraints (proportion, orientation, which segments are obligatory, which optional); a mark is an *a* insofar as it satisfies that specification, and style is what remains over and above it, a set of choices that leave the core intact. On this picture the core is prior: it fixes the identity conditions of the letter, and stylistic coherence is a matter of the cores being realised in a mutually consistent way. The Letter Spirit work seems to me to accept the priority of the core while trying to model how it gets fleshed out, and I want to be told plainly where that is wrong.

So: what would count against the schema view? I want a map of its exposure, not a defence and not a survey of the literature — please give me no reading list and no citations, since I cannot check them and would only have to guess. Concretely, work through (i) the empirical territory: what findings about letter recognition, typographic priming, degradation, transposition, mixed-case processing, or acquisition would support or undermine the claim that a single style-neutral specification is what readers match against, and which such findings the schema view predicts but its rivals do not; (ii) the internal typographic and paleographic territory: cases where a letter apparently changes its skeleton and stays the same letter, forms that survive extreme distortion or reversal, features that are optional in one tradition and mandatory in another, and whether there is any letter for which no non-trivial core can be found that all its accepted forms share; (iii) the modelling territory: what the fact that a coherent gridfont can be generated by propagating stylistic analogy from a few seed letters, rather than by consulting a specification, does or does not show about whether specifications exist and whether they do any explanatory work; and (iv) the purely conceptual territory: family-resemblance and disjunctive-core worries, and the possibility that letter identity is fixed not by shape at all but by a letter's role in an abstract alphabet, so that the "core" is really a functional slot with shape only contingently attached — in which case the schema view would collapse into its rival rather than merely lose ground to it.

For each candidate defeater, say how much damage it actually does: whether a committed schema theorist would concede it as counting against them, absorb it by refining the notion of a core (weighted, probabilistic, hierarchical, or family-resemblance cores), or dismiss it as beside the point — and, where absorption is the reply, whether the refined view still says anything. That last question is the one I most want answered honestly: if every counterexample can be met by making the core more abstract or more disjunctive, is that fatal for a view of this kind, or entirely legitimate given that its subject matter is a cultural artefact rather than a natural kind? The case I personally find most troubling is a lowercase letter whose two widely accepted printed forms appear to share no stroke configuration at all, yet are unhesitatingly the same letter to readers and to typographers; tell me whether that is decisive or whether there is a real reply that does not simply redefine the core. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Finish with a block titled RANKED DEFEATERS listing each one with an ID and exactly one severity tag drawn from {fatal, serious, awkward, absorbable}, and then a final line of the form Verdict: followed by exactly one of "fatally exposed", "seriously exposed but defensible", "well insulated and this is a problem", "well insulated and this is not a problem".
