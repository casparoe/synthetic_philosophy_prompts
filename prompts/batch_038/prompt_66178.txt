You're writing the closer for a philosophy department's comedy night, and you also happen to know decision theory and the moral-uncertainty stuff cold. The room is grad students plus a couple of economists who wandered in from down the hall — they know what a Pareto improvement is, they've heard the phrase "moral trade," and they will absolutely groan at a joke whose punchline would work just as well about any two people who disagree about anything.

Here's the material I want it built on, in my own words so you get the angle. Two people with different value systems — call them a utilitarian and a deontologist — can both come out ahead from a deal, and the deal only works *because* they disagree about the exchange rate: the utilitarian hands over something he weights less (a bit of rights-respecting) in return for something he weights more (lives saved), and the deontologist does the mirror image. Neither of them is being generous, they're just arbitraging a difference of opinion about prices. Then the awkward part I keep chewing on: for the deontologist to strike the deal at all he already has to treat "don't lie" as the kind of thing that can be swapped for something else, which is more or less the utilitarians view of the matter — so the handshake does some of the utilitarian's persuading for him, for free. And if they keep trading, where do they wind up? Some blended system neither of them would have voted for.

Write me one joke. Stand-up shape: a setup, a punchline, and a tag if you've got one — 60 to 120 words total. The laugh has to live in the content: someone who actually understands gains from trade should hear a line they could not have gotten out of a generic "philosophers never agree" gag. Before you write it, brainstorm two or three candidate angles — which wrinkle carries the punchline: the price disagreement, the self-undermining handshake, or the pile of successive trades — pick the strongest, then commit. Format, two sections in this order:

**The bit** — just the joke, no framing, no "so a utilitarian walks into a bar" preamble before it starts.

**Why this one** — about 150 words: which wrinkle you picked and what beat it, what the listener already has to know for the punchline to land, and the one place the joke bends the theory slightly to get the laugh.
