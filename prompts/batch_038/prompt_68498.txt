Last week two people in my graduate seminar got into an argument after a session on coherence arguments, and I ended up taking notes rather than participating. Here is the exchange as I recorded it, with each turn numbered.

(1) Nadia said Dutch book arguments show that the probability axioms are requirements of rationality rather than mere conventions: if your betting prices violate them, someone can assemble a package of bets that loses money no matter how the world turns out, and it would be irrational to accept such a package knowingly.

(2) Owen replied that this begs the question. The construction assumes you value a package by summing the values of its parts. An agent could deny that additivity outright — caring about the pattern of payoffs across states rather than about each wager in isolation — and the theorem would then have nothing to bite on. On his reading the argument yields a conditional, axioms-plus-additive-valuation, not the axioms by themselves.

(3) Nadia said that merely relocates the problem: refusing to value a portfolio by its components is exactly the confusion the theorem is meant to diagnose, since a non-additive agent ends up paying for bets that cancel one another, which is a straightforward loss.

(4) Owen asked a loss for whom. The guaranteed-loss result is stated in money, and money is not utility. An agent whose utility is nonlinear in money can be coherent in utility while looking incoherent in dollar prices, and vice versa, so the theorem either presupposes a linear relation between prices and utility or restricts itself to agents who already have one.

(5) Nadia conceded the denomination point but said it cuts the other way: restate everything in utility and the objection dissolves, since rational betting prices are utility-denominated by construction. She added that the conditional still has empirical bite, because a person who accepted a dominated package while declining a positive-expectation bet would be publicly revealed as non-additive, and no such person survives long in a market.

(6) Owen said that concession showed the argument to be empty. Run it one way and it is near-tautological — of course prices satisfying the axioms cannot be combined into a guaranteed loss, that is arithmetic; run it the other way and it needs the betting interpretation to get started, which is the very thing in dispute. He concluded that static books are better read as stipulative definitions of coherence than as arguments for it, and that anything substantive has to come from the diachronic versions.

Neither of them budged, and I have no horse in the race, so I would rather not settle it myself. Could you go through moves (1) through (6) in order, rating each one clearly right, clearly wrong, or a mixed result, with a sentence or two of justification per rating? Then give me a short paragraph saying who has the better of the exchange overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please keep the entire response under 500 words.
