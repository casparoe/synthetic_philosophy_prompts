So I got roped onto our hospital system's Names and Memorials Committee this year — I'm the faculty senate rep, which mostly means I'm the one who writes things down — and Thursday we vote on the Varnum case, which is the first one that's actually landed on us rather than being hypothetical. The chair asked each of us to circulate a short written opinion beforehand, and mine is the one that has to break the tie between the two camps, so I'd like it to be good. Everything you need is below; please don't go looking up our town or trying to place the institution, the facts are all right here and they're the ones that matter.

Dr. Elias Varnum (1819–1889) founded the teaching ward that became this hospital, wrote an 1862 wound-management manual whose methods are still in our protocols in modified form, and started our nurse training program. The east wing carries his name, there's an endowed annual Varnum Lecture in Clinical Anatomy that's been given since 1911, and there's a portrait in the lobby. Then in the spring a grad student working in the county archive turned up a box that changes the picture: through most of the 1850s Varnum sourced cadavers for the anatomical school from men who were robbing the pauper burial ground and the free Black cemetery on the south side, he negotiated prices per body in his own correspondence, he wrote at least one joke about a delivery arriving before the family had been told, and in two documented cases the body of an almshouse patient was taken after the family had formally asked for it back and been refused. The part the retain-the-name people keep trying to skip is that none of this was invisible at the time. There were riots over body-snatching in three cities he knew about, the state legislature passed an anatomy act during his career specifically to choke off that trade, a rival physician denounced him by name in the minutes of the county medical society, and Varnum's recorded reply brushed the whole thing off as sentiment unfitting a man of science. On top of that: the lecture endowment is his estate money and it's still sitting in the corpus, and his great-great-granddaughter is one of our biggest current donors and sits on the development advisory board, which nobody wants to say out loud.

What I want back is a memo of about two pages that the committee can actually vote on Thursday — a recommendation with reasons, not a survey. It should start by pinning down what a name on a building and an endowed lecture actually *do*, because I'm now pretty sure half our argument is nine people using the word honour for three different acts; if honouring, commemorating, and merely identifying have different tests, say so and sort the four Varnum artifacts (wing, lecture, portrait, the attribution buried in our protocols) under whichever applies. Then it has to take the "man of his time" defence apart into its separate pieces instead of swallowing or rejecting it whole — couldn't have known better, everyone was doing it, you'd have done the same, and whatever else is tangled in there — and rule on each piece against the facts above; I expect the dissenting physician and the anatomy act to do a lot of work, and I want you to be straight about what they don't settle. It also has to face the baseline problem head on: if the standard that condemns Varnum gets applied consistently, four other names on our campus and in our donor program look bad too, and one trustee has already announced that adopting it means renaming the whole map. Treat that as an argument rather than a threat — is it a reductio of the standard, or a discovery about the map? Give real space to the two retain-side objections I think actually have teeth: that pulling the name erases a history this community needs to know, and that we are still living off his methods and his money, so a clean break is a species of self-flattery. And then land it — what happens to the wing, the lecture, the portrait and the endowment, on what dates; what we tell the donor family and when; what we owe the south side cemetery association, who've asked for a marker and a public acknowledgment; and what we say to staff and press. If your recommendation comes out as something like retain-with-interpretation, I need to know precisely what the interpretation is, because "add a plaque" is where these decisions go to die.

Two more things. Don't give me the philosophy-of-history lecture, and don't drag Jefferson, Columbus or Churchill into it — the committee does that already and it helps nobody, since no building here is named after any of them. And be genuinely fair to the retain side, because here's my own problem: I did my clinical rotations in that wing, my mentor gave the Varnum Lecture in '98, and part of me wants the name to stay so the room I learned in stays the room I learned in. I'd rather the memo name that pull where it's doing work than pretend thier neutral arbiter is me. Strongest honest case for leaving everything exactly as it is, then tell me whether it wins and why.
