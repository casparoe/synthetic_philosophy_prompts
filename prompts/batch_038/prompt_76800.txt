My partner and I are about five weeks from a frozen embryo transfer, and an appointment with our genetic counselor has left me unable to think clearly about a decision I now have to make. Two of our embryos were tested. One carries a variant that the counselor expects to produce a significant lifelong impairment; she was careful to say twice that a life with that impairment is worth living, and equally careful not to advise us either way. The other tested clear. Separately, my partner's treatment will conclude in roughly two years, by which point our finances and housing will be considerably more stable. If we wait, the child we have will be conceived from different gametes and will therefore be a numerically different child, not this one at a later date. Both decisions seem to have the same shape, and that shape is what I cannot get a handle on.

Since that appointment I have been reading around the nonidentity problem, and I have acquired the opposite of what I wanted: a dozen confident-sounding positions, and no way to distinguish my load-bearing intuitions from artifacts of how particular cases happen to be described. So I am not asking for the arguments. I want the specification that sits underneath them. Write the desiderata for a theory of procreative choice: what any such theory must deliver before anyone is entitled to tell me that transferring the impaired embryo wrongs the child it produces, or that waiting wrongs the child we would otherwise have had. By adequate I mean something quite demanding and quite practical — good enough to actually settle which embryo to transfer and whether to transfer now, and good enough that the reasoning behind the choice could later be explained to the resulting person without embarrassment.

Your list should cover, at minimum, the following, though I expect you will find more:

- What harm can consist in when the alternative to the act is that this particular person never exists, and how the theory fixes the baseline for that comparison.
- The distinction between wronging some particular person and making things worse overall, and whether the theory can do without either notion.
- Whether same-number choices (which of these two embryos) and different-number choices (now or in two years) are governed by the same principles. If the theory treats them differently, what does the differentiating work.
- The asymmetry between the strong reason not to create someone whose life would be miserable and the weak or absent reason to create someone whose life would be happy. Any theory that treats wellbeing symmetrically owes an account of this.
- Whether the same machinery handles positive selection for a trait the parents share and value, where no impairment reading is available to do the work.
- The licensing worry: if creating someone whose life is worth living cannot harm them, what prevents the theory from treating a barely-worth-living life as no worse a choice than a flourishing one.
- Whether reasons can be owed to nobody in particular, and if so, how they enter one person's deliberation rather than a social planner's calculus.
- Whether the intimate single-family case and the population-scale case — an energy or resource policy that changes who exists centuries hence — are the same problem. If not, say what the relevant difference is.
- The gap between an act's being wrong and any particular person's having standing to complain about it.

For each entry, give the desideratum a name, state it as a requirement, supply the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable-but-costly, or a sectarian preference of one camp that has been passing as a neutral requirement. Where a named position fails something non-negotiable, note it in a clause within the entry; I do not want a leaderboard, and I do not want the scorecard to displace the criteria.

Two proposed requirements of my own should be entered on the list and then interrogated rather than indulged. The first, which I will call retrospective addressability, holds that a choice is defensible only if it can be explained to the person it produced in the second person, in terms whose soundness does not depend on that person's never having existed. The second, which I will call operator transparency, holds that a theory must declare at each step whether it is reasoning about what is worse for someone or about what is worse simpliciter, and may not move between the two without stating the conversion rule. My suspicion is that much of the apparent disagreement between camps is unmarked operator-switching rather than genuine moral difference; tell me whether that suspicion survives contact with the arguments, or whether the switches are doing substantive work.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. The Two Decisions — about 150 words, restating my situation in neutral terms, with any description-dependence you notice flagged rather than smoothed over.
2. The Desiderata — about 1,100 words, numbered, each entry carrying its name, statement, motivating case, and status.
3. Collisions and Redundancies — about 450 words: which subsets of the list cannot be jointly satisfied and why; which single desideratum excludes the most candidates; and which pairs you believe are one requirement stated in two vocabularies.
4. On My Two Proposals — about 350 words: keep, repair, or discard, with reasons.
5. What Survives — about 250 words: the minimal consistent set I can carry into the appointment, what it leaves genuinely undetermined, and which single requirement I should be prepared to abandon if I want the decision to become tractable, along with what that abandonment costs.

One constraint on the whole thing: no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no page references. I have no means of checking any of it, and I would much rather you described a view than fabricated a source for it. Where a position has a standard name, use the name; where it does not, describe it.
