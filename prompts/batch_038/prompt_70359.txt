Longtime reader of the old LW/SSC comment threads here, and Grice gets invoked in argument-culture debates constantly — usually by the person who's about to say "well, technically I never said that" — so over the weekend I finally read a glossy magazine essay about him instead of relying on secondhand mentions. It was well-written and confident and I mostly bought it, but a couple of things itch and I don't have the background to tell whether the itch is justified or I'm just reflexively contrarian. The essay's summary went roughly like this:

"Grice's insight was that meaning is intention all the way down: to mean something by an utterance is simply to intend your listener to come to believe it, and to intend that they recognize your intention while doing so. That's what let him separate what a sentence says from what a speaker implicates. What is said is the literal, truth-conditional content — the words plus the grammar and nothing else — and everything else you convey is implicature. The test for implicature is cancellability: you can tack on 'in fact, all of them did' without contradicting yourself, which is exactly why implicature isn't real meaning. That's what's going on with 'some of the students passed': the speaker implicates that not all of them passed, but that isn't part of the meaning of *some*, so if they all passed she hasn't told a lie — she's merely let you draw your own conclusion, and Grice was clear that a speaker isn't on the hook for what the hearer works out on his own. The same machinery explains irony and metaphor: break one of his four maxims and you generate an implicature instead of a falsehood."

For what it's worth I've writen a few of those threads myself, so I have a stake in the last sentence: I can smell that the "isn't on the hook" bit is doing an enormous amount of work for the essay's moral, that misleading someone by implicature is a free pass, and I don't want to accept that moral just because a nice essay handed it to me. Separately I have a vague memory from somewhere that there are two kinds of implicature and only one of them is cancellable, which would make "the test for implicature is cancellability" too quick — but that memory could be confabulated.
