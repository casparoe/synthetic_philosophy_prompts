I am the treasurer of a small family foundation — a corpus of about $4 million, one staff member, and a board that meets five times a year — and for eleven years our outside investment advisor, Ines, has sat in on every allocation decision we have made. She is a former desk quant who still plays poker for meaningful money, and she is the only person in the room who thinks about position sizing rather than about whether an opportunity is "good." The board has now asked me to be able to defend our allocations in her absence, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer her decision rule for a meeting this Thursday. I have had one graduate course in decision theory and I have read the Kelly material, so I can state the difference between maximizing expected value, maximizing expected log wealth, and the growth-rate or "ergodicity" way of framing the same idea, and I know that the log-optimal fraction for an even-money bet at probability p is p minus q. Her record nonetheless refuses to line up cleanly with any single slogan I can write down, and one trustee keeps saying we should just halve the Kelly number every time and stop worrying, which I suspect is wrong but cannot demonstrate.

Here are the verdicts I have documented. (a) Two years ago a desk pitched us a structured note, available repeatedly, that either returned double the allocation or nothing, with the desk's own model putting the upside at 60 percent. Ines allocated 10 percent of the corpus, $400,000, per round. When I pointed out that 20 percent is the log-optimal fraction for a 60/40 double-or-nothing, she said that 20 percent is the answer "if you take their 0.6 at face value," and that she does not. (b) The same year, a proposal came to convert the entire corpus into a single venture with a 25 percent chance of ending at $16 million and a 75 percent chance of ending at $600,000 — a positive expected value, $4.45 million against $4 million. She refused outright, and the reason I wrote down at the time was that an expectation averages over outcomes of which only one will ever happen to us. (c) Eighteen months ago a futures convergence trade offered roughly 0.9 percent over six weeks with, on her reading, a failure probability of order one in a thousand. She wanted close to the entire corpus deployed, which was as much as the prime broker's margin rules permitted. I checked afterward that the log-optimal fraction for a near-certain 0.9 percent gain is about nine-tenths of bankroll if the failure probability really is 0.001, so this may not have been a departure from the same rule at all — though that figure is brutally sensitive to the failure probability, which is precisely what she said she did not trust in (a). (d) Three years ago we had $1.2 million of irrevocable multi-year pledges outstanding and the corpus had slipped to $3.1 million. A 58/42 double-or-nothing came up, priced by judgment rather than by mechanism. She allocated $150,000 — under 5 percent of the corpus, but almost exactly 8 percent of the corpus net of the pledges, where 8 percent is half of the log-optimal 16 percent for those odds. Her comment was that the pledged money is not ours to bet. (e) Last spring, when the board began discussing winding the foundation down instead of holding the corpus in perpetuity, she said the conversation changes character entirely: a pool that is going to be spent is not a growing organism but a sequence of withdrawals, and she wanted us to stop talking about growth rates and start talking about the distribution of the eventual payout. She did not say what that implies for how much to stake, and she also once declined to raise a per-round allocation on the grounds that a trade would be repeated monthly for a decade, saying that more rounds of a bet do not improve the bet.

The new case is this. The wind-up is now formal: the board will distribute everything remaining to two named charities in twenty months, and nothing will be reinvested after that date. The $1.5 million library pledge is signed, legally enforceable, and payable at the end of the period. A donor has offered a one-time wager resolved by an audited mechanical draw — sealed, inspected, and with 62 of 100 slots marked, so the odds are as close to exactly known as anything we will ever see — at even money, settled in cash within a week, offered exactly once and explicitly not repeatable. Separately, the founder's family has said informally, by email and with no legal force, that they would cover any shortfall if the foundation could not meet its obligations; their finances are opaque and Ines has never once relied on them. Over the same twenty months the corpus can earn about 5 percent annualized in Treasury bills. So: what will she recommend, and roughly what dollar figure will she write on the whiteboard? Please lay out the candidate rules that jointly fit (a) through (e) — I can see at least a full-Kelly rule applied to her own shrunken probability estimates, a flat fractional-Kelly rule, a rule that computes the fraction against surplus above irrevocable commitments, and a growth-rate rule that may simply stop applying once the money is earmarked for spending — and state what each of them implies here in dollars, including whether the informal family backstop enters the base. Tell me which of my five verdicts is doing the most work in your reconstruction and which is nearly uninformative, and name one further case, or one direct question I could put to her, that would most sharply separate the leading reconstructions. Finally, resolve the wrinkle I cannot: if every dollar above the pledge is itself committed to the charities, does the surplus logic leave any stakeable base at all, or does the wind-up make wagering impermissible for us altogether? I want a committed answer rather than a menu — pick the reconstruction you believe is right, give the figure you expect, say what you would advise the board to do if she is unreachable on Thursday, and be explicit about how much of your confidence comes from her record and how much from which rule you would defend on the merits, including where you think the record genuinely runs out and you are filling the gap with your own priors.
