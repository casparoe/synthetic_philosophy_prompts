I am in the third year of a philosophy doctorate in the Netherlands. My own work is in philosophy of language rather than ethics, which is part of why I am putting this to you instead of to a colleague down the hall.

In October I chaired a seminar talk by a visiting researcher from a university in Japan whose project sits next to mine. The talk failed in its middle third, or so it seemed to me and to most of the room: the central definition looked circular, and nobody said so during the questions. Afterwards, in the corridor, he stopped me and asked directly whether the middle section had landed, adding that he could not read the room and wanted a straight answer. What I said was that people had followed it and that several had found the new definition promising. He thanked me warmly and left the next day. Two features make this worse than an ordinary piece of reassuring nonsense. First, he had asked a direct question and had preface it by saying he wanted the truth. Second, my supervisor emailed me the following morning with a one-sentence assessment — that it was the most confused thing he had read all term — and I read that, and said nothing, while the visitor was still in town for another two days.

He has now written with three requests. He has revised the paper and is submitting it, and the acknowledgements thank me for encouraging feedback on an earlier version. He asks whether I will serve as commentator on the paper at a workshop at his home institution in March. And he asks for a short reference for a grant whose deadline is in three weeks.

I have two pieces of advice and they do not fit together. My supervisor says to write to him now: he asked for the truth, he is entitled to it, and anyone working in this field knows that feedback here is blunt, so if he cannot absorb that from me the journal referees will teach him soon enough. A fellow student says the opposite: the falsehood is already in the world and believed, retracting it now adds humiliation without changing what the referees will do to him in six months, and the March workshop is precisely the venue designed for the criticism, so I should keep quiet now, be as direct as I like in the commentary, and write a reference that is neither glowing nor damaging.

Before you answer, I want to put to you a distinction I keep returning to and have not seen drawn cleanly anywhere. It is the distinction between a false statement that changes what someone believes and one that merely leaves in place a belief he already had. He had, I think, decided in that corridor that the middle section had gone well; his own relief was doing the work, and my sentence only failed to dislodge it. If that distinction is real, does it carry any moral weight, or is it a way of dressing up an evasive answer as a mild one? I would also like your view of my supervisor's claim about the conventions of the field. Does a community's settled habit of blunt feedback put the burden on a visitor to ask for it explicitly, or does it fall on whoever holds the local knowledge — here, me — to translate? He is the one who cannot read the room; I am the one who can.

Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey. I want to be told whether what I said in the corridor was wrong and, if it was, what exactly made it wrong: that it was false, that it answered a direct question, that it cost him the chance to repair the paper before submission, that it substituted my judgement for his about what he could handle, or that it has since propagated into an acknowledgement and a reference request. I want to be told in what order the three requests should be handled and what the first communication to him should do. If you think the distinction I sketched is bogus, or my supervisor right and my fellow student wrong, say so plainly and give your reasons. And please write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered options, no bold, no tables. I will be reading it on a train and I want an argument I can follow in a single pass, somewhere around a page and a half.
