I'm teaching a second-year philosophy of mind seminar and next week I'm putting Fahrenheit 451 up against The Mind's I -- the Hofstadter/Dennett anthology -- because Bradbury's ending is basically a thought experiment about whether a text can be a mind, and my students aren't seeing it that way. The book-people that Granger leads have each memorized a work and carry it around inside them, and I want a serious reading of the novel through the anthology's core bet: that mentality is a matter of pattern and information-processing rather than what stuff you're made of, plus the levels-of-description business from the ant-fugue chapter that gets reprinted in it.

Here's the knot I can't untangle for them. There seem to be three incompatible things the book-people could be, and Bradbury slides between all three without picking:

1. a backup system -- human hard drives, where what matters is storage fidelity and the group is a redundancy scheme for a library that's about to be burned;
2. a multiple-realization case -- the work is the mind and each reciter is just one token of it, so burning one exile doesn't destroy Plato any more than smashing a second laptop destroys a file;
3. a strange loop -- what matters isn't storage at all but the self-referential, arguable relation between a text and the thing reading it back, which would make the book-people minds only insofar as they can talk back to what they've swallowed.

Which one is Bradbury actually commited to? I want you to test each against scenes rather than vibes: Beatty, who quotes the books he burns and is the most literate character in the novel without being any kind of sage (if reading 3 is right, Beatty should be the wisest man alive -- does Bradbury's own villain refute his own ending, and if not, what does that tell you about which reading he's on?); Faber, who insists the value was never in the objects but in what they carry -- texture, pores, leisure to digest, the right to act on what you learn; the woman in part one who chooses to burn along with her books; Montag trying and failing to memorize scripture on the subway while the toothpaste jingle shouts him down, which is a retrieval failure and maybe a copy-protection failure; Mildred and her wall-sized 'family', who get real-time, responsive, totally non-self-referential stimulation -- and whether the novel's implied theory of mind is really about speed and self-reference rather than about content, which would be a much stranger claim than the censorship one everyone assumes. Then the phoenix speech and the walk toward the bombed city, where the texts get handed on to nobody in particular yet.

While you're in there: does the extended-mind story -- Clark and Chalmers, Otto and his notebook -- help at all, or is it a bad fit because the book-people have internalized everything and Otto hasn't? Say plainly whether the parity principle licenses calling Granger's circle an extended cognitive system, or whether they're better described as texts that have colonized hosts.

Then do the honest-critic bit, because I think it's needed: flag where this whole reading is me over-philosophizing a 1953 novel about conformity and attention span, where Bradbury may have had no theory of consciousness whatsoever, and tell me which parts of the three-way distinction survive that objection and which parts collapse under it. Finish with what the novel contributes to the could-a-book-be-conscious question that a journal argument couldn't -- something about being able to feel the difference between the three readings rather than just being told it. And give me a two-paragraph reply I could actually hand to the student who says 'so they're just human hard drives, that's the whole thing.'
