I'm drafting a paper for my normative ethics seminar on supererogation, and the more cases I collect, the less sure I am that my verdicts describe one category. Here they are, roughly in order of my confidence. Donating a kidney to a stranger: beyond duty, and highly praiseworthy. A parent who takes a second job so her children can have a tutor instead of going without: her duty, and I don't think she earns any special credit for it. A graduate student who volunteers one shift at a food bank: supererogatory — but once she has signed up for a second shift and the coordinator has built the schedule around her, skipping it strikes me as wrong and blameworthy, so apparently a duty-free act can manufacture a duty. A billionaire who gives away forty percent of her fortune: obligatory rather than supererogatory, even though it costs her vastly more in absolute terms than the kidney does the donor. Volunteering an hour at a museum one loves anyway: still beyond duty, but I don't think it earns any praise — which suggests that "beyond the call of duty" and "meritorious" come apart, though my seminar's readings treat them as one thing. And a man who takes the blame for a colleague's error to save the colleague's job, having caused none of it: supererogatory, yet if he declines I want to say he is not merely neutral but somehow diminished, while conceding he is not blameworthy.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that delivers these verdicts? Please state each precisely, indicate which case motivates each of its clauses, and say what it rules for a case I haven't raised: whether forbearing to collect a debt you are entirely entitled to collect is supererogatory, or merely permissible. Then be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and if two have to go, tell me which one you'd drop and why. Please write in American English.
