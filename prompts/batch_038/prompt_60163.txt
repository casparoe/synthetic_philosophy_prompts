I've been going down a rabbit hole on Jain monastic discipline this week. It started with an old rationalist-blog post arguing that anekāntavāda is basically steelmanning formalised — nothing is true simpliciter, every claim holds only from some standpoint, so you're supposed to hedge your assertions with something like "in some respect" — and then I got into an argument about it in a pub in Leicester (huge Jain community there apparently, and I'd honestly never thought about it) and went and read up on what the monks actually do all day. That's where I got stuck, because the epistemology and the practice look like they're from different planets.

Here's the rulebook as far as I can piece it together. Monks and nuns don't cook, and crucially they don't have anything cooked *for* them either: they go round collecting small amounts from lots of households, taking only food that was already prepared for that family, so that nobody has to light the stove again on their account. No food or water after sunset, and a waiting period after sunrise before even boiled water. All water boiled and filtered. No root vegetables for laypeople, and much stricter during festival periods, on the grounds that pulling up an onion kills the whole plant plus everything living in the soil around it. Travel everywhere on foot, barefoot, no vehicles at any distance however long the journey, with a little whisk to sweep the ground before you step or sit; a cloth over or near the mouth for the ones who wear clothes; and no travelling at all through the four months of the monsoon. All of it aimed at not injuring the single-sensed lives that Jain metaphysics says populate earth, water, fire, air and plants.

So what does this actually presuppose? My half-formed guess is that it isn't a theory about total harm at all, because the grain the monk eats was grown by ploughing, which churns up more soil life in an afternoon than he'll avoid in a decade, and nobody seems bothered by that — so the operative notion has to be something about your own involvement rather than the amount of suffering in the world, and the "don't cook for me" rule reads like a counterfactual-causation test: what matters is whether your request would have made the difference. But the after-sunset rule doesn't fit that shape at all. That one looks like a rule about observability — only eat when you can see what you're eating — and the mouth-cloth and the whisk are the same idea, as is going into the kitchen to inspect the food yourself rather than accepting it at the door. Is the practice about not causing harm or about not being culpably ignorant of harm, and can one rulebook presuppose both without one of them being a post-hoc rationalisation of the other? And then there's the anekāntavāda problem: if every description of reality is standpoint-relative and no single account captures the whole thing, bright lines seem like exactly the wrong shape for a code of conduct, yet this code is nothing *but* bright lines — forty-odd rules just for collecting food, apparently, with no built-in machinery for weighing one against another. Does the practice quietly commit the one-sidedness that anekāntavāda exists to argue against, or is there a reading on which the many-sidedness and the bright lines need each other (I have a vague sense there's some two-levels thing in Jain thought that does this job, but I couldn't reconstruct it if you asked me). Please separate what the rules strictly presuppose from what they're merely consistent with — my suspicion is that several quite different theories would license most of the practice, and that a lot of it is underdetermined — and then tell me concretely what would have to change if the monks reasoned the way the epistemology says you should: expected harm rather than prohibited categories, credences over which beings are in the water, permissions tagged to standpoints instead of flat bans. Would the rulebook get shorter, longer, or just weirder? And is there any version of it that survives if you throw away the soul-and-karma metaphysics and keep only the non-violence?
