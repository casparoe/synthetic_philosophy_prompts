I am a junior in high school, and for a research paper in my history class I have been collecting the ways states respond, decades later, to crimes committed by their own earlier governments — crimes in which almost no living citizen took part, and which most of them were not alive to witness. One structure keeps repeating itself, and I cannot tell whether it is confused or carefully designed.

The state issues a formal apology, delivered by a sitting head of government speaking on behalf of the country as a whole. It builds and then maintains in perpetuity a memorial at the site of the crime. It writes the crime into the national curriculum as a chapter of the country's own history rather than as the history of a regime that has ceased to exist. And it establishes a fund for surviving victims and their descendants. Three features pull in the opposite direction. The agreements setting up the fund generally state that the payment is made without any admission of legal liability. The country's criminal code permits punishment only of the individual who personally committed the offense, so prosecutors charge former guards and functionaries, sometimes in their nineties, but never the polity. And the taxpayers who finance the fund, like the minister who apologizes, mostly did not exist when the crime was committed.

My teacher told me that Karl Jaspers, lecturing in Germany just after the Second World War, separated several distinct kinds of guilt and argued that only one of them is strictly personal, and that this is supposed to explain the pattern. I have not read him, and I would rather work out what the practice itself assumes than be handed a label.

First: reconstruct the theory of guilt and of agency that this practice only makes sense under. For an apology, a memorial, and a curriculum to be something other than theater, what has to be true of the nation — that it is a single continuing agent that survives the replacement of every one of its members, and even a change of constitution or regime? That something in the neighborhood of guilt can attach to a group rather than only to its members? That a claim can pass down a family line from a victim to her descendants, which seems to be a different kind of inheritance altogether from the continuity of the state? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely sit awkwardly beside?

Second: is the practice internally consistent? An apology normally concedes fault, yet the fund disclaims liability and the criminal law denies that anyone but the perpetrator could be guilty at all. Does the first-person-plural voice of the memorial contradict the strictly individual voice of the courtroom, or can one state coherently speak in both registers at once without meaning the same thing by guilt in each?

Third: separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit well. I suspect the payments could be justified with no national guilt whatever — by the fact that present citizens inherited the benefits of the crime, or by a duty to repair a relationship the state is still a party to — and that the apology may be doing something else entirely, such as acknowledging the standing of the people who were wronged. Tell me whether that is right, and whether the practice is underdetermined in this way, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same ceremonies.

Finally: suppose we replaced the presupposed view with a strict individualism on which nothing normative survives the death of the person who acted or was acted upon. Walk me through what would concretely have to change — would the memorial become a museum about some foreign regime's crime, would the apology be downgraded to a statement of regret that no one living is at fault, would the fund become charity, would the curriculum stop describing the crime as ours? I want to know which of those changes actually follow from the substitution and which do not, because the ones that follow seem to me to reveal what the practice was committed to all along.
