I sit on the procurement committee of a mid-sized public housing authority — about 2,400 units spread over 14 properties. We vote in three weeks on replacing every exterior door entry system: locks, fob readers, controllers, the whole access layer. Two live bids, and the committee is split in a way I've come to think is really a philosophy-of-technology argument wearing a procurement costume. I want you to draft the memo I'll circulate to the board before the vote.

Bid A is a national vendor's cloud platform. Roughly 40% cheaper up front, remote credential management, real-time audit logs, vendor handles all maintenance. But it's a five-year subscription, only vendor-certified techs may touch the hardware, and the contract lets them suspend credential services remotely if we're more than 30 days late on an invoice. HQ would get one dashboard covering all 14 properties, and our entire access layer would sit in somebody else's ops center.

Bid B is an open-spec system fabricated and installed by a regional firm. Credentials stored locally on each door controller, no recurring fee, our own maintenance crew can fix everything. About 35% more up front and a slower rollout. The part actually splitting us: each property's resident management committee would hold its own credential-issuing rights and set its own guest and delivery policy. Fourteen buildings, fourteen access regimes.

That last point is where it stops being about cost. Two years ago one property's resident committee used its discretionary guest rules to keep out a tenant's adult son, recently released from prison, on the grounds that he wasn't a resident; the tenant eventually moved out. Meanwhile Bid A hands the same kind of lever to HQ, and through the suspension clause, to a private company that can pull it during a billing dispute.

Here's the argument I need the memo to settle. My colleague says the choice is fundamentally about who ends up holding power over the buildings — that this is Langdon Winner's point, that certain technical arrangements don't just happen to be convenient, they require or at least heavily reward particular ways of organising people, and once they're installed they quietly settle questions a vote would otherwise have to re-settle every year. Our counsel disagrees on two grounds. Procedurally: the policy says award to the most advantageous bid considering cost, durability, safety and operational fit, and control structures aren't a listed criterion, so reasoning about them is out of scope. Substantively — and this is the one I want you to genuinely wrestle with rather than swat: he says the whole artifacts-have-politics line is a category error, because doors and databases don't do anything, people with authority do, and the same hardware under different governance rules produces completely different politics, which supposedly proves the politics was never in the artifact to begin with. Then he adds a jab: the poster-child example everybody repeats, the low Long Island overpasses allegedly built to keep buses and their riders away from a beach, was probably just standard clearance for the era, and if the flagship is folklore the thesis goes down with it.

So, the memo:

- Bottom line up front. Which bid, and under what conditions — contract clauses to strike, carve-outs to demand, governance changes that have to be in place before signature. If your answer is really "B, but only if X and Y," say it flatly, and tell me what has to happen between now and the vote.
- Take a side on the conceptual question, because I think the procurement decision turns on it. Is the distribution-of-control issue a legitimate procurement consideration, or is counsel right that it's just governance dressed up as hardware? Don't hedge at me. Tell me which way the argument actually goes.
- Answer the folklore jab head-on: if the bridge story turns out to be false, does that damage the thesis at all? Be specific about what would survive it and what wouldn't.
- Steelman counsel's best point — I think the same-artifact-different-rules case is genuinely good — and then say what's left of it after you're done.
- Address something I can't make up my mind about: are the resident-committee abuse and the HQ-plus-vendor abuse the same failure wearing different clothes, or are they different enough in kind that one side of the tradeoff is clearly worse?

I don't want a literature review and I definitely don't want references or footnotes — no bibliography, just the reasoning. Naming positions is fine if it helps you keep things straight. The board should be able to read it in ten minutes, so call it 800 to 1,100 words. Casual in tone is fine, this is an internal document, but it has to be something I can defend when counsel pushes back.
