I am assembling the penultimate session of a graduate seminar on value theory, and the topic is what happens when a choice is genuinely hard rather than merely close. The session juxtaposes three broad positions, and my students have read them faithfully, yet the discussion they produce is one of the least productive I have run in years: each party answers objections by restating its own framework in more detail, and nobody appears to be losing. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a survey of the literature or a verdict on which side is right.

The positions, as I have set them out for the students, are roughly these. First, the incomparability camp, associated most prominently with Raz: for some pairs of items under some covering consideration, none of the three standard comparative relations obtains, and the small improvement argument is offered as the engine of this result — if two items were equally good, a slight improvement to one of them would have to make it better, so the failure of that intuition is supposed to show that equality never obtained in the first place. Second, Broome's response, which attacks the engine and offers a rival explanation: the small improvement argument quietly assumes the very trichotomy it is meant to disprove, and the phenomenology of a gap in the comparative ordering is better handled as vagueness in the comparative concepts themselves, so that the hard cases are borderline cases rather than cases in which no relation holds. Third, Chang's parity view, which denies the trichotomy thesis but keeps comparability: on a par is a fourth, irreducible formal relation, distinct both from equality under a covering consideration and from incomparability, and it is supposed to do real work in the theory of agency, since the hard choices it generates are resolved not by discovering which option is better but by committing oneself, thereby creating reasons where the values underdetermined them. Two further features of the debate seem to me relevant to the diagnosis and I want them taken seriously. The parties move freely between the older sense of incommensurability, meaning the absence of a common measure or a single cardinal scale, and the narrower sense of incomparability, meaning the failure of any comparative relation to hold, and it is not obvious that these come apart in the way the argument requires. And there is an asymmetry in what each side treats as evidence: one side presses formal desiderata such as transitivity, acyclicity, and the possibility of rational resolution without arbitrary stopping points, while the other presses case intuitions about career changes, artistic vocations, and the choice between a relationship and a professional opportunity, and the two sets of data do not obviously adjudicate between the same claims.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis to be tested rather than as a conclusion, is that a large part of the impasse is verbal or ideological in character: the parties disagree about what a comparative concept is required to deliver, and in particular about the status of the trichotomy thesis itself — whether it is a conceptual truth about better, worse, and equally good, a substantive hypothesis about value relations, or merely an artifact of a formal vocabulary that the substantive questions never asked for. If that is right, then much of what looks like a disagreement about the structure of value is a disagreement about the semantics of comparatives, conducted under a single heading. But I suspect there is also a substantive residue that cannot be dissolved this way, having to do with whether rational agency requires that any two live options be comparable, and with whether a resolution produced by commitment rather than by betterness can be rational in the same sense. What I want from you is an assessment of how much of the dispute is verbal, how much is a difference in evidential standards, how much is a case of several distinct questions — conceptual, metaphysical, and psychological — being answered under one label, and whether there is a hidden formal question doing the real work, for instance whether parity can be given a coherent formal characterization at all without collapsing back into either equality under a covering consideration or vagueness. Say plainly which parts of this debate are load-bearing and which are decorative, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, an empirical finding about how people actually resolve such choices, or nothing at all — in which case explain why the disagreement should be expected to persist indefinitely and whether that persistence is itself evidence for one of the positions.
