Here's what I keep tripping over. Two pieces of inference that look like they have the same shape:

(a) The doomsday argument. I know my birth rank n among all humans who'll ever exist. If the total N were enormous it'd be a fluke that I landed this early, so being at rank n is evidnece against huge N — Bayes-wise, P(I'm the n-th | N) = 1/N, which drains probability mass off the big-N hypotheses and pushes the end of the species forward.

(b) The inverse gambler's fallacy. I stroll into a casino, happen to see double sixes come up, and conclude the roller's been at it a long while. That's a mistake: whatever the past history, the probability of double sixes on the roll I witnessed is the same, and once you use all your evidence the many-rolls hypothesis gets no boost.

But then in the cosmological cousin of (b) — fine-tuning and the multiverse — a lot of people argue the inference is *not* fallacious after all, on the grounds that the datum isn't that some universe has life-friendly constants, it's that this one does, the one I'm inside, and I could only ever have found myself in a life-friendly one. An observation-selection effect apparently rescues the inference.

My worry is that the doomsday argument is pulling the same trick: trading on indexical evidence — this rank being mine — to convert something that would otherwise be a fallacy into a valid inference. Yet the two cases get treated differently: nearly everyone grants the DA some pull, nearly everyone agrees the casino version is a blunder. So where's the disanalogy?
