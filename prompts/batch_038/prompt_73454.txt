I do hand-lettering, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I've never seen named anywhere. Sometimes I'll draw a shape that's honestly ambiguous on its own — a stem-and-shoulder thing that could be an n or a sloppily drawn h — and which letter it is gets settled entirely by what's sitting next to it. Put it after a t in the middle of a word and everyone reads n; put it at the end of the word and they read h. What gets me is that I can flip the reading without touching the shape at all. Thicken the stroke of the letter to it's left, or lean the neighbours over a bit, and the identical glyph starts reading as the other one. So the letter's identity seems to live partly outside the letter.

It runs the other way too. A shape I'd swear was an obvious a can start looking like nothing at all if I set it between letters that clash with it stylistically — it doesn't become a different letter, it just stops being one.

I know people talk about prototypes and family resemblance, but those are about blurry edges within a single setting. Here the setting is doing the identifying — the neighbours aren't just evidence I weigh, they change what the thing is. So: is there an established concept for that? And I don't want just a label to go look up, I want the edges of it. When does context actually settle which category something falls in, as opposed to nudging a guess that was already there? Is there such a thing as a shape with no letter identity at all even inside a word, and how would you tell that apart from one that's merely hard to read? And does any of the philosophy on this — not just the psychology of reading — take the "identity lives outside the thing" part seriously, or does everything treat the neighbours as optional extra information?
