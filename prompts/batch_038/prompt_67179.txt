I'm a junior, and I sit on my school's Student Philanthropy Committee, which each year directs a $3,000 gift from our alumni association to a cause selected by student vote. This year's slate has deadlocked between two proposals: one would fund a charity that distributes bed nets, whose cost per life saved is well documented, and the other would fund an organization working to reduce risks that could permanently foreclose humanity's far future. The second group's case is that even a one-in-a-thousand reduction in the chance of catastrophe outweighs any quantity of nets, because millions of years of continued civilization could contain an astronomical number of people.

I want to press that reasoning with the simulation argument — Nick Bostrom's claim that if advanced civilizations run ancestor simulations at all, then we are almost certainly inside one. My worry is that this undercuts the far-future case in several distinct ways: a simulation might simply be switched off, its future truncated by the simulators' computing budget, and its inhabitants might be copied so many times that each copy's welfare should count for less. But I can also see that it may cut the other way, if simulated people are conscious and their descendants are just as real to them as ours are to us.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our vote. It should recommend a concrete allocation, including whether to split the funds, and give the committee reasons it can act on rather than a tour of the debate. Tell me plainly whether the simulation argument weakens the far-future case, merely fails to strengthen it, or is beside the point at the scale of a $3,000 gift — and give the losing side its strongest honest hearing.
