I do interpretation for a small historic trust — two house museums, a statue in a town square, some plaques — and two or three times a year we recieve a campaign asking us to take a name off a building or to rewrite a panel about a founder who owned people, or a wartime leader. Our board wants a written procedure the staff can run, not an essay, because most of us have zero philosophy training and we get about three hours per case before a panel meeting where two volunteers from the community council sign off on whatever we bring them.

Can you write that procedure? It has to take the 'he was a man of his time' defence seriously instead of treating it as an automatic cop-out — the point is to work out when it actually does hold and when it doesn't. So: questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite verdict, and where it hands the thing to the community panel because what's left is a values question rather than a factual one. It should end at a recommendation among the options we genuinely have (leave it, add context, relocate, remove the name).

The distinctions I most need to survive the simplification: explaining a thing vs excusing it, the act vs the person's whole character, and whether it matters that people at the time were saying it was wrong and the subject was in a position to have heard them — the example one of our volunteers brought up was las Casas denouncing the treatment of the Taíno in Hispaniola not long after Columbus's own voyages.

Then run it on three cases: Columbus, Jefferson at Monticello, and Churchill over the 1943 Bengal famine. At least one should come out in a way our board won't like — say which and why. And be straight with me about where the procedure will misfire, and which steps are really judgement calls wearing a checklist as a disguise. No reading list, we won't look anything up.
