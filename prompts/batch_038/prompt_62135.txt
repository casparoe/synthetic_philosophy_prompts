Right, I'm prepping a session for an interdisiplinary reading group — half philosophy of mind, half literary — and I want to do something unusual with Heller's *Catch-22*. Please act as a professional philosopher who also does real literary criticism, not as a book-report generator, and think it through properly before you start writing.

The philosophical idea I want to hang it on is Dennett's notion of a free-floating rationale: a rationale that explains why a thing is the way it is, or why a system does what it does, without being represented in anybody's head. His home case is biology — there's a rationale for the eye, for the four chambers of the heart, for the gazelle's zig-zag, and no organism believes or wants anything of the sort. The point isn't that we're merely projecting; the rationale is supposed to be real, because the selection history makes it true. The further claim that interests me, and that I want to test against the novel, is that free-floating rationales aren't confined to biology: practices, institutions and markets can be run by rationales that no participant endorses or could articulate.

*Catch-22* seems to me like an unusually clean test case, and I want help working out whether it actually is one or whether I'm forcing it. The obvious candidate is Milo Minderbinder's syndicate: he buys cheap and sells dear all over the Mediterranean, ends up effectively administering half of it, and eventually takes a contract with the Germans under which his own planes bomb and strafe his own squadron's base at Pianosa, killing and wounding his own men — and then defends this not in terms of anybody's intentions but in terms of the syndicate's accounts, on the grounds that everyone has a share in it, so the syndicate has bombed itself and made everyone a profit doing it. There's also Captain Black's Glorious Loyalty Crusade, where the number of loyalty oaths a man has to sign keeps escalating and then stops as abruptly as it began, with Captain Black never able to say what the point was; Doc Daneeka, killed administratively when his name turns up on the flight log of a crashed plane, who thereafter cannot get anyone to accept that he is alive; Major Major, made squadron commander by an IBM machine in Rome with a sense of humour; and the title rule itself, which everyone appeals to, nobody can produce, and which the novel insists is all the better for not existing.

Three rival readings are floating around in my head and I'd like them sorted out rather than blurred:

1. The genuinely Dennettian one — the syndicate's rationale is free-floating: nobody holds it, not even Milo, and it explains what happens anyway.
2. The List-and-Pettit one — that's a category mistake, because M & M Enterprises is a group agent: commitments formed non-summatively, persistence over time, procedures for deciding, so the rationale does have a reasoner, just a corporate one, and Milo is its spokesperson rather than its source.
3. The deflationary one — there's no rationale out there at all; talk of what the syndicate wants is just the intentional stance applied to a pile of ledgers, handy for prediction and empty as metaphysics, and Heller's joke is that everyone in the novel treats it as though it were real.

What I'd like: first, a short brainstorming section mapping specific episodes onto those three readings, flagging which scenes are genuinely diagnostic and which are just atmosphere; then an argument for the reading the novel best supports. Take the philosophy seriously as philosophy — say what would have to be true for a rationale to float free of every reasoner, and whether a market or a bureaucracy can actually manage that — and take the novel seriously as a novel rather than as a thought experiment with a plot attached.

Then the bit I most want you to push on, which is my own hunch and may well be wrong: that the really Heller-ish thing isn't a free-floating rationale at all but a *deliberately floated* one — an agent who insists that his reasons are nobody's reasons in order to become unblameable. On that view Milo isn't a symptom of agentless explanation, he's a man operating the free-floating reading as a liability shield, and the novel's moral energy lies in the refusal of that move: Yossarian and the chaplain keep asking who, and the horror isn't that nobody is responsible but that everyone knows exactly who is and it makes no difference. Tell me whether that survives contact with both the text and Dennett. If it does, say whether it names something the philosophy of collective agency is currently missing — blame-resistance as a property a rationale can have — or whether it collapses back into ordinary individual vice dressed up in institutional language.

Finally, be honest about where the reading strains. Heller is writing satire and running jokes, not metaphysics, and some of what looks like agentless explanation may just be the surrealism doing its job. Say which parts you think are load-bearing and which are me over-reading.
