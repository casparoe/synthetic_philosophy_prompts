I serve on the product-ethics panel at a software firm that builds case-management systems for public agencies. Last month we reviewed a deployment our company supplies to an immigration authority: it ingests applications, scores them for inconsistencies, and routes flagged cases into enforcement queues. The proposal before us was to build in an escalation step — a lever that would let an individual engineer or case-handler suspend or reroute an automated flag when the underlying data looked wrong.

One of my colleagues argued against it, and his case ran roughly as follows. The engineers and the case-handlers are cogs in a machine; a machine's purpose is set by whoever designed and commissioned it, not by any single one of its parts; therefore personal blame for what the system does is a category error, and responsibility belongs to the agency, its ministers, and the procurement arrangements that produced the contract. He said this was precisely Arendt's point about Eichmann — the banality of evil — and that our instinct to hand individuals a lever is a sentimental hangover from a picture of agency that the twentieth century refuted. He added, as further support, that the historical record of what Eichmann said in private before the trial shows a committed ideological enthusiast rather than a thoughtless functionary, and that this only strengthens his position: who happens to be sitting in the seat is irrelevant to what the machine does.

The panel meets again in a fortnight, and I would rather have the analogy audited than merely rebutted. Please take a definite position throughout — I want a verdict with reasons, not a survey of the options left for me to choose between. Write in British English, and refer to texts and thinkers by name where you need them, but do not append a reference list or a bibliography; where you are unsure of an attribution, say so rather than filling the gap.

Please structure your answer under these headings, in this order:

1. **The mapping** (about 250 words). Set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome. What plays the cog, the machine, the machine's purpose, the designer and the operator, the other parts, the replacement of a worn part? Then identify which of these correspondences my colleague is actually relying on when he moves from the machine picture to the conclusion that individual blame is misplaced.

2. **Where it breaks** (about 350 words). Work through the disanalogies, but separate those that matter for his use from those that are merely decorative — a machine that is only loosely like a bureaucracy is not yet an objection to him. Press in particular on four points: whether a cog has any discretion at all; whether the machine could keep running if a part refused; whether a part can be swapped out without loss to the functioning of the whole; and whether the machine's purpose really is fixed independently of the parts that execute it.

3. **Whose analogy is it?** (about 300 words). My colleague treats the cog-in-the-machine picture as the content of the banality of evil. I want to know whether Arendt endorses that picture, attacks it, or reports it as the defendant's own excuse; what her account of thoughtlessness actually claims about the person in the seat, as opposed to about the system; and whether the archival point he makes about Eichmann's private convictions cuts for him, against him, or neither.

4. **What survives** (about 200 words). State plainly whether his conclusion survives the audit — that individual blame here is a category error and that the escalation lever is sentimentality — and give your reasons. If a deflated version survives, such as a claim about the limits of what an individual lever can achieve rather than about the absence of individual responsibility, say which version survives and why that is the one worth holding.

5. **A better analogy** (about 150 words). If the cog/machine picture misleads, propose the comparison that would carry the same structural facts honestly, and say what conclusion, if any, it licenses about the escalation proposal.
