Background, briefly: I was a physical chemist for about 38 years, mostly kinetics with some modeling on the side, and I retired four years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy the way other retirees take up golf, and I'm in a little reading group at the library. Last month we did a popular book on the philosophy of mathematics by a physicist who does a lot of podcasting. There's one passage I keep going back to because I'm fairly sure it's cheating somewhere, but I can't say where, and everyone else in the group just nodded along.

Here it is, or close enough to it — I retyped this from my notes so don't hold me to the exact wording, the sense is right:

"Benacerraf finished off mathematical Platonism in two moves. In the first, he showed that arithmetic cannot tell us what the numbers are. Set theory lets you build 0 as the empty set and each successive number as the set of all the ones before it, and every statement of arithmetic comes out true; but you can equally well build 0 as the empty set and each successive number as the singleton of the one before, and again everything comes out true. Since nothing in arithmetic selects between these, and nothing outside arithmetic could, the question 'which set is 3?' simply has no answer. So numbers aren't objects at all: a number is a position in a structure, and whether '3 is prime' is true is a fact about whichever progression you've settled on rather than about any object. In the second move, Benacerraf noted that if numbers were abstract objects they would sit outside space and time and could enter into no causal relation with us, while knowledge — as every working scientist assumes — requires a causal interaction between the knower and the known. Platonism therefore cannot explain how we know arithmetic. Benacerraf's own conclusion was that mathematical statements are not literally true; numbers are useful fictions and theorems are true only in the way the rules of chess are true. The lesson for the rest of us is that when a physicist writes down a differential equation she is not describing the world, she is keeping the books."

What I want is an audit, not a defense of the author. Go through it claim by claim and sort the claims into three piles: what a philosopher of mathematics would sign off on as stated, what's a fair simplification that a popularizer is entitled to, and what would make them wince. A few things in particular I'd like settled:

- Are the two "moves" actually two stages of a single argument, or two seperate arguments that the book has welded together? If they're seperate, does the welding change what the reader ends up believing?
- Is "a number is a position in a structure" Benacerraf's considered view, or something he floated and then had reservations about? I have a hazy sense that structures are themselves abstract, which would seem to leave him exactly where he started, but I don't trust my hazy sense.
- Is the causal theory of knowledge really as safe as "every working scientist assumes" makes it out to be? I ask because in my own field we claimed to know plenty about things that don't kick us back — transition states, for one. And did Benacerraf treat that premise as settled, or did he know it was the soft spot?
- The chess comparison. Does "true the way the rules of chess are true" square with what Benacerraf actually wanted from a theory of mathematical truth? My instinct is that this last bit belongs to somebody else entirely and has been draped over him.
- And the closing line about differential equations. Does it follow from anything true in the passage? If it does I have a problem with my whole career; if it doesn't, I'd like to understand why it feels like it does, and whether there's a standard rejoinder that comes from the direction of science itself rather than from pure mathematics.

For what it's worth I'm inclined to think the chapter is basically honest about the first move and slippery about the second, but I have no confidence in that and would rather be told straight out that I've got it backwards. Please don't send me off to find things — no reading lists, I can turn up sources myself at the library. I just want your judgement and the reasoning behind it.
