reading group on the ethics of content moderation, one of the regulars (call him Dan) walked us through an argument last Thursday and I haven't been able to break it since. here it is as best I can reconstruct:

(1) Call something punishment just in case it deliberately imposes something burdensome and condemnatory on a person for a supposed offense against rules that bind them, by someone claiming the authority to impose it.

(2) A permanent ban or a demonitization is exactly that. It costs the person their audience and their income, and the notice they get names a policy violation — that's censure, not cost-recovery, and it isn't like quarantining a sick traveler.

(3) So deplatforming is punishment.

(4) Punishment, unlike a mere refusal to deal with someone, carries legitimacy conditions: published rules, notice, a chance to be heard, an adjudicator who isn't the accuser, proportionality between offense and sanction, and some route back.

(5) So a platform may deplatform only when it supplies those. Most supply none of them. So most deplatforming is illegitimate.

(6) Fine so far. But nothing in (1)–(4) mentions platforms. Run them on the book club that drops a member who plagiarized a submission, the congregation that excommunicates, the conference that rescinds an invited talk, the bouncer who bars someone for life, the parent who takes the phone away. Each of those is a burdensome and condemnatory imposition for a supposed rule violation by whoever's in charge of the rules.

(7) So each of them is punishment too, and each is illegitimate unless the book club holds hearings, the church publishes a proportionality schedule, and the parent appoints an impartial adjudicator.

(8) Nobody believes that. If (7) is right, ordinary associational life is pervasively illegitimate.

(9) So either (5) is false — platforms don't owe process either — or (7) is right and we're all in trouble.

Two things I already tried and he swatted away. First, that the question of what a practice is and the question of who may be subjected to it on what terms are separate — Hart's split between the general justifying aim of punishment and its distribution. He said that split is what makes his argument go, not what stops it: (3) is about what the thing is, and (6)–(7) just apply the same distributional question to every case that fits. Second, exit: you can leave a book club and found another one, you can't leave the internet. He said that's a difference of degree at best, and if degree is doing the work then whether a ban needs a hearing tracks how concentrated the market for audiences happens to be — process duties blinking in and out with the Herfindahl index as rival platforms come and go. He thinks that's absurd, and honeslty so do I.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (5) than (8) if I'm forced to choose, but I don't know what I'd be paying, and I want to know whether Dan gets to keep (1) exactly as stated or whether (1) is where the trouble starts.
