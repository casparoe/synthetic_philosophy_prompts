QUESTION (25 marks, around 500 words expected):

Revealed preference theory is sometimes charged with emptiness: it defines preference in terms of choice, and then explains choice by appeal to preference, so no observation could count against it. State this objection as forcefully as you can. What is the best reply available to a revealed preference theorist? Does that reply show the theory has genuine empirical content, or only that the objection misses its target? In your answer distinguish the weak and the strong axiom of revealed preference and say what work each of them does.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Revealed preference theory starts from the idea that demand theory shouldn't rest on anything mental. Samuelson's point was that utility isn't observable and never was, so rather than assuming preferences and deriving choices from them you take choices as the data and read preferences off them: x is revealed preferred to y when the consumer takes x while y is still affordable. Utility then names a way of summarising patterns of choice, not a psychological state causing them.

The objection says this makes the theory circular and therefore empty. If "prefers x to y" just means "chose x when y was available", then "consumers choose what they prefer" is true by stipulation, and whatever demand-theoretic results get derived from it are the data redescribed in a technical vocabulary. Since no possible observation could contradict it, the objection goes, the theory explains nothing — an account that can't fail tells you nothing about why the thing happened.

The best reply is that the axioms aren't definitions but restrictions on what choice data can look like. Reading preferences off choices only works if the choices are mutually consistent, and consistency is a substantive condition that a real consumer can fail to meet. The weak axiom says you can't have x chosen while y is affordable and then y chosen while x is affordable. And that already makes revealed preference transitive, since any longer cycle has to contain a pair of that kind somewhere in it: working round the cycle you eventually take something over a bundle that has already been taken over it. What the strong axiom adds is the closure — it applies the same condition to chains, so that indirect revealed preference is treated like direct. (Strictly, what you want for rationalisation by a utility function is the generalised axiom, because the strong one forbids ties between distinct bundles, and consumers can of course be indifferent.) Afriat's theorem makes the point precise: a finite data set of prices and quantities satisfies the generalised axiom exactly when there is some utility function that rationalises it. So the axioms are necessary and sufficient conditions on observable data rather than conventions, and the circularity charge simply fails — the theory is falsifiable, and violations have been looked for and found, both with subjects in the lab and in household budget data. The results that do follow, like the downward slope of the compensated demand curve, are real theorems and not restatements.

There's a limit on what the axioms buy you, though, and this is where the theory is weaker than its defenders admit. Giffen goods are the standing case: a good whose quantity demanded rises when its own price rises. Take two observations, before and after a price rise. The old bundle contains less of the good that got dearer, so it is still affordable at the new prices, and the consumer turns it down in favour of the new bundle; so the new bundle is revealed preferred to the old. But the new bundle was affordable at the old prices too, and there the consumer took the old one. The same pair therefore gets revealed preferred in both directions, which is exactly what the weak axiom forbids and the strong axiom forbids more strongly still. So the ordinary law of demand isn't recoverable from the axioms — and since the law of demand is one of the main things utility-maximising theory was supposed to deliver, revealed preference theory ends up with less empirical content than the account it was meant to reformulate. It's testable, but it's also thinner.
