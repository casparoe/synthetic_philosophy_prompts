In the United Kingdom the sovereign meets the prime minister alone once a week: no officials attend, no minutes are taken, and no account of what passed is ever given. The sovereign is nonetheless sent the papers of government and is expected to read them, and is conventionally credited with three rights in relation to ministers — to be consulted, to encourage, and to warn. The rest of the constitution runs on the opposite principle: every act of the Crown is performed on ministerial advice, and the minister who gave that advice answers to Parliament for it. Japan carries the second principle further, requiring Cabinet advice and approval and a ministerial countersignature for each of the Emperor's acts in matters of state, with the consequence that when Akihito wished to step down he could voice the wish only as a personal reflection on age and duty in a televised address, after which the Diet enacted a special law to make abdication possible.

What theory of power and accountability do these arrangements presuppose? Do they assume that power consists in determining an outcome, so that counsel which leaves no trace and may be disregarded at will is not power at all; that responsibility attaches to the act rather than to what influenced it; or that a monarch's will must never figure among the causes of a decision, which is why a wish must arrive disguised as a remark? Reconstruct the implicit view, separating what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably beside it, and indicate where it is underdetermined — which rival accounts of legitimate authority would license it equally. Then assess the coherence of holding both that the sovereign never governs and that the sovereign's most characteristic activity is unrecorded, unsupervised influence on the person who does, and state what would have to change in the audience, in ministerial responsibility, or in parliamentary practice if one insisted that influence over government be as accountable as decision.
