Working on the section of my thesis where I have to deal with the dream debate, and I've come away feeling like nobody in it ever actually addressed anybody else. Malcolm in *Dreaming* says a dream isn't an experience had while you sleep — the sleeper isnt in a position for anything to be going on for them, so all there is is an impression on waking and a report. Dennett agrees dreams aren't experiences but for a mirror-image reason: the story gets put together at or near the moment of waking, maybe confabulated, so there was no experience spread out over the night for it to be a dream of. And then sleep scientists carry on measuring as if lucid dreamers signalling with pre-agreed eye movements settled it.

My hypothesis, take it or leave it, is that these aren't three answers to one question but three different criteria for when a mental state counts as having happened: availability to the subject at the time, a physiological trace at the time, a narrative reconstructible afterwards. Nobody ever argues against the other side's criterion, they just don't grant that it's a criterion at all — which would explain sixty odd years of nobody moving.

Is that right? Diagnose it from what each side actually says, and tell me whether it's a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or just badly posed. Then: suppose someone built a decoder that read out imagery during sleep and it matched the waking report closely. Would Malcolm have to concede, or is his objection immune to that kind of evidence? If it's immune, what does that tell us about what the argument was ever about, and what — if anything — would count as progress here rather than just more rounds.
