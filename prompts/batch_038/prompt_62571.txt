I am a doctoral candidate in political theory co-teaching a session of an interdisciplinary bioethics seminar next month on the regulation of surrogacy and egg donation, and I want to open the discussion with Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale rather than with a policy paper. The reason is a rhetorical fact that irritates me: the novel gets invoked by both camps in this debate, and by each as though it settled the matter. Opponents of compensated surrogacy in continental Europe treat Gilead's Ceremony as the reductio ad absurdum of the idea that gestation can be a service one provides, and they lean on the reduction of the handmaid to a walking womb as evidence that the practice cannot be described without describing the person as a body part. Defenders of the American state-by-state market reply that Gilead is a story about the absence of consent and of exit, not about money — there is no money in Gilead at all — and that a paid arrangement in California, where the surrogate is screened, independently represented, contractually protected and free to walk away, is the precise opposite of the novel's regime. Both sides are using the same book to argue against each other, and I suspect that is a sign the book is being asked to do work it cannot do.

Here is my working thesis, which I would like you to test rather than to flatter. First, I think the novel is structurally incapable of arbitrating between the commodification objection and the coercion objection, because it removes price from the arrangement entirely: it shows us a reproductive transfer with no market in it, so nothing in it can tell us what payment adds or subtracts. Second, I think the novel's real philosophical contribution is to dramatize something Elizabeth Anderson's argument about commercial surrogacy identifies but cannot make vivid — that what is at stake is the norms governing a practice, the way a contract can require a woman to repress and re-describe her own parental love, converting a relationship governed by non-market norms into one governed by use and price. Third, I think both camps mis-describe the arrangement the novel actually depicts: Offred is the genetic mother of the child she carries, which makes Gilead's practice what American clinics would call traditional surrogacy, whereas the dominant commercial structure in the United States separates the egg from the gestation, often with a paid donor, so that the figure of the surrogate in the novel is not the figure of the gestational carrier in the market. On the policy side, so that you are not required to retrieve anything, take my sketch as given: France and Germany prohibit surrogacy outright, Spain treats the contracts as null, Italy has recently criminalized the arrangement even when entered into abroad by its own citizens, the United Kingdom permits only non-commercial arrangements with payments confined to reasonable expenses and requires a court order to transfer parenthood, and the United States leaves the matter to the states, with California enforcing compensated gestational contracts and with a substantial cross-border market in which intended parents from prohibitionist jurisdictions travel to jurisdictions that permit the practice. I would like you to say whether the novel gives any traction on the question that actually divides these regimes — namely whether a ban reduces the inequality of bargaining position and the vulnerability that Debra Satz treats as the marks of a noxious market, or whether it exports them to places where they are worse.

So: give me a critical reading that treats the novel as a work and the philosophy as an argument, not as a caption and an illustration. Point to the specific episodes you would put in front of the seminar — the Ceremony, the labour scene in which the other women perform the birth around Janine, Offred's clandestine meetings with the Commander, her negotiation with Nick, her conduct toward the previous handmaid whose story she inherits, the flight attempt with Moira, and the closing epilogue in which a future academic audience turns Gilead into material for detached scholarship — and say what each one does for or against my three claims. Tell me where the novel resists the commodification reading, where it is actively unhelpful to the market-defenders who claim it, and where I have overreached, including anywhere my third claim depends on a distinction between genetic and gestational surrogacy that the text blurs or ignores. I am not asking for quotations from the novel and I do not want a bibliography; describe scenes and paraphrase arguments instead. Finally, be explicit about your own confidence: give me a verdict on each of the three claims — endorsed, qualified, or rejected — and then tell me what would change your mind, which single consideration or scene would most plausibly flip your assessment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail, whether because it rests on a contested interpretation of Anderson's or Satz's position or because you are uncertain about a detail of the plot you have not been able to check.
