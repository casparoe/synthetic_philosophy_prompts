Ok so I was round my mate's last night, three bottles deep, and we got into a proper two-hour arguement about anekāntavāda — he's just started a philosophy MA and thinks it's the most sophisticated thing anyone has ever said about disagreement, and I think it's relativism in a nice hat that lets you never commit to anything. Neither of us budged an inch. He kept going on about the sevenfold business, syād this syād that, about how you're meant to be able to say "in some respects it is, in some respects it isn't, in some respects it's beyond description" without thereby giving up on truth altogether, and I kept saying that's exactly the giving-up-on-truth bit.

I want to settle it, or at least embarrass him. So: write me a parody of a British newspaper's in-house editorial style guide — you know the genre, the dry numbered manual that tells subs how to hyphenate things, when you may name a defendant, whether "MP" takes full stops — except this paper's editor has adopted Jain epistemology as official house doctrine and means it. Invent the paper, invent the editor, whatever you like. Around 800–900 words, numbered sections with subclauses, deadpan from first line to last, and absolutely no winking.

What it needs to get right for the joke to work:

- The seven conditional modes have to be used *correctly*, as literal rules for constructing sentences — headlines, standfirsts, sports copy, obituaries, the weather box. I want a clause about which modes are permitted above the fold and which are banned from page one, with a stated reason.
- The standpoint idea (naya) should turn up as an actual staffing and desk-structure decision: stories get assigned to a named standpoint, and the guide has to say what happens when two standpoints file on the same event at the same deadline.
- It has to grapple with the parts of journalism that cannot be hedged — a final score, a court verdict, a death notice, a correction, the TV listings. Give us the exemptions and the small print surrounding them, and let that small print get visibly more strained as the guide goes on.
- At some point the guide has to state its own central rule and then, in a footnote or a trailing sub-clause, clock that the rule is itself a claim. Do not explain why that's awkward. Do not have anyone utter the phrase self-refuting. The reader should just feel the floor go.
- And it has to be fair, because my mate will smell a strawman a mile off: include at least one section where the doctrine demonstrably does something *good* — the corrections policy, or the letters page, or how they handle a story where the paper honestly cannot tell which of two accounts is right — written so a Jain reading it would nod and think yes, that's definately the point.

Keep the texture of a real house manual throughout: cross-references to sections that don't exist, a short glossary at the back if you want one, and something about the crossword. All the humour should come from the doctrine being applied faithfully and literally to an operation that has to go to print at eleven.
