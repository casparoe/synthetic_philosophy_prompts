And here's the student's answer in full, with paragraph numbers added by me for reference — they're not in the original:

**[1]** Aristotle starts from the household. You can't understand the polis without first understanding the parts that make it up, and one of those parts is the master-slave relation. He also insists early on that a master's rule over a slave is a different *kind* of rule from the rule of one citizen over another or of a king over subjects, against Plato, who he says treats these as differing only in the number of people ruled. So he isn't just sliding from "families have heads" to "cities can have slaves."

**[2]** A slave, for him, is a piece of property, and property is an instrument; but unlike a loom or a ship's rudder, a slave is an instrument for action rather than for making things. Then the key move: nature is generally not wasteful, it makes each thing for a single use, and among human beings some are marked out from birth to be ruled — those whose contribution is bodily, who can see the point of a plan laid out by someone else but can't originate one themselves. A natural slave is someone who belongs wholly to another while still being a human being. He thinks such a person is actually helped by the arrangement: being directed by someone with foresight is better for him than being left to his own devices, and since master and slave share an interest in the work getting done well, the relation isn't merely exploitative. So slavery in the natural case is beneficial and just, and unjust only where the relation isn't natural.

**[3]** That last clause matters, because Aristotle is careful not to identify the natural slave with the actual slave. He admits that plenty of people who are enslaved are slaves only by law and convention, and he criticizes the view that being on the losing side of a war shows you were meant to be a slave. His own conclusion about war captives is basically a contractual one: if a city is defeated and its people accept enslavement in exchange for their lives, the enslavement is just, because it rests on an agreement, and the stronger plainly have whatever capacity makes victory possible in the first place. That's how he can call slavery natural without endorsing every slaveholder in Attica.

**[4]** Which brings me to whether the argument works. I'd say it doesn't, but not for the usual reason. The usual reason — that Aristotle is just rationalizing the society he grew up in — is too quick, since he's willing to call a great deal of existing slavery unjust. The deeper problem is that his argument is empirical all the way down. Natural slavery is supposed to be a claim about what kinds of people there are, discovered by looking at how households and cities actually function, and he's explicit that nature's intentions show up in observable patterns. So if the observation fails, the doctrine fails. And it does fail: there's no reason at all to think the enslaved population of the Greek world differed in foresight or in the capacity to plan from the free population that owned them. Once you grant that, there's no class left for the doctrine to apply to, and Aristotle's account of what makes one person's rule over another just loses its subject matter entirely.

**[5]** There's a second problem that I think is at least as serious, and it's that the argument is circular in a specific way. Aristotle defines a slave as a piece of property belonging to another, then argues that a human being can be property, and then treats the result as a relation between a person and a thing rather than between two people. Whatever else natural slavery is meant to be, it's presented as an arrangement in which one person is used as an instrument by another — and that's exactly the description that needed justifying rather than being assumed. He owes us an argument for why being good at following plans makes you into an instrument, and I don't see one. What he has is a classification that already contains its own conclusion.
