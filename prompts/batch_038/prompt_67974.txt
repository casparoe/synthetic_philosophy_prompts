I am preparing a paper for a social-choice reading group I attend, and I have already settled on the case I want to argue from; what I need is the essay itself, written carefully. The case is drawn from my own building. Two residents share a bathroom: call them A and B. A's personal matter is whether A dyes their hair at home using an ammonia bleach kit; B's personal matter is whether B bathes their dog in the shared tub with a medicated shampoo that smells of sulfur and lingers for days. Take three social states: x (A bleaches, B does not bathe the dog), y (A does not bleach, B bathes the dog), and z (neither does). A ranks z ≻ x ≻ y: A would rather nobody foul the air at all, but if someone is going to, a stink that clears in an hour beats one that hangs around for a week. B ranks x ≻ y ≻ z: B has been lobbying A for months to go platinum and would rather A get on with it than bathe the dog, which B considers a disagreeable chore, yet B would still sooner do the chore than leave the dog's skin infection untreated. Now grant each of them a protected pair in the sense Sen made famous: A is decisive over {x, z} and B is decisive over {y, z}, where decisiveness over a pair means that if the protected individual strictly prefers one member of the pair, the social ranking must place that member above the other. Add the Pareto condition (whenever both individuals strictly prefer one state to another, the social ranking must agree) and the requirement that the social ranking be complete and transitive. My own working-out gives y ≻ z from B's right, z ≻ x from A's right, and x ≻ y from Pareto, which is a cycle — the same logical shape as Sen's original illustration involving a copy of a racy novel and two readers with meddlesome tastes, since B here is strict between two states that differ only in what A does in A's own bathroom. Please check that working-out and correct me if I have misstated any step.

The essay should take up the following controversy and defend a definite verdict on it: is this kind of result best read as showing that one of the three conditions has to go, or as showing that the entire apparatus — building a social ranking of whole states out of individual rankings of whole states — is simply the wrong instrument for representing individual rights? Please work through, in this order: (1) a clean derivation of the contradiction in my profile, stating at each step which condition is doing the work, and an explanation of what structural feature of a social choice rule the contradiction really depends on (in particular, whether anything more than the pairwise conditions plus a demand that the social ranking not cycle is being used); (2) the domain-restriction response, which would rule out preferences like B's as illegitimately meddlesome — noting that in my case both A and B are strict over the other's protected pair, and that unlike the taste for seeing another person read a certain book, each of their interests in the other's choice tracks an effect that lands in their own nostrils, so consider whether that difference makes the restriction more defensible here or exposes it as question-begging; (3) two reinterpretations of what a right is, one that treats a right as securing an option or opportunity rather than as dictating a place in the social ranking, and one that treats rights as constraints on what may be done rather than as inputs to any aggregation at all — for each, say explicitly what happens in my three-state case, who ends up bleaching their hair, and whether that result is compatible with calling the arrangement liberal; and (4) an extension of the profile to the fourth state u in which both A bleaches and B bathes the dog, with preferences of your own choosing, defended by a sentence or two of motivation, together with a specification of rights over features rather than over fixed pairs of complete states; determine whether the contradiction survives that reformulation, and if it survives in a different shape, say what the new shape tells us. Two members of the group have no formal training, so define decisiveness, Pareto, and any other technical term the first time it appears, and do the algebra in words as well as symbols.

Please write in American English, aim for roughly two thousand words, and feel free to use section headings. I do not want a survey that ends by saying reasonable people can disagree: commit to a diagnosis, rank the alternatives you are rejecting in order of how close they come, and close with a short statement of what argument or finding would make you abandon your verdict. Do not include a bibliography, footnotes, or citations of any kind — refer to positions and lines of response descriptively, and I will supply the references myself. Where my setup above is underdetermined or where you think I have built in an assumption that is doing covert work, say so explicitly rather than papering over it.
