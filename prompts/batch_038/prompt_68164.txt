For about fifteen years I have read rationalist blogs and forums closely enough to know the standard shape this particular argument takes in comment threads, and I have watched it die in the same place every time: someone says that utility is defined by choice and therefore explains nothing, someone else says that is a philosopher's gotcha, and the thread dissolves into people restating their priors. Last month two members of a discussion group I belong to — one a health economist, one a philosopher of science — carried the argument considerably further than the threads ever do, first at a meeting and then across several emails I was copied on. I have reconstructed the exchange in their order and with their own emphases; the labels are mine, the moves are theirs. Vera is the philosopher, Marcus the economist. Neither of them argued badly, which is exactly why I cannot settle it.

1. **Vera.** Utility, as it is actually used in economics, is not an independent quantity. On the revealed-preference reading, to say that an agent preferred one bundle to another just is to say that she chose it when the other was available. So the sentence 'she chose the apple because it gave her more utility' is not an explanation of the choice; it is a restatement of it in a noun phrase. No possible behavior could contradict it, since any choice whatsoever is compatible with some preference ordering that ranks the chosen item first. A claim that no observation could count against is not a claim with content, and economists who talk as though utility were doing causal work are trading on a bookkeeping convention.

2. **Marcus.** That mischaracterizes the theory, which never says 'she preferred whatever she chose.' It says something about patterns across choices. If she takes X when Y is affordable, she must not take Y when X is affordable; consistency conditions of that kind — and, in the price-and-quantity setting, the generalized version of them — are genuine, checkable constraints, and real agents violate them. Moreover there is a theorem that tells you precisely when a finite data set of prices and chosen bundles can be rationalized by a well-behaved utility function: given the data, either such a function exists or it does not, and you can determine which without assuming anything about minds. That is a substantive empirical claim, not a tautology.

3. **Vera.** Two replies, and the second is the one I care about. First, the consistency conditions are the part with content, and they are the part that fails: systematic reversals between what people choose and what they price, choice-set effects that shift preferences over the unchanged options, framing effects, and time-inconsistent behavior across delays. Second — and this is the structural point — the standard response to those failures is never to abandon the framework but to enrich the description of the situation, adding a reference point, a frame, a salience weight, or a present-bias parameter, after which the enriched theory accommodates whatever happened. But there is no independent rule for which enrichments are admissible, so the content Marcus located in move 2 gets bought back with flexibility. And deeper still: even when the consistency conditions hold, all they show is that choice is coherent. Coherent choice is not utility-as-a-thing-someone-has. The framework is either falsifiable and not about utility, or about utility and unfalsifiable.

4. **Marcus.** The move from 'always modifiable' to 'therefore empty' proves far too much. Force in Newtonian mechanics is whatever produces the acceleration, on exactly the pattern Vera is complaining about, and yet the framework has obvious content; you can always save the phenomena by positing an unnoticed force, and nobody concludes that the second law is a tautology. What distinguishes a legitimate enrichment from a rescue is whether it is independently motivated and whether it buys predictions that were not there before. Present-bias and reference-dependence both do: they predict specific patterns that had not been observed and were then found — procrastination concentrated on tasks with distant deadlines, valuation gaps between giving up and acquiring, a directional asymmetry in how options are evaluated jointly versus separately. And the enriched models are more constrained, not less: a discounting model with two fixed parameters commits you to an entire schedule of reversals across delays, which you can then test.

5. **Vera.** Grant all of that, because it is not where the problem is. The real issue is what utility is supposed to be. Either it names a psychological state — felt satisfaction, welfare, some hedonic or evaluative quantity — in which case choice data is at best fallible evidence about it, and the revealed-preference program has quietly changed the subject, since people demonstrably choose against their own reported well-being and report doing well while choosing in ways the framework calls irrational. Or it names nothing over and above the choice pattern, in which case statements about utility are conventions doing no explanatory work at all, and the economists who explain behavior by utility are speaking as though they had a mechanism when they have a notation. Neither horn yields utility as something that explains.

6. **Marcus.** There is a third option you keep stepping over. Attributing preferences is an interpretive act, on the model of the intentional stance: ascribing beliefs and desires to a chess program does not posit a mechanism inside it any more than ascribing a center of gravity does, and yet the ascription is the right level at which to describe and predict the system. Utility is the economist's temperature. It is a macro-level quantity, cashed out by a representation that is unique only up to transformations that make no difference — monotone transformations for the ordinal version, positive affine ones for the cardinal — and it earns its keep by compressing and predicting a great deal of behavior. Demanding that it name a felt state is demanding that temperature name a sensation.

7. **Vera.** The analogy fails at the place that matters. Temperature has an instrument that operates independently of the theory: a thermometer's functioning can be understood without assuming the quantity it measures, and that is what makes thermometry measurement rather than redescription. Utility has no such instrument. Choice is the only access, which is precisely the circularity I began with. And the uniqueness point concedes my conclusion rather than answering it: if two different utility functions state the same claim, then the theory is silent about exactly the features people have in mind when they say that one option made her better off.

I was on neither side going in, and I alternately found each of them convincing, so I would rather not be nudged onto one by the framing of my own question.
