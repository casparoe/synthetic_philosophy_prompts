So I'm applying for this one-year interdisciplinary fellowship (philosophy dept, but the panel has two CS people on it, so the proposal can't be purely armchair) and I need a two-page proposal by Friday. My half-formed idea, which my advisor says is "suggestive but currently unstateable": Hofstadter's slogan that below every tangled hierarchy there has to lie an inviolate level — the way Escher's two hands can draw each other only because the paper and the artist sit outside the loop — has been doing rhetorical work for decades without ever being given conditions of application. Nobody has said what it would take for a level to be inviolate, or how you'd tell. Now it looks like engineered systems are counterexamples on their face: I work on agent frameworks, and we routinely build things where the agent rewrites its own system prompt, edits its own retrieval index, or picks which tool (including a fine-tuning tool) to call next, and in the training-time version the model that proposes rewards is itself in the loop with the model being rewarded. So either there's still an inviolate level and it's just not where anyone points (the gradient computation? the loss as implemented in the framework code? the cluster's power budget? whoever reads the output?), or the slogan is false, or — and this is the version I actually find most interesting — inviolability isn't a property of the system at all but of a choice of description, in which case the whole thing collapses into a claim about us and not about loops, and that would be a real result too, just a deflationary one.

Turn this into a proper proposal. I need: the question stated so precisely that someone could tell me whether they'd answered it; why the existing ways of putting it leave it open (Hofstadter's own usage, the levels-of-description stuff in philosophy of mind, and the safety-flavored talk about self-modification and corrigibility, which is nearby but is about control rather than about whether the loop is well-founded); the method, i.e. what I'd actually do month by month, including whether I should try to write down a formal criterion and then run it on three or four concrete cases (a self-reproducing program, an agent that can rewrite its own prompt, the training loop where the reward model and the policy are co-adapting, and maybe Drawing Hands itself treated as a toy model rather than an illustration); and why that approach beats the obvious shortcut of just declaring the hardware level inviolate and calling it a day. Also be honest about the failure conditions: I suspect one is that the criterion comes out trivially satisfied by everything, another is that it's satisfied by nothing, and a third is that it does get non-trivial results but they turn out to be entirely a function of how you individuated the levels, which is the deflationary outcome above and would need to be reported as a finding rather than hidden.
