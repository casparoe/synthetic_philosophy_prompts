Talk is at a law school's constitutional theory workshop in about three weeks and I want the Q&A prep sorted now instead of the night before. Here's the position, roughly.

I argue that entrenched constitutional provisions should carry expiry dates — call it renewable entrenchment. Every provision gets a fixed term, on the order of a generation, after which it lapses unless it's re-ratified by the same supermajority that would be needed to amend it. The theoretical part is where the fight will be. I claim the standard Ulysses-contract picture has the problem backwards: everyone assumes the worry about precommitment is that the present binds the future, the dead hand, Jefferson's letter to Madison, that whole literature. But I think the deeper problem is that a binding that never gets revisited stops being a commitment and degrades into a habit — nobody alive can any longer say what reasons it was answering to, and the provision keeps its legal force long after it has lost whatever made it a commitment rather than just an obstacle. I call this warrant decay. My positive claim is that precommitment should be modeled as a standing offer to your future self that the future self has to periodically accept, not as a standing command, and the mechanism for that is a recognition test: could the original binder, fully informed about how things actually turned out, still endorse this provision? Renewable entrenchment just institutionalizes the recognition test by forcing a moment where the answer has to be given out loud.

I also spend a chunk of the talk on the Ulysses story itself, arguing people read it in a way that matters wrongly: the crew could have untied him. The philosophically interesting case isn't a man binding himself against his own predicted weakness, it's a binding enforced by people who were never party to it and who have to exercise their own judgment about whether to keep enforcing — which is much closer to what a court does with an old text.

Two concessions I make that you should treat as fixed points, not as things to attack: basic-rights provisions don't lapse, they just have to be ceremonially reaffirmed on the same schedule, and the reaffirmation carries no amendment power, only yes or no; and the schedule is staggered so the whole document never comes up at once.

The audience: mostly constitutional law profs, split between originalists and living constitutionalists who disagree about everything except that entrenchment is the point of a constitution. Two political theorists — one basically a Hobbesian about sovereignty who will say a lapse mechanism doesn't limit the sovereign, it just relocates it to whoever controls the re-ratification calender; the other works on democratic legitimacy and is already sold on the dead-hand worry, so she'll think I've either solved a non-problem or oversold the solution. One comparative constitutionalist who studies unamendable eternity clauses and will treat my scheme as a machine for destroying them. One law-and-econ person who'll go straight at the transaction costs of a permanent campaign season and the rent-seeking a known expiry date invites. Plus grad students, a couple of whom will want to know how any of this differs from simply having a very easy amendment rule, which is the comparison I most need to win.
