**Question as set.** Norman Malcolm argued in *Dreaming* (1959) that dreams are not experiences. In 'Are Dreams Experiences?' (*The Philosophical Review*, 1976) Daniel Dennett argued that Malcolm's argument fails to establish that conclusion. Set out Malcolm's argument as clearly as you can. Then explain Dennett's reply, making explicit the distinction he draws between 'Stalinesque' and 'Orwellian' accounts of how the content of an episode gets fixed, and saying what, on Dennett's view, Malcolm has to assume in order for his conclusion to go through. Finally, consider the following case. A subject sleeps in a scanner. A decoder, trained earlier on that same subject's own imaging data, predicts in real time, while the subject is still asleep, which of twenty coarse categories the content of the current dream belongs to — a person, a building, a vehicle, and so forth — at accuracy well above chance. The subject is then woken and reports a dream whose content falls in the predicted category. Does this case settle the dispute between Malcolm and Dennett? Say why or why not. The final part of the question is the one on which the highest marks turn.

**Answer submitted.** Malcolm's argument begins from a question about what could possibly count as evidence for a claim concerning what happened while someone was asleep. His target is the familiar picture on which a dream is an experience occurring during sleep which the dreamer then recalls on waking, and Malcolm thinks this picture cannot be given any content at all. If I say that I dreamt I was being chased along a beach, there is no way of establishing when in the night that experience would have taken place, how long it lasted, or whether anything was going on at the time: the sleeping person is not in a position to observe anything, and there is no independent record against which the recollection could be checked. Malcolm concludes that the dream cannot be an event or an experience lying in the sleeper's past. What the report actually expresses is the impression the speaker has on waking; to say that I dreamt that p is to say that I now have a certain impression of having dreamt that p. The past tense is therefore misleading, since there is no past occurrence to which it refers. This is also why Malcolm holds that dreams have no duration in the ordinary sense. The familiar puzzle of the dream that seemed to last an hour but which must have occupied the seconds before waking simply dissolves, because there was no hour and there were no seconds — there was only an impression.

Dennett's reply is that Malcolm has proved far too much. The argument depends on the assumption that if a dream had genuinely been experienced, the dreamer would have been able to tell at the time that it was happening, and would have carried a faithful record of it forward to the moment of waking. Dennett denies that this assumption is available to Malcolm. He distinguishes two ways in which the content of a conscious episode might be settled. In a Stalinesque model the content is composed before it ever reaches consciousness, so that what the subject later reports has already been edited before it was experienced; in an Orwellian model the episode is experienced in one form and the memory of it is rewritten afterwards. The crucial point is that these two models cannot be told apart, either from the inside or from the outside: the subject's behaviour, their report and their confidence are exactly the same on either account. Dennett's objection is that Malcolm begs the question, since he assumes that the record must be fixed at the time of the experience and then complains that no such fixing can be verified. Once we see that both models are equally consistent with everything we could ever observe, we should say plainly that dreams are experiences, and that Malcolm is simply wrong.

This also explains why the decoding case settles the matter. If a decoder trained on the subject's own scans can predict, while the subject is still asleep, that they are currently dreaming of a person or of a building, and the subject then wakes and reports a person or a building, we have precisely the independent record that Malcolm declared impossible. The content of the dream has been fixed from the outside, at a determinate moment during the night, and it agrees with what the subject goes on to say. Malcolm's argument was that there is nothing here to be right or wrong about; the decoder shows that there is. Whether the dream lasted an hour is then a straightforward empirical question rather than a conceptual one, and Dennett is vindicated.
