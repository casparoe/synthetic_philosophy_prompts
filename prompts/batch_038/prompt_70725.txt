Six weeks ago I became editor of the journal of a learned society of some nine hundred members, mostly historians and archivists, publishing a quarterly whose circulation nobody has ever mistaken for influence. In my third week an associate member published a short piece elsewhere that a great many people found objectionable. Two of its paragraphs circulated as a screenshot; within four days the society's office had received something over fourteen hundred messages, of which perhaps forty came from members, and several of those asked us to expel him, a sanction our constitution does not contain. We declined to comment, then commented, then declined again. The account we eventually published in our own newsletter is now the most widely read thing the society has ever produced, which I find a peculiar fact to sit with. Two members have told me separately, in confidence, that they thought we handled it well but would not have said so publicly.

I am drafting a short policy on how the journal will handle commentary about its members, and, coming to it as a reader of Kierkegaard rather than as a communications professional, I have been reaching for the vocabulary of the 1846 review he wrote immediately after a satirical weekly had made a spectacle of him, together with his later remarks on the crowd. My concern is that I am decorating the document with concepts that do no work. I should like you to help me sort them, and I should like you to be blunt.

Rank the following by how much diagnostic purchase each has on the episode described above: the public; the crowd; the press; chatter; levelling; reflection; anonymity; the single individual. Not by how famous each is, and not by how well each applies to social media in general, but by whether it lets me say something about those fourteen hundred messages, the newsletter's readership, and the two silent members that I could not say without it.

Calibrate the scale as follows. A ten means the concept licenses a claim about the episode that is both true and non-obvious, and that would be lost if I substituted an ordinary word such as rumour, pile-on, or self-censorship. A zero means it is doing no more work than one of those ordinary words. Score each item on that basis in a table, with one sentence of justification, then give me the resulting ranking in three tiers. Score the eight separately even where you think two of them are really one thing, and tell me in those cases whether treating them as one would change either score. Finally, tell me what fraction of the eight I should cut from the policy document altogether: a number, and which ones.

Three hypotheses of mine I want tested rather than indulged. First, that the public and the crowd are not two concepts but two layers of a single career, the first a diagnosis of a communications environment written in the mid-1840s and the second a religious claim from later, and that treating them as interchangeable is precisely how they get used loosely. Second, that levelling is a process done to persons by institutions and customs rather than a property of discourse, so it belongs in a section about how the society is constituted and not in a section about tone. Third, that chatter is the only item on my list that actually predicts anything here, because it concerns the collapse of the distinction between who is speaking and what is being said, and not volume or falsity.

Then two further questions. Which one of these concepts is, in my hands, doing work that properly belongs to a concept I have not listed, and what is that concept? And is there an idea in this material that I am neglecting which would apply to the two members who would not speak publicly?

Lastly, please tell me what is wrong with the whole exercise. I am conscious that I am asking a critique of a Copenhagen satirical weekly and a small quarterly to grade itself against a screenshot pile-on, and that a ranking of this kind may be an act of appropriation rather than of analysis. Say plainly whether it is, and if it is, say what would be a better use of the material than the one I am making of it.

Please write in British English. I am not after references, page numbers, or a reading list; I want your judgement, with the reasoning visible wherever it is contestable. And please do not tell me that Kierkegaard anticipated the internet. I have read that sentence more often than I have read Kierkegaard.
