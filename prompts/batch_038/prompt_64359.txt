I teach a third-year social epistemology seminar — "Testimony, Rumour, and Collective Belief" — with 26 students, and in week 5 I have one 80-minute slot, a teaching assistant, and a room with fixed tiered seating at the front and four movable round tables at the back. So far the students have done conditionalization (roughly half of them can compute a simple posterior without being walked through it) and two heated weeks on peer disagreement, conciliationism versus steadfastness. Nothing yet on herding: the term "information cascade" is new to all of them, and I want it to stay new until the last ten minutes of the session. I know the Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch (1992) model and the Anderson and Holt (1997) laboratory urn experiment well enough, and I do not need them explained to me. What I need is an activity in which the herding happens *to them*, silently, in a form they cannot afterwards explain away as peer pressure or as a desire to fit in. Last year I improvised a rumour-chain demonstration and it collapsed within four minutes because everyone started shouting across the room and the ordering of the sequence disintegrated; I want the design to make that impossible.

Write as someone who has both run observational-learning experiments with undergraduates and taught the philosophy of testimony, and think the design through before writing the materials: start with a short brainstorming section in which you settle and justify the parameters — signal accuracy, players per table, how the state of the world is determined at each table, what the other students are doing while a table plays, and above all how you stop the show-offs from deliberately breaking the sequence to look independent. Then give me the finished activity. The following are the features I want built in, and I will be checking the arithmetic:

- Every player draws a private signal and makes a public prediction, in strict order, with the history of earlier predictions visible to all and no communication of any kind permitted; payoffs are identical across players and nothing is gained by agreeing with anyone.
- Each player records two things on each turn, in this order: what they privately believe the state is, and what they are publicly announcing. The private belief must be committed before the public action, so that the reveal at the end can display a gap between the two rather than a rationalized agreement.
- The Bayes-optimal action must be reachable in under about thirty seconds by a non-quantitative student with no scratch paper — either the parameters make it transparent or the players get a printed key — and following the herd must be *strictly* better in expectation at every position from the third onward. Show me the computation, and also the ex ante probability that a table ends up in a cascade on the wrong state.
- A second act that makes the fragility visceral: after the consensus has hardened, the group learns something small about the provenance of one early announcement (a coin-flipped prediction, an instructed dissenter, or whatever device you judge best), and the herd has to be reconstituted or dissolved in front of them. If your device involves deceiving a participant, tell me so and give me the consent-safe alternative.
- One student, at the end, is handed the complete record of private signals and reports whether the sum of what the table actually knew pointed the other way from what the table said.

Along with the game itself, give me: the exact materials list with quantities per table; the verbatim text of the student instruction sheet, written so that it does not tip them off; a stakes scheme that feels consequential without my distributing money; a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto specific claims about rational herding, and that then bridges back to conciliationism — was the fourth player deferring correctly?; the failure modes I should expect on a first run, with fixes, including the table where no cascade forms at all and the student who leaks their signal by holding the card badly; scaling notes for a class of 14 and a class of 40; and a frank paragraph on which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply verbally. My guess is that the problem is the participants' awareness that I engineered the situation, so that they will reason about my intentions in a way the model's agents never do, but tell me if the deeper failure lies elsewhere. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations beyond the two names above: everything I need to run this on Tuesday should be contained in your answer, and roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words is the right length.
