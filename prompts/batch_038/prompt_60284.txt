my brother brings up easter island at every family dinner as proof that societies inevitably chew themselves up — rats, deforestation, the statues toppling, everyone starving. i want to argue with him using the concept itself rather than trading one fable for another. so take rapa nui and run it through collapse the way its actually used in archaeology: not a moral story about a people failing, but a technical claim about the loss of sociopolitical complexity — shrinking population, monumental building stopping, settlement patterns coming apart, political centralisation dissolving.

then run it the other direction. does the island actually satisfy that description, or is this transformation wearing a collapse costume — statue carving winding down around 1600 and giving way to the birdman competition at orongo, which looks less like a loss of complexity than a rerouting of it, with the same people still farming and still organising island-wide? i want to know which specific features of the record fit which frame, and what the rival explanations do to the verdict: the rat-eaten palm forest, the rock gardens and lithic mulching, and above all the post-contact slave raids and epidemics that the fable conveniently leaves out while still calling the whole thing a self-inflicted collapse. and who exactly is the entity that collapses here, when the population survives and adapts and is still there — plus the uncomfortable point that monumental architecture is basically all that leaves a durable trace, so the end of the moai era might partly be an artifact of the record going quiet rather than of anything going wrong.

exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no wrap-up.
