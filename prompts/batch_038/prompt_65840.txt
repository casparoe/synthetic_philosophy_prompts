I am a third-year doctoral student working on nineteenth-century philosophies of the body, and I run a small monthly reading group with two friends, one of whom is an endocrinologist who now spends a substantial part of her practice prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and tirzepatide. At last month's meeting we fell into a dispute over a counterfactual that neither of us could settle from the texts, and I would like an outside reconstruction done as rigorously as possible. The question is this: suppose Nietzsche were alive at this moment, in full command of his mature philosophy and with a competent grasp of the relevant endocrinology, and confronted with these drugs. My friend the endocrinologist argues that he would have embraced them with almost no reservation; I argue that he would have directed one of his most withering polemics at them. Neither of us will concede, though I suspect each of us has captured one half of his response and is blind to the other half.

The features of the phenomenon that seem to me philosophically interesting are not the ones usually discussed in the press. These agents do not primarily supply knowledge, willpower, or a rule to follow; they alter the phenomenology of appetite. Users commonly report that the running inner commentary of craving — what patients have come to call food noise — goes quiet, sometimes within weeks, and that in many reports the same quieting extends to alcohol, nicotine, nail-biting, compulsive shopping, and other repetitive appetitive behaviour. Some describe this as emancipation from a lifelong tyranny; others describe something more disquieting, as though the drama of temptation had been among the materials out of which their character was built, and its removal left them flattened, less themselves, and with nothing left to overcome. It also matters that the drugs straddle two quite different uses: the therapeutic treatment of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk, and elective administration to people who are not ill at all.

Please reconstruct his considered position, and make the reconstruction do real work rather than gesture at his reputation. I would ask you to argue from his actual diet-and-health doctrine — the late autobiographical writing in which he treats the question of nutrition as more consequential than any of the questions philosophers normally take seriously, sets out a personal regime of coffee, tea, cocoa, meat and meal timing, reports that a single glass of wine or beer a day was enough to make life unbearable for him, and attributes his productive capacity to the management of climate, locality and digestion — rather than from the aphoristic popular Nietzsche. Then confront the ascetic-ideal objection head on: that a pharmacological silencing of appetite has precisely the structure he diagnosed in asceticism, a will turning against its own drives, treating the body's demands as an enemy, and purchasing relief from tension by abolishing the tension a will needs in order to be a will at all. Tell me whether that is a genuine application of his critique or a lazy one. Weigh against it the considerations that cut the other way, since he was himself a relentless self-experimenter with diets, baths, climates, sedatives and opiates, regarded physiology as the coming master discipline, read most philosophical systems as symptoms of a body's condition, and reasoned explicitly that a sounder digestive regime liberated force that would otherwise be spent on suffering. Distinguish at least three cases in which his verdict may well differ: therapeutic use in genuine illness, elective use by the healthy, and the disappearance of felt inner conflict as such, quite apart from any question of weight. Finally, answer the objection my friend raises last and which I have been unable to defeat: that his own obsessive dietary regulation was itself a symptom of declining vitality, so that his philosophy of diet is not a standpoint from which to judge these drugs but a parallel instance of the same pathology, and a consistent Nietzschean ought to reject the weekly injection and the daily regime together. Having done all that, commit — say which half of my suspicion is stronger and by roughly how much, name the single consideration that does the most work in your reconstruction, and list briefly the points at which you are extrapolating beyond anything his texts can support rather than interpreting them. If you have the appetite for it, close with a short paragraph, explicitly marked as your own invention and not as a quotation, of the sort of polemical prose he might have aimed at the phenomenon.
