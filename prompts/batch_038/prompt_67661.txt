> For his first decade in the movement Douglass was a Garrisonian, denouncing the Constitution from the platform as a dirty bargain struck with the slaveholders and repeating Garrison's line about it. Around 1851 he changed his mind, and the reason isn't hard to find: Garrison's abstentionism was useless in practice, and if Douglass wanted to throw his weight behind the new anti-slavery parties he needed a constitutional basis for doing it, so he went and manufactured one. His argument went like this. The document never actually uses the words slave or slavery — it only ever speaks of persons held to service or labor. What the framers privately intended, and what Madison's notes from the convention show, are beside the point, because only the text governs. The preamble states the document's purposes, and securing liberty and establishing justice are among them while slavery is not. And the opening words say we the people, not we the white people. He also argued that the three-fifths clause cut against the slave states, since counting only three fifths of the enslaved reduced their share of representation — a genuinely clever piece of jiu-jitsu. On this reading the Constitution had already abolished slavery at ratification and slaveholding was illegal everywhere from the start, and once Douglass saw that he gave up moral suasion as naive and became a party man: voting, the Free Soilers and then the Republicans, the war, recruiting black soldiers. The whole thing was a political convenience dressed up as interpretation, which is why it's rich when modern originalists name-check Douglass as one of their own. They want his prestige and none of his actual reasoning, which was that the intentions of the people who wrote the document simply don't matter.
