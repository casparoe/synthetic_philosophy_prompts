I sit on a national bioethics commission that has been asked to recommend a statutory regime for third-party reproduction, and I find that my own judgments will not assemble into anything I could defend in a report. I am setting them out as verdicts about what the law ought to permit, in the hope that you can tell me what principle they add up to — or, failing that, which of them I must give up.

1. A couple pays an agency $120,000, of which $25,000 reaches a woman in another country who carries their child for nine months and surrenders him at birth under a contract enforceable in her jurisdiction: should be prohibited.
2. The same arrangement, except that the woman receives nothing beyond her medical care, insurance, documented lost earnings, counseling, and independent legal advice: should be permitted.
3. A woman carries her sister's embryo for nothing, her expenses covered: plainly permitted, and I would describe it as admirable rather than merely allowed.
4. A regulated clinic pays a screened and counseled woman $8,000 for eggs retrieved in one cycle, under a protocol with a firm limit on how often she may repeat it: permitted.
5. The same clinic pays $80,000 to a woman chosen because she graduated from a leading university and has a specified eye color and athletic record: not permitted — and I want to insist that my objection is not to the size of the payment, since $80,000 leaves her better off than $8,000 does.
6. A public program pays a fully informed, medically cleared living kidney donor $40,000: not permitted, even though I concede the prohibition will cost identifiable people years of dialysis and some of them their lives.
7. A Phase I trial pays a healthy volunteer $3,000 for a week of inpatient dosing with real and disclosed risks: permitted.
8. A commercial plasma center pays a donor $60 a visit: permitted, though I find the practice distasteful in a way I have so far failed to articulate.
9. A public program offers a woman receiving subsistence benefits $1,500 for sterilization, or $1,500 in installments for retaining a long-acting contraceptive: not permitted — although she is an adult, the intervention concerns her own body, the medical risk is small, and the money is worth more to her than to any other party to the transaction.
10. A commercial bank sells pasteurized donor breast milk to hospitals at a profit: permitted. A firm buys human hair for wigs: permitted.
11. A for-profit agency arranges a surrogacy in which the surrogate receives only expenses while the agency takes a $40,000 fee and the intended parents pay $90,000 in total: permitted — which I recognize looks perverse beside 1 and 2, since the money that may not reach the woman reaches a company instead.
12. A man is paid $200 per sperm donation with no cap on the number of offspring: permitted, and I would not cap the number, though I am uneasy about saying why.

For context, I know that the near-universal European position is to allow at most the reimbursement of expenses, while several American jurisdictions permit compensated arrangements run by agencies that charge substantial fees. My verdicts were supposed to help me choose between these regimes, and at present they appear to support both simultaneously.

I have already tried two candidate principles and broken each. The first was that the objection concerns exploitation of the poor; but 5 and 9 involve sellers who are better paid or better treated than in cases I permit, and I find them worse rather than better. The second was that the objection concerns treating a person's reproductive capacity as a commodity; but 3 and 10 pass through it untouched, and 11 is precisely the market I would license.

I am asking for three things. First, the principle — or the shortest set of principles — that generates these verdicts, stated precisely enough that a colleague who does not share my intuitions could apply it and see where it commits her, with an identification of the case or cases that force each of its clauses. Second, its verdicts on arrangements I have not listed: a commission national paying a foreign surrogate in a country where the contract is legal, and whether our statute should reach conduct abroad; a bonus paid to a surrogate for not smoking or drinking during the pregnancy, and a penalty clause for refusing a cesarean section or for terminating one of a multiple pregnancy; a payment made for a child conceived by the surrogate with the intended father's sperm, rather than for the gestation as such; direct payment between friends with no agency and no written contract; and payment for ova destined for research rather than reproduction. Third, an honest reckoning of the casualties: if no principle delivers all twelve, name the verdicts the best candidate requires me to surrender, and identify the principle I am implicitly relying on when I try to keep them all. I should add that 2 and 11 seem to be the load-bearing pair — if the distinction between them cannot be sustained, I suspect most of the rest of my scheme goes with it — so please address it explicitly rather than smoothing it over. I am not looking for a survey of who has argued what, nor for titles, names, or references; I want the account itself, in a form I can put in front of people who disagree with me.
