So my reading group has spent the better part of a semester going back and forth on shared agency, and I've volunteered to open next week's session with a case of my own design rather than recycling the usual suspects (rowing crews, painting a house together, the singing duet). I want to put pressure on whether Bratman's conditions on shared intention can be fully satisfied in the absence of anything like Gilbert's joint commitment, and where Tuomela's we-mode/I-mode distinction lands on the same case. Just so we're working from the same glosses: Bratman wants each participant to intend that they J, to intend that they J by way of each of their meshing subplans, mutual responsiveness in intention and action, and common knowledge of all of it, all held in a way that's stable but individually revisable. Gilbert thinks a plural subject exists only when the parties are jointly committed, which is what generates the standing to demand and to rebuke, so that unilateral withdrawal is a wronging rather than a mere change of mind. Tuomela thinks you need the group's perspective as such — the collectivity condition, group reason, commitment to the group — and that private intentions plus mutual belief only gets you the I-mode.

Build me a case with this profile. All of Bratman's conditions are satisfied as he actually states them (not as a strawman, so the interlocking has to be real and the common knowledge has to be real). Gilbert's conditions are not satisfied: nobody has any standing to rebuke anyone, and any one participant can walk away at any point for any reason with no complaint being apt. Tuomela says these people are squarely in the I-mode. Hard constraints: one paragraph for the scenario, 150 words max, no science fiction and no exotic devices, an ordinary small-scale joint activity of the kind these theories are actually about (building something, preparing something, moving something — not two people who happen to walk in the same direction). No boss, no contract, no outside enforcer who could be the source of the rebuke standing, and no pre-existing friendship, family tie, or romance that might be smuggling the commitment in through the back door. Crucially, the no-strings feature has to be generated by the structure of the situation — some norm of the setting, or the way the participants set it up — rather than just asserted in the narration; a case that says they have no standing to rebuke because the story says so is a fail.

Here's what the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I want to see whether the three accounts genuinely come apart, or whether the apparent separation is an artefact of a description that quietly drops one of Bratman's own requirements. So after the scenario, walk each account through it and show which specific condition does or doesn't fire and why. Then give me three things: which single feature of the case is doing all the work, what happens to the verdicts if you strip that feature out (does it collapse into a case where all three agree?), and how a committed Gilbertian would attack the case — I expect the move is to deny that Bratman's conditions can really be met without a joint commitment, so tell me the best version of that reply and whether the case survives it. Format: scenario in its own labelled block first, then a three-row table with columns for account, verdict, and the deciding condition, then the three notes as short bullets. Keep the whole thing under 550 words — I'll be handing it out on paper and it has to fit on one side with room for annotations.
