I am in the third year of a doctoral program in contemporary metaphysics, and four days ago my department asked me to take over two sessions of a history-of-philosophy seminar on mathematical method in the seventeenth century. The first text is Hobbes's De Corpore, and I have been working through the sixth chapter of Part I, the one on method, where he sorts definitions into kinds and then illustrates the sorting with the circle.

I do not have the volume in front of me to transcribe, so here is the argument of the passage in my own words, as close as I can render it. Hobbes says that of definitions some give the cause and generation of the thing defined, while others give only its effects or accidents, and that the first sort alone belongs to science: from a definition that tells you how the thing is generated you can go on to deduce its properties, whereas from a definition that merely lists its accidents you cannot. His illustration is that a circle is properly defined by saying how it comes to be — by carrying a body around while one of its ends stays fixed — rather than by saying that it is a figure all of whose points are equidistant from a center. And he complains that a definition phrased in terms of the motion of a line instead of the motion of a body is defective, because motion belongs to a body, while a line, having no breadth, is not one.

Three things puzzle me, and I would rather not paper over them in front of the seminar. First, does the second complaint do any work beyond the first? If the job of a definition is to license deductions, why should it matter that a geometer's line is not a body, given that geometers have never supposed otherwise? Second, how demanding is the generation requirement — is it a demand that definitions be recipes for actual construction, or only that they be formulated so that a deduction from cause to property is available? Third, I want to understand why Hobbes thinks all this makes geometry the paradigm, indeed on his telling the only genuine, science, and why he nonetheless holds that civil philosophy is demonstrable in the same way while natural philosophy is not. Some help separating what his position requires from what he merely asserts would be very welcome.
