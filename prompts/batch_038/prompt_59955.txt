I am drafting the methods section for a policy-analysis workshop and need to state, with a number attached to it, whether the precautionary principle can be given decision-theoretic content that is both distinctive and defensible. Please give me your credence in the following conjunctive claim.

Call a decision rule *precautionary-eligible* if, for choices among policies whose outcome profiles include low-probability and poorly specified catastrophic harms, it satisfies all three of these:

1. **Non-redundancy.** It is not extensionally equivalent to expected-utility maximization under any single probability function and any utility function: there must be choice problems, fully describable by an ordinary outcome table, on which it and every such maximizer disagree.
2. **Discipline.** It never prescribes a weakly dominated option, and it never produces cyclical or contradictory prescriptions on finite choice sets, including sets that contain the status quo.
3. **Fidelity.** It delivers the verdicts defenders of precaution actually want: extra weight on risks whose magnitude — or whose probability space itself — cannot be pinned down; a tilt toward the option avoiding irreversible harm when the evidence is thin; and the result that thin evidence shifts the burden onto the party proposing the novel activity rather than onto the party opposing it.

The number I want is for the conjunction: that such a rule exists. Decompose it. Give me a probability and a one-line justification for each of the following, plus any further terms you think belong:

- that rules in the ambiguity-averse family satisfy (1) — here I mean rules that represent uncertainty by a set of probability functions rather than one, and evaluate by, say, the worst member of the set, by a weighted average of the worst and the best, or by a non-additive capacity, together with lexicographic and threshold-constrained variants. If those labels are not ones you trust, work with the underlying idea and say so.
- that at least one such family also satisfies (2), including the status-quo cases behind the familiar complaint that precaution ends up forbidding both acting and refraining once each carries a catastrophic tail. Tell me explicitly whether failures of contraction consistency — a rule whose selection from a menu changes when unchosen options are removed — should count against (2) as I have stated it, or whether (2) ought to be read as forbidding cycles only.
- that any rule about *how to rank options*, rather than a rule about evidential standards and burden of proof, can carry clause (3)'s burden-shifting element, which reads more like a claim about who must produce evidence than about how to compare acts.
- the probability that (1) and (3) pull against each other, on the grounds that the further a rule departs from expected-utility machinery, the harder it is to justify its weighting of the catastrophic tail to anyone who does not already share the intuition.

Then combine these into a single figure, say which term the conjunction is most sensitive to, and identify the argument or result that would move your number the most and in which direction.

My own credence is roughly 0.35, driven almost entirely by the third clause: I suspect the burden-shifting element is doing non-decision-theoretic work and that whatever remains is ambiguity aversion wearing a policy costume. Tell me whether you think my discount is misplaced, and if so which part of it.

Two commitments, not a survey. First, take a definite position on whether relocating precaution from the decision rule to the standard of proof satisfies the claim above or concedes that no decision rule can. Second, if your number comes in below 0.5, state which of the three conditions you would delete to yield a claim you would assign above 0.9, and defend the deletion. Do not answer that everything turns on how precaution is formulated: the formulation is the one above, and what I need from you is how much of it can be had at once.
