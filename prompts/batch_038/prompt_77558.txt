You're a decision theorist who's spent years on diachronic rationality — Dutch books, money pumps, dynamic consistency, the lot. I need you to think hard about a dispute I'm stuck in the middle of, and diagnose it rather than referee it.

Setup so you're not guessing. Our reading group has been going around in circles (pun intended, sorry) for about a year over whether the money pump argument actually shows intransitive preferences to be irrational. Everyone knows the toy case: agent with a cyclic ranking over three things, x over y, y over z, z over x, starts out holding y. Someone offers a swap y→x for a dollar and they take it, then z→y for a dollar, then x→z for a dollar, and they end up back where they started minus three dollars, and the same sequence can be run again forever. Side A says that's a reductio, cyclic preferences are irrational, full stop, and the backing for it is that transitivity plus a couple of consistency conditions (something separability-ish, something no-free-lunch-ish) is precisely what blocks the pump. Side B says the pump is an artifact of the setup: it assumes the agent is myopic and takes each trade as it comes, whereas a sophisticated agent who sees the whole sequence coming just declines the opening trade, and a resolute agent commits to a plan and sticks to it, so once you allow foresight or commitment the vulnerability evaporates. Side B adds that the toy case quietly assumes swaps cost something, and that lots of real cycles — job candidates ranked on different dimensions, or wine versus beer versus coffee depending on time of day — look perfectly sane.

There's also a quieter third camp who say both sides overclaim: the pump shows at most that cyclic preferences are bad to have *in a world where you get offered trades*, which is an instrumental or pragmatic complaint, not evidence of a defect in the ordering itself.

Where I'm stuck, and here's my own little contribution that nobody has bitten on yet. I built a version with zero transaction costs and no myopia: swaps are free and instantaneous, the agent knows the whole menu in advance, so the sophisticated and resolute replies get no purchase — but each good depreciates at a fixed rate while the agent holds it, so time passing is the cost. In that world I think the cyclic agent still comes out worse off than a transitive agent facing the identical menu, even planning optimally, because they can't avoid sitting on the rotting thing while waiting for the next swap. If that's right, side B loose its best move. If it's wrong, side A's argument was never about foresight to begin with and I've been attacking a strawman.

So, the ask. My hypothesis — treat it as a hypothesis and tell me flatly if it's wrong — is that this isn't a substantive disagreement about preferences at all, but two things tangled together: (i) the sides are grading different objects, side A is grading the static ordering and side B is grading a whole decision procedure that includes foresight and commitment, and each is right about the thing it's actually grading; and (ii) there's a hidden parameter about the trading environment — are swaps free, are they offered one at a time or all at once, can the agent precommit — that each side silently fixes at whatever value makes its own argument work. On that reading the debate is stalled because nobody has declared which environment they're in, and it still isn't merely verbal, because once you fix the environment the two sides genuinely disagree.

Is that the right diagnosis? Please actually weigh the alternatives rather than wave at them: verbal/terminological, different-objects-of-evaluation, hidden factual or environmental assumption, question-begging (does the pump argument smuggle in something like the transitivity or separability it's meant to establish?), two distinct questions travelling under one label — rationality-of-preference versus rationality-of-choice — ill-posed, or just plain hard. Say which one, or which combination in what proportions, and ground it in the moves each side actually makes as I've described them, not in a survey of who said what.

And tell me straight whether my depreciation case is a genuine third way or a trick — that's the bit I most want pushed on, because if the cyclic agent can plan around depreciation as easily as a transitive one, my whole objection collapses.
