I teach a masters-level module in cognitive science that devotes two sessions to the philosophical afterlife of the incompleteness theorems, and the room reliably splits into camps that never quite engage each other. Some students are certain the theorems refute the possibility of machine intelligence; some that they establish a more modest point about arithmetic truth; some that they license sweeping claims about legal systems, theological systems, or the impossibility of any science validating its own foundations; some that they generalize into a theorem about self-knowledge; and one or two, usually the logicians, that nothing whatever follows outside the theory of arithmetic and that the rest is noise. I want a handout that sorts the students by their commitments rather than by their slogans, and I want it to work as a flow they can walk themselves through on paper.

The deliverable is a branching instrument. It should begin from a single shared starting point that every camp in my class can accept: a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory containing enough arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency, and there is a sentence of that theory which it can neither prove nor refute. From there it should deliver the reader to a position with a name, or to a position you describe because nobody has named it.

Please observe the following constraints.

1. Construct the space first and populate it second. Before any branching, set out in your own terms the apparatus a diagonal argument requires in order to run at all in some further domain: an effectively specifiable relation of derivability in that domain; a means of representing the domain's own claims as objects within it; a predicate expressing that relation; an assumption of consistency, or of something stronger; and a notion of correctness that is not merely coextensive with derivability. The branch points should then ask whether the target domain actually supplies each item, and what the reasoner is entitled to assume about it. No branch may be phrased so that only someone who already knows where it leads can answer it honestly.

2. At least two branch points should ask the student something about their own epistemic standing rather than about any formal system — whether they take themselves to be able to establish their own consistency, whether they take themselves to be sound or merely consistent, whether they could in principle survey and certify everything they accept as deriving from a fixed finite stock of rules. The standard objections to the anti-mechanist argument are supposed to bite exactly here, and I want that to emerge as a consequence of which branch the reader took, not as an aside from me.

3. Treat as distinct branch points, not as one: the claim that the Gödel sentence is true; the claim that we are in a position to see that it is true; the claim that arithmetic truth exceeds arithmetic provability; and the claim that truth exceeds provability tout court. If you think any of these collapse into each other under scrutiny, say which and why on the branch itself.

4. Endpoints must include, at a minimum: anti-mechanism in its bare form, and in the version that bolsters it with an extra physical hypothesis about the brain; Gödel's own disjunctive conclusion, that either the mind is not captured by any finite system of rules or there are number-theoretic problems unsolvable in principle, together with his stated inclination toward one disjunct; the view that the theorems are inert outside arithmetic; the self-validation generalization; a computationalist view that uses self-reference and tangled hierarchies to account for mentality while denying any anti-mechanist upshot; and whatever you take to be the honest state of play on transfers to law, theology, and physics. For that last group, if in your judgment the transfer is not a position anyone competent holds but only a target constructed by its critics, label it as such at the endpoint instead of according it the standing of the others.

5. At every endpoint, state what it commits the reader to: the assumption its holder would least like to be asked to defend, and which single branch point, answered the other way, would have delivered them to a different endpoint. This is what makes the handout usable as a teaching tool, since students then see the alternative as one flip away rather than as a rival tribe.

6. Name the pairs of endpoints your flow fails to tell apart. I expect at least one involving anti-mechanism and the thesis about truth exceeding proof. For each, say whether the failure is a defect you could repair with a better question or a genuine indeterminacy in the debate itself.

7. Identify at least three answer-patterns your branching permits that correspond to no position anyone holds, and diagnose each vacancy: is the combination incoherent, is it coherent but unappealing, or has it simply not been tried? If your diagnosis is the last of these, say what would have to happen for someone to end up there.

Two notes on how I want you to hold yourself in this. First, no neutral tour: where you judge an endpoint to rest on a confusion — a slide from consistency to soundness, a quantifier shift, an illicit transfer of the diagonal construction — say so in a single clause at the endpoint and carry on, rather than describing all positions as equally live options. Second, and more importantly, if my brief is itself confused, say so at the top before doing anything else. I can think of three ways it might be: that there is no single question about what follows outside mathematics and the instrument must instead be indexed domain by domain; that two of my mandated endpoints are the same position wearing different clothes; or that my stipulated starting point already smuggles in something contested, since the unprovability of the negation does more work for the original sentence than for Rosser's replacement. If any of these is right, tell me plainly and propose the fix, rather than complying politely and handing me something I cannot use in front of a room.

Format: an indented outline with numbered nodes (1, 1.1, 1.2, and so on), not a diagram, with endpoints marked LEAF in capitals. No bibliography, no citations, no suggested reading; names of people and positions suffice. Hard limit of 1,300 words for the whole thing, apparatus list and vacancy diagnoses included.
