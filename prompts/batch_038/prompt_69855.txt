My identical twin and I applied for the same job eleven years ago and I got it. Same genes, same house, same schools, same coaches — basically everything you'd want to hold fixed was fixed. Now the firm is giving me some award and they've asked me to read something at the dinner. My brother will be at the table.

I want a sestina: six six-line stanzas with the six end-words cycling in the proper pattern, then a three-line envoi that has to fold all six in. The constraint that matters is this — every end-word should work two ways, as something I did and as something that happened to me. That's the whole argument. The permutation is the point, because each time a word comes back around it should be readable the other way, until there's no word left in the poem that's simply mine.

Get the philosophy right, and I don't mean name-dropping it. The issue isn't that inequality is unfair or that effort doesn't matter. It's what survives once you subtract everything that was luck — including, if you're honest, the disposition to keep showing up, which is the one thing I'd actually want to claim. And underneath that, the harder question: whether what's left after the subtraction is agency at all or just noise with my name on it. The envoi has to answer that out loud in front of him.

Don't let it collapse into either "nobody deserves anything" or "I earned every bit." And don't let it be me performing humility at a dinner where everyone already thinks I'm the successful one — he dosen't have patience for that and neither do I.

After the poem, one last line starting "What's left:" with a single plain-prose sentence saying what the envoi decided.
