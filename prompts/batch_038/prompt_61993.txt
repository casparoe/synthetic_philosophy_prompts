I am in the middle of a novel, and the argument that its second half turns on is one I cannot write until I know it can be written honestly. What I need from you is that argument, in dialogue form, worked out to the point where I can trust it.

The situation in the book. Nadja is a climate scientist in her early forties; her father has just died during an exceptional heatwave in the unnamed country where the novel is set, and three weeks before his death she published an attribution study of exactly that kind of event. The study compares simulations of the actual climate with simulations of the same climate stripped of anthropogenic forcing, and reports how much more likely such a heatwave became. Her interlocutor is Ruairi, a philosopher of science who was her father's oldest friend and who holds that causation just is counterfactual dependence under a suitable similarity ordering, with interventions being at most how we come to know about it. Nadja holds roughly the opposite view: a causal claim is a claim about what would happen to one variable under an intervention on another, and where no intervention is possible even in principle, either the claim is not genuinely causal or it has to be restated in terms of some variable that could be set. The scene is the kitchen table, the night after the funeral, with a printout of her own paper between them.

What makes the scene difficult, and what I want the dialogue to actually chew on, is a second claim that neither of them can dispose of. Nadja also believes — and wants to say aloud, about her father — that if the national power and municipal services had not been privatised in 1991, the cooling shelter in his district would have been open that week and he would have lived. For that claim there is no intervention: not now, not ever, not even in a model anyone could build, because the relevant variable is a settled fact about the past rather than something a system can be set to. Ruairi, for his part, cannot simply bless the claim, because the counterfactual requires holding fixed an enormous amount of history while varying something structural, and Nadja has a precise charge ready: his counterfactuals either backtrack into the causes of privatisation or dissolve into vagueness, whereas her reading — hold everything upstream fixed, intervene, look downstream — is determinate.

So: write the dialogue, roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words. I want each of them trying to extract a specific concession from the other — Nadja wants Ruairi to admit that his similarity ordering cannot by itself deliver a determinate truth value for either of her claims, and Ruairi wants Nadja to admit that the 1991 claim is genuinely causal or else to say what she is doing when she makes it. The issues I want genuinely engaged rather than name-checked include whether running a model without the forcing is an intervention on the world, on the model, or on nothing at all, and what licenses transferring the result back to the world; whether a type-level probabilistic result about a class of events can carry the token-level claim Nadja wants about her father's death, and what either theory can say about probability of causation for a single event; whether "intervention" can be characterised without already presupposing causation, and if not, whether the position is metaphysics or a piece of methodology wearing metaphysics's clothes; and whether making causal truth hostage to what could in principle be manipulated is a defensible constraint or an anthropocentric scandal. Let them each land real blows on these points, and let Ruairi have something sharp to say about interventions that are possible but too crude to isolate what a counterfactual is about.

The rules I care about most. Neither of them may be a strawman, and neither may be less articulate than the other; Ruairi is not a fogey and Nadja is not a technician out of her depth. Every turn must respond to what the other has just said — no parallel speeches, no one waiting for their own next paragraph. No philosophers, book titles, or journal articles may be named, and no term of art may be introduced without one of them making the other explain it, because that is how two people like this actually talk. There should be very little stage business: a gesture or a silence where it earns its place, no description of the kitchen. And the scene must not resolve. I want it to end with both of them still holding their positions but having discovered that the disagreement is not where they thought it was — and I want that discovery to be a specific claim about causation, not a mood.

After the dialogue, give me a note of no more than two hundred and fifty words: which of the two comes out better and by how much, which objections a serious proponent of the losing side would immediately raise that I have compressed or omitted, and any place where a character has been made to say something no sophisticated holder of that view would say. That note is for me, not for the reader, so be blunt about the scaffolding.

Finally, and I mean this: if you think the scene as I have set it up cannot be written well, say so plainly rather than giving me something agreeable and hollow. If my two cases do not in fact discriminate the views I have described, if one of the positions is incoherent as I have stated it, if the token-level claim is simply outside both theories' remit so that the argument would be a category error rather than a disagreement, or if a genuinely even-handed exchange would collapse into agreement by the second page and leave me with nothing to dramatise — tell me which, and tell me what I would have to change in the setup to get a real argument out of it. I would much rather rebuild the chapter than publish a debate in which the reader can tell who the author thinks is right.
