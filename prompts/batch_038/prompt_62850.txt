Here's the setup our prof gave us, then what happened. A small museum near campus has a pen-and-ink drawing that hung for decades as a study by a well-known 17th-century Dutch master. Last year it came out to be by a pupil in the workshop, working from the master's composition as an exercise. Nothing about the sheet itself physically changed. Its insurance value went from about $2.1 million to about $18,000. Visitors who'd seen it before said afterward that it looked exactly the same to them, and a couple said they liked it better now that it wasn't sitting under a "genius" label.

Rae said the price collapse proves the museum was never valuing the drawing aesthetically at all. It was snobbery about origins, full stop — if it looks identical to you and you'd have admired it either way, the attribution to the master was doing nothing except inflating a number.

Theo said that's too quick. The collapse shows there were two kinds of value stacked on one object: the look of the thing, plus its being a historical link to a particular hand at a particular moment. Losing the second one isn't a failure of taste, it's losing something a copy or a pupil's exercise just can't supply. The market was tracking something real.

Rae came back with a thought experiment. Say the museum secretly puts a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the drawing in a vault and never shows it to anybody. Nobody would pay a cent for the vault copy. So the value clearly isn't in the object's properties, it's in the story attached to it — which means anyone who says "the work just is what's in front of your eyes" is caught out.

Theo said the vault case begs the question. The duplicate isn't a drawing with a bad provenance, it's a thing with no provenance at all, and provenance is a historical relation you can't fake your way into: "made by this man in 1658" isn't a property the sheet could lose and still be the same object in the relevant sense. What we're valuing is an achievement — somebody pulling that off, then, with those materials, under those constraints — and a copy, even a flawless one, doesn't display an achievement of that kind.

Rae said fine, but then it's deception doing the work, not origin. An openly sold facsimile involves no deception either, and it still goes for about one percent of the price. So the achievement story doesn't explain the number.

Theo said open facsimiles are cheap because they're mass-producible, and mass-producibility is exactly what defeats the achievement claim, so the number is perfectly consistent with his view.
