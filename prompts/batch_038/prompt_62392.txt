I cover science and ideas for a magazine with a philosophically literate readership, and I have been commissioned to write a long feature on the question of when an experience happens. The centrepiece is going to be the colour phi phenomenon and the argument it sparked, and my difficulty as a reporter is that I can no longer tell what the argument is actually about.

The facts as I have them. Two small spots of different colours are flashed at different places on a screen, the second within a few tens of milliseconds of the first. At certain combinations of spacing and interval, subjects report not two flashes but a single spot travelling between the two positions — and they report it changing colour partway along the path. The change is therefore reported as occurring at a time when, on any straightforward account, nothing carrying the new colour had yet been detected. Paul Kolers and Michael von Grünau found the effect in the 1970s while chasing a question put to them by Nelson Goodman; Daniel Dennett turned it into a philosophical instrument in Consciousness Explained, and then, with Marcel Kinsbourne, gave the field the two labels everyone has been arguing with ever since. On the 'Orwellian' model, the subject has experiences in the ordinary order — first spot, then second — but the record is revised afterwards, so that what is reported is a mid-path colour change that never happened as reported. On the 'Stalinesque' model, the sequence is edited before it is ever presented: the experience of motion-with-a-midway-change is composed offline and delivered as a package, so nothing is tampered with later. Dennett and Kinsbourne's striking claim was that the two models make exactly the same predictions about anything the subject could say, and that this is no accident — there is no determinate fact about when the experience happened for either model to get wrong.

The critics have been saying versions of the same handful of things for three decades: that the indeterminacy claim quietly presupposes Dennett's own account of how reports are produced; that Orwellian revision is independently implausible because the trace of the first colour survives and can be probed by other means; that representationalist theories of experience give the timestamp a home without requiring any inner event to bear one; and that vision science has since accumulated postdictive effects, flash-lag and its relatives, that look like evidence for one model over the other. Dennett's side replies that every such probe is itself another report, generated by the same machinery, and so inherits the same indeterminacy. Nobody moves. Each camp can predict the other's next paragraph word for word.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that the stalemate is not a disagreement about the brain, nor even really about consciousness, but about the logical type of the word 'when': one side is asking for the clock time of a vehicle, the other for the time at which an experience represents things as happening, and these are not rival answers because they are not answers to the same question. A subsidiary guess is that the political metaphors are doing unearned work. Orwell and Stalin both involve tampering with a record against which the original can in principle be checked, and the disanalogy — that experience has no such independent record — is exactly what the debate keeps failing to name.

I would also like you to deal with a concrete proposal. A vision scientist I spoke to suggested a variant in which subjects do not report retrospectively but rotate a continuous colour dial while watching the display, with dial position sampled at millisecond resolution. The claim is that if the dial crosses from the first colour to the second before the second spot's physical onset, that is an online report which cannot have been retro-fitted, and the Orwellian model is ruled out. I want to know whether that experiment would do the work its proposer thinks it would, whether the Dennett camp's obvious reply is a good reply or an evasion, and — this is the part I care about most — what the answer tells us about the diagnosis. If the variant settles nothing, does that show the question is ill-posed, or only that this particular operationalisation was too crude?

Please write in British English. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and paraphrase rather than quoting any author: I will check quotations against sources myself, so anything you put in quotation marks is a liability. I want the reasoning laid out so that an intelligent non-specialist can follow it, but do not soften the argument to get there.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order, with these headings and roughly these lengths:

1. 'The question being disputed' — about 200 words. Separate the candidate formulations of what the two sides disagree about, and say which formulations are genuinely rival.
2. 'What each side is committed to' — about 450 words. Reconstruct both positions from what their proponents actually argue, and identify the load-bearing assumption on each side, including any assumption both share.
3. 'Diagnosis' — about 500 words. Say what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive, a hidden empirical question, two different questions under one name, or an ill-posed one.
