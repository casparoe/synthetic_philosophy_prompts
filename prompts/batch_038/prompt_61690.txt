so here's the problem my book has turned into. i'm a novelist, and my last two books had one character each, but this one has two I've made deliberately impossible to tell apart — same age, same job, same dead mother, same chipped front tooth — and I've been through the ms with a fine tooth comb to make sure there isn't a single sentence true of one of them and not the other. It began as a stunt. Now half my readers think there's one character narrated twice, my editor keeps writing "which one is this???" in the margins, and the honest answer is that the book doesn't know either.

There's a chapter near the end — the one where they finally sit down in an empty room and one of them tries to prove to the other that they're two seperate things — and I want a sestina for the epigraph. Full 39 lines, standard rotation of the end-words (6-1-5-2-4-3 and so on), but here's the trick, and it's the whole reason I'm asking for a sestina and not something looser: use only five distinct end-words. One of them gets doubled, holding two of the six slots from the first stanza onward, so the pattern keeps turning but there's a word in the poem twice that you can only ever tell apart by where it's standing. Put the two copies in slots 1 and 4 to start, as far apart as the stanza allows, then let the rotation do what it does — they land on back-to-back lines in the third and fourth stanzas, and in the envoi they both fall in the middle line, so the poem ends with the same word twice in one breath. I want the stanza where they're touching to be where the speaker comes apart, and the envoi to not fix it.

The argument the poem has to make, without ever naming it: that "the other one is over there" doesn't tell you which is which when there's nothing else in the room to be over there from; that the only thing left to fall back on is a bare this-one-here that won't fit inside any sentence you could actually write down; and that the speaker genuinely can't tell whether getting hold of that is a victory or an admission. If you know the two-spheres thought experiment — empty symmetrical universe, two exactly similar iron spheres, the whole Leibniz-versus-everyone thing — that's the territory, but no philosopher's name, no "indiscernible," no "haecceity," no archaisms, no riddling. Plain contemporary register, lines loose rather than metronomic, roughly 8-12 syllables, and the end-words should be ordinary words a person would say in a kitchen.

I'd pick here as the doubled word — a word that's supposed to point at a place and can't say which one — but if you find something better, use it. Someone who knows the thought experiment should be able to point at each stanza and say what work it's doing; someone who doesn't should just find a person losing their nerve in an empty room. And the credit line under the epigraph is going to be a dash, obviously, because neither of them would admit to writing it and either of them could have.
