Hey — I'm in an epistemology working group and we've all been asked to hand in a one-pager on where we actually stand before the session, so people can attack the position instead of a paper. This is my first time doing it and it's probably a mess. Here's roughly what I believe about assertion. (1) Assertion has a constitutive norm and that norm is knowledge: assert p only if you know p. What sells me on it is the practice of pushback — "how do you know?" is the natural challenge, and a flat-out assertion seems to present itself as knowledge. (2) The norm is strict, not a matter of degree: the Gettiered speaker and the guy who says "your ticket lost" on the odds both violate it; they're excused, not permitted, and I don't want to trade that in for a justified-belief norm just to make those cases come out pretty. (3) But I'm also pretty belief-first about the mind: assertion is belief made public under a convention, and I'd like the norms of assertion to be inherited from the norms of belief rather than being a free-standing social rule bolted on from outside. (4) The norm on belief is truth — you ought to believe p only if p — with rationality governing whether you're criticizable for it; I'm a fallibilist, so I think there's a lot of rational belief that isn't knowledge. (5) On testimony I lean assurance-side: being told gives the hearer a defeasible entitlement to believe, and I think that entitlement is grounded in the knowledge rule, so uptake isn't gullibility. (6) I also think practical stakes change what you may say out loud — same evidence, and the oncologist may flat-out assert the diagnosis at a conference but shouldn't do it at the bedside — and I want to explain that by stakes changing what she knows, not by adding a seperate pragmatic norm. (7) Methodologically I'd rather keep one norm and revise some case verdicts than index the norm itself to contexts.

Now here's the case I can't make work. Doctor A has excellent but not conclusive evidence and tells colleague B over lunch that it's lymphoma; stakes for B are low, he's just prepping a journal club. B thereby comes to know it's lymphoma, I think — good testimony, low stakes, nothing weird. B then relays it to the patient's family, for whom it's about as high-stakes as it gets. My gut says B's relay is wrong, that he should have hedged or sent them back to A, but (6) as I've stated it indexes to the *speaker's* stakes, B's stakes are low, B knows, so (1) says B may assert, and (5) says the family was entitled to believe him. I can't find the lever, and I don't want to invent a new audience-relative norm because of (7). Separately: a friend tells me (2) is already dead on arrival given (3) and (4) — if assertion just expresses belief and belief's norm is truth-plus-rationality, then the assertability norm can't be stricter than the belief norm, so lottery assertions come out permissible. That looks right to me but I suspect I'm missing a move. Meanwhile the thing that actually keeps me up is whether (4)'s fallibilism makes (1) absurdly demanding, i.e. whether I'm commited to saying most ordinary conversation is impermissible, which I definitely don't want to say.

So: audit the set. Tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — I want the apparent ones explicitly called out as dissolving, with the reason, not lumped in with the real problems. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each give the cheapest repair plus what that repair costs me elsewhere (I suspect every fix here trades something away in (5) or (7)). Then tell me whether there is any lever that preserves the B-relay verdict without an audience-indexed norm, or whether I should just give up the verdict. Don't lay out a menu and leave me to choose — commit to a position and give your reasons, including where you think my friend's argument against (2) goes through and where it doesn't. Last line, and nothing after it, should be exactly: Verdict: <the number of the single commitment I should drop or weaken, and one phrase saying what replaces it>.
