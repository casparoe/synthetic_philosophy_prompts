im giving a 45-minute department colloquium in about three weeks and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The talk develops David Lewis's short paper "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" — the one that opens on an offender justly sentenced whose devoted, innocent friend volunteers to take the punishment in his place, and then argues that our modern revulsion at that idea is local rather than deep: in an honor-and-debt register a wrong creates a debt that third parties can discharge, so the substitution intuition never died, it just got exiled from criminal law.

My extension is what the room will actually be shooting at, so here it is:
- the real work isn't done by the debt analogy, it's done by a normative power: whoever the wrong is owed to has standing to accept a substitute's performance in satisfaction of it;
- so penal substitution isn't a violation of justice, it's a transaction the creditor is entitled to authorize;
- two conditions make it legitimate rather than grotesque: the substitute takes it on freely, and the offender accepts it and identifies with it instead of just cashing out;
- which is supposed to predict our intuitions: what repels us is the unwilling substitute or the smug free-rider, not the transfer as such;
- and it makes punishment continuous with fines, restitution, community service and plea bargains, since money is fungible and anybody can pay someone's fine — retributivists are already committed to some transferability whether they like it or not.

The room: mostly normative ethics and philosophy of law, and nobody has read Lewis except the person who invited me. Two punishment people — one a retributivist who thinks deserved suffering is personal and non-fungible, one a communicativist who thinks punishment is the polity's censure addressed to the offender and can't be redirected. One medievalist who works on Anselm's satisfaction account and will hear theology in every sentence I say. A couple of criminal-law types who care about plea bargains, fines, and the fact that rich defendants already buy their way out. Plus a metaethicist who will want to know what a 'normative power' is and whether im smuggling one in.

Give me the eight-ish objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it in the room, with a two-minute reply and a line on where the reply is softest. Then tell me what to concede in the opening five minutes to buy goodwill, which single reply has to land or the talk dies, and which objection — if it lands — would show the thesis is false rather than merely undefended.

One thing I want your honest read on: I suspect my biggest exposure is a slide from Lewis's descriptive claim (the intuition survives inside honor practices) to my normative claim (the polity has standing to accept a substitute), on the grounds that honor codes arent moral codes, so making substitution intelligible inside a defunct normative register proves nothing about justice. Is that really the soft spot, or am I wrong about where I'm weakest? And finish with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
