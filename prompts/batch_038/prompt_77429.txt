Taking a methods seminar this term on how archaeologists are supposed to know anything, and for next week each of us has to write our actual inferential commitments on one page so the rest of the group can attack them. I've done two seasons of fieldwork (later prehistoric settlement and cemetery stuff in the Danube basin) and honestly most of my views came out of the trench rather than out of the reading, so I want to check the page is internally coherent before I hand it in. Here's what I've got:

1. Analogy is only legitimate where I can show actual continuity between the historic/ethnographic case and the archaeological one — same region, same material tradition, a traceable chain. I don't buy "people are people everywhere, so what's true of a modern pastoralist is true of a Bronze Age one." If you can't demonstrate the link, you don't get to use the analogy.

2. We can reconstruct what people did, not what they thought. I'm very wary of any claim about cosmology, ritual, or "meaning," because that's usually just the archaeologist's own head projected onto a pot.

3. Variation in burial investment within a single cemetery is the best evidence we have for social ranking, and I'm willing to say a given community had hereditary status differences on that basis.

4. I take the site-formation stuff seriously: deposits are palimpsests, a "house floor" can be two centuries of repeated activity, and things move — bioturbation, trampling, roots, later pits cut through earlier ones. So I don't think you can read a single event off a single context, and I'm suspicious of any interpretation that leans on two objects happening to be found next to each other.

5. The natural processes are uniformitarian and that's what makes inference possible at all: sedimentation, decay, use-wear, firing temperatures, the way flint fractures. That's why I'm fairly bullish on experimental replication and use-wear/residue work — if I can produce the same polish on an edge by cutting cereals, then that polish on an ancient edge means cereal cutting.

6. I do take the post-processual point that interpretation is never neutral, that the questions we ask are shaped by our own politics, and that a single assemblage can sustain more than one reading. But I still want to be able to say that some reconstructions are flatly worse than others and that disputes get settled.

7. Where scientific dating and typological seriation disagree, I trust the scientific dating. Seriation assumes a regular pattern of stylistic change, which is basically assuming the thing you're trying to establish.

8. Concretely, and this one I feel confident about: the pit we found sealed under a courtyard surface, with three complete upright vessels, a cattle skull and a dump of burnt grain — I'm happy calling that a deliberate deposit made in one go when the courtyard was laid, i.e. something to do with founding the space. To me that's just describing an observable arrangement, not a claim about anyone's beliefs.

So — audit this. Which of these pull against each other, and how badly? I want them ranked, worst first, and for each one tell me what has to give and what the cheapest revision would be, plus whether the revision costs me something I actually care about. Also please tell me straight which pairs only *look* like a problem and dissolve once you spell them out, because I've got a feeling I'm about to be told everything is inconsistent and that would be useless. I'm not going to hint at which commitment I'd drop, I genuinely dont know, that's kind of the point of the exercise. If there's one single change that would clean up the most damage across the whole list, say what it is and what I'd lose by making it.
