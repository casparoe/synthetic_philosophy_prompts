I spent thirty-eight years in analytical chemistry — mostly trace metals, some method development — and retired three years ago, and since then I've been trying to repair my education, which amounts mostly to reading philosophy badly and slowly. Lately I've been stuck on Plato and alcohol, of all things, which you'd think I'd have a professional opinion about. The trouble is he seems to say at least three incompatible things. In the *Laws*, early in Book I, the Athenian runs a thought experiment I found genuinely startling: imagine a potion that produced fear instead of courage — you could hand a citizen a small measured dose, watch what he does, and thereby assay his character cheaply and safely, the way you test gold in fire or break in a spirited young horse. No such potion exists, he says, but wine does exist and does roughly the opposite, making men bolder and louder and puffed up with the conceit that they're wise and happy — so wine can be used as a controlled instrument, provided there is a sober leader over the drinkers, rules about who may drink and when (nothing for the very young, restrictions on young men, freer rein only after forty), and a civic purpose. Later in Book II he even calls wine a gift of Dionysus, a remedy for the sourness of old age, and he treats education itself as the correct tuning of pleasure and pain in the soul before reason arrives — which is presumably why a substance that throws that tuning out of kilter can also be used to test it. Then you read the *Symposium* and get a totally different picture, where the drinking is called off in favor of talking and Socrates drinks everyone under the table and is the only coherent person at dawn, and then the *Republic*, where drink and dreams are how the lawless beast inside us slips its chain.

Here's what I want to do with this. My wife and I watched the 1966 Nichols film of Albee's *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?* last week — we'd both seen the play years ago and remembered it only as a shouting match — and halfway through it struck me that George is running an experiment. He appoints himself master of the revels, he sets the rules, he announces the games (humiliate the host, get the guests, hump the hostess), and he administers doses: a story about a boy who shot his mother, a story about a mousie and a false pregnancy, a telegram about a dead son. He is not using wine to produce the dread — the bottle is just the medium, everybody is drinking all night from 2:30 until dawn — he is using narrative, which makes me think he's stumbled on Plato's hypothetical fear-potion instead. And then the thing goes out of control, because George is not actually sober, and because his two test subjects, Nick (a young biologist, which I admit made me prick my ears up) and Honey, are exactly the sort of young guests Plato says you have no business dosing at all.

So: acting as a classicist who also does philosophy of literature — someone equally at home in the *Laws* and in mid-century American drama — give me a real critical reading of the play and film through Plato on drunkenness. Think carefully and start with a short brainstorm before you write anything polished: list the ways the fit might work, the ways it obviously won't, and the two or three scenes where the comparison does the most work, then develop it. I want you to take a position on these, not just inventory them. (1) Is George a Platonic symposiarch, a failed one, or something Plato has no category for? Note that the *Laws* insists the leader be sober and accepted by the drinkers, and that in this house the host is the one being humiliated. (2) Does the play endorse the revelation thesis — drink strips away the surfaces and shows you what a person is — or does it show the opposite, that intoxication manufactures the very things it claims to disclose? Consider that the biggest disclosure of the night, the son, is a fiction that the couple has been jointly maintaining, and that George "kills" it precisely by announcing it. (3) Which Plato is the play running on: the civic pharmacologist of the *Laws*, the ironist of the *Symposium*, or the man who thinks drink unleashes the beast? (4) What about the ending — George and Martha holding hands and singing their silly song at dawn. Plato thinks some illusions are load-bearing for a soul that was trained correctly; Albee's couple seem to be better off with one illusion destroyed and no new one in its place. Is that a refutation of Plato, or just a different case? And be an honest critic in both directions: tell me plainly where I'm forcing the comparison. Plato is legislating for a colony and wine for him is a public institution for grown men; Albee's night is private, marital, and hostile, and it may be about illusion and cruelty rather than about alcohol at all. Don't quote Plato or Albee verbatim — I'm not checking your Greek and paraphrase is fine — and don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list, I can't chase those down and I'd rather you spent the words on the argument. Finish by telling me what the play shows about drinking and character that a philosopher arguing about the *Laws* at a seminar table could never get across.
