I am a software engineer. I have spent fifteen years building and maintaining long-lived distributed systems, and I have no training in philosophy, economics, or history. I would like you to translate a debate I keep encountering secondhand into the vocabulary I actually work in, and then tell me honestly what did not survive the translation.

The debate concerns path dependence: whether large-scale outcomes — which technical standard comes to dominate, how an industry is organized, where an economy concentrates — are locked in by contingent early events, or whether the same outcomes would have arrived anyway under the pressure of the underlying problem. The stock illustration is the QWERTY keyboard. One side argues that a layout which was not the best available won an early contest and then became impossible to dislodge, because everyone's training, tooling, and hardware came to assume it. The other side argues that the evidence for the rival layout's superiority was produced largely by its own advocates under conditions no one would accept in a benchmark suite, and that the winning layout was never demonstrably worse. I have also read that the disagreement is commonly sorted into three degrees: cases where history matters but the outcome is the best available one regardless; cases where the outcome looks regrettable in hindsight but was the right call given what could have been known at the time; and cases where the outcome is genuinely inferior and was recognizably so, so that someone could have done better and did not.

Rather than the keyboard, please use my own situation as the running example. I maintain an internal serialization format and wire protocol in use across roughly two hundred services at my company. Its field names, ordering, and error semantics were chosen quickly, in 2011, by three people working against a launch deadline; nothing about them is clever. Every serious attempt to migrate to a cleaner successor has failed — not for want of agreement that the successor is cleaner, but because the cost of coordinating two hundred independent owners exceeds any single team's budget, and because every partial migration produces an interval in which three versions coexist in production. Meanwhile, a team in another division designed a format for a similar problem in 2016 and converged, unprompted, on something structurally almost identical to ours. My colleagues cite that as evidence we would have ended up here no matter how 2011 went.

First, restate the path-dependence debate in my terms: legacy code, backwards compatibility, deprecation policy, migration cost, ABI stability, forks and rewrites, switching costs, coordination failure across many owners, and the tendency of independent implementations of the same constrained problem to converge on the same shape. Be as faithful as you can, and be explicit about which of my terms you are treating as load-bearing equivalents for which of theirs — increasing returns, lock-in, equilibrium, efficiency, contingency, counterfactual.

Second, and this is the part I care about most, give me the residue report. Sort the components of the debate into three buckets: claims that translate cleanly; claims that translate only with distortion, in which case state precisely what the distortion consists in; and claims that have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. For the third bucket I want an argument, not merely a list.

Third, tell me whether the residue marks a defect in my vocabulary — something about history that engineering has no way of expressing and arguably should — or a sign that the economists' and historians' disagreement is less substantive than it sounds. I want your view on four places where I suspect the translation is doing something underhanded. (a) My vocabulary presumes an owner who can decide to absorb a migration cost, whereas "locked in" applied to an entire economy describes a situation with no decision-maker anywhere in it. (b) My vocabulary presumes a measurable objective — latency, incident count, developer throughput — against which "inferior" is well defined; I cannot tell whether the path-dependence literature has an equivalent criterion or is smuggling one in. (c) For me, "would we have arrived here anyway?" is an empirical question, because I can actually run the fork and benchmark the rival design; for the historian, the counterfactual can never be executed. (d) My colleagues' convergence argument looks to me like exactly the evidence the QWERTY skeptics say is missing, and I cannot tell whether that means the two disputes are really the same dispute.

Please do not import my framework's assumptions and then present them as results: if a translation only works because I have quietly supplied an owner, a specification, a benchmark, or a version history, say so explicitly. Do not supply reading lists, references, or attributions. My working hypothesis, which I expect is too glib somewhere, is that path dependence is technical debt at civilizational scale and that the whole controversy reduces to whether the benchmark for "worse" can be measured. Tell me where that is correct, and where it is missing something I cannot see from inside my own discipline.
