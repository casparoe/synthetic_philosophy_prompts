Next term I run week eight of a second-year interdisciplinary seminar — eighty minutes, whiteboard only, students mostly public health and psychology. Two earlier weeks have left them with what they experience as a flat contradiction: one lecturer presented moral judgment as having a structure that develops everywhere, another presented the cross-cultural vignette literature as showing the content of judgment varying far more than the first implied. Half the class has concluded that morality is local custom; the other half has concluded the variation is overplayed and there is a shared core underneath. What I want them to leave with is narrower and harder: which patterns of cross-cultural variation in moral judgment would bear on whether there are moral facts, and what premises the step from people disagree to there is no fact of the matter actually requires.

Plan the session rather than explaining the material to me. I need:

- The order the pieces must go in, with the dependence relations made explicit — where the distinction between a difference in moral principle and a difference in the non-moral beliefs feeding that same principle has to land, and what must already be in place before the disagreement argument can be stated rather than merely felt. I also want the non-moral comparison case (a domain they don't think is up for grabs, where cultures have disagreed) positioned so it does real work and doesn't read as a cheap gotcha.
- The single case that should carry the eighty minutes, and what it must have and must conspicuously lack. It has to be one where their own first reaction is a confident blame judgment, where the same principle plus different background facts could produce the divergent verdict, and where we cannot simply ask the participants which principle they applied. I suspect anything built on food taboos or sexual purity will derail us into arguing about the practice instead of the inference, but tell me if you disagree and why.
- The misconceptions they will arrive with and the ones they will form on the spot, each mapped to the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off. I expect different cultures therefore no right answer, and I expect its mirror image: the principle/belief distinction deployed to dissolve every disagreement until the data stops meaning anything. I also expect universality gets treated as evidence of objectivity, which I think is a non sequitur but can't yet articulate why in one sentence.
- A closing task, five minutes, on paper, that would show me whether they can apply the distinction to a fresh pair of cases rather than recite it.
- What I should refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud to the class.

No reading list, no citations, no named studies — I want the plan and the reasoning behind it, not the sources.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the ordering you propose, and single out the one step where you would bet least: which dependence claim, or which predicted misconception, you are least sure of, and what would show it wrong.
