I teach a small graduate seminar — twelve students, mostly from philosophy, with two from the film studies department — that spends its first half on the paradox of knowability: the argument that moves from the verificationist thesis that every truth is possibly known, together with the factivity of knowledge and its distribution over conjunction, to the conclusion that every truth is in fact known. My students can run the derivation cold, and they have already worked through most of the familiar replies — restricting the knowability principle to some decidable or otherwise unproblematic subclass of truths, adding a situation or a time parameter to the knowledge operator, abandoning the principle altogether. What none of them has done is think about what an unknowable truth looks like as a state of affairs rather than as a formula, which is why the final session is a screening of Citizen Kane followed by a discussion I need to prepare for now. My worry is that the conversation will drift either toward the film's formal innovations or toward a hazy theme about the impossibility of really knowing another person, and I want to hold it to something sharper.

So: give me a reading of Citizen Kane through Fitch's paradox, treating the film as a case study in the metaphysics of unknowable truths rather than as a pretty illustration of them. The obvious hook is the ending. Thompson's assignment, given to him by the newsreel producer, is precisely to establish the meaning of a dying man's last word; the film resolves that question only for the audience, and it does so by destroying the last physical evidence — the reporters leave Xanadu, the workmen begin sorting Kane's accumulated crates and statuary, and the sled goes into the furnace, where the lettering on it burns. I would like the reading to do four things. First, determine which epistemic category the Rosebud truth actually belongs to, since the film seems to slide among at least three: a truth no one ever knew, a truth known by someone (Kane certainly, and conceivably others present in his Colorado childhood) and then lost when the knower dies and the object is burned, and a truth that is recoverable in the sense that it is shown to us. Second, take the temporal structure this creates seriously for the knowability principle: if the proposition that Rosebud names the sled was knowable, indeed known, at one time, and is such that no one can come to know it at a later time, is knowability a permanent feature of a proposition or a transient one, and does the anti-realist have a principled way to say which? Third, deal with the awkward fact that we know what no character knows — can a work that hands the truth to its audience supply a genuine instance of the unknown-and-unknowable, or must fictional truths be relativized to the inhabitants of the story for the paradox to bite at all? Fourth, ask whether the film's own epistemology is verificationist. The newsreel and the chain of testimonies treat the significance of a life as fixed by publicly available traces and by what witnesses will say, while Thompson's parting judgment — that one word would not have explained anything, that Rosebud is only a piece he is missing — reads like a rejection of exactly that treatment. Is Welles making a claim about the explanatory poverty of a truth even once it is obtained, and how does that claim differ from a claim about its inaccessibility?

Point me to the scenes you would actually use in class, in order, and say for each what argumentative work it does. Be candid where the reading strains: the film is also about class, about American self-invention, about the failure of biography as a genre, and if the epistemic-logic framing illuminates the last reel while distorting everything before it, I would much rather learn that now than in front of the students. Please do not build the case on quoted dialogue — I am working from the disc, my students have not yet seen the picture, so describe shots and scenes in your own words rather than reproducing lines — and do not append a bibliography or a list of further readings, since what I want is the criticism itself. Close by telling me what the film contributes to the knowability debate that the derivation and its standard replies cannot: something about what it is like for evidence to be destroyed, perhaps, or about the felt difference between a truth nobody happens to know and a truth nobody could ever come to know. If your honest assessment is that it contributes very little, say that, and say why.
