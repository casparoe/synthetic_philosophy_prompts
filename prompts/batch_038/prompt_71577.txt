I am in my final year of high school, and next month I will defend a position statement on the Anthropocene at our school's humanities symposium: three teachers and two visiting academics on the panel, fifteen minutes of questions after a ten-minute presentation. My supervisor has asked me to write out my commitments in advance, in plain form, so that the panel can attack the package as a whole rather than pick off isolated sentences. Doing so has left me uneasy, because I can no longer tell which of these belong together. What I want from you is an audit, not an introduction to the debate — I have read enough to state a position; what I lack is any sense of where it breaks.

Here is what I hold.

(a) The Anthropocene is real. Human activity has become a force on the Earth system comparable in kind to the processes geology has always used to divide deep time, and that is a fact about the world rather than a rhetorical device.

(b) The right way to fix the start of a geological period is the geologists' way: a boundary must be anchored to a physical signal that is globally synchronous and preserved in strata, and marked at a single reference section. Motives, meanings, and moral judgments have no standing in that determination. I hold this even knowing that the mid-twentieth-century proposal was voted down a few years ago; the rejection does not, for me, show that the method was wrong.

(c) On that method, the strongest candidate is around 1950, because of the artificial radionuclides scattered worldwide by atmospheric weapons testing, along with plastics, concrete, and the abrupt postwar climb in essentially every measurable indicator of Earth-system functioning.

(d) Nevertheless, in the essay I am defending, when I am asked when the Anthropocene began, I answer 1492 — because what defines the epoch is a way of seizing land and labor that was then exported across the planet, and because starting the story in 1950 lets the particular people responsible dissolve into an undifferentiated humanity.

(e) I hold that periodization is never merely technical. Whoever fixes the boundary decides who counts as the agent of the epoch, and that decision ought to be answerable to the communities who inherit its consequences, not only to a committee of specialists.

(f) I also think the name itself should be kept. I oppose replacing it with a term that identifies one particular economic system, on the grounds that a name which assigns blame to a system will be refused by half the world and will therefore change nothing.

(g) I am not a pluralist about the question. There is one date at which the Anthropocene began and my job is to identify it; the suggestion that different dates are answers to different questions strikes me as evasion dressed up as sophistication.

(h) I do not accept that the term is too vague to serve as a genuine period label. A boundary can be fuzzy at the edges and still mark something determinate.

Please work through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than reporting them as equally troubling, and for each one say precisely which commitment has to be revised or dropped, what the revision costs me in the argument, and whether some weaker version of the commitment could be preserved instead. Flag any place where one of these is doing work I have not acknowledged — including any case where a reason I give for one commitment is actually evidence against another. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the largest part of the set at once, and whether what remains after that revision would still be worth defending in ten minutes to a panel that is expecting a thesis.
