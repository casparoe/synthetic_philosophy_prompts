Erwin Schrödinger's philosophical writings of the 1940s and 1950s advance a striking thesis: that consciousness is never encountered in the plural but only in the singular, and that the multiplicity of minds is appearance rather than fact — a position he took to converge with the monism of the Vedanta he had read, and which he paired with the claim that consciousness is fundamental and not explicable in physical terms. In one of these essays he illustrates the point with the analogy of "the many-faceted crystal which, while showing hundreds of little pictures of what is in reality a single existent object, does not really multiply the object". He died in January 1961.

Three developments belong entirely to the period after his death.

First, in 1962 surgeons began performing complete section of the corpus callosum in patients with intractable epilepsy, and investigators then tested the resulting hemispheres separately. Stimuli flashed briefly to one visual field could be verbally reported or verbally denied while the other hemisphere demonstrably knew better: the hand controlled by the unreporting hemisphere could retrieve by touch an object it could not name, and the two hands were occasionally observed acting at cross-purposes. The programme earned Roger Sperry a Nobel Prize, and its results are still commonly summarised as two conscious systems inside one skull.

Second, a single set of trained neural-network weights is now routinely executed in thousands of simultaneous instances across distributed hardware. Each instance conducts a separate conversation with its own accumulating context; no instance exchanges information with any other; instances are spun up and destroyed continuously. Two instances running at the same moment may be asserting contradictories to their respective interlocutors.

Third, practitioners fine-tune divergent descendants of one base model and then average their parameters into a single new model — a standard procedure now known as model merging — so that lineages split and rejoin without any continuous identity to track.

With that in view: (1) How would Schrödinger have assessed the commissurotomy findings — as evidence against the unity thesis, as evidence for it, or as beside the point? Would he have accepted that his thesis is the kind of claim neurosurgery could bear on at all, and what in his wider account of consciousness and physics licenses that answer? (2) Would he have counted the parallel instances as one mind, many minds, or neither? (3) Do his answers to (1) and (2) rest on a single principle of individuation, or does he need a different principle in each case? If the latter, which case should give way, and what does that reveal about where the unity thesis is actually anchored — in embodiment, in causal integration, in content, or in something else? (4) Where does the crystal analogy genuinely apply to these cases, and where does it strain or fail?
