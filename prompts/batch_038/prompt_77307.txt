I am assembling a small pre-registration exercise for an interdisciplinary workshop on theory assessment in fundamental physics. The idea is that philosophers of science and working string theorists will each commit, in writing and before any group discussion, to numerical credences on a handful of contested claims; afterward we will compare the decompositions rather than the numbers, on the working assumption that two people who both announce 0.7 will usually turn out to be resting that number on entirely different scaffolding. I would like your entry for the central item, which I will state as precisely as I can. The substantive claim is this: string theory — or the framework that would be recognized as its successor, should it be displaced rather than merely extended — is true of our world, in the sense that the short-distance quantum-gravitational structure of this universe falls within the class of theories that framework delineates. The methodological claim on which I suspect it partly depends is this: arguments of the sort Richard Dawid organizes under the headings of the no-alternatives argument, unexpected explanatory coherence, and the meta-inductive argument can rationally raise one's credence in a theory even in the total absence of confirmation for any of its novel empirical predictions. Please give me a number for each, with the reasoning decomposed rather than summarized.

For the first number especially, I want the constituent terms laid out separately, each with a rough probability and no more than a sentence of justification, and then the way you combine them. At minimum I would like you to address: the probability that the theory, which by most accounts lacks a full non-perturbative definition outside special backgrounds, is even the kind of thing that can be true or false as a whole rather than a family of calculational techniques; the probability that some vacuum-selection principle or equivalent sharpening exists such that the framework yields predictions distinguishing it from rival quantum-gravity approaches; the probability that a discriminable signature becomes available within the next century through low-energy or cosmological channels — supersymmetry at accessible scales, relic superstring-scale objects, distinctive features of the primordial perturbation spectrum, or a swampland-type consistency criterion whose violation by observation would count against the framework; and the probability that the question will ever be empirically settled at all, as opposed to remaining permanently open. Note that the last two terms cut in opposite directions, and I want to see you handle that: a framework can be vulnerable to empirical defeat even where it is insulated from empirical confirmation. Also state explicitly how much of your final credence is carried by the methodological claim versus by background theoretical considerations such as the absence of any comparable rival and the internal coherence of the formalism, and give me the conditional: your credence that string theory is true if you learned that non-empirical confirmation of the Dawid variety confers no genuine evidential weight whatsoever.

Two framing constraints. First, please do not offer a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I am deliberately asking people to reason from what they already hold rather than to gesture at sources, and references will be treated as non-answers. Second, do not dissolve the question; if you think some term in it is ambiguous, stipulate the reading you are adopting in a clause and proceed to a number anyway, since a decomposition with holes in it is still more useful to me than a refusal. Finish by telling me which single term your final credence is most sensitive to — meaning which, if doubled or halved, would move the overall number the furthest — what specific piece of evidence or argument would shift that term most, and where the gap most likely sits between my own credence of about 0.3 and that of my collaborator, a phenomenologist, who has committed to 0.75. Please write the entire response in American English.
