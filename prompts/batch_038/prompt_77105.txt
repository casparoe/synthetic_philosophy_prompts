Our staff engineer runs a monthly "architecture review" for the backend org, and last month's was entirely framed around Imre Lakatos and research programmes, which he'd apparently picked up from some long blog post. He's smart and I don't want to just dismiss it, but I got into an argument with two other people afterwards about how much of it is actually Lakatos versus him wearing a philosophy costume for opinions he already had. I have zero philosophy background — I've read maybe twenty pages of wikipedia-level stuff since then, becuase I wanted to be able to say something in the next review — so I can't tell which bits are real and which bits he smuggled in.

Here's his summary as best I transcribed it from my notes, in his words where I remember them: "Lakatos fixed Popper. Popper thought a single failed observation could kill a theory, but nobody actually does science that way — when the orbit of Uranus came out wrong, astronomers didn't throw out Newton, they blamed the data and posited an unseen planet, and when Neptune showed up they were vindicated. So Lakatos said every research programme has a hard core of assumptions that's immunized from refutation by fiat, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses you're allowed to modify. The one rule is the negative heuristic: don't point the falsification at the core. Whether a programme is any good comes down to progressive versus degenerating — progressive means it keeps generating novel predictions that then come true, degenerating means it only manufactures after-the-fact patches to explain anomalies away. And Lakatos's actual normative point is that degenerating programmes are irrational and ought to be abandoned, which is why he said things like Marxism and Freudian psychoanalysis weren't science anymore. But he also insisted you can't appraise a programme in real time, only with decades of hindsight, so in practice it's a philosophy of history rather than a method anyone can use, which is basically why Kuhn won that debate."

Then he mapped it onto us. Our hard core is the 2019 decision to go all-in on microservices; the protective belt is the sidecars, the retry/backoff policy, the service mesh config, the eventual-consistency shims and the distributed tracing stack we keep bolting on; every new patch is evidence of a degenerating programme; therefore we should abandon the core and move to a modular monolith. When someone objected that the migration would cost us two quarters of roadmap, he said that objection is exactly the kind of thing Lakatos rules out, because defending the core on grounds of switching cost is just more immunizing — you're only allowed to defend it by showing your rival programme is progressive, i.e. that it predicts stuff the current one doesn't.

What I want is an accuracy audit, claim by claim: for each piece of that summary, tell me what a philosopher of science would sign off on, what's a defensible simplification for a lay audience, and what's wrong or a conflation of two things Lakatos kept apart. The specific spots I'm suspicious about: is the Neptune story really his kind of example, or is he doing something more complicated with the fact that the same protective-belt move can be brilliant or cheap depending on how it turns out; is "immunized by fiat" his own framing or Popper's, and if it's his, is the immunizing itself what makes a programme bad or is it something else; is the negative heuristic really just "don't touch the core," and what's this positive heuristic he never mentioned once, which I suspect is a pretty big omission; is it true that Lakatos thought a degenerating programme should be abandoned straight away, and is it true he thought appraisal always lags decades behind, or does he say something more careful about that; and does Kuhn even belong in this sentence — I thought the actual fight was with Popper and with Feyerabend, and that Lakatos and Kuhn were closer to allies in some respects.

And then the part I actually care about, so please don't stop at the philosophy: does his conclusion about our architecture follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate? My gut says the migration-cost objection has to be admissible somewhere, and that progressive/degenerating doesn't translate cleanly to software because our "novel predictions" aren't empirical facts about the world but forecasts about latency and headcount and on-call load. But I should flag that I wrote a lot of that mesh config, so I'm a conflicted witness.
