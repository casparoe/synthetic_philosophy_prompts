The idea, in as much of a mess as it currently is: everyone assumes that if grounding isn't well-founded — chains of ground that never bottom out, or worse, pairs of things that ground each other, like the familiar set-and-singleton case or the whole-and-its-parts case — then fundamentality goes with it, and with fundamentality goes the entire explanatory point of positing grounding in the first place. But I think that's too quick, because 'the fundamental' is doing two jobs that have been run together. There's an ontological job: being the ground floor, the level where dependence terminates. And there's an epistemic or explanatory job: being the level at which our best account of some domain is stated, the level from which everything else in that domain can be derived. The non-well-founded cases look to me like they wreck the first job and leave the second completely untouched. So the project would be to try to define a notion of explanatory bedrock that doesn't presuppose well-founded grounding at all — roughly, a set of claims is bedrock for a domain if everything true in that domain is reachable from it by grounding steps, even where those steps never terminate and even where some of them circle back — and then to test whether that notion can carry the weight fundamentality is normally asked to carry: whether it can underwrite ontological priority, whether it makes sense of the intuition that physics is more fundamental than sociology, whether you can get an account of levels out of it, and whether it collapses back into the ontological notion as soon as you try to make it precise.
