I've spent fifteen years on rationality forums and, more recently, have been reading comparative economic history for pleasure — Braudel and the Annales tradition, plus the current crop of big-history books about why some regions industrialized and others did not. A recurring argumentative pattern has started to bother me, and I have no name for it. Let me describe it as concretely as I can.

Someone advances a structural claim: navigable rivers and accessible coal made industrial takeoff likely where they were found. I offer what looks like a counterexample — a region with the same endowment that did not take off for another century. The reply is that endowments operate over centuries, so one region's lag sits comfortably inside the noise the claim never purported to exclude; the structure is still there, doing its slow work. Fine. But a few chapters later the same author points to a particular city where coal, river, and takeoff coincide inside a single decade and calls this a vivid confirmation of the structural account. The grain of the claim seems to be selected per piece of evidence rather than fixed in advance: fine-grained when the events line up, coarse-grained when they do not. A second instance: agrarian labor scarcity after plague is said to explain the loosening of serfdom in Western Europe, and when I note that comparable scarcity was followed by tightened bondage in the East, the answer is that the Eastern case shows the same structure operating through a different mediating variable on a longer clock. Neither the confirming nor the disconfirming case is ever refused, and the claim survives both.

I know the family this sits near — ad hoc rescue, immunizing strategies, a protective belt of auxiliaries thickening over time, ceteris paribus clauses doing covert work, unfalsifiability in general. But none of those picks out the mechanism I mean, which is that explanation and evidence are never forced to meet at the same resolution, and, crucially, that the resolution is chosen after the evidence is in hand, always in whichever direction flatters the theory. Nor is it just a small-N problem, since the same switching happens with hundreds of cases.

Does this have an established name in the philosophy of history, in social-science methodology, or in the philosophy of science? If it does, tell me what it is called and how the standard formulation differs from my description; if it does not, coin a term and define it sharply enough that I could apply it to a case I have not described. I want the carving rather than the label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie — in particular, what separates it from a legitimate multi-scale account, since it is plainly true that slow and fast variables coexist in any real explanation and that a structural claim about distributions may genuinely be indifferent to individual cases.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the label, established or coined; (2) the nearest established concept and precisely what separates my phenomenon from it; (3) a second nearest concept and the same; (4) a definition sharp enough to apply to an unfamiliar case, together with one boundary case that falls outside it; (5) a practical test I could use to distinguish the failure mode from a defensible multi-scale explanation.
