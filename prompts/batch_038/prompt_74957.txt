Two parties splitting something. d = (0,0), feasible set is everything with x1, x2 ≥ 0, x1 + 2x2 ≤ 14, and x1 ≤ 10 — that last cap is player 1's outside option, the most she can get alone, and its also the one number the two of them actually disagree about. Taken at face value the utopia point is (10, 7), so Kalai–Smorodinsky picks (5.83, 4.08) while Nash picks (7, 3.5). Now suppose player 1 talks the arbitrator into an outside option of 14. The utopia point becomes (14, 7), the KS point moves to exactly (7, 3.5) — she's up 1.17, player 2 is down 0.58 — and every option that entered the set is one nobody ends up choosing. (arithmetic checks out, don't relitigate it)

Argue that this is a decisive reason to keep Nash's IIA and drop Kalai's individual monotonicity in any setting where the walk-away payoffs are themselves up for argument, which is to say basically always. Strongest version, not a hedge, and take on the obvious comeback head-on: that the feasible set is supposed to be an objective description of the situation, so bluffing about your outside option is a problem about information and mechanism design rather than an objection to the solution concept.
