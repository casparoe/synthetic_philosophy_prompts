For six years I have volunteered three evenings a week on an advice line run by a small charity, helping people who are contesting a decision about a workplace adjustment, a benefit award, or a period of sick leave. Nearly every call turns, sooner or later, on whether the caller's condition counts as an illness at all — and I have noticed that the people making the decisions and the people receiving them are often using the word in incompatible ways without either side realising it. I have no academic training of any kind, but I have read around the subject on my own for some years, and I have reached the point where I want my thinking tested rather than encouraged.

Below are six candidate ways of fixing what "disease" means. I have written them as I would explain them to a caller, not as I found them in articles; I am aware that several correspond to positions with standard names, and where that is so I would like the name, but I want them judged strictly as formulations, not by the reputation of whoever is associated with them.

A. A disease is an internal state that departs from the statistically normal working of the human body or mind for a person of that age and sex, where what counts as normal is settled by biology alone and requires no judgement about what is good.

B. A disease is a two-part thing: an internal mechanism failing to do the job it was naturally selected to do, plus the failure being bad for the person by the standards of their own culture. Remove either part and the word stops applying.

C. A disease is a reduction in a person's ability to do the things that are vital for them, in their actual circumstances, where what counts as vital depends on their goals and their situation. This one openly builds in what the person wants and where they live.

D. There is no biological core to find. "Disease" is a label a society attaches to statistically unusual traits once it has decided they are undesirable, and the search for a value-free underlying kind is a mistake that keeps failing for a reason.

E. Diseases are whatever clusters of internally caused, statistically atypical states that medicine has found it useful to group together and treat. There is no single mark shared by all of them, only a loose family resemblance held together by practice.

F. My own attempt, which I would like treated as seriously as the others: something is a disease in a person if and only if (i) some process is going on in them that is statistically atypical for their age-and-sex reference class and can be described without mentioning what anyone values; (ii) that process limits what they can actually do or be in the life they have reason to want, judged both from their own point of view and from the outside; and (iii) the limitation is not wholly produced by the way their social environment happens to be arranged — and where it is partly so produced, only the remainder counts.

The cases I have in mind are these:

1. A man in his fifties with raised blood pressure, found at a workplace screening, with no symptoms whatsoever, who feels entirely well.
2. A woman whose exhaustion and inability to concentrate began four months after her husband died.
3. A young man told that a scan has revealed something that has never harmed anybody and probably never will, but which insurers now treat as a pre-existing condition.
4. A deaf caller who does not consider herself ill or disabled in any respect, and who is furious that her GP's letter describes her as suffering from an impairment.
5. A man whose only difficulty is that his job requires him to sit still and attend for nine hours, and who cannot; outside that job nothing in his life is restricted.
6. A woman whose menopausal symptoms have ended a career she cared about.
7. A student taking a stimulant to sharpen her concentration for examinations, with no underlying deficit of any kind.
8. A woman born without a uterus who is otherwise entirely well and who asks whether she has a disease, given that she cannot do something most people can.
9. A blood-marker abnormality for which no mechanism has ever been identified, with no symptoms and no difference in outcomes over twenty years of follow-up.

What I want from you:

- For each of A to F, a score from 0 to 10 for reliability: across these nine cases, how often it delivers a verdict I would be prepared to state to a caller as true rather than as a stipulation about a word. Alongside each score, give me a count of how many of the nine cases you think it gets badly wrong, and name them.
- For each of A to F, a second score from 0 to 10 for how much of its disagreement with the others would simply evaporate if we agreed on every fact and were arguing only about the word.
- A tier list on the first score, with a sentence or two saying why each lands where it does.
- A single figure between 0 and 1 for how much of the whole naturalist–normativist dispute, as I have laid it out, is merely verbal.
- Which of the nine cases best separates the top two entries in your tier list, and what verdict each of them gives on it.
- The one repair that would most improve the weakest formulation, and whether applying that repair collapses it into one of the others — if so, say which.
- Whether F should be abandoned, and if not, which of its three clauses is most likely to break first under pressure.

Two constraints. Please give me no references, no reading list, and no book or article titles; I cannot look any of them up and I would rather have your reasoning than your sources. And please do not retreat into saying that everything depends on how one defines the term: the whole point of my question is that a definition has to be defended, and I want the scores and the counts whether or not you think they are precise.
