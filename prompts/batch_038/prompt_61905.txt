hey, could you write me a short story that's basically an argument about what "collapse" even means? Not an essay in story clothing — an actual story with people in it — but one where the conceptual problem does the heavy lifting.

Rough setup I want: an invented valley in an invented bronze-age-ish world. Please don't use Easter Island or the Maya or the actual Late Bronze Age palaces, I want something that isn't a retelling of a famous case. The valley belongs to a bigger palace-centered system: it sends up grain, copper and wool, and in exchange it gets protection, rulings on disputes between hamlets, seed in a bad year, and — this is the important part — a reason to keep tally marks. The protagonist is the tally-keeper, the man whose entire working life is recording what went up the road and what came back down it.

Then the system comes apart, offstage and uncerimoniously. The road closes, the garrison walks home, the tablets stop arriving. No comet, no plague, no dark prophecy, no last king dying in the rain. Keep it administrative and mundane.

Here's the hinge. There's this Tainter-ish line of thinking — a society collapses when it rapidly loses an established level of sociopolitical complexity, meaning collapse is a claim about the machinery and not about the people. So build the story out of four seperate descriptions of the valley and let them come apart over a couple of generations:

1. who holds authority, i.e. who can actually compel you to do something
2. roughly how many people are alive there
3. whether anybody writes anything down
4. what gets sacrificed at the shrine on the ridge, to whom, in what order

One of those four should end outright. One should shrink and never recover. One should carry on as if nothing at all happened. And at least one should get better — some people in the valley ought to be relieved, or better fed, or freer once the requisitions stop, and they should say so without sounding like villains.

I want a real argument inside it, not just mood. Somebody — a son who comes back down the road, a widow who's taken over a workshop, a priest irritated that nobody is counting anymore — should make a genuinely good case that "collapse" is just what it looks like when you keep counting the wrong things, or that it's a word the center gets to use about the edges. And somebody else should push back with something that isn't a strawman: there really was a thing the valley could do before that it cannot do now (settle a feud without a killing, maybe, or get seed through a drought), and losing that isn't nothing just because the language and the terraces and the harvest songs all survived.

Structure: three time-layers, cut together rather than told in sequence. (a) one ordinary day, ideally the day the last delivery goes up the road; (b) about two generations later, somebody's grandchild doing the same job for an addressee who no longer exists; (c) much much later, a classifier who finds the site and has to fill in a single field. Make (c) short and cold, and let it be wrong in a way that is completely reasonable from where they're sitting.

The habit of keeping records for no one should be the emotional spine, but don't make him noble about it — he's a bit ridiculous and he knows it.

About 1200–1600 words. Don't end on a moral or a sentence that spells out the theme, don't let any character say the word "civilization," and no framing device where it turns out to be a museum docent or a grad student dreaming in the present day.
