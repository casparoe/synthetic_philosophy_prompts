I have spent more years than I would care to quantify reading the sequences-era and post-sequences material on the hinge of history, decisive strategic advantage, and the singleton hypothesis: the claim that once a civilization's technology matures, a single decision-making agency at the highest level tends to emerge, whether as a world government, a hegemon, a dominant machine, or something considerably less recognizable. My own working model is essentially the decisive-advantage picture — consolidation happens because somebody pulls far enough ahead that the others cannot stop them. I suspect that model is doing less explanatory work than I assume, and I would like a constructed case capable of showing it.

Please build me a matched pair of world histories, A and B, to the following specifications.

Shared skeleton: A and B must agree on the technology timeline, the roster and character of the great powers, the resource and demographic background, and the sequence of exogenous shocks. They may differ on exactly one institutional variable, which you name up front. That variable may not be a difference in capability, population, values, or luck.

In history A, a functional singleton emerges and stays stable for at least a century, yet at no point does any actor — state, firm, alliance, or system — hold anything resembling a decisive strategic advantage: no one can complete a transformative project that the others could not have blocked within months. In history B, the institutional variable is absent, one actor does briefly acquire precisely that kind of lead, and consolidation fails anyway.

Further constraints on A:

- The consolidating mechanism must be recognizable to a present-day diplomat: inspection and verification, financial or compute chokepoints, mutual transparency, pooled liability insurance, harmonized standards bodies, or something in that family. No superintelligence, no molecular manufacturing, no exotic physics.
- The regime must be supported by most signatories for reasons that have nothing to do with existential risk — trade access, insurance premiums, procurement, prestige.
- At the moment of formation it must not look like a world government from the inside, and a competent observer living through it must be able to hold a defensible but mistaken view about whether a singleton has been achieved.
- No character may be a villain, and no one may be unusually wise.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The two histories. Together, under roughly nine hundred words.
2. The isolation check: show that the named variable is genuinely the only difference, and in particular that you have not smuggled a capability lead into A through the back door.
3. The defection audit for A: identify the moment within the century of stability when defection came closest to paying, and explain what closed it without appealing to anyone's superior power.
4. The failure diagnosis for B: explain why the lead did not convert into consolidation, and identify the feature of B that is invisible to an analyst who tracks only capability distributions.
5. The classification argument: does A actually satisfy the definition of a singleton? The standard definition carries a self-preservation requirement — the top-level agency must be able to prevent threats to its own existence and supremacy, not merely veto third parties' projects — and A's regime may possess only negative authority. Argue this out rather than assuming your answer. If you conclude A is not a singleton, say what weaker category it falls into and whether the hypothesis is nevertheless confirmed or disconfirmed by A.
6. What the pair does to my priors: state whether it raises, lowers, or leaves unchanged the probability that a singleton forms conditional on technological maturity, and be explicit about whether you take the case to be evidence about the world or only about our concepts.
7. The honest postmortem: which single feature of the pair is doing the work, what the case collapses into if that feature is removed, and every place where you stipulated a result instead of deriving one.
8. The strongest available attack on A from someone who maintains that consolidated regimes reliably decay — capture, sclerosis, succession crises, erosion of legitimacy — and your best reply.

So that you know what counts as cheating: the case is meant to test whether a purely institutional mechanism can carry the whole explanatory load of consolidation. A pair that writes a benevolent world government into existence by stipulation, or that quietly hands A's regime a capability edge, fails however elegant the prose. I would much rather see item 7 admit that the mechanism only holds under an assumption I would reject than receive a smooth story that conceals the assumption.
