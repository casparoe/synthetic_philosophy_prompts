I cover philosophy and the history of ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am drafting a long essay on the intellectual pedigree of a claim that keeps resurfacing in American political commentary: that law is organized force wearing a wig, and that the only reason anyone calls a regime legitimate is that it has already won. My editors want the piece anchored in a primary text rather than in contemporary punditry, and I have settled on a fragment from Pascal's posthumous notes on justice and force. My difficulty is that the fragment is brief, elliptical, and, I suspect, deliberately unresolved, and I do not want to build four thousand words on top of a misreading of it.

Because the translation I have been working from is still under copyright, what follows is my own close paraphrase rather than a quotation, rendered sentence by sentence in the order the sentences appear. Please treat it as reliable in substance but not in wording, and tell me where that caveat bites:

1. It is just that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed.
2. Justice without force is helpless, because there are always those who offend; force without justice is tyrannical.
3. Justice without force is therefore gainsaid, and force without justice is therefore condemned; the two must be joined, and to join them we must either make what is just strong, or make what is strong just.
4. But justice is disputable, whereas force is immediately recognizable and is never disputed.
5. We could not give force to justice, because force has already contradicted justice and declared that it — force — is what is just.
6. And so, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just.

I want four things from you, and I would rather have precision than reassurance. First, reconstruct the argument as an explicit sequence of steps, and for each step say whether it is a normative premise, an empirical observation about human beings, or a report of something a political actor says or does. These fragments are written in a clipped register that frequently hides the speaker, and I am worried that at least one of my six lines is not Pascal's own assertion at all but the boast of the powerful, recorded without comment. Second, tell me what line 6 is actually doing: a description of how legitimacy is manufactured in practice, a recommendation, or a bitter diagnosis by a man who thinks the alternative unavailable to fallen creatures. Third — and this is the point my editors will press hardest — a colleague in political theory insists the fragment anticipates a conventionalist account of political obligation: the idea that obedience to any settled rule is rational because the alternative is not better rules but no rules, and that the move from the disputability of justice to the conclusion that we should make the strong just is exactly that move, made well before the convention-based accounts that came later. I want to know whether that reading is a legitimate recovery or a projection, and what feature of the passage would decide the question. Fourth, elsewhere in the same cluster of notes Pascal argues that custom is the entire foundation of equity and that the person who sees how arbitrary the law is should nonetheless obey it — not because it is just, but because it is customary — while in a nearby fragment he mocks the reverence people feel for a magistrate's robes and regalia as imagination mistaken for authority. Does force in line 6 mean coercion, or the manufactured awe of ceremony and habit, and can those two be pulled apart within his account without the argument collapsing?

Please write in American English. Do not supply any quotation from a translation you cannot vouch for, and wherever my paraphrase leaves a genuine ambiguity — a choice of French term that would settle it — tell me what to look for in the original rather than guessing at what it says.
