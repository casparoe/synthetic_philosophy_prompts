I am working through Book III of *The World as Will and Idea* (in the Haldane and Kemp translation) on my way to §52 on music, and I have got stuck in the long section on poetry that immediately precedes it. Here is the passage:

> Whoever accepts the view that has been expressed of the lyrical frame of mind, will also allow, that it is the sensuous and poetical knowledge of the principle which I established in my essay on the Principle of Sufficient Reason, and have also referred to in this work, that the identity of the subject of knowing with that of willing may be called the miracle κατ' εξοχήν; so that the poetical effect of the lyric rests finally on the truth of that principle. […] men are always separating more between their subjective feeling and their objective knowledge. In the child the two are still entirely blended together; it scarcely knows how to distinguish itself from its surroundings, it is at one with them. In the young man all perception chiefly affects feeling and mood, and even mingles with it, as Byron very beautifully expresses—
> "I live not in myself, but I become
> Portion of that around me; and to me
> High mountains are a feeling."
> This is why the youth clings so closely to the perceptible and outward side of things; this is why he is only fit for lyrical poetry, and only the full-grown man is capable of the drama. The old man we can think of as at the most an epic poet, like Ossian, and Homer, for narration is characteristic of old age.

Could you help me with four things? First, what exactly is the claim that the identity of the subject of knowing with the subject of willing is a "miracle," and why should the *poetical effect* of the lyric rest on the truth of the principle of sufficient reason? I cannot tell whether he is saying that lyric succeeds because it suspends will-less contemplation or because it exposes the unity underlying the distinction he has been insisting on. Second, do Byron's lines actually illustrate what he needs? They sound like a loss of the self in the object rather than an intrusion of willing into knowing, and I suspect he is trading on that ambiguity. Third, is the child/youth/old-man ladder a piece of developmental psychology, or a disguised ranking of the arts by how much of the willing subject each retains — lyric most, drama less, epic least? Fourth, and most important for my purposes: if lyric is the art form that keeps the first person inside the work, and he goes on in §52 to say that music is a copy of the will itself rather than of the Ideas, does this passage license reading his theory of music as lyric without a lyrical subject? I am trying to decide whether his account of music is fundamentally an expression theory or a structural-copy theory, and I want to know whether this text tips the balance or whether I am over-reading a transition he never intended to be load-bearing.
