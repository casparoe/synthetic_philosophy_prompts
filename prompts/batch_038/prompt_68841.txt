Next term I am presenting a paper at a research seminar jointly run by my department's Kant group and its metaphysics group, and I want to be prepared for the question period rather than merely for the paper.

The thesis, as narrowly as I can state it. On the standard model, a transcendental argument begins from some uncontested feature of our cognitive or practical situation — call it Y — argues that X is a necessary condition of Y, and concludes X. Stroud's 1968 discussion then generates the familiar squeeze: either the argument delivers only a claim about what we cannot help believing, which is not the anti-sceptical prize it was after, or it needs idealism or a verification principle to bridge from our thought to the world. My claim is that this dilemma mislocates the problem, because it assumes the conclusion of a successful transcendental argument must be a modal claim about the thinker. I argue instead that the proper output is a constraint-satisfaction result: the argument shows that a candidate description of reality is not composable with a description of the thinker's own situation that the sceptic has already granted, and that this incompatibility is a fact about the descriptions, not a fact about our psychology and not a fact we legislate. Stated that way, I contend, the ambitious/modest distinction simply fails to get a grip, and no appeal to idealism, compulsion-to-believe, or constitution is required. My two worked examples are an argument from the temporal ordering of experience, in the shape of Kant's Refutation of Idealism, and an argument against a purely dispositional account of what it is to mean something by a sign. The three premises of the reframed schema are: (i) the sceptic concedes Y; (ii) any description of reality on which X fails renders Y unintelligible rather than merely surprising; (iii) unintelligibility here is not epistemic difficulty but failure of the two descriptions to be jointly satisfiable.

The audience is about twenty people. Six or seven are Kant specialists working on self-consciousness and the Deduction; they are fluent in Strawson's descriptive-metaphysics reading and in the Evans and McDowell line on singular reference and self-ascription, and they will be annoyed if I appear to be re-treading Strawson without acknowledging it. Two are committed transcendental idealists who think that keeping the argument while jettisoning idealism is either evasive or bound to fail. The remainder are analytic metaphysicians — grounding, fundamentality, essence, metaphysical explanation — who largely regard transcendental arguments as a confused species of modal epistemology and will immediately ask what the modal force of "not jointly satisfiable" is supposed to be, and how it differs from ordinary metaphysical necessity. One of them works on the epistemology of modality and will press on how I know the incompatibility claim. There is also a graduate student working on philosophy of AI who will try to convert every exchange into a question about whether an artificial agent could meet my constraints.

Please prepare the following.

1. The objections in the order they will actually arrive, each attributed to the kind of person who raises it and phrased in that person's own vocabulary. The metaphysician's objection should sound like a metaphysician's, with their technical demands intact, not like a paraphrase of a general worry.
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, followed by an explicit note on where the reply begins to strain: where it depends on a distinction my interlocutor has no independent reason to accept, or where it quietly reintroduces the idealism I claim to have discarded.
3. A separation of the objections that genuinely damage the thesis from those that merely reveal that I have stated it badly, together with an indication of which of the latter I can defuse by rewording the claim in the first five minutes of the talk.
4. What to concede up front — in particular, two or three concessions that cost me nothing but buy credibility with the Kant specialists on one side and the idealists on the other.
5. The one objection I am hoping nobody raises, and an account of why it is the objection my position is least able to absorb.

Two further requests. First, because one of my examples concerns meaning and rule-following, flag any point at which the audience's prior familiarity with the Kripkenstein literature will let them shortcut my argument by assimilating it to a reading I do not endorse, and say how to forestall that assimilation in advance. Second, tell me whether premise (ii) — the move from "makes Y false" to "makes Y unintelligible" — is load-bearing in a way I have not noticed, and if it is, what the strongest available repair looks like.
