I chair a working group that drafts definitions for a state technology regulator. Twice a year we deadlock on a definitional question — whether a system that merely ranks options for a human who then ratifies counts as an "automated decision system," whether "harm" in our statute reaches third parties or only the subject — and the deadlock burns months because each side insists it is tracking what the term really means while the other insists the term has no meaning to track. I want a triage procedure my group can run on a stalled definitional dispute in a single two-hour sitting, operated by people with no philosophical training and handed at the end to counsel, who signs off.

Specify the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the terminal verdicts the procedure may return — at minimum: merely verbal, genuinely substantive about the world, and substantive-but-not-decidable-by-argument, only by a choice among explications. Draw on the tools that actually exist for this — interpretive charity, the test of banning the contested term and asking whether the disagreement can be restated and survives without it, the thought that some disputes are settled by which precisification carves the subject matter up better, and the explication criteria of exactness, fruitfulness, and closeness to prior usage — but tell me which of these a non-expert can really apply and which need a philosopher in the room. Mark every point where the procedure is a judgment call dressed as a step, and show how it handles the mixed case in which the parties are simultaneously talking past each other and disagreeing about the facts.

Then run it on three live disputes: the ranking-system case above; whether a stablecoin-like instrument is a "deposit"; and whether "resident" in our rulebook means domicile or physical presence — a fight I have personally staked my credibility on. Let at least one verdict be one I will resent, and close with what this procedure can never settle for us.
