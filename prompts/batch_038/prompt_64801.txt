I work as a backend engineer on the ranking layer of a large product-search system, and I have just been admitted to a part-time master's programme in philosophy; my first real deadline is a dissertation proposal of roughly 3,500 words, covering eight months of work alongside a full-time job. The half-formed idea I want to develop comes from a recurring problem in my day job. Several of our relevance signals are computed over catalogues described at different levels of granularity — some over individual SKUs, some over product families, some over broader categories — and when we merge them into a single ordering, the result is not invariant under the choice of level: aggregate at the SKU level and then roll up, or roll up and then aggregate, and you get materially different orderings of the same catalogue. I have been reading about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I noticed that its standard formulation assumes all the individual preferences and the social preference are defined on the same set of lotteries, generated from one common finite set of basic prospects, each satisfying the expected-utility hypothesis. My hunch is that this shared-domain assumption is doing far more work than it appears to, and that my engineering problem is a special case of a question nobody in that literature is asking.

The question I want to pursue is what happens to the theorem when the agents do not share an outcome space but instead evaluate over different, non-aligned descriptions of a common underlying situation — a lattice of partitions, with each person's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility defined on their own level and constrained only by some coherence condition relating utilities across refinements. Two sub-questions interest me. Formally: under what coherence conditions does a Harsanyi-style linear representation still exist once we move across levels, and is it unique, or do the weights become level-dependent? Philosophically: if the weights shift with the description level, does that vindicate the familiar objection that the theorem aggregates expected utilities rather than utilities and that the weights ride on arbitrary choices of zero point and scale for each person, or does it instead reveal a substantive normative requirement — a demand that social evaluation be invariant under mere re-description — that has gone unstated? I also suspect, though I cannot yet articulate it, that refinement might supply a natural calibration of interpersonal scales, because shared sub-alternatives across levels could anchor the relative units; if that is right, the granularity machinery might actually answer the comparability objection rather than deepen it.

Please turn this into the proposal itself, and treat my framing as raw material to be sharpened or, where necessary, corrected rather than as a brief to be flattered. I need: a precise statement of the research question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a general research programme; an account of why the positions already on the table — the utilitarian reading of the theorem, the reinterpretation on which it delivers only an ex ante Pareto constraint, and the state-dependent-utility variants — leave my question open, described in terms of the arguments rather than the papers, since I will assemble the references myself and would rather you not invent any; the method I would actually use, with an explicit defence of it against the two obvious alternatives, namely recasting the whole thing as a standard impossibility theorem in the Arrow tradition with no philosophical pay-off, and treating it as an empirical study of aggregation levels in deployed ranking systems; a month-by-month work plan that is credible for someone working four evenings a week; a description of what a successful result would look like and, just as important, what would count as the project failing; and a candid assessment of scope, given that my mathematics stops at linear algebra, elementary probability and some real analysis, with no measure theory. Finally, please tell me directly if you think the question is either already settled in the literature or, conversely, a purely technical exercise wearing philosophy as a costume — I would much rather hear that now than in month six.
