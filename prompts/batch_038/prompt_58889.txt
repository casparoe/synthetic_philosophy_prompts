So, slightly weird situation. I teach history at a community college, and after an op-ed I wrote got picked up by the local paper, our county's teachers institute asked me to give the opening talk to about sixty AP US history and government teachers this fall. My argument is that "American exceptionalism" is really two claims that get smuggled in together: (1) the comparative one, that the US is a statistical outlier among wealthy democracies — unusual religiosity, no serious labor party, gun laws, incarceration rates, that sort of thing; and (2) the mission one, that the country has a special destiny or standing obligation to export its model. My thesis is that only (1) is the kind of thing you could actually check, and (2) borrows whatever credibility it has from people assuming (1) licenses it — which it can't, since you can be an outlier with no mission and have a mission while being perfectly average. I basically want to present (2) as ideology wearing (1)'s lab coat.

The problem is the room isn't philosophy people. It's mostly teachers who live on historiography and document sourcing, a few social studies coordinators who care about what's on the state standards, and at least two who I'm pretty sure will hear the word "ideology" as an attack on patriotism. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in the words they'd use — not the ones a political theorist would raise — ordered by how much damage each does in a fifteen-minute question period. For each one, a reply I can say out loud without notes, what I should concede up front to keep it from becoming a fight, and where my answer is honestly weakest. Also tell me which question I should be praying nobody asks. Please keep everything in American English.
