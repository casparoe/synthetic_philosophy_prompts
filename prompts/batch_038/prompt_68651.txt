I am assembling a set of considered verdicts about comparative truthlikeness — closeness to the truth — and I want help determining whether any single principle orders all of them. Some background on the constraint I take myself to be under: the notion has to do real work for false theories, since almost everything science has ever believed was false, and I accept that the original content-based definition of the notion breaks down precisely there, making comparisons between two false theories impossible when that is the one comparison it was designed to license. So I am looking for something that can rank falsehoods against falsehoods, and ideally falsehoods against truths, without collapsing into the useless rule that true claims are good and false claims are bad.

My verdicts, stated as comparisons:

(1) Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotelian dynamics. Both are false. The first is false in a way that still tracks the world, and I want my ordering to capture what that difference consists in rather than merely record it.

(2) "The sample weighed 21.4 grams," said of a sample that weighed 21.5 grams, is closer to the truth than the same sentence said of a sample that weighed 34.0 grams. Closeness comes in degrees along a dimension the claim is about.

(3) "Water is H₂O and combustion releases phlogiston" is further from the truth than "Water is H₂O" alone. Preserving every truth you already had, while adding a falsity, must cost you something.

(4) But a theory that gets combustion wrong and also describes the composition of water correctly is closer to the truth than one that gets combustion wrong and says nothing about water. Adding a truth to a falsehood can help. So (3) cannot be generalized into the claim that added content always costs.

(5) Suppose the population of China is 1.412 billion. "The population of China is 1.4 billion" is false; "The population of China is between one and two billion" is true. I judge the false and precise claim closer to the truth than the true and vague one. This is the verdict I expect to be in the most trouble: it sits badly with (3) and (4), which treat getting it right as the primary good, and it implies that being wrong informatively can beat being right emptily.

(6) Cross-subject comparisons should be meaningful. I want to say that general relativity is closer to the truth than Newtonian mechanics, full stop, not merely with respect to some stipulated set of quantities. Yet every candidate measure I can construct requires fixing a space of states and a weighting over the respects in which two claims can differ, and that weighting looks like a reflection of my interests rather than of the world. I want to hold both that the weighting is interest-relative and that truthlikeness is objective, and I suspect this is untenable; I would like to be told which half to surrender.

(7) Necessary claims are where my intuitions give out. "There are infinitely many primes" is true, and I do not want it demoted to the status of a trivial truth. But "2 + 2 = 5" is not, I think, further from the truth than "The Eiffel Tower is in Peru," and I do not know what a measure built to handle (2) and (5) is supposed to say about claims that could not possibly have been true.

What I want in return: a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see how to formalize it — what the relata are (sentences, propositions, theories, ranges of possibilities), what the ordering ranges over, and what does the generating work. For each clause, say which of the verdicts above it exists to capture. Then tell me what it implies for cases I have not listed: a necessarily false theory compared with a merely false one; a true theory that is right about everything it addresses but addresses almost nothing, against a false theory that addresses everything and is right about most of it; and two theories that differ only about matters that are in principle unobservable, where no investigation could ever disclose which is closer. Say whether the relation you posit is total or partial, and if partial, which of my comparisons fall outside its domain. Finally, tell me plainly which of (1) through (7) it cannot accommodate. If your conclusion is that no single ordering does the work, argue for that, and then say whether the right move is to divide the notion — accuracy, informativeness, estimated closeness — and which of my verdicts belongs to which fragment.
