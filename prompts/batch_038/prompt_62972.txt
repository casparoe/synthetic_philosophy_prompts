Writing the term paper for my philosophy-of-AI seminar and I've argued myself into a corner. My thesis was going to be that the fast-vs-slow takeoff debate is massively overrated, and here's the argument I built for it:

(1) What makes an advanced AI dangerous is that it ends up controlling a large share of what happens in the world, under goals we didn't choose. How long the process took isn't part of the danger.

(2) Suppose takeoff is slow: years of gradual capability growth, systems deployed widely, plenty of warning signs. Then unaligned systems are out in the world while still unaligned, many actors are racing to field them, and competitive pressure means whoever is furthest along ships first. So an unaligned system gets a decisive head start.

(3) Suppose takeoff is fast: capability jumps in months or weeks. Then there's no interval in which to notice the problem and fix it before the system is decisive. So an unaligned system gets a decisive head start.

(4) Slow and fast exaust the options. So in every scenario, an unaligned system gets a decisive head start unless alignment is solved before the capability arrives.

(5) Whether alignment gets solved before the capability arrives doesn't depend on takeoff speed; it depends on how hard the alignment problem is and how much effort we put into it.

(6) So the only variables that matter are the difficulty of the problem and our effort, and takeoff speed drops out of the decision entirely. Both camps are arguing over a parameter that doesn't appear in the equation.

Trouble is (6) feels obviously wrong. Speed is treated as one of the most load-bearing empirical questions in the whole field, and intuitively it changes whether you get a warning shot, whether interpretability can keep up with capability, whether a single lab could even try to pause. But I can't locate the bad step, and every premise looks fine to me in isolation. Which one do you reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it? I'd really like to keep (4) and (5) if that's possible, but tell me honestly if it isn't.
