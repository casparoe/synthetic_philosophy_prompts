I want help extracting a principle from a set of verdicts about when a person has standing to blame. The cases and my reactions are below. I have tried to record the reactions as I actually have them rather than tidily, and I have flagged two that look to me irreconcilable. Treat the verdicts as data to be systematized, not as positions to be defended, and do not bother with attributions or the secondary literature; I want the principle, not the bibliography.

To hold the subject fixed: in every case the blamer's judgment is accurate, the conduct really is blameworthy, and the blamer is lying about nothing. Only the question of standing varies.

1. A colleague who is himself chronically and conspicuously late rebukes me, in front of the whole group, for arriving ten minutes after the start. Verdict: he lacks standing. I feel entitled to brush the rebuke off entirely, even though he is right.

2. The same colleague, with the same record, takes me aside afterward and says quietly that my lateness cost us the client's confidence. Verdict: this one lands. If I answered with the usual retort about who is talking, I would be dodging. Cases 1 and 2 differ only in manner, audience, and apparent purpose, and my verdicts differ with them; so standing cannot be purely a function of the blamer's own record.

3. A friend who has never been unfaithful tells me my infidelity is a serious wrong. Verdict: full standing, unremarkable.

4. My brother, who has skipped exactly as many visits to our mother as I have and for equally poor reasons, blames me for not visiting. Verdict: he lacks standing, though what he says is true and though I would accept the same words from the friend in 3.

5. A man who drank heavily for twenty years and has been sober for ten tells our mutual friend, who is now drinking the same way, that it will destroy him. Verdict: standing, and more of it than I would have with the same words.

6. The same man relapsed last month and has told no one. He says the same thing to the same friend. Verdict: I think he now lacks standing. This is the pair I cannot reconcile with 5. Nothing about his epistemic position, his concern, or the usefulness of the warning has changed, and the friend could not detect the difference. If the loss of standing is real it must be produced by a fact invisible to everyone but the blamer; if it is not real, then my reaction to 5 was tracking something I have misdescribed.

7. Someone who plagiarized once thirty years ago, was sanctioned, made amends, and has been scrupulous since tells a student that plagiarism is a serious wrong. Verdict: standing intact; the past is beside the point.

8. A stranger of impeccable character with no connection to my family sees me speak sharply to my child in a supermarket and says that how I spoke was cruel. Verdict: he lacks standing, and not because of any fault of his. My reaction is not that he is a hypocrite but that it is not his.

9. The same stranger sees a man kick a dog in the street and says so. Verdict: full standing, and he ought to say something. As far as I can tell, 8 and 9 differ only in whether the wrong is the sort of thing that is anyone's to address.

10. A company publishes a statement blaming consumers for plastic waste while lobbying against deposit schemes. Verdict: no standing. I have the same verdict about the identical statement from an employee who privately opposed the lobbying and said so in meetings, and I am not confident I want to keep that second half.

11. The person I wronged has forgiven me; a third party who was not wronged keeps blaming me for it. Verdict: the third party lacks standing to blame me on the victim's behalf but retains standing to blame me for what the conduct shows about my character generally.

My tentative principle is that standing requires both that the blamer not be exempting himself from a standard he is endorsing, and that he stand in some relation to the wrong or to the person wronged that makes it his to address. Both clauses feel necessary, neither feels sufficient, and I suspect the first is misstated because of 5, 7, and 9.

What I want back: a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that a novel case could be run through it mechanically. For each clause, name the case that motivates it. Then tell me what the principle says about cases I have not raised, at minimum these: someone who blames another for a wrong he fully intends to commit tomorrow; someone who committed the same wrong but believes non-culpably that he did not; blame directed at a person long dead; two equally complicit people blaming each other simultaneously and sincerely; and whether a person can lack standing to blame himself. Finally, say plainly which of my eleven verdicts the principle cannot preserve, which single one you would have me give up, and what the cost of giving it up would be.
