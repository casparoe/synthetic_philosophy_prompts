I have spent twenty years in a furniture conservation lab, and two related puzzles keep recurring there; I have a word for neither.

The first: once a chair has had a leg, a rail, and half its seat replaced over the decades, arguments about whether it is still the same chair turn out to hinge on which question was actually being asked — whether this is the same wood, or the same chair — and the disputants almost never notice that they have switched. I assume this is adjacent to the ship of Theseus, and I know there is a standard textbook version involving a statue and the lump of clay it is made from.

The second is the one I actually want identified. The argument for counting two things where there looks to be one always has the same shape: the clay could have survived being squeezed into a ball, the statue could not, therefore they are two. Nobody ever tests that. The claims about what could have survived appear to be read straight off the way the thing was named. I watched this shape convert a single oak table into two objects during an insurance dispute, and watched it do the same to a chair and its arrangement of parts in a catalogue meeting. So the furniture of the room seems to multiply with the number of ways of describing it, and the reasoning that licenses the multiplication looks to me like swapping one name for another inside a sentence about what might have happened, which feels like a move one is not entitled to make.

Is there an established concept for this second pattern? Please name the nearest candidates and compare them, say what each fails to capture, and state which fits best; and if none fits exactly, coin a term with a definition and the boundary cases that separate it from my first puzzle and from the mere charge that people are arguing about words rather than things. A label alone will not help me — I want the carving.
