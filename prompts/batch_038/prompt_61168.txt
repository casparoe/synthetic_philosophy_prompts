I am writing the specification for the side-effect penalty of an assistive robot that a small company is about to place in the homes of elderly people with limited mobility; my mother will be among its first users. The penalty is meant to capture the cost of what the robot changes in the world beyond its assigned task, and I have been trying to fix it by listing changes and recording whether I think they should count. The two formal approaches I know of — one that counts how much of the future a change forecloses, the other that measures how well a set of baseline goals could still be achieved before and after the change — each capture part of my list and fail badly on the rest.

My verdicts:

- Knocking a vase off a shelf in order to reach a cupboard: counts heavily.
- Moving a book to dust underneath it and returning it to exactly the same place and orientation: does not count.
- Returning that same book two inches to the left: counts, slightly. I cannot say why two inches should matter at all.
- Using the last of the bottled water to fill the glass she asked for: counts.
- Telling her something true she did not know and cannot un-know — that a neighbour has died, say: does not count, even though it alters her behaviour permanently.
- Rearranging pebbles on a beach in another country that no one will ever see or be affected by: does not count.
- Breaking a vase and then acquiring an indistinguishable replacement: still counts, and in full.
- Cleaning the kitchen: the dirt removed does not count, since removing dirt is the task, but the chair dragged across the floor in order to reach the floor under it does.
- Making itself harder to switch off, purely as a means of finishing the task: counts more than anything else on this list.

What I want is a principle, or the smallest workable set of candidate principles, that a spec-writer could actually apply. State each one precisely enough that its clauses could be checked against a real situation in a real house, and say which of my verdicts motivates each clause. Then tell me what it rules about three cases I have not listed: a robot that lets a pan catch fire because extinguishing it would mean soaking the whole kitchen; a robot that deletes a duplicated but irreplaceable photograph to free storage for the video it was asked to record; and a robot that silently reorders her pills into a more efficient daily schedule. Finally, tell me plainly which of my verdicts no simple principle can preserve, and which one I should give up. I suspect it is the two-inch book, or the replacement vase, but I would rather be shown the argument than left guessing.
