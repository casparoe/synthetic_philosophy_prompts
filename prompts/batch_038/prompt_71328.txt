I've been trying to pin down when somebody loses the right to blame, just going off my gut on cases, and the verdicts are a mess. Here they are:

1. My neigbour blasts music till 2am and then complains to the building about my kids running around at 7pm. I think he can't complain. Not that the complaint is false — the kids are loud — but he's out of line making it.
2. Same neighbour, except he's actually been working on his own noise problem, put a note on the fridge, apologised to people. Now I think his complaint is fine.
3. A friend who cheated on his wife tells me not to lie to my girlfriend about where I was last night. Fine, and useful.
4. That same friend tells me cheating is unforgivable while still doing it. Not fine.
5. A politician who took bribes, got caught, did the time, and now campaigns against corruption. Fine — I really like this one.
6. A politician who took bribes, quietly paid it back, never got caught, and now campaigns against corruption. Also fine?? I want to say this is the same as 5 but something feels off and I can't say what.
7. Me telling my brother he's throwing money away on bets when I throw the same amount away on restaurants. Fine, I think.
8. My ex-wife telling me I was emotionally absent during the marriage, when she was too. Not fine — though I also think she's completely right about it.
9. Someone I actually wronged blaming me for that wrong, even if they're worse than me on the same thing generally. Always fine, I think.
10. A company's HR writing me up for padding an expense report in a firm where every executive does it. Not fine.
11. Blaming myself: always allowed, no standing problem with self-blame ever, even for the thing I'm still doing.
12. Asking a mutual friend to tell him because I can't face it. Fine, and I think what matters is her standing, not mine. Which is weird, because it lets me launder the exact thing I'm not allowed to do myself.
13. A guy who worked in a slaughterhouse for ten years, went vegan, and tells me my burger is murder. Fine.

The pair that breaks me is 5 and 6. Structurally identical — same fault, same turnaround, same message — but I want to say the caught one is legitimate and the uncaught one isn't, and I can't see why getting caught would matter morally. Also 7 against 8: both are two-guilty-people cases and I flip.

My tentative principle, which I don't trust: you can blame someone for X only if (a) you're not currently doing X yourself without owning that, (b) you're not blaming them partly to keep attention off your own X, and (c) you've actually done something about your own X rather than just intending to.

Does anything like this survive contact with the list? What I want: the principle, or two or three rival ones, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen; each clause tied to the case that forced it in; its verdicts on cases I didn't list — like blaming someone for a fault that's literally impossible for you to commit, or a group blaming one of its own members for something the group pushed him into; and a straight answer on which of my verdicts have to go to keep the principle simple. Also: is standing a property of the person, of the act of blaming, or of the relationship between the two people? Skip the names and citations, I'm after the principle, not the literature.
