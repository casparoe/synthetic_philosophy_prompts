Third year political theory PhD student here. Chapter three of my dissertation is a defence of Simone Weil's case against political parties — the short posthumous essay where she argues they should be abolished outright. But I'm not defending it on her terms, and I need to know where that leaves me exposed.

Weil's own mechanism is about the individual member's mind: the party turns its own growth into an end, and the member, wanting that growth, ends up treating truth as a function of it. She's pretty explicit that the party spirit is a corruption of the capacity for attention, and that the damage is done to thinking rather then to institutions. My reconstruction moves the mechanism. What I argue is that joining a party is entering a standing pre-commitment to treat a shifting and largely unpredictable set of questions as closed, and that the real harm isn't false beliefs held by members but a damaged agenda: questions that stop being askable inside the organisation, so the party systematically fails to register facts about thier own constituencies. A vice of agenda foreclosure, not a vice of motivated reasoning. I think this version is stronger partly because it dodges the standard political science reply that partisan cue-taking is a rational heuristic for people with no time to read policy — on my account the problem isn't that members outsource judgements they can't make, it's that certain judgements never get made by anybody.

My committee is going to attack this from every direction and I'd rather get hit now. So: what would count against it? Ranked, from "chapter three doesn't survive this" to "awkward but I can footnote my way through it." For each defeater I want to know what would actually have to be true for it to bite, how much of the thesis it takes down with it, and whether it's a live threat or merely a logically possible one. Include the empirical side, but I can't check references: if you're not confident a study or finding exists, describe the kind of evidence that would do the work instead of inventing a citation — a well-specified hypothetical finding is far more useful to me than a made-up one. Tell me too what my position predicts that the rival accounts don't, and where those predictions could come apart from what we actually see. I've already got the obvious ones in my head (parties as the vehicle through which neglected issues like prison conditions or environmental damage reached the agenda at all; the biographical point that Weil herself spent time inside left organisations in the 1930s and knew precisely what she was describing, which cuts both ways), so push past those.

And be straight with me about the soft spot I suspect: whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability. My worry is that any counterexample gets absorbed by saying a party that keeps its questions open isn't really in the grip of party spirit, so the view ends up compatible with anything. If that's right, say whether it's fatal for a thesis of this particular shape — a normative claim wrapped around an empirical mechanism — or whether insulation is more forgivable here than it would be in a purely empirical theory.

Two more asks. First, commit: I don't want a balanced survey with the decision left to me. Take a position on the ranking and on the insulation question and give your reasons. For what it's worth, the things that would move me most are (a) a finding or comparative pattern of exactly the shape my thesis says shouldn't exist — parties measurably widening what gets asked rather than narrowing it — and (b) a case where the foreclosure mechanism is clearly present but the predicted damage is absent, which would show foreclosure isn't doing the work I'm claiming for it. Second: if you think my reconstruction just isn't Weil, and I'm using her name as scaffolding for something else, say so in the first section.
