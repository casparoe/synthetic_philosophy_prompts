I spent thirty-five years as a benthic ecologist working on encrusting colonial invertebrates, and have come to philosophy of biology only since retiring. Next month I give a talk to a philosophy of biology reading group defending the following. In modular organisms — a bryozoan colony, a clonal stand of seagrass — there is no principled way to fix the boundary of a reproductive episode, and therefore no principled way to assign a fitness value to any particular level of organization. My proposal is not that the units question is indeterminate, but that the unit of selection is always relative to a chosen timescale of transmission, and that the choice is constrained rather than free: it must be anchored to features of the organism's own schedule of growth, fragmentation, and gamete release. I hold this to be neither a conventionalism nor a redescription of trait-group pluralism.

The room will contain two senior figures, one who defends the replicator–interactor framework and suspects that talk of interactors does no real work, and one who holds that selection genuinely operates at several levels and that most of the debate concerns explanatory aims; a population geneticist from the biology department who performs Price-equation decompositions as a matter of routine and is impatient with conceptual worry; and half a dozen graduate students, several of whom work on microbial consortia.

Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this particular audience will press, in the order and roughly in the words they would use them, ranked by how much damage each would do to my thesis. For each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and a frank note on where that reply is soft. Tell me also what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, which single reply must land if the talk is to survive, and the question I should most hope nobody asks. I would ask that you write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
