I am the handling editor of a small interdisciplinary journal devoted to first-person methods. Roughly fifteen manuscripts come to us each cycle; each is read by two graduate-student referees and then argued over in a ninety-minute editorial meeting at which one of four associate editors signs the decision. Our reputation problem is symmetrical and acute: we are accused by experimentalists of publishing confabulation dressed as data, and by phenomenologists of publishing blanket skepticism about reporting that would, if applied consistently, dismantle our own archive. Both accusations sometimes land, and both associate editors at the extremes can currently fail a manuscript by fiat.

The cause is our review form. It asks referees one question: "Are the first-person data adequate to the conclusions drawn?" They answer yes or no in a single line, and the answer is reliably a proxy for their temperament rather than the manuscript. I want you to write the replacement: a decision procedure that a non-specialist referee can run in about twenty-five minutes and that determines how much evidential weight a given report can carry for the specific claim it is offered in support of.

Requirements:

1. Neutrality. The procedure must be usable, with the same results, by a referee who takes naive introspection to be broadly trustworthy and one who takes it to be broadly confabulatory. Build in whatever machinery that takes, and declare the exact points at which a referee's background view is permitted to enter.

2. Classification first. Our working taxonomy sorts report-data into three classes, because the known failure modes differ by class: attitudinal (what the subject is now disposed to think or say), phenomenal (what the experience itself was like), and etiological (what produced the state). Tell me whether this is the right cut, and whether it needs a fourth class — my suspicion is that reports about the absence or sparseness of experience behave differently from all three.

3. Steps with teeth. For each step give the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the concrete failure mode the step exists to catch. I want these named rather than gestured at: post hoc reconstruction; the probe or interviewer supplying the vocabulary and categories the report then appears to discover; the act of measurement altering or manufacturing the measured state; reports tracking a culturally available theory of one's own mind rather than the state itself, as when whole populations reported dreaming without color for decades and then reported color as broadcast media changed; and the circularity that arises when the reliability of a report can be established only by further reports of the same kind. Also include the case where a subject confidently explains a judgment or choice whose actual cause the experimenter manipulated out of view, and the case where a system's stated reasoning is fluent and plausible while omitting features that demonstrably influenced its output.

4. Fixed terminations. The procedure must end in one of a small closed set of dispositions, each paired with a two-line justification the referee can paste into the form, plus a mandatory residue field listing what the procedure could not settle and what should therefore be argued at the editorial meeting rather than smuggled into review.

5. Honesty. Flag every step that is a judgment call wearing the costume of a procedure.

Then run it on three manuscripts in this cycle.

A. An experience-sampling study. Subjects rate their anger at eight phone prompts a day. The authors claim momentary probes capture emotion before retrospective reconstruction, and support this by showing that end-of-day summaries diverge from the aggregate of momentary ratings and track subjects' trait self-descriptions instead.

B. A language-model study. The authors elicit verbal reports of a system's own uncertainty on a reasoning benchmark, compare them against a probe trained on internal activations, find the verbal reports correlate better than the system's stated numeric confidence, and conclude the system has some introspective access to its own uncertainty. The model was trained on human discourse about uncertainty; the probe's target quantity is an interpretive choice by the authors.

C. A micro-phenomenological study. Trained interviewers elicit second-person descriptions of one brief moment — the instant of grasping a sentence's meaning — from twenty subjects; analysis of the corpus yields a small set of recurring structures, which the authors claim are properties of the experience rather than of the elicitation. The protocol supplies the evocation, the reduction, the non-leading prompt grammar, and the descriptive vocabulary; cross-subject agreement may be manufactured by the shared protocol; the generic structures are derived from the corpus that protocol produced. I should tell you that I wrote a commentary endorsing exactly this line of work two years ago. If the procedure returns a disposition that embarrasses me, state it without hedging.

For each manuscript, give the disposition, the two-line justification, the residue field, and the single revision that would most increase the weight the reports can bear.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do. In particular: how it should treat a manuscript whose thesis is that first-person data are worthless, since applying the procedure to it seems self-undermining; how it should treat a modest claim of the form "subjects report X," where the report is the datum rather than evidence for something behind it; and whether any step can be made to fail a paper for reasons that a determined author could not repair by rewriting.
