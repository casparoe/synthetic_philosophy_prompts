The examination was sat under two-hour, closed-book conditions. Students were permitted one unmarked extract sheet, containing the opening sections of the Principles of Human Knowledge, the sections in which the argument under discussion appears, and the corresponding exchange from the first of the Three Dialogues. There was no word limit. The question, worth 25 marks, was as follows:

*Berkeley holds that the notion of a sensible object existing unperceived is not merely false but unintelligible. (a) Reconstruct the argument he gives for this claim in the Principles, setting out its structure explicitly. (b) Identify the premise on which the argument turns, and explain what work that premise is doing. (c) State the strongest reply available to a defender of mind-independent objects, and assess whether that reply succeeds.*

The script I want you to mark runs to about 650 words and is complete; the candidate did not attempt anything further. It reads as follows.

Berkeley's target throughout the Principles is the doctrine he describes near the outset as strangely prevailing among men: that houses, mountains and rivers, and sensible things generally, have a real existence distinct from their being perceived. His own thesis about such things is that their being consists in being perceived. Two things need saying before the argument itself. First, Berkeley is not denying that tables and trees exist; he denies that there is any material substance underlying our ideas of them or causing them, and he holds that this makes his position the commonsense one, with scepticism properly laid at the door of his opponents. Second, he does not deny substance altogether. Minds or spirits are substances, and they are the only active things there are: ideas are inert and cannot bring anything about, so the cause of those ideas of sense that I do not will must be a spirit other than myself, and ultimately God, whose perception is what keeps things in being when no created spirit perceives them, and whose regular manner of producing ideas is what we mean by the laws of nature.

The argument usually called Berkeley's master argument is his way of closing off Locke's escape route. Locke wished to retain the primary qualities — extension, figure, motion, solidity — in objects themselves while conceding that colours, tastes and sounds belong only to the mind. Berkeley's reply is that the primary qualities cannot be conceived apart from the secondary ones: one cannot frame an idea of an extended, moving thing that has no colour whatever, and to try is to produce an idea that is, in fact, coloured or invisible in some determinate way. That is the whole of the master argument, and once it is granted the doctrine of a material substratum has nowhere left to stand. Berkeley puts the matter as a challenge rather than as a proof: let the materialist imagine trees standing in a park, or books shut in a closet, with nobody by to perceive them. He can certainly produce ideas of trees and books while omitting any idea of a perceiver; but that is all he has done, for in imagining them he was himself perceiving them, and he has not conceived those objects existing unperceived. The argument first appears in the Three Dialogues of 1713, where Philonous presses it on Hylas, and Berkeley afterwards restated it more compactly in the Principles of 1710.

The premise on which the argument turns is that whatever cannot be conceived is impossible. This makes the argument a modest, purely epistemological one: it establishes a limit on what can be thought, not a limit on what there is, and it therefore leaves open whether unperceived matter exists. Berkeley accordingly concludes that matter neither does nor can exist, and he thinks that having shown this he has secured everything that commonsense requires. The stock reply is that he equivocates on conceiving, since in one plain sense we do conceive of an unperceived tree when we describe it as a tree that nobody perceives, and Berkeley has merely assumed that this sense is unavailable to him.

I take the reply to be the stronger, though not for the reason usually given, because Berkeley concedes the point himself: he allows that we can form ideas of trees and books while omitting any idea of a perceiver, which is to admit that we can represent to ourselves an object nobody is perceiving. With that admission on the table the argument reduces to a dispute over a word, and what survives of the system is the dispositional analysis Berkeley sketches early in the Principles, on which to say that the table in my study exists is to say that were I in the study I would perceive it. God's role, on this reading, is to underwrite the truth of such conditionals; Berkeley also speaks of things subsisting in the mind of an eternal spirit, and the two remarks sit comfortably together, but it is the conditional that is doing the work.
