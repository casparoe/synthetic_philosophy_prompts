You are, for the purposes of this request, a philosopher of language who specialises in speech acts and the epistemology of assertion, and who is used to explaining fine structural distinctions to graduate students without dissolving them into a survey of the literature. Please think the problem through carefully before you write anything polished: I would rather have a rigorous, tightly organised explanation than a comprehensive one.

I am a second-year master's student currently taking a seminar on the norms of assertion. We have covered the familiar ground — the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the belief norm, the lottery and Gettier-style pressure cases against the latter two, the objection that the truth norm issues permissions no speaker could ever act on, and the stock cases designed to embarrass the knowledge norm (the speaker who asserts what the evidence overwhelmingly supports but does not believe, and the teacher who asserts what she does not believe because it is what her students need to hear). I can reproduce all of that. What I cannot get straight is a narrower and, I suspect, more fundamental question about the division of labour between the norm that governs the assertion and the norm that governs the decision to assert. Our seminar leader called these the primary and secondary norms (I believe the labels are Williamson's, but please work with the distinction rather than the provenance, and correct me if I have the wrong source).

So that we are reasoning about the same material, here are two cases I have constructed. Please take them exactly as stipulated rather than replacing them with better-known ones.

Case A (Guess). A colleague catches Ada in the corridor and asks whether Seminar Room 3 is free at 3 p.m. Ada has no evidence either way and no time to check, but she also cannot decline to answer: if she says nothing, the colleague cancels the session and a dozen people lose the only appointment slot they have. So she says, "It's free," as a pure guess. It is in fact free.

Case B (Misleading Higher-Order Evidence). Bea reads a value off a laboratory instrument. The instrument is in perfect working order and the reading is accurate, and — stipulate this for now — Bea's resulting belief amounts to knowledge. Unbeknownst to her, however, an apparently authoritative message from the lab director, which was forged by a disgruntled former student, states that the instrument has been silently miscalibrated since Monday and that every reading it produces is wrong by a factor of two. Bea has no way of detecting the forgery. She reports the reading to her supervisor.

With those two cases in hand, please do the following, in this order.

1. Before analysing anything, brainstorm in short compass: enumerate the candidate jobs a primary norm of assertion might be doing — constituting the practice, setting a standard of correctness, licensing the audience's challenge to produce one's warrant, determining whether a retraction is owed, generating or withholding blame, and so on. For each candidate job, say in a clause whether a well-chosen secondary norm could discharge it instead.

2. Then address the structural worry directly. If the truth norm and the knowledge norm are each supplemented by a secondary norm telling speakers to assert only what their total evidence supports, is there any case in which the two packages give different advice about what a speaker ought to do? If not, what residue is left for the primary norm to explain, and is that residue philosophically substantive or merely a difference in bookkeeping?

3. Then run Cases A and B through the machinery. For each case, under each of the three candidate norms, give me a compact table with at least four separate verdicts: was the assertion correct; was it permissible; was the speaker blameless; is a retraction owed, or is the audience's challenge to produce a warrant legitimate? Where the two theories allocate the same verdicts, say so explicitly; where they diverge, say precisely which intuition each divergence is meant to respect and what it costs.

4. In Case B, please treat both readings: first on the stipulation that Bea's knowledge survives the misleading evidence, and then on the rival view that undefeated misleading higher-order evidence is itself knowledge-defeating. I want to know whether the choice between those readings changes anything about the structural question in part 2, or whether it merely relocates the problem.

5. Finally, tell me whether this structural worry is a genuine objection to the truth norm, or whether it threatens the knowledge norm just as much, and flag the two or three steps in your own reasoning that a partisan of each side would contest.

Format and constraints: roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words. Do not provide references, a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I have no library access this week and I do not want plausible-looking guesses. Do not spend any space re-introducing the three norms or re-narrating the standard objections; assume I have those. Use my two cases rather than substituting canonical ones, and where you think a stipulation in a case is doing suspicious work, say so rather than silently revising it.
