I am about to circulate a draft platform statement on electoral reform to a working group, and before other people start pulling at it I would like a consistency audit of the position it commits me to. The statement defends three reforms jointly — extending the franchise to sixteen-year-olds, extending it to long-term resident non-citizens, and making voting compulsory — on the ground that they stand or fall together. These are my commitments:

1. A single principle fixes the demos, and only one: anyone subject to the coercive law of a polity has a claim to a say in making that law. Nationality, allegiance, and shared cultural membership are not independent grounds of enfranchisement; at most they are proxies for subjection, and unreliable ones.

2. Competence is not a legitimate condition of the franchise. The argument I rely on is the familiar anti-epistocrat one: there is no neutral specification of the political knowledge that would be relevant, every competence test actually administered has functioned as a device for excluding the already disadvantaged, and the claimed epistemic gains of a restricted electorate are not demonstrable.

3. It follows that the voting age should be sixteen, in every election including constitutional referendums. I draw the line at sixteen rather than lower because that is roughly when a person has entered the bundle of legal and economic relations that subjection consists in: liable to tax on earnings, triable as an adult in serious cases, able to work, able to leave school at the end of that year. I want to be clear that I do not take this to be a competence claim and I resist its being read as one; it is a claim about which legal relations a person stands in, which is exactly what (1) is about.

4. It follows likewise that long-term resident non-citizens should vote in national elections. They are subject to the law, and exit is not available to them at tolerable cost.

5. Voting should be compulsory, enforced by a modest fixed fine and nothing more. The obligation, however, should attach only to citizens aged eighteen and over. My reason for compulsion is that voluntary turnout is skewed by income and education, and that an electorate which systematically over-represents the advantaged does not treat those subject to it as equals. But sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds would hold the vote without the duty, because a fine levied on a dependent minor is in practice a fine on their household; and long-term resident non-citizens would hold the vote without the duty, because legally obliging someone to participate in a polity that retains the power to deport them strikes me as indefensible.

6. My justification of democracy is procedural rather than instrumental. Legitimacy derives from the fairness of the process that produces a decision, not from that process's tendency to produce correct or good decisions. I do not think I owe anyone a demonstration that a wider or more evenly distributed electorate chooses better, and I would prefer not to rest anything on such a demonstration.

7. There is no separate right not to vote. I do not think a liberty-of-conscience interest in abstention can outweigh the interest each person has in an electorate that reflects the composition of the population subject to it. I nonetheless want a formally counted none-of-the-above line on the ballot, since under compulsion that is the only way a voter's dissent is distinguishable from indifference or from an inability to choose.

8. A verdict on a case. Suppose a jurisdiction adopted the whole package and its first election produced turnout of 91 per cent among the obliged citizens, 34 per cent among the newly enfranchised sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds, and 29 per cent among resident non-citizens; and suppose a careful post-election study found the composition and agenda of the resulting legislature statistically indistinguishable from those produced under the previous voluntary, citizens-only, eighteen-plus regime. I think this outcome counts as a success for the reform package and that no adjustment is called for.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey of the field. Identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to, and say so explicitly in both directions — I would rather not have merely apparent problems flagged alongside real ones. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity instead of presenting them as a list of equal complaints, name the numbered commitments involved in each, and state the smallest revision that would remove it together with what that revision would cost me. I have no prior about which of (1) through (8) I would sacrifice, so please do not assume I am attached to any of them; equally, do not simply recommend abandoning the package. Where a tension can only be closed by an empirical claim, identify which claim is doing the work and whether the rest of my position entitles me to it. Finally, tell me whether some single revision repairs more than one of the problems at once, and what would be lost by making it.
