ok so for this assignment I have to do something on whether NOT doing something can literally cause something, and I'd way rather write a dialogue than another boring essay, so here's what I need.

Two people arguing. One thinks causation is always actual stuff going on — energy moving, a process you could in principle trace from cause to effect — so an absence can't be a cause because there's nothing there to trace. The other thinks that's completely missing the point: causation is difference-making, if the world would've gone differently without it then it's a cause, full stop, and demanding "oomph" is just physics envy dressed up as metaphysics. Both of them are sharp and neither one is allowed to be a pushover or get talked into anything cheap. Give them normal names, not "the process theorist" and "the counterfactual theorist." They should interrupt each other and get a little annoyed, no lecturing.

Here are my three cases, and they should actually work through them in order instead of just gesturing at the general problem:

1. My friend's family had a smoke detector with a battery going dead. The dad yanked the battery out because it chirped at 3am and he was gonna replace it "tomorrow," then forgot for like a week. Kitchen fire in the night, nobody woke up until it was way past where a hose would've done anything. Both characters have to say whether the missing battery is a cause of the house burning down or just part of the background conditions. The oomph person especially has to deal with the fact that everybody — the fire report, the insurance adjuster, my friend's mom — talks about that battery like it caused the thing.

2. Twist on it: suppose the detector was fried anyway, dead wiring, so it would not have sounded even with a brand new battery, and the fire moved so fast that an alarm would've bought them a minute at most. So taking the battery out made no difference to the outcome at all. Does that mean it wasn't a cause? Be careful here, because you'd think this is a win for the oomph person and I'm not convinced it is — make them figure out who this case actually hurts.

3. The one I cant figure out. If absences are causes, why is the dad's failure a cause but not the failure of some random guy three states over who's never heard of this family to drive down with a fire extinguisher? Neither of them did anything. There's no difference in the "there's nothing there to trace" department, and there's no difference in the "the world would've been better" department either, because the guy three states over couldn't have gotten there in time anyway but also never even thought about it. So the difference-making person owes me a principled line, and the oomph person owes me an explanation of why one of these absences feels cause-y and the other feels like nothing at all if neither of them has any process in it.

Then I have my own idea and I want them to genuinely attack it, not be polite about it: maybe an absence is only a real cause relative to what was supposed to happen. Detectors are supposed to sound. Dads who pull batteries out are supposed to put them back. Random guys in other states aren't supposed to do anything, so their non-action isn't an absence in the relevant sense, it's just nothing happening. So maybe causation by omission is real but always smuggles in a norm about what's expected, which is why it feels weirder than ordinary causation. Have the two of them temporarily team up on this — does it actually dissolve case 3 or just move the problem somewhere else? Does it collapse into one of their views, or is it a third thing? And can they come up with a case where the norm-based rule gives a clearly wrong answer?
