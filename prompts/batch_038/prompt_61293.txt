I'm drafting a paper for a political theory workshop and I want comments on one half-finished section — mainly whether the central example is doing what I think it's doing, or whether I've talked myself into something trivial. Be blunt, and don't bother summarizing my own argument back at me.

Here's the context you need, then the section, then my questions.

**The framing.** Everyone knows the jury theorem story: n voters, each independently right with probability p > 1/2 about some binary question, majority decides, accuracy goes to 1 as n grows. The standard objections get their own headings — p might be below 1/2, the votes aren't independent, or there's no fact of the matter to be right about. My claim is there's a fourth objection that usually gets folded into the independence one, and that it shouldn't be, because it survives even when you fix independence up. I'm calling it the *constitutive* objection: for a certain class of political questions, the fact the jury is tracking is partly made out of the jurors' beliefs about it, so aggregating doesn't just reveal the fact, it edits it.

The clean cases are expectation-driven ones — bank runs, currency crises, whether a protest movement "wins," whether a language survives, whether a platform collapses under an exodus. Enough people believe the run is coming, and it comes. The correct answer is a function of the distribution of answers.

**The mechanism.** Suppose a fraction q of the population gets a genuine private signal with accuracy r, and everyone else has no signal at all but can see the running tally — the polls, the queue outside the branch, the vibe. The signalless voters aren't random noise, they're followers of the aggregate. That's correlation, fine, but a specific kind: the correlation is generated by the aggregation procedure itself, not by some shared background the voters brought with them. So you get a fixed-point problem where you were expecting a limit theorem. Two things I want to claim:

1. Monotonicity in n fails, and fails *earlier* than in the ordinary correlated-vote case, because followers scale with n while signalers don't. Growing the jury mostly grows the follower bloc.
2. There's a lock-in: past the point where followers outnumber signalers, a wrong majority becomes self-confirming, and the accuracy curve isn't just flat, it's locally unfixable by giving the followers more information. You have to raise r or q. n is the lever that stopped working.

**The section I actually want comments on.** A town of 1000 deciding whether to pull their deposits. Forty of them have real information about the bank's books — say r = 0.8, and suppose they're right that it's solvent. The other 960 decide by counting how many neighbours are already in the queue. Suppose withdrawals past roughly a third of the town sink the bank no matter what was in the books. So the truth value of "the bank is sound" flips at a threshold that the voting process itself can cross.

The bit I'm pleased with and suspicious of in equal measure: the forty informed voters were right, p was comfortably above 1/2, and their votes were as independent as you like — the theorem still fails, because the theorem was pointed at the wrong proposition. What the town needed to vote on was a conditional: *is the bank sound if we don't run?* And that's not a proposition anyone can act on collectively, because acting on it is what would have made it true. My move is that the constitutive objection isn't about the voters' competence, it's about the description under which the question was put to them.
