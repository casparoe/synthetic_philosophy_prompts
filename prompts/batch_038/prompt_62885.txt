I help write the triage module for a regional search-and-rescue outfit — I'm a paramedic, not a philosopher, I got drafted onto it because I apparently like arguing. We've got a couple of academics on the advisory board, and one of them said something during a session on allocating a single helicopter hoist that's been rattling around in my head for a week. His line: Taurek's coin toss isn't a refusal to let the numbers count, it's the numbers counting probabilistically instead of deterministically. Give every trapped person one ticket, draw one ticket out of the pot, and the five collectively hold five of the six, so they get a five-in-six chance — that's what "flip a coin between the one and the five" really *is*, just described at the group level. On his telling the whole Taurek-versus-the-aggregators fight is a pseudo-disagreement, because both sides want the five to be more likely to survive and they're only quibbling about how much more likely. He also said this makes the coin flip respectable to policy people, since it's "a lottery, and we already run lotteries."

Here's what I think is wrong with that, or at least what I want checked. Our actual protocol can't work that way, because the aircraft goes to a *site*, not to a person. If there's one climber pinned on a ledge in the north ravine and five hikers on a sandbar downriver and we can only do one, we randomize between the two sites and the odds are 50/50 — the five get no extra tickets, because the ticket is the sortie, not the survivor. When I raised this he said fine, that's a coarse implementation, philosophically it's the same lottery. I don't think it is, and I don't think the two stay equivalent once you stop looking at a single case. Run a one-ticket-per-person lottery every day for a year and the bigger sites come out ahead most days, so the annual body count lands very close to what a straight maximize-the-number policy would have produced — the "equal chance" version looks like it collapses back into the thing Taurek was objecting to, just spread out over time. Run 50/50 site flips for a year and that doesn't happen at all. Neither version lets anyone be compensated afterwards, which feels like another place the raffle picture is doing quiet work, since in a real raffle the losers aren't dead, they just didn't win.

So I'd like you to treat "it's a raffle with one ticket per person" as the analogy it is and audit how far it holds. Concretely: lay out the mapping explicitly — what plays the ticket, the pot, the draw, the prize, the fact that the prize is indivisible, the person who runs the draw — and be clear about which slot the *site* occupies, since that's the one I think breaks the thing. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just decoration (I'm sure some of what I listed above is a red herring, tell me which). Tell me what the raffle quietly imports that a rescue case lacks — voluntary entry, a common pot, the possibility of a rerun, losers who are no worse off than if there'd been no draw, chances that can be traded or sold — and whether any of those imports are load-bearing for "pseudo-disagreement." Then: does the collapse-into-maximizing point I made actually go through, or is there a difference between what a policy does ex ante over many cases and what it does in the one case in front of you? And say what you think the right description of Taurek's own proposal is — equal chance per person, or fifty-fifty between the two options — and flag that you may be unsure, because I've seen it argued both ways and I can't tell if the difference is textual or just people reading their own view in. If the raffle is the wrong picture, tell me what would be a better one, even a rougher one.

Please answer as a moral philosopher who's actually worked on allocation policy rather than only on the papers — think it through before you commit, and if it helps, brainstorm the mapping informally first and then tighten it up. No bibliography and no reading list, I can't look anything up and I don't want guessed-at references; I want the reasoning laid out so I could take it back to the board meeting. Last thing: tell me how confident you are in your overall verdict, and single out the one step in it you're least sure of.
