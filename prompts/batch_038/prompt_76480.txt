I have been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my own commitments in advance of a workshop on higher-order evidence and disagreement, so that the sessions can be organized around the actual fault lines between participants rather than around introductions to the literature. My dissertation sits at the intersection of that literature and the epistemology of machine informants, which is why the list below mixes fairly standard items with two I have arrived at on my own. I would like a consistency audit before I send it out.

Here is the list, in the form I intend to circulate it:

1. Uniqueness. For any total body of evidence and any proposition, at most one doxastic attitude toward that proposition is rationally permitted. I am not a permissivist.

2. Evidential internalism. Whether a belief is rational is settled entirely by the agent's total evidence. A process's being reliable, or unreliable, makes no difference to the rationality of its outputs unless the agent has evidence about that reliability.

3. Higher-order defeat is real. There are cases in which a person's evidence bearing directly on a proposition is untouched, but she acquires strong evidence that her own reasoning on that question is presently untrustworthy — a reliable informant tells her that she is mildly hypoxic at altitude and that her arithmetic goes wrong at altitude, or that she has recently taken something that distorts a certain kind of inference — and in such cases the belief her first-order evidence supports is not rational for her to keep holding.

4. The Enkratic Requirement. It is never rationally permitted to believe P while believing that one's evidence does not support believing P. I intend this as a requirement on combinations of states rather than a wide-scope requirement that can be discharged by dropping either member: an akratic combination is always a defective one.

5. No level-splitting. I reject the diagnosis on which the subject in a defeat case ends up rationally believing P while also rationally believing that believing P is irrational for her. I take that combination to be ruled out by (4), and I think any theory that licenses it has gone wrong somewhere, though I would not want to say in advance where.

6. Conciliationism, with an independence condition. On learning that an acknowledged epistemic peer disagrees with her about P, an agent should move her confidence substantially toward the peer's, and not only because the disagreement is evidence about P. But she owes this conciliation only where the peer's judgment is independent of her own: no significant common causal ancestry between the processes that produced the two assessments. People who trained in the same program, read the same texts, and absorbed the same intellectual culture are not independent in the relevant sense, and I would not treat them as full peers.

7. Machines can be informants. A well-calibrated, well-informed model that I consult and that disagrees with me about P counts, for the purposes of (6), as an epistemic peer. That it is not a person makes no difference to the norms governing the disagreement.

8. Rational polarization is possible and blameless. Two people exposed to all the same public evidence and all the same arguments over a long period can rationally end up far apart about P, and neither need be guilty of any epistemic failing.

What I want from you:

- Work through the list pair by pair where it matters and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way. I expect that some of these will appear flatly contradictory to a reader and turn out to be compatible once a term is pinned down; I would much rather you said so than padded the audit with pseudo-problems.
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to give: which of the two commitments is the cheaper surrender, and what that surrender costs me elsewhere in the list or in the work it was doing for me.
- Flag any term that is doing covert work — total evidence, rational, peer, independent, reasoning — such that the tension appears or dissolves depending on a reading I have not spelled out. Say which reading I am implicitly relying on.
- Tell me which single revision to the list would eliminate the largest number of tensions at once, whether you would actually recommend it, and what the resulting position loses.
- Tell me which commitment you expect me to defend most stubbornly once you flag it, and whether that stubbornness is a good reason or a bad one.
- If the package as a whole is one that no careful person should want to be caught holding, say that plainly rather than distributing the blame evenly.

Two ground rules. First, no bibliography, no reading list, no attributions to named philosophers or papers: I want the reasoning, and I know the literature well enough to track down the sources myself once I know what to look for. Second, where your verdict on a given pair depends on a claim that specialists in this area genuinely dispute — as opposed to one that is settled or close to settled — mark it as disputed. Several of my commitments are minority positions, and I would rather have an audit that is honest about which of its own moves are contested than one that reads smoothly by treating a live debate as closed.
