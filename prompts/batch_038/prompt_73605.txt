So — I've got bipolar I, been diagnosed about six years now, and at my last stable-as-anything checkup my psychiatrist handed me a form and said I should fill it out. It's basically a "if I go off the rails later, here's what to do" document. On it you write which meds have worked for you before, which hospital you'd rather go to, who they're allowed to call, and — this is the part I keep turning over — you can pre-authorize them to treat you even if, at the time, you're flatly refusing and telling everyone you're fine. I've had two manic episodes where I was absolutely convinced I didn't need anything, and I'd have signed a paper saying "please don't listen to me right now" if anyone had offered it.

But the same form also says you can change it or cancel it whenever you want, just by saying so. And there's no separate procedure for deciding whether the person doing the cancelling is "really" competent at that moment — it's basically the doctors on the ward who judge that, the same doctors the form is supposed to be tying down.

Here's what I find weird, and I want you to dig into it rather than give me a brochure. Over in regular medicine, if a competent adult says no to a blood transfusion or to chemo, that's it — nobody can force them, even when the refusal kills them. We treat the current "no" as basically sacred. And on the other hand, when someone dies we honor their will about their house and their money for years afterwards, and nobody thinks the dead person's present wishes matter at all. So the same legal/medical system treats a person's earlier word as worthless in one place, as controlling in another, and as controlling-but-conditioned-on-a-capacity-judgment in my case.
