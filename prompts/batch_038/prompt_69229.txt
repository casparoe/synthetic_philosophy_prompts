A review in a general-interest magazine paraphrases the argument of Samuel Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* roughly as follows: 'Scheffler asks you to suppose that you will live out a normal life span, but that the Earth and everyone on it will be destroyed about a month after you die. He observes that most of us find that prospect more appalling than the prospect of our own death, and infers that we therefore care more about the survival of strangers who do not yet exist than about our own continued existence. This is essentially Parfit's argument: Parfit had already shown that whether my death is bad for me depends on whether there are future people, and Scheffler's contribution was to extend the point, revealing that our fear of dying rests on a confusion, since what we are really afraid of is not our own nonexistence but the end of the species. The upshot is a philosophical foundation for treating the reduction of extinction risk as an overriding duty, one that can demand substantial sacrifices from the living; and because the afterlife conjecture is an empirical psychological hypothesis, further survey research could in principle confirm or refute it.'
