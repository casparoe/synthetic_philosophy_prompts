My friend Maya has this whole system and I can't figure out what it actually is. We're juniors and we got hooked on this question after our ethics teacher assigned an article arguing that a perfectly moral life might be a worse life to live than an ordinary one, because of everything you'd have to give up to get there — no time for the stuff that isn't about helping anyone. Maya took that and made a spreadsheet of real people she's read about, and she gives each one two separate verdicts: is this a "saint life" (yes/no), and would she want that life for herself (yes/no). So far she's filled in five rows. A hospice nurse who worked like sixty hours a week, never married, gave away most of her paycheck, and said in an interview that she was happier than anybody she knew: saint life yes, would want it yes. A billionaire who gave away nearly his whole fortune to whatever global health charities the numbers said were most cost effective, but who told reporters he never really thought about individual sick people, just totals, and who spent his weekends restoring old motorcycles: saint life no, would want it no. A mom who volunteers at a food bank every Saturday and plays cello seriously in an amateur orchestra and freely admits she'd skip a shift for her kid's recital: saint life no, would want it yes. A guy who dropped out of med school in his third year to work in a refugee camp, who is obviously doing way more good than the cello mom, and who admits some nights he misses being a doctor: saint life yes, would want it no. And a surgeon who does tons of pro bono operations and gives away most of her free time, but who says the reason she went into surgery in the first place is that she loves the craft and the puzzle of it and would still be doing it even if it did nobody any good: saint life "kind of but not a clean yes," would want it yes.

The new case she won't commit to, and she's been dodging it for like a week: a documentary filmmaker who has spent fifteen years making films about wrongful convictions, and whose films have actually gotten people out of prison. She works constantly, no hobbies, no friends outside her crew. But she cares enormously about the filmmaking as filmmaking — framing, sound, how a scene is shaped — and she's said she resents that the movie she most wants to make is one about her grandmother's garden with no message in it at all. She's also said that if her films stopped getting anybody released she'd probably still make them, just for the sake of other people's stories.

So: what do you think she says on each of the two verdicts, and why?
