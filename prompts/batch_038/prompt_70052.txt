So there's this case I keep seeing dragged in both directions in seminars and I can't get it to sit still — Board of Education of the Kiryas Joel Village School District v. Grumet, 1994. Facts as I've got them, correct me if I've mangled something: Kiryas Joel is a village in the town of Monroe in Orange County, New York, incorporated in the late seventies, populated essentially entirely by Satmar Hasidim, with village affairs answerable in practice to rabbinic authority. Satmar parents whose children had disabilities were getting special-ed services through the larger Monroe-Woodbury district, but pulled the kids out because of what they described as the trauma of being in a setting with non-Satmar children. New York's legislature then passed a one-off statute carving out a new public school district whose boundaries were exactly the village, which let the village run its own school, receive state special-education money, and contract out for services it didn't provide itself. Taxpayers led by Grumet sued. The Court struck the statute down 6-3: Souter for the majority, with the core complaint being that the state had handed civic and political power to an electorate defined by a common religious belief, compounded by the fact that this was a bespoke grant with no neutral criterion the legislature had committed to applying to the next community that asked; Scalia dissenting hard that the Court was manufacturing a principle hostile to accommodation; Stevens concurring, with Blackmun and Ginsburg, on the narrower ground that drawing a political boundary around a group identified by its belief is itself the constitutional problem; Kennedy concurring in the judgment on the basis that the state could have accommodated these children in a neutral way without a new district.

I know the holding and I know the case law isn't the interesting part. What I want is the thing run carefully through the communitarian-versus-liberal debate, in both directions, and I want it disciplined rather than vibes.

First, set the positions up precisely enough that they can do work, and then say what each actually demands here. I mean: Sandel on the self as constituted by its attachments rather than prior to them; Taylor on recognition and on communities that want to survive and reproduce themselves rather than merely be left alone; Kymlicka on societal culture and his external-protection versus internal-restriction distinction; Kukathas on freedom of association and exit; and on the other side, political liberalism's idea of a reasonable comprehensive doctrine and neutrality of justification. My suspicion, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that most of these have no clean verdict on this case and that the ones that do are getting the verdict from somewhere they don't announce.

Second, do the mapping honestly. Who is the agent? The village, the parents, the dissident families, the legislature? What was the real option set — was Kennedy's "accommodate them neutrally inside the existing district" genuinely available, or is it a polite way of saying the community never gets to hold the pen? And what counts as evidence for the Court's key empirical premise, that the village electorate and the religious group are the same thing?

Third, the knot that's actually bugging me. The Satmar were not asking for toleration and they were not asking for an exemption, which is what nearly every political theorist writing about them assumes. They asked for jurisdiction: a public school district, meaning a slice of the state's taxing and coercive apparatus with borders drawn around a group defined by a comprehensive doctrine. Which side has the resources to grant that? Communitarian theory talks endlessly about recognition but I cannot find anywhere in it that says what follows when recognition means a border, a taxing base, and a membership rule. Liberalism looks like it has a clean answer — Kymlicka says a religious enclave isn't a societal culture so no self-government rights, Kukathas says association yes but jurisdiction no — except I worry that answer is just neutrality restated as a conclusion with a theory citation attached. Argue it out and tell me where each side actually breaks.

Fourth, the episode biting back. Scalia, a conservative originalist, is the one making the accommodationist argument that sounds communitarian; Stevens, a liberal, is the one arguing that a community may not be allowed to define itself politically. The judicial alignment doesn't track the theoretical axis at all. Does that show the axis is a philosophers' construct that misdescribes what these people were fighting about — federalism, denominational preference, the collapse of the Lemon framework — with the communitarian vocabulary just borrowed for flavor? Or is there a real sense in which Scalia and Stevens are on the sides the theory predicts and the surface labels are misleading? I want you to pick a reading and defend it, not give me both.

Finally, be blunt about what the framing is silent on. The internal-restriction stuff: who counts as a member of the village, what happens to a Satmar family that dissents, and whether the children who were withdrawn ever got a say in any of this. Can the liberal-communitarian debate even pose those questions, or does it need to import something else — a theory of exit, an account of the child's own interest, some story about internal minorities — to get to them? Say where you're filling in with your own judgment rather than reading it off the theories.

One more thing if you can use it: my recollection is that the legislature responded by passing a general law letting villages form school districts on facially neutral criteria, and the village's district survived on that footing. If that's roughly right, does the second statute show liberalism absorbing the communitarian demand by running it through a neutral form, or does it show that the neutral form was doing the substantive work all along and Souter's objection was about drafting rather than principle?
