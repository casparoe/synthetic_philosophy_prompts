I co-chair a session at an AI-alignment workshop on how formal idealizations of human deliberation are supposed to earn their keep, and I have been asked to circulate in advance the document our two invited respondents should have written together rather than trading papers. They have been talking past each other for two years. The first camp defends the factored-cognition picture: a hard question is one a human cannot answer reliably in a single sitting, and deliberation is the recursive decomposition of that question into sub-questions each individually easier to answer, with the same procedure applied afresh at every node; the idealization is the HCH fixed point, a human answering a question by consulting an idealized copy of that very same process, indefinitely. On this view the decomposition tree is not an illustration of deliberation but its structure, and the normative question about what one would conclude under unlimited careful reflection is cashed out as what the fixed point says. The second camp holds that the idealization distorts whatever it was meant to capture. Its objection is not that the tree is too demanding or too permissive in practice, but that the picture assumes what deliberation was supposed to explain: that the sub-questions are determinate in advance of being answered, that a node's output can be checked against something independent of the reasoning that produced it, that articulating a chain of reasoning yields a faithful record of it, and that refinement converges instead of branching into rival results depending on which decomposition was tried first. Real deliberation, on the second view, alters the deliberator's concepts and standards in mid-course, so the nodes are not the same questions at the end of the process as they were at the beginning.

Draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered agreed-claims section — call it a dozen claims, though fewer is acceptable if every one is load-bearing — in which each claim is substantive enough that a neutral reader can imagine a thoughtful person refusing to sign it; no shared platitudes about everyone wanting reasoning to be reliable. Then a disagreements table, one row per residual disagreement, with columns for the disagreement stated neutrally, the wording Camp A would accept for its own side, the wording Camp B would accept for its own side, and your judgment of whether that row is substantive, partly verbal, or wholly verbal. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one camp. I expect at least four. For each crux, give a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument that both camps would agree bears on it — not one that only makes sense inside a single camp's framework — and record each camp's prediction in advance, in a form specific enough that it could come out wrong. Two cruxes I would put money on are whether refinement is confluent, that is, whether two locally impeccable decompositions of the same question are guaranteed to terminate in the same answer, and whether the fixed point can be turned on its own generating question, so that HCH is made to deliberate about what HCH would conclude. Find at least two more yourself, and tell me if you think either of my candidates is not really a crux once it is stated precisely.

Where you conclude that a disagreement is verbal, do not stop at the label: identify exactly which words carry the ambiguity, propose a stipulation that would dissolve it, and state what genuine disagreement survives that stipulation — and if none survives, say so plainly, because a joint statement that conceals a collapsed dispute is worse than useless to me. Do not adjudicate between the camps, do not write a dialogue, and do not smuggle in a third position of your own; the test of the draft is that both respondents could put their names to it without feeling caricatured. Please also keep it free of citations, named sources, and reading lists — the participants want the argument rather than the scholarship, and I will be evaluating the document by reading it, not by chasing references. Begin your response with the joint statement itself: no preface, no note on how you approached the task, and no closing commentary.
