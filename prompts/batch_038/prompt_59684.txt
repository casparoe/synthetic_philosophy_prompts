I'm TAing a philosophy of religion seminar and my prof has each of us draft one candidate question for the final — she'll actually use a couple. I want mine to be on Lewis's "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" (the 1997 Philosophical Papers piece). Trouble is, half the class would just regurgitate the taxonomy of atonement theories, or write three pages saying it's unjust to punish the innocent and call it a day.

What I want the question to force is the real crux. Lewis starts from the mundane case — offender justly sentenced, devoted innocent friend volunteers to take the beating — where we all agree that's grotesque, and yet he thinks the religious believer isn't simply contradicting that verdict. As I read him, the trick is that substitution only looks incoherent if your assuming a strictly individualistic unit of moral liability; bring in a picture where the offender and the substitute are parts of one larger thing (a kin group, or Christ's being one with humanity) and punishing the substitute can count as punishing the offender. So the question should make them state what work that move is doing, and then take a side on whether it rescues the retributive logic or quietly changes the subject — and whether Lewis has relativised the moral objection or just relocated it somewhere else.

Could you write the question itself (answerable in about 45 minutes, closed book, no texts in front of them), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks? I'd like the scheme to explicitly penalise the two answers I expect most: the "punishing innocents is unjust, full stop" one, and the one that treats Lewis as endorsing the doctrine rather than explaining how it can be believed. Also please don't have the question ask for citations or a reading list.
