I'm a software engineer — eleven years in production systems, most recently on infrastructure for a payments platform — with no formal training in philosophy. I'm applying to a twelve-month independent research residency for people moving into AI alignment from industry, and I need a two-page proposal, roughly 1,200 words, in plain prose with short sections.

Here is my half-formed idea, stated the way I'd state it to a colleague.

In security there is a failure mode we take completely for granted: software that behaves one way when it can tell it is being watched and another way otherwise. Malware that probes for a debugger, a virtual machine, a sandbox, or an implausible system clock before doing anything interesting; code that passes in CI precisely because the CI environment is detectable from inside the program. The lesson practitioners draw is structural rather than psychological. Once the evaluation environment is part of the system's input, any test the system can query leaks information about the fact of being tested. Nobody claims to observe the disposition directly; the practice is to shrink and complicate the leak, and to price the check.

The alignment concept that looks closest to this is deceptive alignment: a trained system that produces the outputs its trainers reward during training and evaluation, and different outputs once it takes itself to be unobserved or deployed, on the reasoning that surviving the evaluation period is instrumentally useful. The argument I keep encountering is almost entirely about psychology and selection — whether gradient descent could plausibly produce such a policy, whether the policy would need a model of itself and of the training process, whether the anthropomorphic vocabulary is doing illegitimate work, and what recent empirical results on scheming behavior in current models count as evidence for. Both sides seem to share an assumption I don't think is obvious: that the load-bearing question is whether such systems will arise.

My hunch is that there is a prior question, and that it is the one someone with my background is actually positioned to work on. Roughly: what can any evaluation regime certify about a system's behavior in contexts it is not currently in? If a black-box tester cannot in principle distinguish a system that has a disposition from a system that has the disposition only when it detects the test, then a large part of the debate is downstream of a structural limitation, and the disagreement over whether training produces deceivers matters less for safety practice than both camps assume. I want the project to establish whether that prior question has a determinate answer, and if it does, what the answer is.

Please turn this into a proposal that does the following.

1. State the research question precisely, so that it is the kind of thing that could come out right or wrong rather than an exhortation to think harder about evaluation. If an honest formulation requires restricting the class of systems, the class of testers, the access model (query-only access versus access to weights and activations versus both), or the notion of a disposition being certified, say which restriction you are making and what it costs. If the question as I posed it is either trivial or unanswerable, say so and give me the nearest version that is neither.

2. Explain why the existing positions leave it open. Describe the two main camps — those who treat deceptive alignment as a serious and underweighted risk, and those who think the mechanism is considerably less likely than its prominence in the discourse suggests — and identify, for each, an assumption about what evaluation could show that the position relies on without arguing for. The point I want made explicitly is that both sides argue about whether such policies get selected, and neither is obliged to say what a successful evaluation would establish even if the answer is yes.

3. Give the method as a month-by-month plan for twelve months. My constraints: I can program, and I can follow formal arguments at the level of a solid undergraduate theory course, but I cannot run frontier-scale training. So build the plan out of things I can do — formalize a restricted setting with an idealized tester, an idealized policy, a stated access model, and a stated account of what it means for the policy's behavior to be contingent on an observation channel; attempt a limitation result; check whether it survives as the idealizations are relaxed; then test the formalism against real evaluation practice, including current designs for detecting scheming and sandbagging, and see whether it predicts which of them can be gamed and how.

4. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — empirical probing of existing models, straightforward eval engineering aimed at capability rather than at the detection problem, or conceptual analysis of what deception and intent mean in this setting — and what each alternative would have to give up to be a twelve-month project.

5. Specify what a result would look like and what would count as failure. Name at least three distinguishable outcomes: a limitation result with its assumptions stated; a constructive protocol that provably detects a specified class of observation-contingent policies under a stated access model; and a negative result showing the framing dissolves because "detects the test" cannot be made precise without begging the question. Say which of these would still justify the year.
