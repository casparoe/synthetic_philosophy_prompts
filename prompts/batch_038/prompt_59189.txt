My great-grandfather was a locomotive fitter. Clearing my grandmother's flat in Howrah last month turned up a biscuit tin with his appointment letter, a handful of pay slips in rupees and annas, and a photograph of him standing beside an engine with a European foreman seated in front of it. For as long as I have been alive the family has treated those papers as a kind of proof, and my uncle's contribution to any argument about the Raj is a single sentence: 'at least they built the railways.' He said it twice at the funeral lunch — once against my cousin's remark about the Bengal famine, and once against a question about whether Britain owes India anything — in both cases to close the discussion rather than to continue it.

I audit banks for a living, which is presumably why it is the accounting language rather than the history that has got under my skin. Because the railway line is only the visible entry in a much larger piece of rhetoric: the claim that empire had a balance sheet, with a credit column and a debit column, and that the only interesting question is what the net came to. That is the framing the argument between Niall Ferguson and Shashi Tharoor is usually reduced to in the room I am about to walk into, and this autumn I am chairing a public panel on exactly that subject at my old college. I do not want a verdict on the Raj. I want to be able to say precisely what treating it as a balance sheet commits anyone to.

So: lay out the distinct claims that framing could be making. For each one I want the precise formulation; a verdict on whether it is a platitude that both a defender and a critic can sign without embarrassment, a substantive and defensible claim, a claim that is false, or not a factual claim at all but a decision about how to describe things; and a note on what evidence or argument would bear on it. Do not stop at my list — correct me where I have mis-stated a reading and add ones I have missed. The candidates I keep running into are:

- whether the metaphor assumes costs and benefits are commensurable in some single unit, and if so which unit it could be: money, life-years, real income, capability, or nothing at all;
- whether the netting is being done within a party or across parties — a gain to British shareholders and to the colonial exchequer set against a loss to Bengali cultivators — and whether that second operation is even the same kind of thing as the first;
- whether the ledger is being read as a going concern or as a wound-up estate, and what turns on that: whether independence is the closing date at which assets transfer, on what valuation, and with which liabilities assumed by the successor rather than discharged by the predecessor;
- which items are being kept off the books altogether, as distinct from being booked at an uncertain value — sovereignty, humiliation, the destruction of an indigenous textile industry, the demographic losses of famine years;
- whether the counterfactual is an empty set of books, in which nothing is entered because nothing happens, or somebody else's books, and how much work that silent choice is doing;
- who the auditor is taken to be, and whether a party who never consented to the enterprise is entitled to be handed the statement at all rather than to dispute its format.

Then run the railway line through the best of those readings, since it is always the test case, and here I want mechanism rather than gesture: how such a network came to be financed in the first place, including any guarantee of returns to private companies and whose revenues stood behind it; what the lines were built to carry, and for whom; what freight rates and passenger fares did to the people who paid them; how the network behaved in famine years, moving grain into a distressed district or out of it; what was remitted to London out of Indian revenues and under what headings; and what the physical and financial inheritance looked like at independence — in what condition, and with what debt attached. Where you are unsure of a number, say so and give me the shape of the estimate rather than a confident figure. I have no interest in a reading list and will not be citing you; I want the reasoning.

Finally, tell me which of these disagreements would survive two people agreeing on every first-order fact of the history — that is, where the dispute is not about what happened but about whether 'net' means anything here at all — and, given that, what question I should put to a panel that cannot be answered by my uncle's sentence. Please mark the platitudes as you go. I would rather spend twenty minutes on the two or three readings that are genuinely contested than on the ones where everyone in the room is already nodding.
