ANSWER

(a) Two-dimensional semantics assigns each sentence two intensions instead of one. An intension is a function from possible worlds to truth values, so what distinguishes the two is the way in which a world is taken as argument. The secondary intension evaluates a sentence at a world considered as counterfactual: we hold fixed what our own world is actually like and ask what would be the case if things had been otherwise. The primary intension evaluates a sentence at a world considered as actual: we ask whether the sentence would be true if that world turned out to be ours. Because a scenario taken as actual has to settle how our terms pick things out, the primary intension is determined by meaning alone and is a priori accessible; for any sentence whatever, we can know a priori which scenarios, considered as actual, verify it. Where a sentence contains indexicals the primary intension must be evaluated at centered worlds, since it matters not only which world is actual but who and when one is within it. The primary intension is what people mean by narrow content, being shared by duplicates who differ in their environments, whereas the secondary intension is broad content.

Kripke showed that necessity and a priority come apart. His framework explains this by holding that a term's primary intension is given by a description which fixes the reference relative to a scenario, while its secondary intension is settled once reference has been determined and thereafter tracks that same referent rigidly across worlds. So the claim that water is H2O has a contingent primary intension: in a scenario considered as actual where the clear, drinkable liquid in the lakes is XYZ, the claim is false, since there the term picks out XYZ. When chemistry discovers that our world is not like that, the secondary intension is settled, and because the term rigidly designates H2O the secondary intension of the claim is true at every world. The identity is therefore genuinely and metaphysically necessary, while the sense we have that water might not have been H2O is a sense about the primary intension, which really is contingent. The two-dimensionalist thus keeps the link between conceivability and possibility intact: conceivability guides primary possibility, and Kripke's cases are counterexamples to that link only if one assumes that the interesting kind of possibility is the secondary one.

The case of names is harder than the case of natural kind terms, because it is not obvious that Hesperus has any non-trivial primary intension at all. The usual two-dimensionalist answer is that it is given by something like the object that actually causes our perceptual evidence and our use of the name, but this is disputed even among those who accept the framework.

(b) The zombie argument applies this apparatus to consciousness. Let P be a complete true description of the physical facts of our world and Q the claim that there is phenomenal consciousness. P and not-Q is conceivable: we can form a positive and coherent conception of a world physically just like ours in which nobody has any experience. It follows that P and not-Q is primarily possible — there is a scenario which, considered as actual, verifies it.

Type-B materialists deny that zombies are conceivable. They hold that what seems to be a positive conception of a zombie world is only the absence of any detected contradiction, which is too weak to carry an argument. Chalmers's reply is that this is the wrong diagnosis, and that the stronger materialist line — the phenomenal concept strategy developed by Loar and Papineau — accepts the conceivability but denies that it carries over into possibility. On the two-dimensional picture, an a posteriori necessity is available wherever the primary and secondary intensions of the relevant terms come apart, as they do for water. The materialist therefore needs phenomenal concepts to behave that way too. Chalmers's central claim is that they do not: for phenomenal concepts the primary and secondary intension coincide, and this is something we discover a posteriori, by investigating how such concepts actually work in us. Given the coincidence, the primary possibility of the zombie world is also its secondary possibility; so the zombie world is metaphysically possible, the physical facts do not metaphysically necessitate the phenomenal facts, and materialism is false.

What the framework does is therefore to relocate the dispute. It is not a dispute about semantics, since both sides accept the two intensions; it is a dispute about one substantive claim, namely whether phenomenal concepts have the structure that would let an a posteriori necessity close the gap. A materialist who can show that they do has an exit that costs nothing at the level of the framework. A materialist who cannot must fall back on denying conceivability, which is a far less comfortable position to defend.
