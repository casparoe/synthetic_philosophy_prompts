Our communications directorate has been pressing the methodology panel I sit on to add generation to the core household questionnaire as a standard classification variable, alongside sex, region and highest qualification, with every respondent assigned to a fixed band by year of birth. Several of us objected that the bands are drawn where someone happened to draw them, and that most of the supposed generational differences in our own data go away once you hold age, household income and education constant. In reply a colleague produced an analogy he evidently thinks settles the matter: birth cohorts, he said, are like wine vintages. A vintage is fixed once and for all by the conditions of a single growing season; its boundaries are sharp because the harvest is an event rather than a judgement call; a good nose can identify a vintage blind; and nobody supposes that a great claret is really just the sum of its grape variety and its cellar. On that basis he wants generation treated as a hard variable, so that we can, in his phrase, blind-taste our respondents' attitudes.

I would rather test the comparison than wave it away, so please audit it. Give the mapping explicitly — a terse two-column table saying what in our survey corresponds to the vineyard, the growing season, the harvest, the grape variety, the cellar and the connoisseur — and then the disanalogies, separating those that bear on his proposal from those that are only local imperfections in the picture. Three pressure points matter most to me, and for each I want to know whether the analogy survives it or depends on not looking at it. First, a vintage is bounded by an event, whereas a cohort band is bounded by a decision taken at a desk, and the same human beings look like different kinds of thing depending on where the cut falls. Second, a grape's exposure to conditions ends the moment it is picked, but a person's does not: what we would credit to a cohort may be a period effect falling on everyone alive at the time of interview, or an age effect falling on everyone at that stage of life, and I am not convinced the three can always be teased apart in the data at all. Third, a bottle has no view about its own vintage, while our respondents read the labels in the papers; a category that people come to organise themselves around may stop behaving like a category we merely detect.

Finish with a plain verdict on whether the analogy supports his conclusion — generation in as a hard classification variable — and, if it does not, whether some other comparison would carry his underlying point better. There may be something real in the idea that the conditions of early adulthood leave a lasting trace, and I do not want to lose it along with the rhetoric. Keep the whole thing to 600 words at most, table included.
