I'm in the second term of a metaphysics seminar on individuation and haecceitism, and one of the other students has a habit of ending exchanges with the proverb "no two things are exactly alike," delivered in the tone of someone closing a file rather than opening a question. Last week the sentence did three distinct pieces of work in three separate discussions. When we reached Black's imagined universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres — the ones the paper dubs Castor and Pollux — he said the case is not a genuine possibility at all, on the grounds that the proverb shows any description on which the spheres are exactly alike simply fails to describe two things, so the thought experiment is empty rather than a counterexample. When someone else pressed the quantum-mechanical objection, that identical particles occupying a symmetrized state seem numerically distinct while sharing every state-independent property, he used the same sentence to insist that the particles must differ somewhere and we just have not found where. And when the discussion turned to whether the identity of indiscernibles is a logical, a metaphysical, or an empirical principle, he said the proverb settles it in favor of the empirical, since we have never once turned up two exactly alike things and the burden lies with anyone claiming otherwise. I left that session unable to say what was wrong, though I am confident something is, and I suspect the difficulty is that one sentence is being made to do at least three jobs it cannot do simultaneously.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to the next session, the distinct precise theses that "no two things are exactly alike" might be expressing? I would like each entry to state its claim in reasonably formal terms — quantifying over an explicit range of properties (all predicates whatsoever, only non-trivial ones, only intrinsic or qualitative ones, relational and spatiotemporal ones included or excluded, identity properties such as being identical with Castor included or excluded), and with the modal operator made explicit: does it govern this world, every world, or every world in which the objects in question exist? For each entry, say whether it is a platitude that follows from the logic of identity alone, a substantive but defensible thesis, or false, and where it is substantive, say roughly what would have to be true of the world for it to hold. Please flag any reading that comes out true precisely because it is uninformative in the way the objection requires, and any reading on which the two-spheres description ceases to be a coherent description of a possible world rather than merely an improbable one.

Then, separately, tell me which of those readings actually licenses each of the three uses above, and which is most likely what a person means when reaching for the proverb mid-argument — in particular, whether any single reading is strong enough to do all three jobs at once, or whether my classmate is sliding among an empirical generalization, a semantic or verificationist claim about what counts as describing two things, and a substantive principle about individuation. Finally, I would like your verdict on the move I find hardest to diagnose: if the two spheres are symmetrically related, so that the only relational difference on offer is that one is two miles from the other and vice versa, is it legitimate to say that they differ after all, or does that response presuppose exactly what is in question? I am not after a bibliography or a survey of who holds what; I want the distinctions stated and the verdicts given.
