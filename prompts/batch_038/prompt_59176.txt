Background: retired ecologist, thirty-odd years designing long-term monitoring plots, so I have a professional weakness for anything involving counts and samples. Since I stopped working I've been reading around the naturalistic study of religion — the stuff that treats gods and rituals as phenomena to be explained the way you'd explain a bird's nest, rather than claims to be judged.

The bit that's hooked me is one particular proposal: that religious ideas get around not mainly because they comfort people or because priests had leverage, but because of the shape of the ideas themselves. The ones that survive being passed along are the ones that are just slightly impossible — a snake that talks, a woman who became a pillar of salt, a god who is somehow three people. Ordinary enough to picture, with one violation that makes them stick. And there's a second half I find more interesting than the first: it's supposed to be a sweet spot, so that an item that violates too much at once — invisible, timeless, made of geometry, sad, forgets that it exists — should spread *worse* than the moderate ones, not better, with plain ordinary items worst of all. I gather there's a term of art for the exact degree of weirdness; use it if it helps, but don't make me read a monograph to follow you.

I run a monthly evening ideas group at our town library. Twelve to fourteen people, nearly all retired, a couple in their eighties with dodgy eyesight and hearing. Ninety minutes, whiteboard, index cards, pens, a kitchen timer, no budget, no projector, and nobody wants homework.

Design one session's activity that puts that hypothesis through its paces with the group — where they handle or generate items and the pattern falls out of what they actually did, not out of me explaining it. They shouldn't know what's being tested going in, and the too-weird-to-stick end has to be in there somehow, not just weird-beats-boring.

What I want back:

- Rules and materials, and what literally goes on the cards. If you want a prepared deck, tell me how to build it and what the items should look like. If having the room invent the items is better, say so — and tell me how to stop them inventing items that give the game away.
- A timeline for the ninety minutes, including where the counting happens.
- The arithmetic, honestly. With thirteen people, is there any real chance the pattern shows up in our own numbers? What's the fallback if it comes out flat or backwards — is there a design choice (pooling everyone's scores, asking them what they'd *choose* to pass on rather than what they recall, more items per person, whatever you think) that makes a result robust at that sample size? I've collected enough null data in my life to know a demonstration that depends on a real effect appearing in thirteen subjects is a gamble. I'd rather be told the odds and handed a plan B than have the room fall flat.
- The debrief, mapping what they did onto the hypothesis, and marking which parts of their performance are actual evidence for it and which parts are just a party trick.
- The content problem. The natural items here are religious — miracle stories, saints, the burning bush. One of our regulars is a Methodist lay preacher, sharp as anything and a lovely man, and I don't want an evening that lands on him as a debunking ambush. Should the cards be religious at all, or secular cousins (folk tales, cryptids, urban legends) with the transfer made later in discussion? Tell me what each option costs.
- Where this will jam on the first run — the question from the floor that exposes the point, the scoring dispute, the person who turns it into a theology argument — and what I say in the moment.
- Bluntly, which piece of the hypothesis a card game in a library cannot exhibit, so I know what I have to supply by talking.

One last thing, and I'd ask it of any answer. Separate for me what's settled here from what is genuinely still argued about among the people who study this. My suspision — tell me if I'm right or if I've soaked up a cheap skepticism — is that the memory advantage for slightly-impossible items is shakier now than the popular books let on, and that the sweet-spot half has never been nailed down at all. If parts of this are contested, say so plainly and don't hand me a live argument dressed as consensus, because I'm the one who'll be standing at the whiteboard. No bibliography needed and I'm not asking you to look anything up: I want the reasoning and the design.
