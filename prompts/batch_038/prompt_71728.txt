I'm a junior, and for the second semester of my school's ethics sequence I'm writing a long paper on Augustine's two treatises on lying: the early one, written before he became a bishop, and the much later one he composed at the request of a correspondent who wanted to know whether Catholics might pass themselves off as Priscillianists in order to get at Priscillianists, who held that a person may keep the truth in his heart while saying something false to outsiders. My teacher has let each of us open our seminar discussion with something other than an argument, and I have chosen to open with a short story. I would like you to write it: 1,800 to 2,200 words, set anywhere in the Latin-speaking Mediterranean in the last decades of the fourth century or the first decades of the fifth.

What I need the story to do is dramatize the hardest part of his position rather than explain it, and to do that it has to take seriously three things that I think most people who invoke him on lying skip over.

First, his account of what a lie is turns entirely on the speaker's own mind and not on the facts. A lie, for him, is a doubled heart: one thing held inside, another put out in words or signs. It follows that a person may say something false and not lie, if he believes it, and may say something true and lie all the same, if he believes it false and puts it forward as true. He is judged by the sense of his own mind, not by how things turn out. He also sets joking aside as not lying, because the tone and manner of the joker announce that no deceit is intended.

Second, he does not treat all lies as equally bad. He ranks them, from the worst, which are lies about religious teaching, down through lies that injure somebody and help nobody and lies told for the sheer pleasure of deceiving, to the least culpable kind, which harms no one and protects somebody from death or from violation. But the ranking never turns the bottom rung into a permission.

Third, and this is the hinge I want the story to sit on: he appears to leave a gap between lying and concealing. You are not obliged to disclose everything you know; you may decline to answer, deflect, fall silent, or say something you expect to be taken wrongly, so long as you do not assert what you yourself hold to be false. He handles the Hebrew midwives by treating their reward as owed to their mercy rather than to their lie; he refuses to do evil so that good may come; and he argues, among other things, that lies cannot be prosecuted by lying, since that destroys the credibility on which any witness depends. He also holds that a person's own soul is worth more than another person's body, so that one may not sin, even mildly, in order to keep somebody alive.

Here is the situation I have in mind, though you should change whatever needs changing:

- A young clerk attached to a bishop's household, whose assigned work is copying out the treatise on lying. He knows the ranking by heart and can recite the gap between lying and concealing back to anyone who asks.
- Somebody is being hidden on the property. Your choice as to who and why, but it should matter to the clerk personally and not merely as an instance of a rule.
- A magistrate or a military officer asks the clerk a direct question about it. The clerk should answer twice, at different points in the story, and the two answers should not be the same kind of answer.
- Build the definition into the plot rather than into the dialogue. At some point let him say something that turns out to be true while he believes it to be false, and let the story make clear that on his own account this was a lie and not a lucky truth. I want that asymmetry felt, never explained.
- Let the story ask whether the gap between lying and concealing can actually be inhabited by a person under questioning: whether the effort to stay on the permitted side of the line begins to look like the very duplicity the line was drawn to forbid, and what keeping it costs him.

Two things I would rather you avoid. Please do not resolve the case tidily, and do not let the ending function as a verdict on the theory: if the person hidden is caught, that should not read as punishment for a lie, and if that person escapes, it should not read as a reward for one. And please do not write a modern secular rationalist in period dress. The clerk should genuinely believe what he has been copying, including the parts that are inconvenient for him. You may have characters paraphrase the treatises in dialogue, but do not put wording inside quotation marks as though it were Augustine's own.

After the story, add a note of no more than half a page. In it, separate what you are treating as settled in his texts from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material seriously: for example, whether the distinction between lying and concealing is a real and stable part of his view or a difficulty it cannot escape, whether the two treatises agree with each other about the ranking of lies, and whether his whole argument depends on a definition of lying that most readers now reject. Then mark the particular places where the story takes a liberty or leans on one side of a contested question. If you are unsure whether something is settled, say so plainly rather than writing it as though everyone who knows the topic agreed.
