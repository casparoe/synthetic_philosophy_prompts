I am preparing the third session of a graduate seminar on Nietzsche and the philosophy of the body, and I have decided to devote it to a conceptual difficulty that two of my students have, I believe, walked straight into without noticing that they had. The difficulty concerns the dietetic material in *Ecce Homo*: the sustained treatment of climate and locale, of nutrition, and of the whole ordering of daily life — meals and their timing, recreation, the company one keeps, what one reads — in the chapter "Why I Am So Clever," read alongside the account of inherited illness, convalescence, and self-experimentation in the chapter "Why I Am So Wise," where Nietzsche casts himself as his own diagnostician and prescriber and traces his migraines, his nausea, and the deterioration of his eyes back to his digestion and to the errors of his earlier regimen. One student has written a paper reading all of this as an instrumental optimization programme: advice of the form *if you wish to secure the clarity of spirit that philosophy requires, then dwell thus, abstain thus, eat thus*, structurally continuous with contemporary talk of biohacking, circadian management, and nootropic stacks — differing from it only in the era of its pharmacology. Another student objected in the margins that no such reading can survive Nietzsche's denial of free will and his insistence that a man's instincts, his metabolic capacity, and indeed his whole mode of valuation are fate rather than choice: on her account the chapter is pure symptomatology, a self-portrait of a type written up in the imperative mood, and the prescriptive form is at best a rhetorical residue.

What I want from you is an explanation of what is genuinely at issue between these two readings, and a careful assessment of whether the second student has in fact identified a contradiction or has only restated one side of a distinction she has not yet drawn. Please begin by separating at least three logically distinct roles that the dietetic claims could be playing: (i) causal-instrumental claims about which regimes produce which cognitive states; (ii) expressive or diagnostic claims, on which a regimen manifests a constitution without altering it; and (iii) claims about conditions of possibility, on which the arrangement of one's meals, air, and hours is not one more empirical cause among others but the standing condition under which any thinking of a certain order can occur at all. For each, spell out what would have to be true of the text for it to be the dominant register, and — this is the part I most want, since my students skipped it — what each implies about the *addressee*: who could act on such advice, given Nietzsche's repeated suggestion that a reader extracts from a book only what his own constitution already permits him to extract.

Then take up the concept of health itself, since I suspect the instrumental reading founders there rather than on free will. Explain how the conception of health developed in the preface to the second edition of *The Gay Science* — health not as a possessed state but as something one must perpetually win back and perpeturally spend, a rhythm of acquisition and forfeiture rather than a plateau to be optimized toward — bears on the very idea of an optimum regimen, and explain what a target-less notion of health does to the notion of dietary advice as means to an end. Finally, consider whether Nietzsche's persistent praise of discipline, self-overcoming, and the deliberate arrangement of one's circumstances supplies a middle position that neither student has articulated: not a free choice of physiology, but a capacity to place oneself where the dominant instinct is fed and the subordinate one starved — an aesthetics of existence that is neither biohacking nor mere symptom-reporting. Take one concrete strand of the material (the remarks on alcohol, say, or on tea and coffee, or on the preference for dry, thin air over damp northern climates) and show how that same material reads under each of your three registers, so that my students can see the interpretive difference doing work rather than merely being asserted.

Two constraints on your answer. First, no bibliography, no citations, and no guessed-at page numbers, translators, or quotations: if you want to point to a passage, describe it and its location in the argument rather than reproducing it, since I will be checking your claims against the German and the seminar's edition myself. Second, write for an audience of first-year graduate students who have read the texts but have not read any secondary literature on Nietzsche's physiology; aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, organised under your own headings, and end with a short paragraph identifying which textual or rhetorical features would, in your judgement, decide between the readings — that is, what a student should go back and look for in the chapter itself rather than what she should read next about it.
