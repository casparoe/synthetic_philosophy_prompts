And here's the student's response, unedited apart from fixing one formatting thing:

> The jury theorem comes from Condorcet's 1785 Essay on the Application of Analysis to the Probability of Majority Decisions, and it says the following. Suppose a group of n voters each has to choose between two alternatives, exactly one of which is correct, and suppose each voter, reasoning on their own, lights on the correct one with probability p. If p is greater than 1/2, then the probability that the majority chooses correctly is greater than p, it rises as n rises, and it approaches 1 in the limit as the group grows without bound. If p is less than 1/2 the majority probability approaches 0 instead, and if p is exactly 1/2 the majority is no better than a coin flip at any size. So the theorem is fundamentally a claim about limits. It doesn't promise that any particular jury of twelve gets it right — it only tells you which way the odds move as you add members.
>
> The assumptions doing the work are: (1) two alternatives only; (2) there is a fact of the matter about which one is correct, and it's the same fact for everyone; (3) each voter's competence p exceeds a half; (4) the votes are independent; and (5) everyone votes what they actually believe rather than strategically. Assumption (2) is the philosophically loaded one, since plenty of political questions look like clashes of interest rather than questions with a right answer, and if there's no such answer there's nothing for the procedure to track. Assumption (5) is the one people forget. If jurors vote the way they expect the majority to go, they aren't contributing any information, and the theorem's inputs break even though the arithmetic is fine. There's a puzzle sitting right next to this that I find interesting: once the group is large, each member's own belief that the majority will be right is very high, so a member who just wants to be right should go along with the expected majority instead of voting their own judgment, and then it's hard to see why anyone votes sincerely at all.
>
> Independence is usually named as the assumption most threatened in real deliberative bodies, but I think that's overstated. Independence here just means nobody's vote causes anybody else's — it's an assumption about voters not colluding or being bought off, not an assumption about them being ignorant of the same facts. In a citizen assembly everyone reads roughly the same reporting and hears the same expert testimony, but each of them still has to make up their own mind about it, and if anything shared evidence pushes everyone's p up rather than down. What actually threatens the theorem is deliberation in the sense of social pressure: people deferring to whoever is loudest or most prestigious, or trading votes. That's a competence problem as much as an independence problem, and its the reason Condorcet wanted jurors to reach their verdicts separately.
>
> On the main question, yes, the theorem licenses preferring the 400-person assembly, provided we can establish that its members are individually better than chance. Once p clears 1/2 the majority's reliability climbs toward certainty without limit, whereas the panel is capped at twelve. A panel of experts might have a high p, but it has almost no room to improve by aggregation, and if one of the twelve is wrong or captured there's no crowd available to outvote them. So the comparison reduces to a single threshold question about competence, not to a trade-off between how big the group is and how good its members are. This is the same lesson the wisdom-of-crowds results teach generally: the diversity prediction theorem says a crowd's error equals the average error of its members minus the diversity of their predictions, so a large diverse group beats even its own best member, and you can see it empirically in Galton's ox-weight contest, where the crowd's middle estimate of the dressed weight landed within about one percent of the truth while the individual guesses were scattered. Add people and the errors wash out. That said, the whole argument rests on a premise about competence that a randomly selected lay body is exactly the sort of body most likely to fail to satisfy, and thats the point worth pressing.
