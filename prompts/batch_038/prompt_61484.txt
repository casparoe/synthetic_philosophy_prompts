So — slightly unusual request, and I'm writing it in a hurry between sections, so bear with me.

For about five years now I've been the token philosopher on our law school's criminal-justice clinic advisory panel. What actually happens is this: a couple of times a term a student team brings me a packet — a defense motion, a neuropsychologist's affidavit, sometimes a single highlighted paragraph from an expert's CV-length report — and it invokes the neuroscience of free will. Usually it's Libet-style stuff: readiness potentials, the wrist-flexion clock task, "the brain began the movement before the subject was aware of deciding," occasionally the veto finding, occasionally a general gesture at "diminished top-down control" from addiction or impulse-control imaging. The students have zero neuroscience and, honestly, only a hazy grip on the metaphysics either. Their default is to assume that if a PhD says it, it's load-bearing. My job is to tell them, fast, whether the science in front of them is doing real work in the argument, doing decorative work, or is actively misleading and should be struck from the brief.

Over time I've started sorting what I see into four moves, and I use my own nicknames for them, which the students have picked up: the **delay move** (awareness of deciding comes after the neural onset, so the conscious decision isn't the cause); the **machine move** (the buildup is just stochastic noise crossing a threshold, so there's no authored decision anywhere in the story — this one is usually waved at with a citation to Schurger's accumulator reanalysis in PNAS back in 2012); the **threshold move** (the person's evidence of impaired control shows their accumulator sits closer to the bound, or their urgency signal is steeper, so the outcome was likelier regardless of what they deliberated about); and the **veto move** (even granting all that, late cancellation is intact, so what survives is only a thin kind of authorship, and thin authorship supports mitigation but not exculpation). I'm genuinely not sure this taxonomy is right, and part of what I want from you is a straight answer on whether it carves things well or whether it's collapsing distinctions that have to stay seperate.

Here's the ask. Write me a screening procedure my students can run on one of these packets in twenty minutes, with no neuroscience background, that ends in one of three verdicts: the neuroscience is load-bearing for the legal claim being made, it's decorative (harmless but not doing work — say so and move on), or it's misleading and should come out of the brief. I want the questions in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, and where the procedure stops because the remaining question is philosophical or legal rather than empirical — because a lot of these packets fail at exactly that seam, e.g. they assume that "voluntary" as the criminal law uses it has to mean whatever the free-will literature means by it, and I need my students to see when that assumption is carrying all the weight. Tell me which of the moves in my taxonomy each question is designed to catch. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an assault motion attaching an affidavit that the defendant's "intent was formed after the motor program had already been initiated," offered to negate mens rea; (2) an insurer's argument, in a civil matter, that a claimant's addiction-related imaging findings show his later decision to enter treatment wasn't really his; and (3) the one I'll hate — a student note I've twice assigned good grades to, arguing that the stochastic-accumulator reading is *better for the defense* than the Libet reading, since on that picture there is no decision at all to be responsible for. If case three survives your procedure and I'm wrong about it, say that plainly; if it doesn't, say why, because I want to stop assigning it.

Also: mark every step that's a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothing. I'd rather the checklist admit where it needs a human than pretend to be a decision procedure all the way down. And at the end, give me your own confidence — how sure are you about the empirical claims you're relying on at each stage, and which single step are you least sure of? If there's a place where you're reconstructing what the science says rather than recalling it, point at it so I can check it before I hand this to nineteen-year-olds.
