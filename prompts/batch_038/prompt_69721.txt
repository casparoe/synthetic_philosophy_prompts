You are a philosopher of medicine who has also spent years on hospital ethics consults, and you are equally at home with the conceptual debate over the determination of death and with the working realities of an intensive care unit. Please think carefully before drafting: what I want first from you is a scratch-pad section, not the finished instrument.

**Context.** I run an interprofessional teaching session on the question of when a patient has died. My room is mixed — three transplant surgeons, two ICU nurses, a chaplain, and the hospital's counsel — and nobody in it does philosophy for a living. All of them arrive with confident self-labels ("I'm a brain-death person"; "I only trust the heart and lungs"). I am building a nine-item sorting quiz whose point is to show them that those self-labels do not predict their own judgments about cases.

**Deliverables, in this order.**

1. *Axes and logical space.* Identify the independent axes along which positions on the determination of death genuinely differ — which function must be lost; whether that loss must be irreversible or may be merely permanent; whether the criterion states a biological fact or confers a normative status; whether the determination is relative to who is asking and to which decision it serves; and any further axes you think belong. Then lay out the combinations, marking which are occupied by a defended position and which are empty, and for each empty cell say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, politically unavailable, or simply unexplored.

2. *Item brainstorm.* Sketch twelve to fifteen candidate discriminating situations, one line each, annotated with which pair of positions the situation separates and which pairs it leaves untouched. Select the nine you will use, and say in one sentence each why the rejected candidates were cut.

3. *The quiz.* Nine items, each a concrete vignette of no more than eighty words followed by two or three answer options. Hard constraints:
 - In the printed item text (stem and options), the words *death*, *dead*, *dying*, *brain death*, *irreversible*, and *permanent* may not appear, and no position may be named. Every item must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the labels.
 - Options must be first-order commitments — what the respondent would authorize, what they would tell the family, how long they would wait, what they would accept as evidence that they had been wrong — never statements about which account they find most plausible.
 - No item may be a recognizable stock thought experiment with a name attached to it.

 The nine should cover, at minimum: a patient on whose behalf a neurological determination has been made and who is nevertheless maintained on ventilatory and hormonal support for well over a year, healing wounds, gaining weight, growing; a newborn with no cerebral hemispheres whose brainstem functions and who breathes unaided but will never be conscious; a cardiac arrest in profound accidental hypothermia with recovery after unusually prolonged resuscitation; a controlled donation-after-circulatory-death case in which our unit observes a five-minute standoff, where the item turns on whether that number tracks a biological fact or expresses a policy choice; an out-of-hospital arrest arriving under mechanical compressions and cannulated for extracorporeal support before anyone has made any determination at all; a jurisdiction whose law invalidates a neurological determination that offends the family's religious commitments; a pair of patients in adjacent beds who have lost different functions, where the respondent must say whether one word can govern both; and a case in which a family gives fully informed, fully voluntary authorization to procurement from a patient the respondent does not take to have died. Choose the ninth item yourself.

4. *Scoring key.* For each position you treat as live — loss of the organism's integrative unity; loss of the capacity for consciousness; cardiopulmonary determination under a strict irreversibility reading; determination resting on permanence rather than irreversibility; a gradualist view on which there is no single moment; and a view on which the status is conferred by decision rather than discovered — give the answer signature the instrument produces and identify the single item carrying most of the discriminating weight for that leaf. Then give at least two coherent but unoccupied signatures the key would yield, with a diagnosis of why no one stands there. State plainly that this is a facilitator's instrument rather than a membership card: a signature records a stable pattern of judgment, not an allegiance.

5. *What the instrument cannot do.* List every pair of leaves the nine items leave indistinguishable, and for each say either what an additional item would have to look like to separate them or why no such item exists because the difference is verbal. Flag any item you suspect is measuring two things at once, or on which you expect answers to shift across repetitions.

6. *Confidence and the weakest link.* Close with a short paragraph on how confident you are in what you have claimed. Separate your conceptual claims (which leaves exist, which cells are empty, which distinctions do real work) from your clinical and institutional claims (what units actually do, what law and protocol actually permit), and say which of the two you stand on more firmly. Then single out the one point in the entire deliverable you are least sure of, and say what would settle it.

**Manner.** No bibliography, no citations, no attributions to named authors, reports, or court decisions: where a position has a champion, describe the position rather than naming its defender. Do not quote from any source; paraphrase throughout. If you are reconstructing a position that nobody defends in exactly that form, label it as your reconstruction. Keep sections three through five tight enough to hand out as a printed sheet.
