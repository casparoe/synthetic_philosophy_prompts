You are a professional philosopher specialising in seventeenth-century moral and legal thought, and you have spent part of your career helping non-specialist committees convert ethical theory into procedures they can actually run. Please think carefully before drafting. Begin with a short analytical section — not yet the procedure — in which you brainstorm which elements of Leibniz's practical philosophy could bear procedural weight at all and which are metaphysically load-bearing but unusable by a lay committee, and commit to a selection before you write anything operational.

I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate, currently taking a seminar on Leibniz's moral and legal thought. Outside the seminar I chair a student-run emergency hardship fund at my university's students' union. Nine of us sit on the committee, drawn from across the faculties, none with any training in ethics beyond whatever they have picked up incidentally. We meet for forty-five minutes each week of term and get through roughly eighteen applications; awards are capped at three hundred per application, the termly pot is fixed, and in each of the last two years it was exhausted by week seven. Every refusal must be minuted with a reason, because refusals are appealable to the union's welfare board, and a trustee signs off on the month's decisions in aggregate. I do not have time per application, and I cannot rely on the committee agreeing about values.

What I need is a reusable decision procedure — an ordered set of questions the committee runs on each application — derived from Leibniz's account of justice as the charity of the wise: benevolence directed at another's good, but benevolence disciplined by knowledge of what actually produces that good and of the consequences of one's own intervention, together with his insistence that the grades of justice (strict right, equity, piety) are nested, so that the wider obligation corrects and encompasses the narrower rather than simply overriding it. Paraphrase his claims in plain language; do not quote him, and do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. The committee-facing sheet itself must carry no philosopher's name and no technical vocabulary — it has to be runnable by a biology student in the fourth week of term.

The procedure must specify: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it terminates in a refusal; where it produces a reduced or redirected award instead of the amount requested; and, crucially, where it stops and hands the decision to a human being because what remains is a matter of values or of judgement that no rule can settle. Flag explicitly every step that is a judgement call wearing the costume of a procedure. Then add an annex addressed to me rather than to the committee, which maps each step back to the Leibnizian commitment it encodes; states which distinctions you deliberately allowed to fall out in translation and why they were safe to drop; and identifies what you had to leave behind entirely, and whether any residue of it still does work in the procedure.

Please also handle the objection I expect from my seminar tutor: that Leibniz's confidence that this is the best of the possible worlds, in which apparent evils serve a greater good, licenses inaction, and that a fund built on his ethics would be a fund that never pays out. Say how his own reply survives the translation into a forty-five-minute meeting with eighteen files, or concede where it does not.

Then run the procedure on three applications. First: a student who has already received two awards this term, whose written statement is the most compelling in the pile, and who needs a housing deposit — where the same sum, given to a thinner file, would plausibly go further. Second: an applicant who is a close friend and flatmate of two committee members, genuinely in need, and about whom we consequently have far better information than about any stranger in the queue. Third: an applicant asking us to clear a debt of exactly the kind they have asked us to clear twice before, where payment will not change the pattern that produces it and where our own precedent is at stake. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like; say so plainly rather than softening the result, and show which step does the damage.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do and where it will predictably misfire in practice — including how it behaves once the pot is empty in week seven, whether it systematically advantages articulate applicants, and whether it risks turning a disposition of the heart into an audit trail that the welfare board will read as coldness. I want the honest failure analysis, not reassurance.
