I serve on the program committee of a private conservation foundation that manages a network of preserves totaling about 14,000 acres in the northeastern United States. The committee has five members; I am the only one without a background in ecology, and our September meeting will decide a petition I expect to divide us badly.

The petition: a regional animal-welfare organization asks us to fund a three-year, $210,000 pilot on our largest preserve. That preserve carries white-tailed deer at roughly forty per square mile. It has never permitted hunting, and the watershed's predators were eliminated long before the land was assembled. The herd is now under an extreme winter-tick burden; volunteers have documented fawns dying of blood loss in May and adults rubbing themselves raw against fencing, and our staff euthanized nine animals last winter at the request of hikers. The proposal is to install a grid of baited stations that apply a topical acaricide to each deer that pushes through, with corn to draw them in.

Three features of the petition will drive the argument:

1. The petitioner's own ecological consultant projects that the stations will raise deer density by roughly forty percent within three years and increase aggregation at the bait sites, with unknown consequences for chronic wasting disease transmission and for the regeneration of the understory on which a suite of ground-nesting songbirds depends.
2. The petitioner offers to pay for all monitoring itself, through a contractor of its choosing, and asks us to fund only the stations.
3. Our own record is mixed. In 2021 we funded the dusting of prairie-dog burrows with insecticide to suppress plague-carrying fleas. In 2022 we declined a petition to treat sarcoptic mange in urban foxes, on the stated ground that the petitioner could not rule out a resulting rise in fox density and predation on ground-nesting birds. Separately, our staff euthanize injured animals found by volunteers and cull invasives as routine. Our mission statement commits us to "maintaining ecological processes, unmanaged wherever possible" and says nothing whatever about individual animals.

One member, an ecologist, holds that the preserve exists for the system rather than for its members, and that funding this pilot begins a slide whose logic ends with feeding, medicating, and fertility-controlling everything on the property — a zoo with a land trust's tax status. Another holds that the petitioners' comparison is decisive: if the same tick load appeared on a neighbor's sheep, we would treat it this month, and the only differences are species and setting. A third worries that we cannot possibly fund a general obligation to relieve wild suffering, and that approving this pilot amounts to endorsing one we will default on.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting — roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, with headers and a numbered recommendation. It should:

- Recommend one of fund, fund with conditions, decline, or defer. Treat deferral as a decision rather than the absence of one: the acaricide must be applied before the first hard frost, so postponing to the spring cycle costs the pilot a year and, at current mortality, some determinable number of deer. State the timeline your recommendation implies.
- Set out three or four tests the committee could apply to the next petition of this kind without reopening first principles, framed in terms of monitoring data we can actually afford to collect.
- Confront our 2021 and 2022 decisions together with our routine euthanasia and culling: either they commit us here, or they must be distinguished on grounds we are willing to state aloud.
- Give the two strongest objections to your recommendation in their best form rather than as strawmen — one of them should be the worry that a successful pilot increases the number of animals living lives dominated by parasites and winter starvation — and say what would have to be true for you to change your mind.
- Specify what first-year data would trigger a halt and who holds the authority to call one, given that the petitioner has offered to collect that data itself.

Do not cite or name anyone and do not append a bibliography; argue from the case. The memo must be intelligible to our staff ecologist and to our largest donor, neither of whom has studied philosophy, without sounding as though it is talking down to either.
