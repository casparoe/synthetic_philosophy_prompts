For a philosophy of biology seminar I have committed myself to the strong version of the gene's-eye view: genes are the only replicators, organisms and groups are vehicles, and so every apparent organism-level or group-level adaptation must in principle be resolvable into differential replication among genes sharing a common pool. Tell me what would have to be true for that thesis to be false. I would like the objections sorted by severity, and I want them to include both the biological candidates — mergers of formerly independent lineages, siphonophore colonies that behave as single organisms, culturally transmitted traits that seem to replicate with no genetic substrate — and the purely conceptual ones. Two questions matter most to me. First, is the charge that the thesis cannot fail well founded, given that the label "replicator" can apparently be reassigned to whichever level turns out to do the faithful copying, or does the view retain substantive content that trait-group and two-tier accounts of multilevel selection lack — for instance, about when genuine group-level adaptations should be rare rather than common? Second, which costs would a serious defender of the gene's-eye view concede rather than deny, as distinct from the complaints that only its critics press?
