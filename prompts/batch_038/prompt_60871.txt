I am preparing a position statement for an interdisciplinary workshop on explaining perpetrator behavior in the Holocaust, and before I circulate it I would like a candid audit of whether the commitments I intend to declare are jointly tenable. Here they are.

(a) Explanation is situation-first. Across contexts, the standing features of a person explain far less of the variance in what they do than the features of the situation they happen to be in; the low cross-situational consistency documented in the classic obedience, bystander, and helping studies is a general fact about human beings rather than an artifact of those particular studies. It follows that global character traits of the kind virtue ethics posits are not a well-supported posit.

(b) On that basis I treat Browning's account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 as the default explanation. Conformity to peers, careerism, deference to authority, routinization, and wartime brutalization explain why, at Józefów, roughly a dozen of some five hundred men publicly stepped out when their commander offered anyone who did not feel up to the task the chance to do so without penalty, while at least four-fifths of those assigned to shoot went on shooting until the Jews of the village were dead.

(c) I also think Goldhagen was right about the motive. The perpetrators were operating on a culturally supplied cognitive model of Jews that was eliminationist in the sense that it represented their removal as legitimate and desirable; a great deal of the killing was eager rather than reluctant, went beyond what any order required, and continued on the death marches in the last weeks of the war, when the regime had nothing further to gain. I accept his methodological point as well: postwar interrogation transcripts are precisely the setting in which a defendant would suppress ideological motives, so the near-silence about antisemitism in them cannot be treated as evidence of its absence.

(d) I think the comparative record favors (c) over (a): broadly comparable occupational structures in different countries produced markedly different levels of local participation and enthusiasm, which is what one would expect if what varied was the ideological culture of the perpetrators rather than the situational pressure on them.

(e) On responsibility, I hold that blameworthiness for an atrocity requires that the act issue from something in the agent — their own reasons, values, or character — and not merely from the pressure of a role, a group, or a set of circumstances. Coercion can excuse. Being swept along by a group does not, or at any rate not automatically.

(f) I hold that the perpetrators were vicious: cruel, not merely compliant, and that this is a fact about them and not only about their situation.

(g) At the same time I hold that ordinary character talk is systematically error-prone. Inferring that someone is honest, cowardly, or cruel from a sample of their behavior across a handful of situations is to commit the fundamental attribution error, and most such attributions are false of the people to whom they are made.

(h) Methodologically, I want my account of the perpetrators to be fixed by the evidence rather than by whatever would let me keep my habits of blame and praise intact.

(i) Consistent with that, I admire the men who stepped out at Józefów, and I think they did something genuinely better than their comrades did, even though they stood in the same formation, heard the same order, and were offered the same way out.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Specifically:

1. Identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and for each say what would have to give, what giving it costs, and whether the cost falls on the historical explanation or on the moral assessment.

2. Answer this directly, since several items seem to turn on it: what, if anything, is the difference between a collectively held, culturally transmitted disposition and an individual character trait, and is that difference one my list is entitled to rely on?

3. Tell me whether (e), (f), and (g) can be held jointly at all. If they cannot, say which single revision would clean up the most and what I would lose by making it.

4. Be discriminating. Some of these pairs will look bad and be perfectly fine; I want to know which ones, and why.

Please do not read my ordering as a ranking of attachment: I have no idea which of these I would drop, and I would rather be told. Write your answer in American English. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading — the historical landmarks are already in the commitments above, and what I need from you is the conceptual work.
