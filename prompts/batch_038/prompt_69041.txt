I did not attend university. I read on my own, mostly from library books, and for the past year I have been turning over one question in particular: whether Gödel's incompleteness theorem really tells us anything about minds and machines. A friend lent me a popular book on the subject — I would rather not name it, since my question concerns the argument rather than its author — and one passage has unsettled me. It reads smoothly, I can follow every sentence, and yet something in the middle feels slippery, and I cannot say where. I have transcribed the passage here:

"Gödel's theorem of 1931 showed that any formal system strong enough to do ordinary arithmetic contains true statements that the system itself cannot prove. He proved it by constructing, inside arithmetic, a sentence that in effect says of itself that it is not provable. If the system could prove that sentence, it would be proving something false, and a system that proves falsehoods is inconsistent; if it cannot prove it, then the sentence is true but unprovable. A companion theorem shows that no such system can prove its own consistency, and that is what finally killed Hilbert's programme. The consequences reach well beyond mathematics. A computer is nothing but a formal system, so there are arithmetic truths a computer can never establish, while we, standing outside it, can see plainly that they are true. The human mind therefore is not a machine, and no machine could ever equal it: every system has a limit, and minds alone can step outside the system and see what the system cannot."

Would you audit this for me, sentence by sentence? For each claim, I want to know whether a working logician would accept it as stated, call it a simplification that quietly changes the subject, or reject it outright. Three points especially trouble me. First, the step from "the system proves something false" to "the system is inconsistent" — are those really the same thing, and what does it cost the argument if they are not? Second, what conditions the system actually has to satisfy for the theorem to bite at all: are there consistent, arithmetic-flavoured systems that escape it, and is "true but unprovable" a conclusion the theorem delivers on its own or one that needs a further assumption about the system's trustworthiness? I also wonder about the sentence on Hilbert, and whether "killed" is too strong a word for what the second theorem did.

Then I want to know what survives. If the earlier sentences are repaired, does the conclusion about minds and machines still follow, or does it depend on separate premises that the passage never states — about what we are, and about what we can know of ourselves? I understand there are serious people who press some version of this argument; if so, what would the careful version look like, and what would its proponent have to concede? Finally: when the passage says a mind can step outside the system, does stepping outside actually leave the problem behind, or does it merely relocate it?

For what it is worth, I am inclined to think the conclusion is right and the reasoning wrong, which is not a comfortable position to be in. I would like to be told whether that instinct holds up.
