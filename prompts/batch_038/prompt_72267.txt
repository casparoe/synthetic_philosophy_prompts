I am a novelist, four years into a book that will take three more, and what I want from you is philosophy rather than literary criticism. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher with a working command of Bayesian epistemology — degrees of belief, conditionalization, and the diachronic norms governing how a rational agent's opinions should change over time — and think the problem through carefully before you begin to write. Start with a short, deliberately undisciplined brainstorm: list every step of the argument below that could plausibly be where it fails, and say in a sentence or two what it would cost to give that step up. Only then commit to a single diagnosis and defend it. I am not after a survey, and I am not asking you to supply references or a reading list; argue it out on your own authority.

Here is the argument. I sketched it in a notebook during a bad month, partly as a joke, and I have since been unable to locate the step where it breaks. Every premise strikes me as true, and the conclusion is one I cannot live with — and not only as a writer, since nothing in the reasoning depends on the fact that the activity in question is composing a novel.

(1) Reflection: if I now know what degree of belief my future self will assign to a proposition, then my present degree of belief in that proposition should match it. My future self is an authority on the matter in something like the way a well-informed stranger is an authority on the weather, and I am no better entitled to overrule her than to overrule him.

(2) I have kept a revision journal through four finished books. In each of them the record is the same: within eighteen months of finishing a draft, I come to regard roughly three-quarters of the passages I had thought finest in it as overwrought, dishonest, or merely clever, and I cut or rewrite them.

(3) That pattern is not luck and not self-dramatization; it is the same mechanism each time, and I understand the mechanism. So I know, of any given passage that I currently believe to be among the best in the manuscript, that in eighteen months I will believe it to be bad.

(4) By (1) and (3), I should now believe of each such passage that it is bad.

(5) What I should believe about a page, I should act on. So I should cut those pages this month rather than in eighteen months.

(6) But the mechanism I claim to understand in (3) is nothing other than the labour of revising: it is by rewriting the bad pages that I acquire the eye that sees them as bad. Someone who had never written those pages would not hold the opinions (3) attributes to her. She holds them because I wrote them, and then sat with them.

(7) So if I follow (5), there will be no one who holds the opinion that (3) tells me to defer to, and the ground of (4) is removed by the very act (4) commands.

(8) Generalized: whenever I can predict my own future opinion, and whenever that opinion is produced by the activity that my prediction would license me to skip, I should skip the activity — and thereby destroy the authority of the opinion I was deferring to. Rationality would then require me to abstain from drafting, from revising, and from any learning that takes time, and to arrive directly at the settled judgments that only the work could have produced.

(9) That is absurd. If anything is absurd, that is.

(10) Therefore one of (1), (2), or (3) is false: either Reflection does not hold, or I do not know my own future mind as well as I suppose, or my future self is not the sort of authority whose judgment I am bound to adopt.

If I am forced to choose, I would most like to keep (1). I cannot see how else to explain why the judgment of my published, finished self should outweigh the judgment of my drafting self — and a five-year project seems to presuppose exactly that a later me has standing over an earlier me. Give that up and I lose the ability to plan a book at all. But I suspect the price of keeping (1) is high, and I want it stated plainly rather than waved at.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the step you reject looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down. Price each of the three exits I can see — abandoning Reflection, restricting it, and denying that (3) amounts to knowledge — and tell me whether any restriction survives, or whether every restriction that blocks this argument also blocks the ordinary cases that made Reflection appealing in the first place. Two further questions. First, (6) is the step I am least sure I have stated correctly: is it a real point about the conditions under which deference to a future self is legitimate, or is it special pleading dressed up as insight? Second, is (8) a fair generalization of the case I described, or has the argument quietly narrowed and then re-widened between (5) and (8)? Do not repeat my argument back to me, and do not finish on a non-committal note. Choose, and own the cost of the choice.
