I am a high school junior and I run my school's game theory club. This term we ran a series of modified dollar auctions — one dollar as the prize, penny increments, the top two bidders both pay their last bid, only the highest bidder receives the dollar — in front of the club, and we kept notes. The results were odd enough that I want to produce a handout that sorts our members by which explanation of escalation they actually accept, rather than by which label they would choose if asked.

Here are the six setups we used and roughly what happened in each:

(a) Standard rules, bids called aloud, audience present: within a minute bidding passed a dollar, and the last two members paid $2.06 and $2.05 for one dollar.
(b) Same rules, but bids written on folded paper so nobody knew the current high bid: escalation was much weaker, topping out near $1.10.
(c) Same as (a), except the second-highest bidder was refunded: bidding stopped at or just below $1.00 and no one took a real loss.
(d) Same as (a), but announced in advance and repeated five times with the same pair: the first round reached $2.60, the fifth ended at $1.05.
(e) Same as (a), with a hard cap of $2.00 announced beforehand: every pair ran straight to the cap, the winner paying $2.00 and the runner-up $1.99.
(f) Same as (a), but the two bidders were allowed to talk before each bid: they usually agreed on a deal and together paid well under a dollar.

Please write the handout as a decision tree with six to nine nodes. Each node should be a prediction about one of these setups, or about a seventh variant of your own design that we did not run; none should ask the reader about theories, mechanisms, or names. The leaves should name explanations, and must include at least: escalation as a failure to ignore sunk costs; escalation driven by the bidder's shift into a situation where every option is a loss; escalation as something the structure of the game genuinely licenses, so that the bidders are not making a mistake at all; escalation as the mixed-strategy outcome of a war of attrition; reputation effects across repeated rounds; not wanting to be seen backing down in front of an audience; and collusion or performance for the onlookers. Include a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves.

Two points I want handled directly. First, identify which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate on the basis of observable bidding behavior alone, and say what additional data — another variant, a different measurement — would separate them; do not paper over these. Second, lay out the logical space of your questions before locating the explanations in it, and wherever a combination of answers corresponds to a position nobody holds, say whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because no one has simply proposed it.

Since some members will read this as settled science, please also mark, at each leaf, whether the mechanism is broadly agreed upon by people who work on this or genuinely disputed — disputed as to whether the effect is real, how large it is, or whether the relevant fields are even describing the same phenomenon. I would much rather see contested material flagged as contested than the entire tree presented as a consensus view.
