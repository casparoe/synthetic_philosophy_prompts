For thirty-eight years I worked as a plant pathologist, latterly directing the diagnostic and epidemiology unit of a national agricultural research service. I retired eighteen months ago and have since been reading, rather belatedly, in the philosophy of history. I should be grateful for an essay on a controversy I have been unable to settle for myself.

The controversy concerns the standing of non-human causes in historical explanation. It has become ordinary — in popular history, and increasingly in professional work too — to write of a pathogen, a volcanic winter or a shift in rainfall as though it were a protagonist: something that intervenes, exploits an opening, and turns the course of events. The depopulation of the Americas after 1492 is commonly narrated with epidemic disease in the leading role; the potato blight of the 1840s is cast as the villain of the Irish catastrophe (strictly speaking, the organism responsible is no fungus at all but a water mould, a detail my colleagues never let pass); and the cold spells of the sixth century, apparently volcanic in origin, are pressed into service to explain political turmoil across much of Eurasia at once.

Two objections are pressed against this way of writing. The first is conceptual: that it is at best a figure of speech and at worst a category mistake, since only beings with intentions, or at least with some capacity to adjust their conduct to reasons or circumstances, can properly be called agents. The second is older and political: that installing climate or disease as the leading actor revives the environmental determinism of a century ago, dissolving human choice into environmental fate, and quietly relocating responsibility for what were in substantial part decisions taken by identifiable people — about labour, land, relief and export — onto a harvest or a microbe.

Let me now put forward a proposal of my own, which I should like the essay to take seriously enough to break. My suspicion is that the two sides are talking past one another because a single word is being made to cover at least three distinguishable properties:

(i) efficacy — the item makes a difference to what happens;
(ii) initiative — the item's contribution is not fixed in advance by human plans, so that the outcome was underdetermined by intention, and would have been otherwise had the item behaved otherwise;
(iii) responsiveness — the item adjusts itself to the situation it finds itself in, as a pathogen does when it evolves under the pressure of a control measure.

On my account most non-human items qualify for (i), a good many for (ii), and only living or engineered ones for (iii); and it is (ii) and (iii) that do the interesting work, while (i) is a red herring, since nothing turns on the truism that drought causes hardship.

I should also like the essay to examine a test I have been applying informally. Call it the redescription test: an attribution of agency to a non-human item is merely decorative if the same narrative can be rewritten so that the item appears only as a circumstance within which human agents choose, with nothing explanatory lost — no loss in the account of timing, of why this outcome rather than that one, of why these people rather than those. If the rewrite cannot be carried out without loss, the attribution is doing real work.

Please write an essay of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words that adjudicates this controversy and arrives at a conclusion you are prepared to defend: is the agency vocabulary legitimate, and if so in which of its senses; and does the charge of environmental determinism genuinely bite against its use? You should state the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting — and, if you reject my scheme, an account of what it fails to capture, or of the distinction it draws in the wrong place — and say why that position nonetheless fails. Please do not conclude by splitting the difference, nor by observing that reasonable historians disagree; I want a verdict and the grounds for it.

Two housekeeping matters. Write in British English. And do not trouble yourself with citations, a bibliography or a reading list: I am interested in argument and in well-chosen examples rather than apparatus, and I would rather you reasoned from cases than from authorities.
