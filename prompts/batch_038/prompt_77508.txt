So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, mostly policy-flavored stuff, and I'm working on a piece about the nudge decade — the UK's Behavioural Insights Team, which got set up inside the Cabinet Office around 2010, was supposed to be shut down if it didn't pay for itself, and then got partly sold off to Nesta a few years later. The poster child is always the organ donation work, and I want to get it right rather than just repeating the press release.

Facts as I have them, correct me if I'm off: they ran tests on the government's driver licence renewal page, which is where people in England sign up to the organ register. Millions of visitors got randomly assigned different sentences on the page. The winner was a reciprocity line, roughly asking you to imagine whether you'd want a transplant yourself if you needed one, and if so to help somebody else. Reported effect was on the order of a hundred thousand extra registrations a year, which is a big number for one sentence. Meanwhile Wales went a different route entirely and moved to a deemed-consent system where you're registered unless you opt out.

Can you analyze that episode properly through Thaler and Sunstein's libertarian paternalism? I want the mapping done carefully first: who exactly is the choice architect here, what's the choice set, what's the default, which bias is the winning message actually working on, and what the supposedly neutral alternative would have looked like given the inevitability argument. Then run it through the theory's own internal tests — the as-judged-by-themselves criterion and the transparency principle, the idea that a nudge should be one the architect wouldn't be embarassed to have disclosed publicly.

The bits I find tricky: the benefit lands on strangers who aren't in the room, so whose judgment is supposed to settle whether the nudge was good for them? Is a moral appeal even a nudge, or is it closer to persuasion, or to one of those competence-building boosts people contrast with nudging? And the message was disclosed — in a government report, to the press, everywhere — so does that satisfy the transparency principle or dodge it?

Then turn it around: does this episode confirm the theory, strain it, or suggest the theory was never really built for cases like this? I'd like you to be honest about where libertarian paternalism just goes quiet. Two things worth folding in if you think they're relevant — the accuracy problem with social norm messages, like the tax reminder letters where the localized everybody-around-here-pays-on-time claim seems to have had to be pulled because it wasn't true everywhere it was sent; and the finding from a couple of large comparisons that the effects nudge units get in the field run several times smaller than the effects published in academic trials. No bibliography, just the argument.
