I'm a staff software engineer at a specialty reinsurance analytics firm — no philosophy training beyond a paperback of Popper's The Poverty of Historicism that a colleague pressed on me last winter and that I've since been working through, slowly, on the commuter train. The colleague in question, whom I'll call R., is our head of research; she read the same book in graduate school and has made a habit, in our model-governance meetings, of ruling on whether a given long-range forecast counts as legitimate science or as what she calls "armchair prophecy." Because I built the intake pipeline for our forecasting vendors, I've been logging her rulings in a spreadsheet with a column for her stated reason, which is almost always one sentence and rarely illuminating on its own. Here is the track record as I have it, in the order she issued them. First: Marx's claim that capitalism's internal contradictions would produce a proletarian revolution and then a classless society — rejected, because, she said, he told us where history was heading rather than what would follow from what. Second: Comte's sequence of theological, metaphysical, and positive stages of society — rejected, "a stage sequence is not a law." Third: a national statistics office's projection of the school-age population in 2075 under three stated fertility assumptions — accepted, "conditional and revisable." Fourth: a 2080 sea-level estimate for a single coastline given an assumed emissions pathway — accepted, "physics plus a hypothesis about the boundary conditions." Fifth: a monograph's two-hundred-year projection of a remote pre-contact island population from carrying-constraint data, never revised since publication — accepted, "no growth of knowledge inside that system." Sixth: a 1970s national energy-demand forecast whose stated assumptions were explicit at the time but which is still cited unchanged today — rejected, "the conditions died and nobody buried them." Seventh: a futurist's claim that within fifty years artificial intelligence will have abolished wage labor and dissolved the nation-state — rejected, "a whole-society story with no failure conditions." Eighth: a ten-year extrapolation of unit compute cost used in our own capital plan and revised quarterly — accepted, though only with a caveat, and she called it "a trend on a leash."

The case that now has to go in front of our governance committee concerns a third-party product I'll call Global Horizons 2100. It is conditional on four socioeconomic pathways, each with explicit driving assumptions about technology-adoption curves, policy stringency, and trade regimes; it has a physical module built on an Earth-system model that the vendor says is falsifiable and validated against held-out historical periods; and it has a socioeconomic module whose documentation states that the pathways are illustrative storylines rather than forecasts, declines to specify what evidence would count against any of them, and describes its five-yearly revisions as updates to the storylines rather than corrections of errors. Its outputs include projected cross-border migration volumes for 2100 and a composite sociopolitical stability index. The intended use at our firm is twofold, and I expect the two to come apart: setting capital reserves and pricing long-dated property lines, and — if the board likes what it sees — justifying a wholesale withdrawal from certain geographies by 2040.

What I'd like from you is a prediction of R.'s ruling, and the reasoning behind it. Please lay out the candidate rules that fit all eight recorded rulings, noting for each which ruling or rulings it has to strain to accommodate — I'm fairly sure at least one of them can't take the fifth ruling and another can't take the eighth, but I can't tell whether that kills them or just forces a qualification, and I don't have the vocabulary for the options (if you use terms like holism, trend-versus-law, or the growth-of-knowledge argument, please define them in a clause). Then say what each surviving rule predicts about Global Horizons 2100, including whether any of them would split the verdict between the physical module and the socioeconomic module, and whether any of them would let the reserve-setting use through while blocking the geographic-withdrawal use. Give me your best single guess at her ruling together with the reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals, and then name the sharpest additional case I could put to her — real or constructed — that would discriminate between your top two rules, along with the answer each rule would give it. Finally, tell me where my own log is probably unreliable: I have a stake in the license going through, since a negative ruling would descope a year of my work, and I'd rather you point out which of my eight entries looks like motivated recording than have me discover it in the meeting.
