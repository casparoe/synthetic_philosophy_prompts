I'm prepping week two of an MSc seminar on minds, machines and incompleteness and I keep hitting the same wall every year, so I'd like some help getting through it properly.

The setup, which you'll know: Hofstadter's MIU-system. One axiom, MI, and four rules — append a U to any string ending in I; double whatever follows the M; replace any III with a U; delete any UU. The puzzle is to derive MU. You can't, becuase the number of I's in any derivable string is never a multiple of three, and MU has zero I's, and zero is a multiple of three. My students pick up that invariant proof and then immediately draw two conclusions I don't think follow: that MU is undecidable in anything like Gödel's sense, and that the fact we can see the invariant from outside while the system can't is a working demonstration of jumping out of the system — hence a cheap scale model of the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism.

Here's the wrinkle I've made up to shake them out of it, and I want you to actually engage with it rather than stand back and comment. Keep the axiom and all four rules, but add a fifth: wherever there's a U, you may turn it into an I. First question — is MU now derivable? If it is, give me the shortest derivation you can find, and then explain conceptually, not by grinding through cases, why that one extra rule demolishes the whole impossibility proof. What I need from this part is an account of what the invariant was ever doing: was it tracking a structural limitation of the system, or was it a contingent arithmetical accident about this particular bundle of rules? Spell out what makes a rule invariant-preserving and what breaks it, since I want to be able to tell them in advance which kinds of new rule would and wouldn't have this effect.

Then the main business. Explain the difference in kind between MU not being derivable in MIU and the Gödel sentence not being provable in PA. Four things I can never get across crisply:

- MIU's strings aren't sentences. No negation, no way for a string to say anything, let alone anything about MIU's own derivations. So does undecidability even get a grip here? I tell them MIU is in fact decidable — there's a complete criterion for exactly which strings come out — and they think that's a quibble, since MU still isn't derivable. Get me an account of why it isn't a quibble at all.
- Hofstadter has to climb well outside MIU, up into arithmetic, before he can say anything whatsoever about MIU's theorems. In the Gödel case the sentence is expressible in the very system that can't prove it. Why is that difference the whole ballgame rather than a technicality?
- With MU, strengthening the system makes the obstruction vanish outright and permanently — my fifth rule and it's gone. With Gödel, adding G as an axiom to PA gives you a system where G is provable and a fresh G' is not. Explain why the obstruction behaves so differently under strengthening, and what that shows about whether the outside view is a special cognitive faculty or just ordinary mathematics done one level up, where the next level has its own outside.
- And the payoff: any programme can count I's modulo three, so the MU-style jump is trivially mechanisable. Does that undercut the anti-mechanist use of jumping out of the system, or can the Lucas–Penrose line be recast so it survives the objection? I want a real verdict here, not a both-sides shrug — and if you think the MU case and the Gödel case pull in opposite directions on this question, say which one the mechanist should be happy about and why.

No bibliography, please, I know the literature. What I'm after is the conceptual anatomy, laid out cleanly enough that I can use it in front of a room without hand-waving.
