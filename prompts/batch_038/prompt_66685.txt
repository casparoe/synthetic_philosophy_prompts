I write a science-and-ideas column for a monthly and I've been sitting on a piece about work and identity for three months now — layoffs, "quiet quitting", people who can't tell you what they want because they can only tell you what they are. What unstuck it was an interview with a philosopher who works in Confucian role ethics, the Ames-and-Rosemont line: roughly, that there's no bare self underneath the roles, that you are your particular relationships and stations rather than an individual who happens to occupy them, and that being a person is something you do rather than something you are by default. She also had a coinage I liked — "role remainder", the idea that when two of your roles collide and one loses, the loser doesn't get switched off, it keeps making a claim on you. I'm trying to figure out whether my own gut verdicts actually fit that picture or whether I'm a liberal individualist in a role-ethicist costume. Here's what I think, case by case:

1. My brother's an ER doctor. During the worst stretch of the pandemic he kept going in with a pregnant wife at home and a desk transfer on offer. I don't think that was a heroic cost-benefit sacrifice; I think it was just him being a doctor, with no self standing outside the role doing the weighing.
2. A friend stayed in a marriage for fifteen years after it turned violent, and her reason was always "I'm a wife, this is who I am." I think she did something wrong — not stupid, wrong. And I can't see how that differs from my brother's case unless something outside the roles is doing the judging, which is exactly what the view says there isnt.
3. A colleague got cut and told me she didn't know who she was anymore. I took that literally, not as a figure of speech.
4. Same colleague, six months later, turns down a well-paid job at a firm she thinks is doing harm because "that's not me." I believed her and I thought it was the strongest reason she had. But what's the "me" in that sentence if not another role?
5. My grandmother, widowed, kids scattered, most friends dead, no job, no caregiving left. She paints all day and says she's more herself now than at any point in her life. I believe her — though on the role-ethics story she should be evaporating.
6. A guy I know is decent in exactly one place: an anonymous forum, no audience, no reciprocity, no expectations, no relationships at all. He's kinder there than anyone I know is anywhere, and I count that as real virtue — the purest case I've got, and the one with the fewest roles in it.
7. Mine. My father once asked me to kill a story about a firm his oldest friend runs. Son versus journalist. I killed nothing and I don't think I did wrong, but I felt the remainder exactly as she described it. Here's the version that bothers me: if the tradeoff had been between the story and something that would wreck my kid's life, I'd take the kid and I wouldn't feel for one second that I'd betrayed journalism. Same structure, opposite verdict, no idea what's doing the sorting.
8. A nurse I profiled who is flawless at the bedside — every task, every script, never a miss — and told me she feels nothing for her patients. My verdict: she's a good nurse and a bad one at once, and the second isn't a private add-on that leaves the first intact.

So: give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that comes closest to delivering these verdicts. State each clause precisely and tell me which case forces it, because I want to see the machinery rather than a mood. Then tell me what it says about two cases I didn't list — someone who takes on a role deliberately as pure performance, say a monk who renounces roles in order to have a role — and someone whose only real relationship is with a companion system that is by design nothing but a role-holder, a friend and only a friend to one person: does the human in that arrangement come out more of a person or less? I also want you to name the position my verdicts actually add up to — is this Confucian role ethics, virtue ethics with roles as the location of virtue, or a narrative-identity view wearing a costume — and say what would have to change in my verdicts for them to be genuinely the Confucian thing. Finally, be straight with me about the residue: which of the eight your principle can't get, which one I should drop and what it costs me to drop it, and whether you'd rather have a simple account that overturns two verdicts than a baroque one that saves all eight. And does it need the remainder idea, or is that just decoration?
