The assignment question was:

"Explain Glymour's problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory, and evaluate at least one proposed solution. Say what the solution buys you and what it doesn't."

Here's the student's answer (I fixed their typos, not their argument):

"Bayesianism says that when you learn evidence E, the rational way to revise is to conditionalize: your new credence in a hypothesis H is your old conditional credence in H given E. On that picture, E confirms H just in case P(H|E) > P(H).

Glymour's problem is that a lot of what counts as confirmation in actual science involves evidence that was already accepted long before the confirming theory arrived. The standard example is the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion: the discrepancy with Newtonian predictions had been measured for decades before general relativity came along and derived it, and that derivation is one of the things people point to when they say GR was confirmed. But the Bayesian apparatus can't represent that. Once E is part of your total evidence, P(E) = 1, and a probability-one proposition can't move anything: P(H|E) comes out exactly equal to P(H), so the evidence is neutral. There's also no route back to it, since conditionalization runs one way — you'd have to unlearn the perihelion figure before GR could be confirmed by it, and unlearning it is exactly what you don't want to do. Glymour took this to show that strict conditionalization can't be the whole story about confirmation, and he went looking for a non-Bayesian account.

There are two familiar repairs. The first is Jeffrey conditionalization, which generalizes the rule so the incoming evidence doesn't have to be certain: you get a new distribution over a partition of experiential propositions, and everything else shifts while the conditional probabilities stay fixed. That's obviously the right fix here, because the whole difficulty was that E was pinned at one; if the rule lets the probability of the evidence itself move, it can move back down again, and old evidence becomes usable.

The second is Garber's. It keeps strict conditionalization but denies that E is the proposition the scientist learns. What she learns is the entailment — that GR implies the perihelion figure — and that can be treated as a new proposition added to the language, with a prior short of one. Conditionalizing on it can then raise P(GR). Importantly, none of this requires weakening the assumption of logical omniscience: the entailment is a logical truth, so the agent can be said to have known it all along, and what changes is only which propositions she has written down. The prior over the new entailment sentence does the work, and I think any positive prior at all will confirm the theory, since the probability of the entailment sentence given the theory is one and given its negation is less than one.

Both repairs cost something. Garber's needs a new proposition for every entailment relation the agent might discover, which threatens to blow up the language, and it makes confirmation depend on an arbitrary-seeming choice of prior. The Howson-style strategy of conditionalizing on a counterfactual prior with E surgically removed from it is cleaner in principle but can't say which parts of your evidence you're allowed to strip out. Neither repair speaks to the harder case where the theory rather than the evidence is new: if the agent couldn't have entertained H at all, there is no prior credence in H for anything to raise."
