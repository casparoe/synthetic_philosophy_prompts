A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about whether publishing the source code of a decision procedure — an algorithmic pricing engine, a trading bot, an automated negotiation agent — is a genuine commitment device, or merely an elaborate way of describing the fact that whoever moves first has an advantage. I would like the argument settled by construction rather than by assertion, so please build a case to specification.

**The setting.** Two agents each hand in the source code of a decision procedure. Each agent's code is then delivered to the other, in full, to read before acting; each then makes one move. There is no repetition, no predictor, no randomisation, no private information of any kind, and no channel between the agents other than the exchanged source text. The game is Chicken rather than the Prisoner's Dilemma: each agent chooses Hold Firm or Give Way, and each agent's ranking of outcomes is Hold Firm while the other gives way (best), both give way, give way while the other holds firm, both hold firm (disaster). Concretely, let the two agents be an incumbent whose procedure must by regulation be filed publicly and frozen before trading opens, and an entrant who writes its own procedure after reading that filing.

**Specifications.**

1. The incumbent holds firm and the entrant gives way. Write both procedures in pseudocode, ten lines each at most.
2. The entrant must be correct rather than gullible: its procedure gives way against a filing that is genuinely firm and holds firm against one that is not. State precisely what it inspects, and why a bluff — a filing that appears firm but retains a reachable give-way branch — cannot defeat it. If your answer is that full transparency eliminates bluffing as a category, demonstrate that rather than asserting it.
3. Neither procedure may contain a stored copy of, or a fingerprint of, any particular opponent's code, and neither may compute a similarity score between the text in front of it and a template. What an agent does must not depend on who its opponent is. (This rules out recognising whom you are playing; it does not rule out computing anything you like from the text actually presented to you.)
4. The incumbent's firmness must be ex post irrational: at the moment its move is executed, had the entrant held firm, the incumbent would have preferred to give way. Show why that costs it nothing here.
5. Then the variant that decides my argument: both procedures are filed simultaneously, and each is firm-holding. Construct a tie-break under which the disaster is avoided without submission order, without an arbiter, and without randomisation. The rule must be anonymous — it may depend on the two texts in front of the programs, but any two procedures implementing it must resolve the tie the same way regardless of who filed them — and tell me what your rule does when the two filings are byte-for-byte identical.

**After the construction**, and without restating it: name the single feature of the case that does the work; say whether the incumbent's advantage survives if the filing reaches the entrant over a channel that garbles it with probability 0.05; and give the strongest version of my friend's objection — that all this describes is a Stackelberg leader, and the transparency of the code adds nothing to what moving first already supplied. Then say whether the objection lands, and what would have to be true of the setting for it not to.

The verdicts must be generated by the procedures as written rather than stipulated: if you find yourself telling me what an agent does instead of showing it from its code, the case has failed. Hard limit of 750 words for the whole response, pseudocode included.
