I am in the middle of revisions on my third novel, and one scene has turned out to depend on a sentence I cannot yet defend. The book concerns two siblings: Ilse, a relativist at a small institute, and her brother Tomas, who restores carousel horses in the winter and runs the ride at a seaside fairground through the summer. In the ninth chapter Tomas lets her onto the platform before the season opens, at five in the morning, and starts the motor so that the two of them can stand on the turning floor while the whole empty fairground slides past. He asks whether they are moving. Her answer is the verbal tic I have been seeding through the book since page forty: all motion is relative. He says, relative to what, then? She says, to everything else there is; and then she gives him Newton's bucket, the water climbing the sides of the spinning pail, and tells him that the climb has nothing to do with the pail and everything to do with the rest of the mass in the universe, so that if the platform stayed still and the whole of everything else went round it he would feel precisely the same pull at his shoulders. She adds, almost as an afterthought, that in her own subject a rotating shell of matter does in fact drag the local frames of inertia round with it a little, which she takes to be a point in her favour.

My first reader — a former philosophy tutor I keep on for exactly this kind of trouble — tells me the first sentence is either a platitude nobody disputes or a claim that is simply false, that I plainly do not know which, and that I have confused a symmetry principle with a metaphysics. He thinks Ilse is either restating Mach's point or overclaiming on his behalf, and he has not said which. My copy-editor has meanwhile raised a query about whether the tic should read "all motion is relative" or "motion is only ever relative," and I have stalled her, because the two phrasings may not be interchangeable and I cannot yet say why. I would much rather repair the scene than cut it, since the tic pays off in the final chapter. So: lay out the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making, ranging from something about the grammar of the verb to something about the furniture of the world, and treat her follow-up — to everything else there is — as one of the readings in its own right rather than as a gloss on the others. For each, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive position that must be argued for, or false; whether it fares any better or worse in general relativity than in Newtonian mechanics; and above all whether it would actually license what Ilse does with the bucket, which is to dismiss it. Where a reading needs a further premise to carry that weight, say what the premise is, and tell me honestly if none of them can.

Please give me exactly five numbered readings, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion; within each item, name the reading, classify it, and state whether it saves the scene.
