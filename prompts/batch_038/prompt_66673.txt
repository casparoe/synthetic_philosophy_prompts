I teach history at the comprehensive in a fairly small market town, 22 years now, and in November I have to give the address at the rededication of our war memorial. Some background first, because the speech only works if its honest about how we got here.

Our memorial has 84 names from 1914–18. Three years ago I led a campaign to add a plaque that named the governments responsible for the war — not in some hedged everybody-was-responsible way, but pointing at the decisions taken in Vienna and Berlin in July 1914, and at the annexation aims the German leadership then went after once the war had actually started. I read around this for years and I think the case is strong, roughly what Fritz Fischer made in the sixties, though I am not asking you for a seminar about him or anyone else.

The parish council voted 6–5 against naming anybody. The plaque they approved says: "In memory of the eighty-four people of this parish who died in the war of 1914–1918. No one here chose it. No one anywhere should have."

That second sentence has no subject, and that's the whole thing I can't stand. Somebody did choose it. Choosing is the only kind of thing responsibility can attach to. The plaque's grammar is precisely the argument I lost, cast in bronze. And I'm still the one asked to speak, mostly because they want the row to look finished.

Who's there: maybe 150 people, outdoors, in the wind. Families with names on the stone, the Legion, a couple dozen of my own students, about twenty people who signed the petition and are still furious, and a delegation from our twin town in Nordrhein-Westfalen whose mayor speaks right after me. Also in the front row, the granddaughter of a German-born woman who ran the bakery on the high street for thirty years, was taken away as an enemy alien in 1914, and never lived here again. Her family backed my campaign and thinks the compromise plaque is cowardice, so I can't comfort them with softness. And I can't pretend not to notice that the logic I was pushing — this nation is the responsible party — is close cousin to the logic that put a woman in a van because of where she was born. That's the objection actually in the room, and the speech has to face it rather than step around it.

So please write the speech. It needs to:

- run six or seven minutes spoken aloud, in a place where nobody wants a lecture;
- make a genuine responsibility claim — that governments and nations are the right kind of thing to hold responsible, and that in this case responsibility is not evenly spread — without using the word "guilt" once. That word is radioactive here: the people who beat me ran on the line that naming Germany would be the treaty article about Germany and her allies all over again, and I don't want to hand them that;
- carry the substance of the July 1914 decisions and the war aims without naming a historian, a book, or a document, and without a date-heavy timeline — concrete plain-English facts a cold person can hold in their head, not citations;
- not be a concession speech. No "we were all wrong," no everyone-blame-therefore-no-blame, no pretending the council's vote was wise. I have to be able to say every sentence and mean it;
- be hearable by the German delegation as something true rather than as an accusation, and leave their mayor something to respond to rather than a wall;
- sit with the bakery case honestly, without resolving it in a neat line;
- end by working with the plaque's own wording instead of arguing against it out loud, and give 150 cold people something they can actually say together.

Then afterwards, in five or six lines, tell me where you think I'll be tempted to chicken out while delivering it, what it costs each time, and give me a cut-down three-minute version for if the rain moves us into the church hall.
