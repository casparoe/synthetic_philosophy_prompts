For an independent study I am doing this year on the metaphysics of ordinary objects, I have been reading about the case of the statue and the lump of clay, and I have constructed a variation of my own that I cannot find discussed anywhere. I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who argue about it.

Here is my variation. A museum holds a small terracotta statue, and the lump of clay it is made of weighs exactly 4.0 kilograms. A conservator begins making a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the statue out of fresh clay from the same deposit, working in a second room, at a rate of 10 grams per day. Every evening she removes 10 grams of the original clay — always from the interior, never from a part that carries the surface — and ships it off to be analyzed. Because she replaces what she removes with 10 grams of the new clay, matched to the surface exactly, the finished piece on display never changes in weight, in any of its outward dimensions, or in any of its visible marks. After four hundred days, none of the clay in the first room remains; all 4.0 kilograms of it now sits in labeled trays in the second room, where, over the following year, the conservator assembles it into a figure that is molecule-for-molecule indistinguishable from the original and that she has every intention of calling the original. At the end there are two such figures in two rooms, each weighing 4.0 kilograms, and at no moment in the process was there any visible interruption, any gap, or any occasion on which an observer could have pointed to the statue and said that it had ceased to be.

The two speakers should be:

- **Speaker One**, who holds that the statue just is the clay arranged statuewise, so that there is one object here, not two. Speaker One should further hold that what makes a lump of clay the very same lump over time is not the retention of all its matter but the continuity of its career, so that the piece on display survives the four hundred days and the trays in the second room hold merely clay.
- **Speaker Two**, who holds that the statue and the clay are two distinct objects that coincide while the statue is intact but come apart in cases of this kind. Speaker Two should have a positive account of what makes something the same statue at different times — something like the continued realization of the same form, or the persistence of the artifact's own history — rather than appealing to any mysterious ingredient over and above the clay.

Neither speaker should be a strawman, and neither should win by default. I want each of them to press the other on at least the following points, and to answer rather than evade: what the words "the statue" refer to, if anything, while the conservator is halfway through; how many statues there are before the process begins, during it, and after it ends, and on what grounds that number could differ from two; whether the four-hundred-day replacement is relevantly like the slow replacement of a person's cells, or whether the analogy fails and where; and what is supposed to explain the fact that both speakers find themselves pulled toward opposite verdicts about the very same case.

Please write it in American English, in a plain script format with the two speakers labeled and no stage directions, and aim for roughly 1,000 words. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each speaker responding to what the other has just said rather than delivering prepared speeches, and each should concede small points where concessions are honestly owed. It should not end in agreement or in a handshake about how interesting the puzzle is. End instead with each speaker stating the one question the other has failed to answer and explaining why that failure is not merely a matter of taste or of describing the same facts in different words. Please do not cite any books, articles, or philosophers by name, and do not attach a reading list; I want the argument to stand on the case itself.
