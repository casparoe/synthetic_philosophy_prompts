I'm enrolled in a graduate seminar that sits between decision theory and public administration, and our final assignment is a piece of speculative fiction that has to do conceptual work rather than decorate it. The instructions we were given are blunt: a story that merely name-drops its topic in dialogue fails, and a story that pauses to explain the topic fails harder. My topic is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — the result that if each person's preferences over a common menu of prospects satisfy the expected-utility axioms, if the social ranking over those same prospects satisfies them as well, and if the social ranking respects unanimity, then social utility must be a positive weighted sum of individual utilities, and if symmetry across persons is added the weights must be equal. What actually interests me is not the theorem but everything an institution built on it would have to settle that the theorem is silent about. Please write that story, roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words.

Here is the seed I have settled on; adjust the surface details freely, but keep the architecture. The setting is a port city still rebuilding after a catastrophic flood, where the reconstruction budget is allocated by an office whose charter is explicitly derived from the theorem: every resident's preferences are elicited through standardized interviews over small gambles and represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, and the office's social ranking over the same prospects is required to satisfy unanimity, the expected-utility axioms, and symmetry. Everyone in the city therefore treats the equal-weighted sum as a proof that they live under a fair constitution, and the charter's author — an old director who came to power after the post-flood councils collapsed — defends it on precisely those grounds: it is public, it cannot be argued with, and it was the only arrangement the districts would all sign. Your viewpoint character should be a junior clerk, newly transferred into the office, whose actual job is administering the anchoring interviews, so that she is not an expert and has to acquire the theory in fragments from forms, memoranda, and arguments overheard in corridors.

The story should carry four beats, and they may be compressed — the fourth can be a page. First: she discovers that an equal-weighted sum is determined only once each person's scale has been given a zero and a unit, and that the office fixes those two points by anchoring every resident to a common reference life and a common standardized stake, a choice that appears nowhere in the charter, is made by clerks of her rank, and is unappealable; an organizer from a poor district works this out too, and starts a campaign not about weights but about anchors. Second: a resident — a woman whose household sits on the wrong side of the seawall line — answers the anchoring questions in a way that admits no real-valued representation at all, because no quantity of anything else compensates her for the smallest loss of the one thing she is being asked to trade, and the clerk must either write her out of the polity or quietly falsify her answers until she becomes legible. Third: a hearing on a proposal to fund one of two districts by drawing lots, where each district's expected position is the same under the lottery as under a certain, middling, equal split, so the charter renders the office indifferent, and the crowd objecting is not confused about the arithmetic but objecting to a condition — the one that lets the office ignore how things stand in each state once the coin lands — that nobody ever voted on. Fourth: someone asks the office to rank two allocations containing no risk whatsoever and it returns nothing, because the charter only ever produced rankings of prospects, and the city's actual decisions all live in the space the charter never reached; fold into this a district that has been poor long enough to have stopped staking claims at all, so that the machinery reads its diminished asking as diminished need. Keep the logic honest: the theorem is never refuted in your story, and no character should dismiss it by observing that utilities are unmeasurable, since that difficulty is already inside the setup and the drama lies in what the office does about it. Both the director and the organizer should get their strongest case on the page. No exposition dumps, no glossary, no character delivering a summary of the result, no epigraphs or citations of any kind, and no closing moral: end with the clerk understanding that the charter's fairness was never a theorem but a stack of choices she herself handles every morning, and also understanding what would be lost the day the city admits it.
