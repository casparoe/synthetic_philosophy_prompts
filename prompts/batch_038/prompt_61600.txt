i run a small reading group that's already done the Nyāya groundwork — the four pramāṇas, the standard dialectical move for dealing with a rival candidate (show it collapses into one you've already got), what a good inference needs (the reason present in the locus, a pervasion between reason and target established by repeated observation with no counterexamples, no defeating conditions), what makes a speaker an āpta, and how cognising an absence differs from inferring from non-apprehension. so don't re-teach any of that. the live disagreement in the group is this:

a language model is asked a dry factual question nobody in the room has any independent grounds for answering — say, whether a treatise whose only known copy was in a library that burned in 1891 is listed in the surviving fragment of that library's accession register. it answers yes, names the register folio, and the answer is right; weeks later one of us confirms it against a scan. but at the moment of the output, nobody in the room had a thing to go on except the screen.

write a dialogue between the two people in the group who disagree about what to call that. Vasanti holds that the output is a fifth pramāṇa — a route to a true cognition that is not reducible to any of the four, and whose deliverances don't have to be laundered through something else before you can act on them. Raghunātha holds the four are complete as a list of irreducible causal routes to knowledge, so the reduction must go through; and he is a four-pramāṇa man, which means he cannot borrow postulation or non-apprehension as his rivals the Mīmāṃsakas would — he has only perception, inference and word left, and that handicap is the interesting part, so make him feel it.

give them real names, as above, and about 800–1100 words. they should be listening to each other: every turn takes up the specific thing the other just said and pushes on it, no parallel monologues. neither is a strawman and they're both very good — Raghunātha's best shot at reducing the case to inference or to word should be strong enough that I genuinely can't tell whether it works, and Vasanti should have to pay something for her fifth pramāṇa (what stops a thermometer, a lucky guess, or a horoscope from being a pramāṇa too?). small concessions both ways, nobody converts, and don't hand down a verdict: close on a concession plus a challenge that's left standing.

gloss the Sanskrit terms in a few words in parentheses the first time each one comes up, so a non-specialist can follow, but don't slow it down into a lecture. and keep it out of the modern AI-angst lane — no consciousness, no alignment, no jokes about the fact that a model is writing this. it's a seminar room and the question is pramāṇa theory.

start with the first line of the dialogue itself — no title, no preface, no note afterwards about who you think is right. open with Vasanti, and let her first move be one Raghunātha actually has to sweat: ask him to name the pervaded relation his inference is supposed to run on.
