Academic journals retract a paper by publishing a separate notice stating who is retracting it and why, while the paper itself stays online, watermarked and linked to that notice; the stated aim is to correct the literature rather than to punish the authors, and yet the notice almost always records whether the failure was honest error or misconduct — and journals have devised a distinct device, retraction with replacement, precisely to avoid imputing dishonest intent. Reconstruct the account of speech acts this practice presupposes. What kind of act is a retraction: does it alter the paper's standing, withdraw an endorsement the journal made at publication, or simply warn readers? What are its felicity conditions, given that editors, authors, and institutions can each initiate one, and that an author may be dead or unwilling? Why does the practice mark rather than delete, and can marking do its work at all if retracted papers keep being cited — is uptake a condition of the act's success, or only of its effectiveness? And how should we understand a document that disclaims blame while assigning it: one act doing two jobs, two acts bundled into a single notice, or does the epistemic work genuinely require knowing why the result failed? Say what would have to change in the practice if journals dropped the reason field entirely. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on performative utterances from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether declarations form a natural class, or whether any sincerity condition applies to a retraction at all — and do not present a disputed analysis as the consensus.
