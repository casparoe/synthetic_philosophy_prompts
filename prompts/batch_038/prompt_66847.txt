I'm the grad student rep on my university's animal ethics board. It's normally an IACUC-style operation — protocols for lab rodents, the 3Rs, minimize pain, minimize how many animals you use — but last year the charter got rewritten to cover any university project involving vertebrates, and I'm pretty sure nobody thought through what that would mean.

Now there's a proposal in front of us. A biology PhD student wants to run a two-year pilot spreading bait laced with a slow-acting contraceptive to the gray squirrels on a preserve the university owns, aiming to flatten the boom-bust cycle that leaves a bunch of them starving every other winter. Her case is that starvation is the worst thing happening to these animals and fewer births means fewer starvers. She's got data from a captive colony showing the drug cuts litter size by something like 60% with no obvious side effects, but nothing from wild populations, and the bait isn't species-specific — chipmunks and probably songbirds will get into some of it. The preserve manager is for it because squirrels raid bird nests; the ecology faculty are against it on the grounds that messing with a population you don't understand is how you wreck a small woodland; and a student animal-liberation group has emailed saying a rejection would prove the board cares more about not being inconvenienced than about animals actually suffering.

Can you draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? It needs to come down on something — approve, reject, or approve with conditions specific enough to actually enforce, plus a timeline. It should also say what our charter's reduce-the-number-of-animals language is even supposed to mean when the animals aren't ours and the whole point is to end up with fewer of them. And give the ecology objection its strongest version, then tell me honestly whether whatever you recommend survives it.
