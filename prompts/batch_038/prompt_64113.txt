I retired early from a career in experimental geophysics, most of it spent arguing with colleagues about whether a given instrument reading meant anything, and I have filled the retirement with philosophy: a Wednesday reading group, a good deal of Collingwood, whose contempt for the historian who merely cuts and pastes his authorities together I find bracing and, at the desk, quite useless. Let me describe the desk, since my request depends on it. Our county historical society publishes a quarterly journal of local history, and two years ago the board co-opted me as its lay member on the reasoning that it wanted someone accustomed to weighing evidence and unmoved by a good story. We receive twenty-five to thirty submissions a year, nearly all from amateur local historians of real diligence and no methodological training. I allow myself perhaps ninety minutes per submission; I have no palaeography, no Latin, and no archive within twenty miles; every decision goes out over my signature with a reason the author can read in two sentences; and the board's one professionally trained historian, who tolerates me with great politeness, maintains that source criticism is a craft that cannot be written down. I do not believe him, and I should like your help in showing that he is wrong.

Here is what I currently do, and why I have come to distrust it. I treat each source as an instrument and estimate three quantities for it: its calibration, by which I mean how close in time and place to the events described it was produced; its systematic bias, meaning the author's interests, loyalties, expectations and the conventions of the genre in which he writes; and its noise, meaning memory failure, garbled figures, scribal corruption in transmission. I then ask whether the errors of two such instruments are independent, and if they are I treat their agreement as compounding my confidence, much as repeated measurements do. I now think the analogy is worse than I supposed, and I want you to say precisely where it fails rather than reassure me. Two witnesses may share an ancestor, in which case their agreement carries no information whatsoever, and independence turns out to be a matter of genealogy rather than of statistics. Reliability may not be a property of a source at all but of a source relative to a particular claim, since our village diarist is authoritative on the weather of March 1911 and worthless on why the rector resigned. And the whole frame may misdescribe what happens when I read a self-serving memoir, which is not a miscalibrated instrument but evidence of something else entirely, namely of what its author needed to believe. I have the same suspicion in the other direction about my own instincts: I keep wanting to rank physical and scientific evidence — tree rings, isotope ratios, the fabric of a building, a coin in a dated pit — above testimony, when a single document written a century after the fact may carry less weight than an account carefully handed down within the community it describes. As I recall him, Thucydides said in effect that he made his speakers say what the occasion demanded and warned his readers against taking the first story they were offered, and Herodotus keeps marking a distinction between what he saw, what he was told, and what he makes of it; if I have either of them wrong, correct me in a clause and do not build anything on my having got it right.

Write to me, then, as a philosopher of history who has also sat through a great many editorial meetings: think the matter through privately, beginning not with the textbook categories but with what a careful reader actually does in the hour before deciding that a claim is usable, and give me only the finished article. What I want is a decision procedure in the plain sense — the questions to ask of a submission, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure stops with a reject or a revise-and-resubmit; and, marked explicitly, the points at which it hands the matter back to a human being because the question is one of judgment or of what the journal is for rather than of evidence, since I need to know when I am merely dressing up my own taste as method. Fold in the tests I have picked up, mostly from the study of the gospels, and tell me where each is abused in practice: multiple attestation and the common-ancestor trap that hollows it out; the criterion of embarrassment, which I have watched amateur writers invert into a rule that anything unflattering to its subject must be true; the standing difference between establishing what a document says and establishing that what it says happened; the peculiar problem of a source transmitting an earlier source I cannot see; and what to make of well-transmitted oral tradition reaching back three or four generations, which I suspect I currently underrate. Then run the procedure on three submissions now on my desk. The first is a memoir of a colliery explosion here in 1926, dictated in 1971 to his grandson by a man who was twenty-one at the time, in which his own conduct is uniformly courageous and the mine manager's negligence is described in technically specific and, so far as I can judge, accurate detail; the society holds nothing else on the disaster beyond two newspaper paragraphs, and the author's widow is a founding subscriber to this journal. The second reconstructs an enclosure riot of 1847 from two newspaper accounts published within four days of it in the same market town, one of them in a paper owned by the family whose hedges were torn up; the author treats their agreement on the sequence of events as decisive corroboration and builds the entire paper upon it. The third is a short note on a seventeenth-century parish schoolmaster resting wholly on an antiquarian's notebook compiled in the 1890s, which quotes a document now lost and not otherwise known without saying where it was kept, the compiler being a man of enormous industry and, in our trained historian's estimate, of variable care in transcription. Tell me which of the three verdicts I shall dislike and why the procedure is nonetheless right, and finish by being candid about what the procedure cannot do — the classes of submission on which it will generate confident nonsense, and what I should do when I suspect I am looking at one. Please give me all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables; where sequence matters, put it in sentences, first this, then that, and if the answer to the third question is so-and-so, stop. And give me no bibliography, no reading list and no citations of any kind; where a particular text matters, describe in your own words what it says and does not quote it, since I have no means of checking quotations and would far rather have your honest paraphrase than your recollection of a wording.
