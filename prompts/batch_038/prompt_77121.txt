I do model risk at a mid-size US health insurer — I'm the person who has to sign off before a claims-prediction, readmission, or utilization-management model goes into production, along with a compliance attorney and one of our staff physicians. We review maybe fifteen models a quarter, and the standard validation checks whether it fits, whether it's stable on a held-out slice, whether it trips our fairness thresholds by protected class. Almost none of that touches the failure mode I actually lose sleep over, which is a model that has latched onto a predicate that looked perfectly projectible inside the training window and quietly isn't. Everyone in the field knows the old chest X-ray story about the classifier picking up the portable-scanner artifact or the hospital's film marker rather than the pathology; we've had our own versions. A readmission model of ours keyed heavily on a case-management program code that turned out to exist for about eighteen months and then got retired in a systems migration. A pharmacy model treated "fills at this particular chain" as a signal about the patient when it was really a signal about a formulary deal. My reading of the grue problem says this isn't a defect in our feature pipeline that better tooling would fix — it's the normal condition: any finite set of cases is fit by indefinitely many predicates, and which ones we're willing to project onto the next quarter is settled by something other than the data. The trouble is that entrenchment is a description of what we already do, not a test I can hand a data scientist on a Tuesday.

So write me the procedure my team could actually run at model review. I want the ordered questions or tests to apply to a model's top features — say the top ten by whatever attribution measure the team already computes — with what counts as a passing answer at each step, roughly how long each step takes (assume the data scientist can give me a day of their time and I can give an hour per model), and, importantly, where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a human because the remaining question isn't factual but is about which population we think we're sampling from. I'd like the Goodman material to be load-bearing rather than decorative. Specifically: what does entrenchment buy you that a plain "is this feature causally upstream of the outcome?" check doesn't, and where does it mislead you? What's the deployment-world species of a grue predicate — my guess is a predicate that stays perfectly stable for as long as observations keep arriving and only breaks when some institutional arrangement changes, but tell me if that's the wrong shape. And where do overhypotheses come in, i.e. at what point does the procedure need to ask not "does this feature hold in the training data" but "what makes this training sample a sample of the population we'll actually face"? Be blunt about which steps are genuinely Goodmanian and which are ordinary robustness hygiene wearing his name — I'd rather have a checklist that admits its limits than one that implies the 1950s puzzle got solved somewhere along the way.

Then run it on three features and show the worksheet, not just the verdict. First: a high-cost-episode model that uses the count of prior authorizations a member's provider filed in the last twelve months — it reads like a severity proxy, and we're about to onboard a large hospital system that outsources its prior auth to a vendor. Second: "member is enrolled in our wearable-device rewards program" as a predictor of low ER utilization — I personally argued for this one in committee and it'll be awkward if your procedure kills it, but I'd rather find out now, so don't soften it. Third: "currently prescribed metformin" in a diabetes-complications model, which our data scientists flag as leakage because it sits downstream of the diagnosis but which has decades of clinical practice entrenching it — I want to see whether your procedure treats entrenchment as evidence of projectibility here or as a reason to be suspicious. Finish with what the procedure can't do: the cases where it'll pass a model that later blows up anyway, and why.
