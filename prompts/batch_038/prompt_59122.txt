For eleven years I have read the rationalist blogosphere, and one of its most durable bad habits is the deployment of impossibility theorems as conversation-enders. Arrow's theorem especially gets waved around meetup arguments as though it established that collective decision-making is arbitrary, when what it establishes is a conditional claim whose entire interest lives in its hypotheses. Next Thursday I am running a seventy-five-minute whiteboard session for my local group: about twenty people, mostly programmers and grad students, comfortable following a small table of numbers, no formal social choice theory, and several of them already carrying the slogan version of Arrow. The nominal topic is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. The actual topic is how to read an impossibility result.

I am not asking you to explain the theorem to me. I want a plan for explaining it, and I want you to be specific about sequencing, because my own attempts keep collapsing into either a definition dump or a set of anecdotes with no theorem attached.

Two regulars will shape the room, and the plan has to account for them. One runs a prediction market and will say, correctly, that the theorem concerns deterministic functions from profiles of strict ordinal preferences onto a set of at least three alternatives, and will conclude that it therefore says nothing about any mechanism anyone actually operates and is not worth his time. The other does election-method advocacy, will point out that our group already selects by approval voting, and will conclude that the theorem does not touch them either. I think the first is right about the scope and wrong about the consequence; I think the second is right about the hypotheses and wrong about what follows for approval voting, since falling outside the theorem's class of functions is not the same as being immune to strategic misreport. Tell me whether you agree, and if so, where in the sequence each intervention should be made so that it lands as a refinement rather than as a deflation.

Then work through the following:

1. The dependency structure. What must be in place before the theorem can be stated at all — a profile, a social choice function as opposed to a rule-of-thumb, onto versus merely non-constant, strategy-proofness defined at the level of an individual voter at a particular profile rather than as a global property — and why each of those has to precede rather than follow the statement. Flag any component that can be introduced only *after* the theorem, because introducing it early lets the audience discount the result before they have felt it. I suspect the distinction between "there exists a profile at which someone can gain by lying" and "lying actually happens and pays" is exactly such a component.

2. The carrying example. I want one table that does all the work: three alternatives, the fewest voters at which the phenomenon becomes visible, and a single display that shows both the cyclic structure and a profitable misreport by a named individual. Should it contain a Condorcet winner or not? Should the profitable misreport be available to one voter acting alone, or should it require a pair coordinating? What must the example conspicuously lack? And should I warm them up with the two-alternative case, where sincere voting is weakly dominant, before going to three — or does that give away the punchline?

3. The substitute for a proof. I do not intend to prove the theorem, but I do not want them to leave having merely been told it is true. What is the strongest available argument that a smart non-specialist will accept as showing the result *must* hold — and is there a smallest case that can be settled by exhaustion at the board rather than asserted? If so, is twenty minutes of board time a fair price for it?

4. The misconception map. Give me the ones they will arrive with and the ones the sequence itself will generate — the conflation with Arrow, "this shows all voting systems are equally bad," "randomize and you escape," "manipulation is computationally hard to find, so it is moot," "the theorem is about a formalism nobody uses" — and for each, the precise point at which to head it off and the one sentence that does it.

5. What I should refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud. Single-peaked domains and the median-voter escape, cardinal utility and its comparability problems, lotteries over outcomes, the complexity results: tell me which to cut, which to name without developing, and what to say when cutting them.

6. One closing question, asked of the whole room, whose answers will discriminate real understanding from slogan retention — along with what a good answer looks like and what the specific wrong answer is that would tell me the session failed.

Please give no readings, citations, or further-reading list; the plan should stand entirely on its own.
