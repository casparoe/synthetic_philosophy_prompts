You are a researcher working at the intersection of the philosophy of mathematics, probability theory, and the formal study of defeasible reasoning. Please treat what follows as a design review, not a survey: I want scores, tiers, and a probability, and I want you to be willing to give low ones. Think carefully, and do some brief brainstorming before you commit to ratings.

Background, so that the exercise is self-contained. Mathematicians routinely estimate a quantity by treating two arithmetically defined events as independent whenever no specific correlation has been identified — call this the presumption of independence. It is what licenses estimating the number of twin primes below x by multiplying the density of primes by a density corrected for the relevant small moduli, and what licenses estimating the number of integral solutions to a Diophantine equation as a product of local densities. Such estimates are defeasible: exhibiting an overlooked correlation — a congruence obstruction, a parity obstruction, a common factor — licenses revision and typically changes the constant. The formalization goal is a heuristic estimator: an efficient program that takes a growing list of arguments about a quantity and returns a subjective expected value, revising it as arguments arrive, which accepts intuitively valid applications of the presumption of independence while refusing spurious ones. An argument is spurious when each of its steps looks like a harmless independence default but the steps jointly force a conclusion that is false or absurd. Note the built-in squeeze: the trivially spurious-proof estimator ignores its arguments entirely and is therefore dogmatic, while the trivially non-dogmatic estimator accepts everything and is therefore gullible, and any requirement of stability against short counter-arguments costs computational tractability.

Evaluate these six candidate components:

- H1, local exhaustion: independence is presumed between X and Y only after every correlation attributable to congruence structure modulo any fixed integer has been computed exactly; only the residual is presumed independent.
- H2, truncated cumulants: the joint estimate is a cumulant expansion in which all cumulants above a fixed order are set to zero unless an argument supplies them, so low-complexity arguments can only move low-order cumulants.
- H3, presumption budget: each unverified independence default contributes a multiplicative uncertainty factor greater than one, and reported confidence is the product of those factors, so a spurious conclusion would require implausibly many defaults to line up.
- H4, short-counterargument stability: an estimate is accepted only if no argument of length at most L can move it by more than epsilon, with L raised as available computation grows.
- H5, restricted self-reference: arguments whose content depends on the estimator's own outputs, or on the list of arguments it has already ingested, are inadmissible, in order to block diagonal constructions.
- H6, model relativization: the estimator never asserts that the real objects are independent; it maintains an explicitly specified random model in which they are, plus a separately scored claim that model and reality agree on the statistics at issue, so the presumption becomes an empirical hypothesis about the model rather than a default rule.

For each of H1 through H6, assign two integers from 0 to 10. The first is coverage: how much successful existing practice it licenses. Test it against the prime-k-tuples constants, product-of-local-densities estimates for Diophantine equations, random-model predictions for prime gaps that are known to fail at fine scales and to need correction, and heuristic support for conjectures that were later refuted — for instance the conjecture that the Mertens function stays inside the square root of its argument. The second is resistance: how well it blocks spurious arguments. Test it against an argument list that accumulates many individually plausible defaults into a conclusion contradicting a known theorem, and against self-referential constructions in which an argument is about the estimator's own current estimate.

Then give me a tier list of the six — S, A, B, C — as components of a workable formalization, with one sentence on why each lands where it does. State which of them are really desiderata rather than mechanisms. Identify any pair you believe to be in direct tension, and say whether you think the tension is provable or merely suspected. Flag any candidate that you think no polynomial-time estimator can satisfy, and explain why.

Before the ratings, spend a short paragraph brainstorming what a spurious argument actually looks like in concrete terms: give at least one explicit example of a chain of independence defaults that a naive estimator would accept and that you would want refused, and say which component would catch it. Finish with a single number between 0 and 1 — your subjective probability that a heuristic estimator exists which is efficient, non-dogmatic, adequately covering, and adequately resistant all at once.
