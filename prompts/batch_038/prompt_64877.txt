I'm auditing a graduate seminar on knowledge and action this term, and there is a knot in the material on knowing how versus knowing that that I have not been able to untangle. The setup is familiar enough: Ryle's argument in The Concept of Mind is usually presented as showing that intellectualism — the thesis that knowing how to F is a species of knowing that something is the case — collapses into an infinite regress, because acting intelligently would first require contemplating a proposition about how to F, and that contemplating would itself have to be done intelligently, requiring a further such proposition, and so on. Our instructor's response was that this is a bad argument, or at any rate an argument against a straw man: what it actually refutes is only the claim that an agent must occurrently consider a proposition in order to act intelligently on it, and no intellectualist needs that claim, since the intellectualist can hold that a stored piece of propositional knowledge guides action without ever being brought before the mind. She added that the point cuts against the other side too — whatever stops the regress for the intellectualist, namely that the knowledge is simply there in the right relation to the action without being surveyed, seems to stop it equally for the anti-intellectualist, whose dispositions are likewise supposed to guide action without being exercised in any further act. I think I follow this, but I would like a careful explanation of what the regress does and does not establish: which premise is the vulnerable one, why the intellectualist can reject that premise without giving up intellectualism, and whether her symmetry claim really holds or is too quick.

The reason I am not satisfied is a second worry that surfaced afterwards and that we ran out of time to resolve. Suppose the intellectualist, having escaped the regress, still owes an account of what makes a piece of propositional knowledge the right kind of knowledge to constitute skill. Take a cycling commentator who can recite everything true about countersteering — lean the way you want to go, steer briefly the other way, the role of trail and of gyroscopic effects, the correction rate as a function of speed — and who, put on a bicycle in an empty parking lot, goes down within ten feet. Intuitively she knows all the propositions and still does not know how to ride. (This is not a hypothetical: my partner teaches adult swim lessons and meets roughly this person every season.) The intellectualist's standard move is to say that the propositions must be grasped under a practical rather than a theoretical mode of presentation, so that the commentator lacks not the content but the way of having it; or, alternatively, that knowing how requires employing the relevant proposition in action rather than merely assenting to it. Both moves strike me as doing all of the work and none of the explaining. If grasping a proposition practically just means grasping it in whatever way is required for knowing how to F, the account is circular; and if employing a proposition in intentional action is itself something done skillfully, then it is itself a know-how, and the regress has not been stopped but relocated one level down — this time to the notion of a mode of presentation or of employment, rather than to the notion of contemplating.

So here is what I want: an explanation of this conceptual issue, not a survey of the positions. Walk me through the structure of the disagreement — what the regress is meant to show, what it actually shows, and what it presupposes about how knowledge has to relate to action in order for the intellectualist to be in trouble. Then take up the sufficiency problem I sketched and lay out clearly what resources are available for closing the gap between having the right propositional knowledge and being able to perform: a substantive account of practical ways of thinking that is more than a label; a restriction on which propositions count, so that the way must be one the agent can actually execute; an appeal to the difference between possessing and exercising knowledge, perhaps together with cases of the temporarily incapacitated expert who keeps her know-how while losing the ability to display it; or a concession that the gap is primitive. For each option, tell me what it costs and whether it reintroduces the regress somewhere new. I am content for you to conclude that one option is the best of a bad lot, or that the whole dispute rests on an unstable notion of guidance — but I want the reasoning that gets there spelled out rather than a verdict announced, and I would rather you reason from the cases and the concepts than send me anywhere to look things up.
