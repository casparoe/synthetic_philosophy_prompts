I've been trying to raise my 8-year-old roughly along the lines of Locke's education book — the healthy body, virtuous character, no rote memorization thing — and it's become embarrassingly clear I can't actually state what I'm doing. So here's the track record, verdicts from the last year that I'd still stand by:

- I've never hit him and wouldnt. But when he kicked the dog last month I grabbed his arm hard enough to leave a mark and walked him to his room without saying a word, and I still don't think that crossed a line.
- I won't pay him in money or sweets for anything — I pulled him out of the school's "read 20 minutes, get a pizza" scheme. But I make a real performance of praising him in front of his grandparents and his cousins, and I'd say that's my main lever.
- We do no grammar drills and I've never made him memorize a thing. And yet I put him on 15 minutes of handwriting a day with a star chart on the fridge, and it worked, and I'd do it again.
- No scary stories, no hell talk, no "the goblin'll get you if you don't sleep." But when he got frightened by a film I'd let him watch, I didn't switch it off — I sat with him and we talked about it untill he went back to watching it himself.
- I've given reasons for every rule since he could talk. Except the stove and the road. Those are just "no, stop, now," he obeys without asking why, and I feel fine about that.
- For a while he whined and cried to get things; I ignored it completely and it stopped in about three weeks. But when his best friend moved away he cried and I held him for an hour and we talked about her the whole time.
- He quit piano after a year of hating it and I let him quit. He wanted to skip school one Tuesday because he "didn't feel like it" and I sent him anyway.
- We do the cold water, the thin shoes, the hard mattress. I'll admit I think that's doing character work, not immune-system work.
- I let him fail a spelling test rather than cram with him the night before. When his volcano project collapsed the night before the science fair, I stayed up till eleven helping him rebuild it.

Is there a principle behind all of that, or am I just improvising with a philosophical alibi? I want it stated precisely enough that I could run a new case through it — one clause per thing it's there to explain, so I can see which of my habits each clause is actually doing work for. If the only way to save all of them is to keep bolting on clauses until it's ad hoc, say so and give me the simpler version instead, plus which verdict it costs me.

Then tell me what it comes out as on four cases I haven't landed on: whether I can pay him $5 for every week of cello practice (cello was his idea, he's bored now); a phone at 10 on the grounds that every kid in his class has one; Santa — I've been telling him Santa is real, and I want to know whether that's consistent with anything else I do or whether it's a free-floating exception; and his grandmother offering him a candy bar if he finishes his dinner, which I allow at her house but not at mine.

And be honest about which of the verdicts above can't be saved. I suspect the arm-grabbing one and the film one are in trouble, but I might be wrong about which. Finally: if it turns out what I'm running isn't Locke at all — some modern respect-the-child's-autonomy view, or a straightforward habituation view, or something else — just say which, and tell me which of my practices would have to change to be genuinely Lockean rather than merely Locke-flavored.
