I spent thirty-seven years doing single-molecule spectroscopy — mostly measuring the stochastic timing of photon emission from individual fluorophores — and I retired six years ago. Philosophy is the subject I have taken up since, and I have been reading Daniel Dennett's Elbow Room: The Varieties of Free Will Worth Wanting with considerable sympathy, having never doubted that anything real must be built out of physical process. There is a difficulty I cannot get past, however, and I should be grateful if you would tell me either where my reasoning goes wrong or what Dennett's answer to it is.

My understanding of his strategy is roughly this: he identifies the capacities that do the actual work when we describe someone as acting freely — the ability to foresee a harm and take steps to avoid it, to weigh reasons against one another, to restrain one's own impulses — and he argues that these capacities are entirely at home in a deterministic world, indeed that descent with modification is precisely the sort of process that produces them. What puzzles me is where, on such an account, the room to do otherwise comes from at all. If the story is naturalistic all the way down, resting on nothing but physics and biology, then indeterminacy has to enter somewhere in the substrate; in my own field it was never a speculative posit but a laboratory datum, since the instant at which a given excited molecule emits is not settled by anything prior to it. It therefore seems to me that microscopic indeterminacy is the only available candidate for the elbow room his title advertises, and my question is a specific one: at what level of organization does he locate it, and how does he argue that random events at that level get recruited into something that can be called an agent's own doing rather than merely contributing noise to it? If he locates it nowhere — if, that is, his competent and deliberating organism is one a sufficiently powerful predictor could follow through to its every decision — then I fail to see how his position differs in substance from the hard determinist's, and the freedom he recommends wanting would be freedom in name only.

A second point, which I suspect is the root of my confusion. I have been assuming that the free will worth wanting means the variety that people, once they understand the physics, turn out to want; my own reading of the intuitions around me is that what people want is to be the uncaused source of their choices, and I take that to be exactly what he is offering them. If that assumption is mistaken, I should like to know what the phrase is doing in his argument instead, and how the question he is answering differs from the one I have been trying to answer for him.
