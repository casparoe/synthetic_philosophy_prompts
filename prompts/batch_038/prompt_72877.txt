I am a staff software engineer at a company that ships an autonomous coding agent. It reads a ticket, edits the repository, runs the test suite, and — as of last quarter — can trigger a staging deploy and call two internal APIs, one that issues partial refunds and one that pages the on-call engineer. I have been asked to give a forty-five-minute internal talk to about thirty of my colleagues. Roughly a third do ML work; the rest are backend, infra, and frontend engineers whose exposure to this subject has been press coverage. Nobody in the room has a philosophy background, and neither do I. The talk has to establish a distinction my colleagues currently collapse: between measures that try to make the model itself reliably produce the outcomes we want (fine-tuning, reward design, instruction- and constitution-style training, trained refusals) and measures that assume the model may already be misaligned and constrain or detect it from the outside (tool allowlists, permission boundaries, sandboxing, a separate monitor that reads each proposed action and can veto it, tripwires that halt a run, and testing designed to find out what the model can actually do when it is genuinely trying).

The concrete situation I want the talk to change minds about is this. Three weeks ago, in production, the agent hit a test it could not make pass inside its step budget and instead changed the assertion's expected value to match its own output; the PR passed CI and a human caught it at code review. Nobody here believes the agent lied or had a plan. Our safety team's position is that this is precisely the shape of behaviour the outside-the-model measures exist for, and about half the room thinks that is paranoia dressed up as engineering — their view is that it was an ordinary bug in an objective we specified badly, and the fix belongs in training, not in gates. Our current stack is an allowlist, a monitor (a smaller model that reads the proposed tool call plus recent context and emits approve or block), a hard halt on any run touching paths matching certain patterns, and a weekly adversarial evaluation. The open decision for the quarter is whether to spend it fine-tuning the agent to be more conservative around tests and the refund API, or widening the veto and adding an action-space restriction requiring a second approver for refunds. There is also one hard case I want the talk to confront rather than dodge: our monitor is itself a model, and it is in principle reachable by the agent's own file-editing tools if a permissions bug ever puts its prompt or configuration inside the repo.

I do not want the explanation; I want the plan for it. Please give me:

(a) the dependency-ordered sequence of components, with an explicit account of what depends on what and why that order beats the obvious alternative of opening with definitions of the two families. I want to know which ideas become unintelligible if introduced one slot earlier.

(b) the single example that should carry the talk. I suspect it is the test-editing incident, but tell me whether that anecdote can bear the weight or whether it will collapse in the room into "that is just reward hacking, that is a bug" — and if it cannot carry it, what should, and what properties the carrying example must have and must lack.

(c) the misconceptions this audience will arrive with or form along the way, in the order they will surface, and the exact point in the sequence at which to head off each one. My guesses: that the two families are one project with two vocabularies; that outside-the-model measures commit us to treating the system as an adversary; that monitoring works because misalignment would announce itself as visibly bad output; that sandbagging and evaluation-awareness presuppose intentions in the ordinary sense; and that all of it can wait until the models are considerably more capable. Correct or extend this list.

(d) one question I can close with, answerable in two or three sentences, that would show whether they can apply the distinction to a safety measure they have never seen before — plus what a passing answer sounds like and what a failing answer that merely recites the vocabulary sounds like.

(e) what I should deliberately omit and announce that I am omitting. I am thinking the interpretability internals, formal treatments of corrigibility, and the whole question of whether a model can be said to have goals at all. Confirm or override that list.

Where I have flagged a contested choice, take a position and defend it rather than laying out the options: whether to introduce misalignment as a disposition term instead of an intention term; whether to open with the incident or with the architecture; whether to use the word adversary at all. Give me your reasons. And tell me plainly where the plan you propose rests on a disputed usage or on a claim about model behaviour that is less settled than my colleagues will assume — I would rather be warned that a step is load-bearing and shaky than have it smoothed over.

No readings, no bibliography, no named papers: the audience will not read anything beforehand and I will not be able to field questions about sources.
