I am a retired experimental physicist. I spent thirty-five years running a measurement laboratory and have come to philosophy late, from that side. My reading group has just worked through Good's theorem — the 1967 result on the principle of total evidence, according to which an agent who expects to choose by maximizing expected utility and who updates by conditionalization can never be made worse off, ex ante, by free information. Half the group treats this as a deep norm of inquiry; the other half treats it as a trivial artifact of a decision rule. We have been talking past one another for months, and I should like the document we failed to produce.

You will need to write it as an expert in Bayesian decision theory and formal epistemology who has drafted adversarial-collaboration protocols before. Do not adjudicate between my two camps: the deliverable is a text both could put their names to.

The camps, under labels I coined myself and that you may keep or amend:

- **Constitutivists.** The theorem's assumptions — a fixed act set, consequences valued independently of what the agent believes, conditionalization, and choosing by maximizing expected utility at the moment of choice — are constitutive of having beliefs and preferences at all. So an agent who pays to remain ignorant is either facing a different decision problem than the one the theorem describes, or is not a Bayesian agent; either way the theorem stands as a norm.
- **Situationists.** Rationality is assessed in the situation an actual agent occupies, including her inability to model her own inference and her inability to bind her future self. So there are well-posed cases in which an entirely reasonable agent buys ignorance, and the theorem's idealizations exclude precisely the cases in which information policy matters.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. First, a working note of no more than a paragraph, in which you identify where each label risks caricaturing a real position, and adjust. Think carefully here; I would rather have two positions worth arguing with than two I invented.
2. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. This must include claims about the mathematics itself: I want the inequality chain written out, at the level of an average of maxima compared with a maximum of averages, together with agreement about which steps are uncontroversial. No citations, please — describe results rather than naming papers.
3. A disagreements table, with columns: topic; the constitutivist formulation, in wording a constitutivist would sign; the situationist formulation, in wording a situationist would sign; and whether the difference is substantive or verbal.
4. The cruxes. For each, give a test or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Run the following four cases through the cruxes, and for each say which assumption of the theorem it attacks and which line of the inequality chain it stresses:
 (a) a blinded analysis in which the analyst cannot model her own wishful thinking, so the collaboration withholds a calibration constant until the pipeline is frozen;
 (b) an interim look at trial data by a team that will stop at the first flattering result and has no way to commit otherwise;
 (c) a prognostic test whose disclosure changes the outcome it predicts, so that act and state are no longer independent;
 (d) a case in which the payoff depends on what the decision-maker believes and not only on what is so — this one I leave you to construct, from laboratory practice if you like.
5. A closing section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal. In particular: are the two camps asserting and denying the claim that the expected value of free information is non-negative in the same sense?

One further request, and it is the part I most want. I have been calling a case *Good-breaking* if the agent is offered free, truthful evidence, the act set and the description of the consequences are unchanged, and her ex ante expected utility is nevertheless strictly higher if she refuses the evidence. Do not simply adopt my definition. Have each camp say whether it accepts it, and if not, how it would amend it — then record each amendment as a disagreement rather than smuggling it into the agreed claims. If any of my four cases turns out not to be well-posed, say so plainly. I have no use for manufactured consensus.
