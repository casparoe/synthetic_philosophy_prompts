A friend of mine — sharp, widely read in the popular evolutionary psychology literature, and impatient with what he takes to be the field's reflexive critics — has been leaning on a single image in a long-running argument with me, and I would rather have it audited than merely contested.

His image: the mind is a Swiss Army knife. Instead of one general-purpose blade turned to every task, it carries many tools, each shaped for a recurrent problem in our ancestors' lives — a cheater detector, a mate-preference mechanism, a snake-and-spider alarm, a physical-object tracker — and those tools line up with folk-psychological categories no better than the awl and the corkscrew line up with a list of things a camper expects to need. From this he draws two conclusions. First, that the just-so-story charge demands something impossible: you can read a tool's function off its shape, just as a physiologist reads the kidney's function off its structure without ever having watched a kidney evolve. Second, that the inventory of tools is largely fixed at manufacture, so cross-cultural universality is the default expectation and heavy developmental plasticity would be the surprising finding requiring argument.

I have told him the image is load-bearing for both conclusions in a way he has not earned. Here is what I want from you.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly. For each element of the knife — the blades, their shapes, the handle, the factory, the catalog of tools, the user — what is it supposed to be in the mind, and what in selection history plays the role of the factory?

2. Enumerate the distinct inferences the analogy is being made to carry. I count at least four: that there are many function-specific mechanisms; that their functions are legible from their design; that the inventory is innate and fixed rather than assembled in development; that the inventory will cut against folk psychology. Add any I have missed, and flag any that are really one inference in two costumes.

3. For each inference, separate the disanalogies that actually bite from the merely decorative ones. My own suspects: a factory has a specification and foresight while selection has neither; blades are individually inspectable and mutually inert, whereas candidate psychological mechanisms are described in the vocabulary of the describer and interact constantly; an artifact has no ontogeny, so the analogy has no place to put development; tools get repurposed and worn smooth. Test these, add what I have overlooked, say whether the handle has any honest counterpart at all, and say in particular whether the physiology comparison — the kidney as filter — is an independent warrant or the same analogy wearing a lab coat.

4. Assess whether any of three alternative images carries the same loads better: a city whose old street grid constrains what later generations can build; a software base of legacy layers and repurposed routines; a kitchen remodeled four times by occupants with different habits. For each, state which loads it supports and which it quietly drops, and note whether it earns the conclusions or merely restates them more cautiously.

Please give the correspondence as a table and the inference-versus-disanalogy assessment as a second table or matrix, with prose only where a cell needs qualification. I want the audit, not a both-sides summary: some of his conclusions may survive intact, and if so I need to know which. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of HOLDS, HOLDS FOR A SUBSET OF LOADS, ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY, or FAILS, and then one sentence naming which inferences survive.
