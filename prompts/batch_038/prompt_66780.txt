I am teaching Arthur Miller's *Death of a Salesman* in a unit on the metaphysics of social kinds, and I want a reading of the play against the question of whether generations are real.

Please set the reading against a technical wrinkle from demography. In age–period–cohort analysis a cohort is arithmetically just a period minus an age, so any pattern attributed to a birth generation can be redescribed as a life-cycle stage plus a historical shock that struck everyone at once, and separating the three requires assumptions the data cannot supply. Willy Loman looks like a test case for that redescription: the Depression, the postwar economy embodied in the young boss who fires him while demonstrating his new wire recorder, and the closed frontier his brother Ben walked into all do explanatory work that his generation may be doing none of.

Against that, the play keeps pairing people who share a cohort, a city, and an economy and diverge completely — Willy and Charley, Biff and Happy, Biff and the Bernard he once mocked, who ends up arguing a case before the Supreme Court. That pattern reads less like an argument against generations than like Mannheim's point that a shared generational location never produces a single outlook, only a handful of self-conscious generational units inside it.

Which reading does the play support? Cite particular scenes and speeches, say plainly where the work resists both framings, and tell me whether Miller dramatizes something about the transmission of an ethos from father to son that the age–period–cohort apparatus cannot represent. Commit to a definite verdict on whether the play tells for or against the reality of generational kinds, give your reasons, and say what in the play would have to weigh the other way for you to change your mind.
