EXAM QUESTION (25 points; 700-word limit). Assigned reading: Michael Walzer, Just and Unjust Wars, chapters 2 and 3; Jeff McMahan, The Ethics of Killing in War.

(a) [5 points] Walzer defends the moral equality of combatants — the thesis that combatants on both sides of a war have an equal right to fight, whatever the justice of their own side's cause. State the two considerations on which his defense principally rests, and say how they are meant to work together.

(b) [8 points] Explain McMahan's account of the conditions under which a person becomes liable to defensive attack, and explain how he applies that account to combatants fighting for an unjust cause. In your explanation, indicate what role the combatant's own beliefs and intentions play.

(c) [7 points] Explain what McMahan thinks should and should not follow for the law of armed conflict from the conclusions in (b). Address in particular whether his view implies that a combatant who fights for an unjust cause may be tried and punished as a murderer after capture.

(d) [5 points] In your own view, does retaining the legal symmetry described in (c) drain the moral asymmetry described in (b) of practical significance? Give reasons.

STUDENT ANSWER

McMahan's rejection of the moral equality of combatants is best read as a two-stage argument, and each stage is aimed at something in Walzer.

The first stage is diagnostic. McMahan thinks the traditional theory treats war as a self-contained activity, rather like a duel or a sport, in which the ordinary moral rules about killing are switched off for both participants at once. On his reading, Walzer's defense of the equality is fundamentally legalist: the law of armed conflict gives combatants on either side the same privilege to fight and the same immunity from prosecution afterwards, and it is this legal package that makes killing in war something other than murder. Walzer does add that the ordinary soldier is not responsible for the decision to go to war — he is often conscripted, and in any case the decision was made over his head — but on McMahan's account this functions as a supporting consideration rather than as the ground of the equality, which rests on the fact that both sides' soldiers stand in the same legal relation to their own acts.

The second stage is constructive. McMahan says the duel analogy fails, and that the only model we actually have for the permissible use of lethal force is the one we already apply in individual self-defense. There, whether you may kill someone depends on whether that person is culpable for an unjust threat against you: the armed attacker is liable to be killed, the innocent bystander is not. Applied to war, this generates an immediate asymmetry. The combatant who fights for a state with a just cause is in the position of the person defending himself, while the combatant who fights for an aggressor state is in the position of the attacker, and the fact that both wear uniforms and observe the same rules of engagement changes nothing morally. McMahan is explicit that the asymmetry does not track the combatant's own beliefs: what matters is the objective justice of the side he fights for, so a conscript who is sincerely and reasonably convinced that his country has been wronged stands in the same position as an attacker who is mistaken about his own case.

Turning to part (c), McMahan is careful not to draw the inference his readers expect. He holds that the moral asymmetry should not be written straight into the law of armed conflict, and that international law should go on treating combatants on both sides alike, extending prisoner-of-war status and immunity from prosecution for acts that comply with the laws of war. So the answer to the question posed at the end of part (c) is no: on his view, combatants who fight for an unjust cause should not be tried as murderers after capture. His reasons are practical and epistemic rather than moral. In the middle of a conflict it is frequently impossible to establish which side is in the right, and any tribunal that tried would be the tribunal of the winner. And a rule that made the justice of one's cause a condition of immunity would remove the incentive for a losing army to surrender or to restrain its own conduct, since a soldier with nothing to gain by fighting on would have everything to lose by stopping.

For part (d), I think the concession in (c) costs McMahan more than he admits. If the legal rules are to stay exactly as they are, and if the law is what actually determines how wars are fought, then the moral asymmetry is left with nothing to do. His revisionism therefore ends up back where Walzer started, only described in the vocabulary of liability instead of privilege: the two of them agree about what soldiers may permissibly do, and disagree only about the account to be given of it.
