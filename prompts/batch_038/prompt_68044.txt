I am grading the final essays for an upper-level course on the ethics of markets, and there is one paper in the pile that I cannot place. A friend and I argued about the underlying issue until two in the morning — whether the corruption objection to markets is genuinely independent of the objection from unfair background conditions, or whether it collapses into that objection once you imagine the inequality removed — and neither of us budged. I mention it only to explain why I want a second pair of eyes. I have deliberately kept my own view out of everything below, and I would rather you not try to infer it.

The question set:

> Sandel distinguishes two ways of objecting to a market: an argument from fairness, which appeals to the background conditions under which exchanges take place, and an argument from corruption, which appeals to what market norms do to the goods they govern. Explain the distinction. Then consider a counterfactual: a regulated market in kidneys in which donors are never in financial straits, consent is fully informed and revocable up to the operation, and the price is set by a public agency rather than extracted by a broker. Under these idealized conditions, does each objection still have work to do? Say where Anderson's argument against commercial surrogacy and Walzer's account of blocked exchanges fall on the two sides of Sandel's distinction, and what follows for policy.

The student's answer:

> Sandel gives two ways of objecting to a market. The first is an argument from fairness. The objection is not to the exchange but to the conditions surrounding it: if the seller is poor, badly informed, or facing an option set in which selling is the only realistic way to meet a need, then what presents itself as a free agreement is closer to a forced one. This objection is empirical and contingent. It depends on how unequal the society is, and it would lose its grip in a society where everyone had a decent alternative.
>
> The second is an argument from corruption. Here the objection is to what market norms do to the good being traded. Some goods are governed by norms other than price — parental love, civic duty, solidarity — and putting them up for sale displaces those norms with commercial ones, degrading both the good and the relationships it structures. Paying children to read books is Sandel's stock example, alongside the older dispute over paid blood donation.
>
> Applied to kidneys as they are actually traded, the fairness objection does most of the work: brokers, donors recruited from slums in South Asia, inadequate aftercare, contracts signed by people who cannot read them.
>
> But the case described in the question removes precisely those features, and I think this is where the corruption argument is usually fudged. Corruption is not a property a good has in itself; it is what happens when market norms are imposed on people who have no way of refusing them. If the donor is not desperate, is fully informed, can withdraw, and receives a price set by a public agency rather than a broker, the kidney is being valued the way the donor herself values it, and nothing degrading is left over. The corruption argument therefore has no independent content: it is the fairness argument applied to a subtler description of the same transaction. Sandel's insistence that corruption survives the removal of inequality looks question-begging, since the cases he offers of corruption without obvious inequality all involve people whose options are constrained in some other respect.
>
> Anderson's argument against commercial surrogacy belongs on the fairness side, not the corruption side, however it is usually taught. Her objection is that women in financial straits are recruited into contracts requiring them to do something they would not otherwise do and to hand over a child for a fee. The wrong lies in the recruitment of desperate people, not in the fact that gestation is priced at all. Walzer's blocked exchanges are the genuinely corruption-side argument: votes, divine grace, and political office are the kinds of things that are morally degraded by being priced, so that the exchange corrupts the good no matter who the parties are. Debra Satz is a useful cross-check, since her account of noxious markets is largely fairness-side: a market is noxious when it harms people's capacity to function as equals and exhibits weak agency, extreme vulnerability, asymmetric information, or background inequality. But she allows that a market can stay noxious even when inequality is reduced, because the other features can persist on their own.
>
> If this is right, the debate about repugnant markets is really a chapter of distributive justice. We should stop asking whether kidneys may be sold and start asking how to reach a distribution in which nobody needs to sell one — and if we ever got there, the question would no longer have content.

Grades are on a 100-point scale: 90 and above for answers that are both accurate and independently argued, 80–89 for strong answers with minor defects, 70–79 for competent answers containing at least one substantive mistake, below 70 for answers that misrepresent the material in ways that damage the main claim.

For each load-bearing claim in the answer — including the characterizations of Sandel, Anderson, Walzer, and Satz — say whether it is right, wrong, or defensible but imprecise, and give your reason in each case.
