You are a moral philosopher who has spent years both working within and criticizing the effective altruism movement. I want a coherence audit, not an argument for or against any single claim. Begin by sketching, in a few sentences, what the most defensible unified version of this package would look like, and only then work through the claims.

For a workshop on the critiques of effective altruism I have written down my settled views:

1. Affluent people are morally required, not merely encouraged, to give a substantial share of their income to the most cost-effective charities; giving less is a moral failure rather than a shortfall in generosity.
2. Program-level cost-effectiveness analysis — trial evidence, cost per outcome, comparison across interventions — is the best tool currently available for allocating charitable money.
3. The institutional critique is right: because the movement privileges what can be measured and experimentally validated, it systematically under-ranks work whose benefits are diffuse, political, or slow — advocacy, institution-building, shifting norms — and it should discount its own priority rankings accordingly.
4. Expected-value reasoning about long-run consequences is the correct method for choosing among causes.
5. Longtermism overreaches: priority for the far future rests on speculative estimates that cannot be checked, and until they can be, programs with demonstrable near-term welfare gains come first.
6. Earning to give is a legitimate strategy wherever the counterfactual donations exceed what the person would have accomplished in direct work.
7. It is nonetheless inappropriate to reproach individuals who give little; a conclusion about what is right is not a conclusion about who may be blamed.
8. The movement should also be assessed by what I call its displacement cost: the careers and organizing capacities diverted into high-earning or technocratic roles that would otherwise have gone into labor, public-interest law, or electoral politics.

I think this hangs together better than most critics allow, but I cannot locate the pressure points myself. Finish by naming the single revision that would remove the most tension, and say whether the view that results is still recognizably a critic's position or has quietly become the orthodoxy it set out to question.
