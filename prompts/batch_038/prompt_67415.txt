So, background: I spent about 35 years as a comparative physiologist, mostly invertebrate nervous systems — some electrophysiology on crustaceans and cephalopods — and I retired four years ago. Since then I've fallen down a hole in philosophy, animal ethics mostly, and I've been going to a small reading group where we take turns presenting our own views and everyone else tries to break them. My turn is next month and I've writen down my position on brain size and moral standing. I'd like an honest audit before I read it out. Not a survey of the literature, I've read plenty of that — just: does this set hang together?

Here's the list.

1. What gives a creature moral standing is that there's something it's like to be it, and in particular that it can feel pain and pleasure — valenced experience. Intelligence as such confers nothing. A very clever but insentient system, robot or alien, would have no standing at all.

2. Sentience isn't a switch. It comes in degrees, and the degrees matter: at equal intensity and duration, the suffering of a more sentient being counts for more than that of a less sentient one. By "more sentient" I mean something like the resolution and integration of experience — how many things there are that can matter to it, and how much of it is bound into one perspective.

3. The best natural proxy we have for that is neural complexity, and in practice I mean something like absolute neuron number combined with how much genuine central integration there is — not brain-to-body ratio. I'm very sceptical of the encephalization quotient tradition, because it fits an expected scaling curve mostly to mammals and then scores everything else as a deviation from that curve. It's a rank ordering wearing a lab coat.

4. But I also don't think you can read a mind straight off a brain. Convergent behavioural evidence beats anatomy. The cephalopod lineage got to flexible, curious, individually-recognisable problem solving with a completely different architecture and the bulk of its neurons sitting out in the arms. So where behaviour and neuron counts disagree, I go with behaviour.

5. No bright lines. The mental gap between us and other animals is a gap of degree, and I don't believe there is any cognitive property humans have that no other animal has any share in.

6. Verdicts I'm committed to: an octopus has serious moral standing — I wouldn't eat one, and I think a bare tank is a real wrong. A honeybee, I doubt it. Its nervous system does remarkable things with under a million neurons and that isn't nothing, but I can't convince myself there is anything it's like to be a bee, and I would not put a bee's death on the ledger anywhere near a fish's.

7. Whether killing is a harm depends on temporal structure and not only on the moment. A being that remembers, anticipates, and has preferences about its own future loses more by dying than one that doesn't, even if the two are equally sentient in the instant. So I'm comfortable saying death is a much bigger harm to a dog than to a trout.

8. Insects: if they are sentient the numbers are astronomical and that swamps everything else on the list. I'm genuinely agnostic about insect sentience, I think agnosticism is the right posture, and I don't think we should let low-probability-times-huge-number reasoning drive policy, becuase that's how you end up spending a fortune preventing nothing.

9. I'm a naturalist about all of this. There aren't extra non-natural moral properties floating around; whatever makes for standing has to be some natural fact about nervous systems, behaviour, and function. That's the whole reason I keep reaching for measures in the first place.

10. I object to any scale rigged so that humans come out on top by construction — but I accept that on the properties listed in (7) we do in fact come out on top. That's not the objection.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Please order the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what has to give and what it costs to give it up. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. And where a pair looks like a tension to you but isn't, say so explicitly and say why — I can't yet tell the difference between a real crack and a surface one, which is exactly what I'm worried about presenting. Bluntness welcome; I'd much rather find this out at my own kitchen table than in front of eight people with red pens.
