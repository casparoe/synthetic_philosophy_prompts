For thirty-seven years I worked as a physical chemist, largely on reaction kinetics in catalytic systems, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy in a more or less disciplined way — ethics first, then personal identity, which led me rather uncomfortably into the literature on advance directives. My wife died of Alzheimer's disease four years ago, and shortly afterward I executed a directive of my own refusing antibiotics and artificial nutrition should I reach a comparably advanced stage. My daughter, who is an intensive-care physician, signed as a witness but told me plainly that she doubts she would honor it. That disagreement is what sent me looking for the underlying conceptual problem, and I have found what I can get at my local library surprisingly hard to parse: everyone seems to agree there is a difficulty here, but they do not agree about what kind of difficulty it is.

Here is the framing I have arrived at on my own, which I suspect is naive, and I should like you to tell me where and why it fails. I think of my directive as a standing order: an instruction issued once, by a competent principal, to be executed later by an agent who cannot telephone the principal for clarification. The trouble, as I see it, is that in the ordinary case the principal still exists at the moment of execution — she may have changed her mind, but there is somebody whose mind has changed. In the dementia case my daughter's objection seems to be that at the moment of execution there is no longer anybody standing in the right relation to the issuer for the instruction to be his instruction: the contented, confused old man in the bed who enjoys strawberries and familiar music is not, she says, the person who signed the paper, so honoring the paper harms somebody who never consented to anything. My question is whether the difficulty about authority here is really one problem or three entangled ones — a problem about identity (is the later subject the same person as the issuer?), a problem about authority (can a past self bind a present one?), and a problem about welfare (can a subject be harmed by the frustration of an interest he no longer has and can no longer conceive of?) — and, if they are separable in principle, which of the three is doing the actual work when a directive is overridden.

Second, and more specifically: I understand Dworkin distinguished interests in how one's life goes as a whole from interests in the immediate quality of one's experience, and argued that the former can retain authority after the capacity to form them has lapsed; and I understand Dresser replied, roughly, that this treats the demented patient as a mere custodian of somebody else's biography while giving no weight to what the patient actually wants now. Please explain what conceptual work that distinction is doing — in particular, how an interest can supposedly be frustrated without the subject ever registering the frustration, whether that is coherent on its own terms, and whether it covertly depends on some claim about the narrative unity of a life. Then take up the objection that has stuck with me: if the answer to my daughter is that the demented patient is relevantly a different person, that answer appears to prove far too much, since it would equally undermine saving for a retirement one will not be around to enjoy, keeping promises made to the dead, and punishing a man who has genuinely reformed. Does appealing to degrees of psychological connectedness — more of it between me and the retired man I shall be in a decade, less between me and the demented man — rescue the situation, or does it merely convert a yes-or-no question into a question about weights that nobody has a principled way of setting? I would much rather have your own reasoned exposition than a list of references, and please write in American English.
