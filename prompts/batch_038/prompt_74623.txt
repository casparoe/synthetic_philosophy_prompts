I'm working on a piece about other minds and I want to run a real case through the zombie argument instead of another thought experiment, because I think the thought experiments have gone stale. I'll give you the facts up front so you don't have to guess at them.

In 2006 a Cambridge team put a young woman who'd been diagnosed vegetative after a car accident into an fMRI scanner and asked her to imagine playing tennis, and, on a separate instruction, to imagine walking through the rooms of her house. Her activation in the motor-planning area and in the navigation-related regions came out matching what healthy volunteers produce on the same two tasks, and it did so repeatably, following the instruction rather than occurring at random. Then in 2010 the same group used the same trick to let a man who'd been diagnosed vegetative for more than five years answer yes-and-no questions — tennis for yes, walking through his house for no — with usable accuracy, entirely through brain activation, with no muscle output of any kind. Since then there's a growing literature on the pattern, including recent multicenter work that labels it cognitive motor dissociation: patients with no command-following behavior whatsoever who show task-appropriate activation anyway.

Now analyze that episode through Chalmers's conceivability argument. Not a summary of the argument — I know it. I want the mapping done carefully and then tested in both directions.

Some of what I'm after:

- Where is the zombie in this story, exactly? The standard zombie is globally functionally identical to a conscious being, and that's the whole point of the construction. These patients are almost the reverse: at the behavioral level they are indistinguishable from something with no experience at all, and the machine is what breaks the tie. Call that a diagnostic zombie — something that has experience but is, output-wise, indistinguishable from something that doesn't. Does the conceivability argument need diagnostic zombies to be possible, or does it quietly assume the two only come apart in the other direction? And what does it mean that this one isn't merely conceivable but apparently actual?

- Walk me through the bottleneck versus the gap. My working distinction: the bottleneck is the output channel between an inner state and observable behavior; the gap is the explanatory distance between physical/functional facts and phenomenal facts. The scanner seems to widen the bottleneck without touching the gap. Is that right, or does removing the bottleneck do something to the gap that I'm not seeing? Be blunt if you think the distinction doesn't hold up.

- The 2010 case is the interesting one, because there the patient produced something that looks like a report about his own inner life, with no behavior in the ordinary sense. Take a zombie duplicate of that man in the scanner. It gives the same answers, on the same tasks, with the same activation. So what is the evidential weight of the report, and does this episode hand Chalmers the paradox of phenomenal judgment on a plate or does it blunt it?

- Which side, if either, does the episode actually help? I want you to say whether the neural-correlate move plus inference to the best explanation gains anything here, or whether the dualist can concede every datum and lose nothing.

- And then the honest part: where is the theory simply silent. It has nothing to say about detection, nothing about degrees, nothing about which patient is which — and a zombie's scan would look identical to this one. Say what the episode can't be made to carry, and refuse to make the argument speak where it doesn't.

Finish with your verdict on whether this is a confirmation of the conceivability argument, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built to handle — and if it's the third, say what the argument would have to add to handle it.
