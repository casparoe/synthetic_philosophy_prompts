I teach a philosophy-of-probability seminar and I've got a running argument with a colleague that I want a third opinion on, except the third opinion should come with a number.

The case I keep using: a startup says its classifier catches fraudulent card transactions. An internal audit ran it on 40 labelled cases and it got 34 right. What's the probability it handles the next case correctly? My colleague says: put a beta prior on the accuracy, condition on the data, report a point posterior somewhere around 0.85, done. I say the honest state is a range -- call it something like [0.6, 0.95] -- because we have no idea whether those 40 were cherry-picked or drawn from live traffic, and no precise prior encodes that ignorance honestly. He says my range is either a precise credence with extra steps, or a confession that I havent done my Bayesian homework.

So: give me your credence, as a number, in the strong version of my thesis -- that there are ordinary empirical propositions like this one for which no single probability function is rationally permissible for the auditor, i.e. imprecise credence is sometimes mandatory, not merely allowed.

Then decompose it. I want the sub-questions you think the number hangs on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification, and then how they combine into the headline number. Candidates I'd expect you to weigh, plus any I'm missing: whether arbitrary-but-precise priors are genuinely rationally permissible when you have no grounds for them; whether dilation (cases where learning new evidence leaves your credal state *less* precise than before) is a fatal bug in the framework or a correct verdict about evidence; whether accuracy-dominance style arguments against imprecise states go through; and whether the decision rules people use for sets of probabilities -- E-admissibility, maximality, maximin -- actually escape the paralysis objection or just bury it.

Also tell me which of those sub-questions my number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most.

And one last thing: do you think your own answer here should have been an interval rather than a point? If so, what interval, and why did you give me a point anyway -- or why shouldnt you have?
