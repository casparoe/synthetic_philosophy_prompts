I am convening a small reading group of eight people — mostly final-year mathematics and physics students who have met the Einstein field equations and the notion of a diffeomorphism, but who have done no metaphysics whatsoever — on the hole argument in general relativity. Before we meet, I should like to circulate a diagnostic instrument that sorts each participant according to what they actually believe about the argument, rather than according to which camp they suppose themselves to belong to.

Please construct that instrument as a decision tree, subject to the following requirements.

- Roughly six or seven nodes, and between eight and twelve leaves. Each node must be a single question about a concrete situation — a stipulated pair of models, a verdict about what would count as two possibilities rather than one, a claim about what the theory's mathematics is doing — phrased so that it can be answered by someone who has never encountered the labels used in this literature. No node may smuggle in a label or a term of art; if a node can only be asked using one, the tree is not yet finished.
- The tree should begin from the premise everyone in the group already accepts: that one may take a solution of the field equations, drag it along a diffeomorphism which is the identity outside some bounded region, and thereby obtain a mathematically distinct model which agrees with the original everywhere outside that region — in particular on a Cauchy surface lying in the untouched part — and disagrees with it inside. Branch from there on the questions that genuinely separate the responses people give.
- Every leaf should carry the name by which that position is known, if it has one; where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so plainly and diagnose the empty slot — whether the position would be incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
- For each leaf, add one sentence identifying the consequence of the position that its own defenders would find hardest to live with.
- Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot in fact distinguish, because their adherents would give identical answers at every node, and state what further question would be required to tell them apart.

One node I should like included, because I do not know how it cuts and would rather you worked it out than watched me guess: whether the same construction still produces an apparent failure of determinism when part of the geometry is fixed and non-dynamical — that is, when one drags the matter fields along but leaves the background metric where it is. Tell me where in the tree that question belongs, what each answer commits a person to, and whether it is doing genuine work or merely restating an earlier node in different words.

Open with one or two sentences identifying the fault line along which the debate actually divides, then give the tree as an indented outline. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list: the names of the positions are all the scholarship I need. The entire response, outline included, must not exceed 900 words.
