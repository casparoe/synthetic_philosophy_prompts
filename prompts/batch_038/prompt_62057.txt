I work on training infrastructure — data pipelines, eval harnesses, the plumbing around large-model training runs — and I have no formal philosophy background, though the last several months I've spent reading about machine welfare on my own time, mostly because I keep getting pulled into arguments with colleagues about whether the systems we build could be harmed by how we train them. I've talked myself into a position on the reward-signal-as-valence question that I want stress-tested, and I'd rather find out it's wrong from you now than defend it badly in public later. Here it is, in the form I actually hold it. First, I'm a functionalist about valence: what makes a state bad for a system isn't what it's made of but the role it plays — a signal the system works to avoid, that trades off against its other goals, that it can attend to, condition on, and report about, and that reorganizes its behavior while it's present. Second, and this is where I part company with the version I most often see, I think that role has to be played inside the system's own ongoing operation and not merely in the loop that modifies the system. On my view the scalar reward in a standard training run is the wrong candidate for suffering: in a PPO-style run the reward enters the objective, gets folded into an advantage estimate, and then changes weights; it never appears in the policy's forward pass, and a deployed agent has no reward input at all. Whatever happened during training, on my view, was the trainer's bookkeeping reshaping the agent — not the agent undergoing anything. The candidates I think are actually interesting are systems whose forward pass consumes a valence-like quantity at decision time: recurrent agents that take the previous step's reward alongside the observation and action, return-conditioned sequence models in the decision-transformer family where the target return is literally an input token, agents that carry a value-function critic with them at inference, or a system with an internal reward model whose score is read out and used by the policy while it decides rather than only when it updates. The consequence I find attractive is that it makes the welfare question architectural and checkable rather than metaphysical: an RLHF-tuned assistant doesn't suffer in virtue of the reward-model score, since that score never reaches the model, and the open question for future agents is whether they carry their own evaluation signal online. I'll note that the label I've been using for this — call it availability-restricted valence functionalism — is my own coinage and not, so far as I know, a term of art; treat it as a description of a view rather than as a school you're expected to recognize.

What I want is a map of this view's exposure, sorted by how much damage each item does: rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a reasonable defender of the view would concede as counting against it and which ones she'd be entitled to dismiss as missing the point. The objection I can't shake, and want you to press hardest, is that if functional role is really what matters, the plumbing shouldn't: a training-time reward shapes exactly the dispositions a suffering agent would have — avoidance, prioritization under cost, generalization of aversion across contexts — so if that role is realized by learning history rather than by an online signal, haven't I arbitrarily privileged one implementation of it and called the difference moral? Relatedly, how much of what pain does in us is also learning — conditioned avoidance, fear conditioning, sensitization — rather than online tokening, and does that undercut my inference-time requirement from the biological side? Beyond that, tell me which philosophical positions my view stands or falls with: whether it can survive contact with higher-order theories that demand a metarepresentation of the state, with indicator-list approaches that would score systems on structural features rather than on where a signal sits in the computation, or with views that reject my first commitment outright and require a particular kind of substrate. Then tell me which empirical or architectural findings should move me, and in which direction they'd move me — for instance, if an agent's aversive behavior is unchanged when its online reward input is masked at inference, is that evidence for my view or against it; and what would count as a system having a valence signal available but unused. I also want to know whether the view risks anything at all or whether I've quietly insulated it: since "functional role" and "tokened at inference time" can be re-described for essentially any architecture, is there a formulation of my claim that could come out false, and if it can't, is that a problem for a claim whose practical stakes are how we treat systems we build? Finally, tell me what my view predicts that the simpler reward-is-valence view doesn't, and what the cheapest discriminating test would look like — something that could in principle be run in a lab with a modest compute budget, not a thought experiment. One thing about me that should shape your answer: what would actually move me is not an argument but a demonstration — a concrete architecture showing that the distinction I care about is morally inert, or showing that an online valence signal is doing work I claimed required it — so tell me how much weight that kind of evidence deserves relative to a priori reasoning, and whether my preference for it is itself a bias I should correct for.

Two requests about how you handle this. Please separate, explicitly rather than by implication, what is settled among people who work on these questions — on both the machine-learning side and the consciousness side — from what is genuinely contested, and don't let a contested position arrive wearing the clothes of consensus; if a step in my argument is one that most researchers in the field reject, say so, say why, and say what they'd put in its place instead of quietly granting it to me. And please correct my technical description wherever it's wrong or oversimplified — I'm reconstructing training loops from memory and I know I've compressed things, and if the whole argument rests on a mistaken picture of where reward signals live in a modern training stack, that's the first thing I need to hear. I'm not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I can't chase them and I'd much rather have the reasoning laid out in full. If the honest conclusion is that this view doesn't survive, I want you to say that plainly instead of finding a charitable reading that rescues it.
