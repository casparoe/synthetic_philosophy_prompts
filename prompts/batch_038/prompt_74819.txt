I'm writing a novel where a few dead philosophers get to weigh in on the present, and C.S. Peirce is my epistemology ghost. The chapter I'm stuck on puts him in front of a big online conspiracy community — a couple hundred thousand people holding one elaborate story, treating any counter-evidence as further proof of the plot, mostly only ever talking to each other but very much aware that the outside world disagrees. Right now I have him shrug and call it the method of authority wearing a new costume, then move on. But that bothers me, because as far as I can tell he thought tenacity and authority were methods that had to come apart: the tenacious believer gets rattled the moment he notices other people think differently, and authority can't police every subject forever. Here the noticing is happening around the clock and nothing comes apart — if anything the story gets sturdier.

So what would he actually make of it? Is there something in his own toolbox that explains a group like this — the social impulse, his insistence that doubt has to be genuinely felt rather than manufactured on paper, truth as whatever the community of investigators would settle on in the long run — or does this break a prediction he was pretty confident about? Don't let me off with a both-sides answer: pick the reading you think is right, argue for it, and tell me whether my shrugging line survives. And would his verdict differ from what William James would say about the same crowd, or do the two of them land in the same place here? I need to know which of them gets the better scene.
