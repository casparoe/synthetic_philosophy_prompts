I'm a fourth-year PhD student in epistemology, and I'm running a reading group on peer disagreement whose members have all read enough of the literature that asking them which camp they belong to yields nothing but labels. I want to build a diagnostic tree that sorts them by what they say about cases instead. Please design it to the following specification.

The tree should have six to eight branch nodes, each a concrete case or a plain-language claim about a case, phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing which position the answer feeds into. At least four nodes must concern features of the disagreement rather than the respondent's self-conception: whether the two parties have seen the same evidence; whether the respondent has grounds for assessing the other's reliability that do not themselves depend on the disputed question; whether learning that one was reasoning badly at the time — fatigued, distracted, or told the task was harder than it looked — gives one a reason to change one's mind even when it supplies no new first-order reason bearing on the case; and whether a given body of evidence ever permits more than one reasonable level of confidence in a proposition. At least two nodes must move up a level, to disagreements about how disagreement should be handled, and the tree must say what it outputs there — whether it settles on a stable leaf or terminates in something skeptical.

Please supply the following sections, in this order:

- **Nodes** — the questions in order, each with a one-line note on the distinction it tracks and on what each available answer commits the respondent to. Roughly 400 words.
- **The space** — the combinations of answers the nodes make available, set out compactly, with any combination that cannot coherently be filled in marked as constrained rather than merely unfilled. Roughly 200 words.
- **Leaves** — the named positions (total and partial conciliationism, the equal-weight view, right-reasons steadfastness, the total-evidence view, permissivism, and anything else you think belongs on the list that I have omitted), each located at the relevant cells and given one sentence on the commitment it is least able to give up. Roughly 350 words.
- **Empty cells** — answer combinations no one occupies, each diagnosed as incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or simply unexplored. Roughly 200 words.
- **Unseparable pairs** — which leaves the tree cannot in fact distinguish, and why the questions available to it fail to do so. Roughly 150 words.

Finally, run the tree through three cases: two graders of equal ability who disagree about whether an argument in a student essay is valid; two researchers who disagree about a drug's efficacy, one of whom has read a trial report the other has not; and the case my group is actually arguing about, in which I and an equally informed colleague disagree about whether we should each halve our confidence in our own account of disagreement. If the tree assigns the first and third cases to the same leaf, report that as a limitation rather than adjusting the nodes to avoid it.
