I spent thirty-five years calibrating instruments at a national measurement laboratory, and only since retiring have I taken up the mathematics of algorithmic fairness — where I learned that "calibration" does not mean what I was trained to think it means, and that a score can be perfectly calibrated and still hand out its errors unequally. That discovery has become, for me, a case study in how far a measurement can be technically impeccable and still answer a question nobody asked.

Our county health department and the community coalition that has spent three years demanding changes to the department's maternal-risk triage model are holding a joint two-day retreat. I have been asked to give the opening remarks, about six minutes. The model's architect, an epidemiologist, is being honored at the same session: she is retiring, and much of the coalition regards her life's work as harmful. Half the room are statisticians and clinicians who know the impossibility result linking calibration to equal false-positive and false-negative rates cold; the other half are midwives, nurses, and mothers who have watched the model send the wrong people home.

Please write those remarks. They must do three things I cannot manage. First, they must state the impossibility result correctly and in language a nurse can follow — one small worked example with actual numbers is permitted, and probably required — without letting anyone in the room conclude either that the mathematicians have settled the question or that they have ducked it. Second, they must honor this woman without inviting her to apologize and without inviting the coalition to soften anything it has said. Third, they must locate the real decision — which errors we choose to tolerate, and who gets to make that choice — where it genuinely sits, given that the theorem tells us a tradeoff exists but not where to stand on it.

Write in American English. Do not include citations, references, or a reading list; if you are unsure of a specific, leave it out rather than guess.
