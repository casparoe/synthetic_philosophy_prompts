I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the better part of a year I have been reporting a piece on the argument inside AI laboratories over how much autonomy to hand a system. In the course of that reporting I have heard the same six words repeated often enough that they now function less like a position than like a password: we build tools, not agents. The instance that started me worrying about the phrase came from a researcher at one of the larger labs. I had asked him a fairly specific question — whether his company's model, when handed an open-ended request such as a literature review, decides on its own to write and run code, to search, to spawn further subtasks, and effectively to issue instructions to itself — and he waved it away by saying that the company builds tools, not agents, that a tool does not want anything, and that the worry only makes sense if you have already confused the product with a person. Later in the same week, a former colleague of his, now an independent critic, told me the phrase is a distinction without a difference: once the thing plans, it is an agent no matter what the interface looks like or who presses the button. And a third interviewee, who works on certification standards, told me the opposite of the critic — that the distinction is the only one that matters, because a tool can be inventoried, audited, and switched off, whereas an agent cannot, and regulators can only attach obligations to the first sort. I am trying to work out which of these three is actually disagreeing with which, and whether any of them is entitled to the confidence with which they spoke.

So I would like you to take the slogan apart. Please lay out, as a menu rather than as a single favored interpretation, the distinct precise claims that "we build tools, not agents" could be making, and keep them separate even when they tend to travel together: a claim about how the system is initiated and what happens when nobody is asking it anything; a claim about persistence, memory, and whether anything carries over between invocations; a claim about its objective, that is, whether there is any standing quantity it is optimizing across long horizons or only a target defined per-request; a claim about the legitimacy of intentional language, that ascribing goals to such a system is a useful shorthand rather than a report of what is there; a claim about instrumental subgoals, that whatever it is optimizing it cannot generate the derivative aims — resource acquisition, self-preservation, goal stability — that people fear; a claim about deployment, that a human authorizes every consequential action; a claim about governance, that tools are the kind of thing one can register and inspect; and a claim about us, that users do not extend the moral and psychological credit of personhood to something framed as an instrument. For each reading, tell me plainly whether it is a platitude that nobody denies, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and where you can, what sort of evidence would settle it — an architectural fact, a behavioral test, a philosophical argument. Then do the two things I actually need the analysis for: say which reading, or which conjunction of readings, would have answered the question I put to the researcher, and whether any of them does; and say which pairs among my three sources are genuinely contradicting one another and which are talking past each other because they mean different claims by the same sentence. Finally, address the objection that agency is graded rather than binary — that there is a spectrum from a lookup table to something that sets its own agenda — and tell me whether the slogan survives that, or whether it collapses into a claim about thresholds that its users have not stated.

A note on how I would like this delivered. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a considered letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bolded terms, and no tables; give each reading its own paragraph and take them in whatever order makes the argument easiest to follow. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not feel obliged to name the people who have held any of these positions — I want the reasoning, not the sourcing, and I would rather you say that a reading is confused than inflate it charitably to make it look respectable. If two of the readings I listed turn out to be the same claim wearing different clothes, say so and merge them; if there is an important reading I have missed, add it. Length is not a concern beyond that it should be readable in one sitting by someone with no technical background and no patience for vagueness.
