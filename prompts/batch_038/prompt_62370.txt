I'm a 4th-year PhD student in HPS working on the second chapter of my dissertation, which is an attempt to read Poincaré's account of mathematical invention — the Science and Method material, where the unconscious churns out enormous numbers of combinations and only the harmonious ones get through to consciousness thanks to an aesthetic sensibility he thinks ordinary people lack, plus the whole omnibus-at-Coutances anecdote — as a proto-theory of search rather than as a piece of introspective psychology. My gloss, roughly: for Poincaré the aesthetic filter isn't a report on how genius feels from the inside, it's an implicit claim about which search spaces are navigable at all. Call it the aesthetic-gradient view. A problem is humanly tractable to the extent that perceived beauty correlates with proximity to a solution, and when that correlation drops to zero you get something that behaves exactly like intractability. I'm not convinced it's defensible and I half suspect I'm just draping 1990s complexity theory over a dead Frenchman, so I want a reality check coming from his direction rather than mine.

So: suppose Poincaré gets dropped into a present-day theory group, is given a working command of the formalism, and is asked four things. For each one I want (i) what he'd actually conclude, and (ii) an honest split between what's grounded in commitments he demonstrably held and what's probably me projecting.

1. The verification/search asymmetry. NP is basically the class of problems where checking a proposed solution is cheap and finding one may not be. That looks like Poincaré's distinction between invention and verification getting turned into a definition. Would he read NP-completeness as vindicating his anti-logicist line against Russell and Couturat — mechanical checkability of a proof says nothing about the findability of one, and now there's a theorem-shaped reason to think so — or would he wave it off as bookkeeping about step counts, since the interesting question for him was never quantitative?

2. Relativization. There are oracles relative to which P equals NP and oracles relative to which it doesn't, so the standard toolkit provably can't settle the question. Given his conventionalism about geometry — axioms as free choices, and the question whether Euclidean geometry is true simply malformed — would he say the same thing here? Is the polynomial-time boundary a convention or a discovery about the world, on his view? Note that he also thought mathematics had to stay tethered to intuition and to something like usefulness, so please don't just hand me the cheap conventionalist reading because it's the easy one.

3. Natural proofs. Roughly: if one-way functions exist, then no proof of circuit lower bounds can be natural in the technical sense — constructive, applying to a large set of functions, and useful. So the broad, structural, conceptually elegant style of argument that mathematicians actually favor is provably not going to settle this particular question. That seems like a direct hit on the aesthetic sieve: not beauty sometimes fails, but beauty is ruled out in advance as a route to this truth. Does Poincaré concede that, or does he have a move available — something like, a truth that aesthetic intuition can't reach isn't a mathematical truth worth having? Separate but related: random 3-SAT instances get hardest right around the satisfiability threshold, meaning the hardest problems are the ugliest ones, with no structure to grab onto. Is that a counterexample to my aesthetic-gradient reading or the strongest possible confirmation of it?

4. Learned evaluation functions. Modern game-playing systems pair brute-force tree search with a trained network that scores positions, which is functionally an aesthetic sieve built out of statistics. Would he take that as demystifying the sensibility he reserved for mathematicians, or would he insist the sensibility is doing no work there anyway, since the network was itself produced by enormous undirected computation — exactly the sterile mechanical route he thought could never generate mathematics?

Two ground rules. Don't put any words in his mouth as quotations; I don't want invented French or paraphrase dressed up as citation. And don't give me the he-would-have-been-fascinated-and-ahead-of-his-time answer — if the honest response to one of these four is that his framework just lacks the resources to say anything interesting, say so and tell me which one it is. End with a short list of the specific spots where you think my aesthetic-gradient gloss over-reads him, since that's the part I actually need for the chapter.
