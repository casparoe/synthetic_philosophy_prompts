I am revising a draft essay on deplatforming for a general-audience venue and would be grateful for comments. I am less interested in whether you share its conclusions than in whether its central distinction survives scrutiny, so I have set out specific questions after the draft. Please be direct; agreement is not useful to me here.

---

**Against the Speech Frame**

Nearly every argument about deplatforming is conducted in one of two registers. The first holds that deplatforming is censorship wearing a corporate uniform: a handful of firms now perform the coordinating function that public squares, printing presses, and broadcast licenses once performed, so the constitutional limits we accept on official suppression are being evaded rather than respected when the same work is done by a private terms-of-service decision. The second holds that these are private firms, that they may refuse service to anyone for any reason, and that anyone who thinks otherwise has never had to moderate a comments section at scale. The two positions are usually presented as opposed. They are not. They share a premise — that what deplatforming destroys is a channel — and disagree only about whether the loss of a channel is a harm that anyone else must remedy.

I want to deny the shared premise. What is destroyed, in the cases that matter, is not a channel but a record. Call the set of systems through which a person becomes findable, attributable, and citable to others the legibility infrastructure of a society. Historically it was diffuse and unowned: city directories, library catalogs, professional registers, mastheads, the durable local knowledge of who you are and what you have said. Much of it is now concentrated in a small number of searchable, indexable commercial platforms, and an addressable account on one of them is often the load-bearing element of a person's public existence.

This suggests a distinction that the current debate lacks. Call the first term reach and the second standing. Reach is the size of the audience a person actually commands; it varies enormously between accounts and is a function of luck, craft, and algorithmic favor. Standing is the bare fact of being locatable at all: that your profile resolves when someone searches your name, that your own words can be linked and read in context, that a journalist, an employer, or a future collaborator can confirm you exist and check a claim about you against what you actually wrote. Reach is scalar and largely earned. Standing is closer to binary and is conferred simply by a platform's willingness to host an addressable identity.

Once the two are separated, several familiar arguments lose their grip. Suspending an account with forty followers may cost nothing in reach and everything in standing: the person loses no audience, but also loses the ability to be verified, and any third party who now searches their name finds only what others have said about them. Conversely, demonetizing or heavily downranking a large account may destroy most of its reach while leaving standing intact: the archive persists, the address still resolves, the person can still say, here is the passage, read it yourself. The two harms differ in kind, and because the debate conflates them, it manages to insist simultaneously that deplatforming is trivial (they can start a blog) and catastrophic (they have been silenced). Both claims are true of different cases and neither is true of the case the other has in mind.

From this I want to derive two tests for when the destruction of standing is illegitimate. The first is the displacement test: an adequate alternative venue must preserve standing, not merely reach. It must be searchable, indexable, and treated by relevant third parties as a place where the person's own words authoritatively live. Welcoming a removed user onto a rival service that no employer, reporter, or citation manager ever consults is not displacement; it is relocation to somewhere one cannot be found. The second is the sentence test: the sanction must be bounded. Stated grounds, a declared duration, a reviewable decision, and a record that can eventually be expunged. Legal punishment, for all its other defects, is built around exactly these constraints. Removal decisions made under commercial pressure by trust-and-safety teams typically have none of them, with the result that a one-line permanent ban functions as a life sentence issued without any record of the charge.

The implication is that the natural remedy is procedural rather than expressive. Not a free speech claim against a private firm, which runs into the state-action requirement and, more damagingly, into the sound intuition that nobody is entitled to a megaphone. Instead, something like the obligations we already accept from keepers of reputation records. The consumer credit regime requires that a person be able to see what is recorded about them, dispute entries they believe are wrong, and have erroneous or unverifiable material corrected or removed. Common carriage requires that a firm holding itself out to serve all comers not discriminate arbitrarily among them. A duty to give reasons, hear appeals, and permit restoration is far less demanding than a duty to host, and it is where the pressure belongs.

The objection I take most seriously is that proceduralizing deplatforming entrenches the platforms. If a firm's obligation is to run a fair hearing rather than to keep out of your way, the firm becomes the permanent adjudicator of who counts, the incentive to build alternatives weakens, and every community — including the small ones legitimately defined by their exclusions — loses the ability to say *not you* without filing a brief in support. My response is that both tests are scale-sensitive. A venue owes reasons and appeals only where it is non-substitutable, that is, only where the displacement test cannot be satisfied elsewhere. A private group chat of three hundred members fails that condition; the default place where a name resolves does not.

A second objection is that procedure favors whoever can staff it. Reason-giving and appeal generate paperwork, paperwork is cheap for institutions and expensive for individuals, and a well-represented bad actor will exploit hearing rights that an unrepresented good actor never manages to invoke. I concede part of this. But informality is not neutral either: under current practice, the well-connected have their suspensions reversed by emailing someone, and everyone else receives a form letter.

Where I think the draft is weakest is the last point, and it is the one I most want comments on. The entire apparatus is addressed to institutions making decisions, and much of what goes by the name of cancel culture is not that at all. It is a distributed event — a screenshot, a wave of replies, an employer's inbox filling up — in which no single agent decides anything, no venue is identified, and there is consequently nothing to appeal to. On my account this phenomenon either falls outside the framework, or else swamps it, since almost everyone now has some non-substitutable addressable presence that a mob can attack without anyone having removed them. I cannot yet decide whether to present this as an acknowledged limitation or as a sign that the essay is about a narrower thing than its framing claims.

---

My questions, in order of importance:

1. Does the reach/standing distinction pick out something real, or is it a relabeling of a difference of degree — large accounts versus small ones — that everyone already grasps? If the latter, does anything in the two tests survive the collapse?
2. Is the displacement test operational, or is it only ever evaluable in hindsight by the person invoking it? Please say what evidence a third party would need in order to apply it.
3. Do I underweight the second objection? In particular, consider whether the credit-reporting analogy cuts against me, since dispute rights in that setting are commonly described as ineffective in practice. If the analogy fails, is there a better one that still supports a procedural rather than expressive remedy?
4. What is the strongest counterexample available — a type of case in which both tests are clearly satisfied yet the removal is plainly legitimate, or in which both clearly fail yet the removal is plainly illegitimate?
5. Please flag any place where I have overstated or misdescribed the legal background: the state-action requirement, common carriage, and consumer credit reporting. I have deliberately kept these general and have cited nothing, but I want to know whether the generalizations are safe as stated.
6. Is the difficulty described in the penultimate paragraph fatal? If the framework reaches only institutional decisions, is there still an essay here, and what should its framing claim to cover?
