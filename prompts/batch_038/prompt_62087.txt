I am in the second draft of a novel whose narrator is a retired investigator of industrial failures, and Part Three is interrupted by a run of intercalary documents — memos, transcripts, official findings — that the reader assembles before the narrator does. One of these is the formal inquiry report into the collapse of a certified monitoring system, and I would like you to write that document so that it stands alone. In the novel's world, a manufacturer has placed on the market a slow-response supervisory device — call it the Custos, and set it in whatever installation you think handles the material best, a long-duration cold-storage vault, a sealed respiratory loop, a grain silo's climate envelope — certified under an unusual design standard. The Custos is required not merely to register whether a certain condition obtains, but to be in a position to know that it obtains, and, under the operative certification clause, to know that it knows: iterated self-knowledge treated as a testable, warrantable engineering property, which the regulator has accepted on the manufacturer's assurance. The condition it monitors drifts minutely and continuously over something like a hundred minutes, and the report is about what the drift does to that assurance at the margins.

Please write it as a numbered official investigation report of roughly 1,000 words — factual information, analysis, probable cause, safety recommendations, and a short appendix reproducing the device's own minute-by-minute status log — in flat, unhedged bureaucratic-technical prose that never raises its voice. Invent the firm, the installation, the regulator, and the personnel; do not target any real person or institution. The satire is aimed at the certification regime and at the presumption of transparency behind it, and the comedy has to come from the epistemology being exactly right rather than from exaggeration: a specialist reader should see that the catastrophe was not a defect in the machine but the inescapable consequence of the standard it was built to satisfy, that a condition which drifts by imperceptible degrees cannot be one the machine is always in a position to know obtains, and that a device whose every act depends on knowing that it knows must fail precisely where its own output gives it no way of telling. The appendix log should make the cruel point visually — the device's self-report never registers the transition, and nothing in its own output marks the difference between the minutes when its claim amounted to knowledge and the minutes when it did not. Include one Supplementary Opinion from a board member who responds to the finding by recommending that the standard be tightened rather than abandoned, and keep him internally coherent; he is not a fool. No named philosophers, no citation apparatus, no footnotes, no winking at the reader.

After the report, add a short note — committed, not a menu of alternatives — stating which construal of the knowledge norm you built the piece on: whether you took the certification clause to import a margin-for-error-style constraint on knowledge across neighbouring moments, or something else, and why you chose that reading over the others available. Then name the two or three places where you knowingly bent the argument for the sake of the fiction, and say in a sentence each what the bend costs you. I would rather have a definite interpretive stance I can argue with than a survey of the options.
