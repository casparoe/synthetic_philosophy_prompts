I write novels, and for four years I have also served as the fiction editor of a small quarterly: twelve stories a year, a flat honorarium, roughly forty submissions a week. I read the shortlist myself alongside one volunteer first reader who has no technical background and can give the work perhaps five hours a week. Only I can accept or reject; she may annotate and flag, nothing more.

Two seasons ago we adopted a disclosure policy. Contributors are asked to state whether and how a language model was used in composing a piece, and we undertook not to reject work merely because one was. The policy has proved unworkable, and the reason is that our own wording does no work when someone presses it. We wrote of "substantive use" and of "the author's own creative work," and we can now say neither what makes use substantive nor what a person does that counts as their own. Worse, our masthead carries a line I wrote in a hurry and have come to regret: that we publish writing which could not have been written by anyone other than the person who wrote it. I would like that clause either turned into something a tired reader can apply or shown to be inapplicable, with a replacement stated.

What I need is a procedure, not a commentary. Please write a sequence of questions in a fixed order that my reader and I can run on a single submission in about fifteen minutes, specifying for each question what counts as a sufficient answer, where the sequence terminates in a reject, where it terminates in an accept, and where it stops and hands the matter to me because the remaining question is one of value or of what this magazine exists to publish rather than one of fact. Ground it in whatever aesthetics and the philosophy of art actually offer that is usable — the old argument over photography, about whether a picture produced by a mechanism that merely registers light can still be an achievement of the person who made it; whether what a work is can come apart from how it came to be, and whether we ever legitimately value the second thing; what is being claimed when a piece of writing is called original. Tell me which of those distinctions you are keeping, which you are discarding as unusable by a non-specialist under time pressure, and why.

Two constraints are hard. First, the procedure must not begin from anything resembling detection: we have no reliable means of identifying model-written prose, and I will not run a process whose opening step generates accusations more or less at random about named people. State what to do when a relevant fact simply cannot be established, and whether silence from a contributor is itself evidence of anything. Second, be candid about failure modes — which submissions the procedure will wrongly clear, which it will wrongly condemn, what each error costs the writer whose name is attached to our decision, and which error we should be more willing to absorb.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

(a) A story of unusual polish on which the contributor has disclosed nothing either way, and which we cannot distinguish, by reading, from model prose.

(b) A story by a contributor whose first language is not English, who discloses having run every paragraph through a model to smooth the prose while inventing the situation, the characters, the structure and the ending.

(c) A story we have reason to believe was generated almost entirely by a model, and which is the best thing in this month's pile by a considerable margin.

At least one of the three must come out against my preference — I would rather print (b) and refuse (c), and I expect the procedure will not simply ratify that. Where it does not, say so and explain what in the procedure overrides me, rather than adjusting the outcome to suit the editor. Finally, close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: the questions it looks as though it answers but does not, and the point at which I should stop applying it and simply make an editorial decision.

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of positions; invoke particular thinkers only where doing so changes what my reader is to do on a Tuesday afternoon.
