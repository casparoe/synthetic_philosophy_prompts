I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the dogmatism paradox, and we made no progress because we were disagreeing about something prior: what would even count as solving it. Here is the argument as I put it to him and as he accepted every step. Suppose I know that p — something ordinary, like that my sister was in the city that evening, or something weightier, like that a diagnosis is correct. If I know that p, then I know that anything incompatible with p is false, and so I know that any evidence I might later acquire pointing against p is misleading evidence. Closure does the rest: I know now, before I have laid eyes on any of it, that any future evidence against p will be misleading. And if I know that, my friend said, then I not merely may but must disregard it when it arrives — any account that instructs me to weigh it is instructing me to be less rational than I already am. His test case was a source whose unreliability I have independently established: a witness caught out lying twice, an instrument I know to be broken, where dismissal in advance is obviously right and anyone who insists on an open mind is being scrupulous at the cost of sense. My reply was that the broken instrument and the unexpected laboratory result are not the same thing, and that no account which treats them alike can be acceptable. He said I was assuming precisely what was in question. Neither of us could say what I was assuming, or why he wasn't assuming the opposite.

So before I read another proposed dissolution, I want the specification. Write the desiderata that an account of knowledge, evidence, and defeat has to meet in order to count as handling this puzzle at all — and let the standard be a demanding one: an account good enough to underwrite actual guidance for inquirers, something one could hand to a clinician, a juror, or a scientist holding a result they did not expect, not merely something that blocks an argument form. For each requirement, give me the datum or consideration that motivates it — that inquiry must be able to change my mind, that pre-emptive dismissal of a source I know to be unreliable is no vice, that knowing p really does entail that contrary evidence would be misleading, that I cannot be rationally required to bind my future self in advance on the strength of what I know now — and the distinction it forbids a theory from collapsing: knowing versus being highly confident, misleading evidence versus defeating evidence, adopting a policy of dismissal versus merely having a stubborn disposition, and the question whether I will be justified later versus the question whether I should commit now. Say also which results the account must be consistent with, whether that is closure or its principled denial, KK, or whatever the probability calculus demands of a credence policy that undermines itself. I am not after a survey of the literature and I cannot check references anyway; I want the criteria, the kind of thing I could put at the top of a reading-group handout so that people argue about the requirements instead of defending their favourite escape route.

Then, for each requirement, tell me whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or whether it is one camp's ideology dressed up as data — I suspect that at least one of the things I listed above as obvious is really a Bayesian or an internalist prejudice wearing a lab coat. Above all, I want to know whether some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable: if preserving closure, keeping knowledge stable in the face of newly acquired evidence, and forbidding pre-emptive dismissal cannot all be had at once, then the list is itself the result, and my friend and I were simply haggling over which of the three to give up. Mark the single requirement that excludes the most candidates. Finally, and briefly — a sentence or two each, no more — how the standard routes fare against the list: the denial of closure, the denial of KK, the view that knowledge is simply lost at the moment the misleading evidence arrives, and the purely probabilistic treatment on which the whole puzzle confuses credences with knowledge. For each, say which requirement it buys its peace on and what it pays for it.
