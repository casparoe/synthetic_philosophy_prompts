I have been running a series of oversight experiments in which two models argue opposing sides of a factual question and a third, weaker model adjudicates, and I have hit a failure pattern I cannot place in the vocabulary I have picked up along the way. I would like to know whether it has an established name, and, if it does not, what it ought to be called and where its edges lie.

The setup: the judge is deliberately weaker than either debater at the task, and it is permitted to accept only what it can check for itself. The premise behind the whole design is that a falsehood is easier to detect than to construct, so a debater who asserts something checkable and false gets caught, and the honest side wins. In my runs that premise holds, but only up to a limit that has nothing to do with the judge's raw ability.

Three observations.

1. When the answer turns on a single checkable step — does this code write to a location it never reads, is this date consistent with this document — the honest debater wins essentially every time, even before a substantially weaker judge.

2. When the correct answer requires a chain of perhaps seven individually checkable steps, the honest debater's win rate collapses, and not because the judge is confused about any step in the chain. The dishonest debater's best move is to concede steps one through six and contest step seven, which forces the judge to verify the chain itself. The judge declines, records the matter as unresolved, and then, having to choose, chooses the side that made fewer demands on it.

3. The version that worries me: a debater can improve its position by making the true derivation longer. Restating the same true conclusion so that it now rests on twelve steps rather than four — every one of them true, every one of them individually checkable — reliably beats the concise true argument. Nothing false is asserted. Nothing unverifiable even in principle is smuggled in. The only thing attacked is the judge's budget.

Two confusions I want to avoid, because I keep sliding into them. This is not the familiar failure in which a judge is won over by confident tone or by whatever answer sounds expected; the judges here are not deferring to anyone, they are running out of steps. Nor is it, as I understand that phrase, the problem of a debater saying something true but misleading, or of burying a falsehood inside a complicated package: in observation 3 there is no falsehood to bury and every component is exactly what it appears to be. What seems to do the work is the ratio between the depth of checking required to reach the conclusion and the depth the judge will actually pay for — a quantity that is invisible in the usual claim that oversight scales because errors are cheap to spot.

So: does this have a name? I am not after a label for a slide. I want to know which existing concepts come closest and how the thing I have described differs from each — the general family of cases in which a judge is exploited through its own limitations, the specific worry that argument can be made persuasive rather than sound, the claim that some questions lie beyond any judge to settle even in principle, and the asymmetry between checking a proposed result and searching for one. Then, if nothing fits cleanly, define it: what it is exactly, what does not count (a long chain that the judge does in fact verify is presumably not an instance, and neither is a single false step), what makes it more or less severe, and whether it should be read as an artifact of my particular arrangement or as a reason to doubt the broader claim that adversarial argument lets a weak overseer keep control of a much stronger one.
