Our uni philosophy society ran a session on whether dreams are experiences — the old Malcolm vs. Dennett thing — and afterwards two members kept at it in the kitchen for close to an hour. I couldnt follow who was winning which bit. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right where, rather than just who came out ahead overall?

The exchange, as best I remember it:

1. Rae: Malcolm's point is that the sentence you use when you tell someone you dreamt isn't a report about something that went on inside your head overnight. So asking what the dream experience was like is already malformed — you've smuggled in a picture of a private screening room and then asked for a ticket stub.
2. Jonas: that's just shuffling words. Whether anything happens in my skull between lights out and the alarm is a fact about my skull, not about English. You can't legislate an empirical question out of existence with a grammar lesson.
3. Rae: there's no empirical question left to legislate if the two answers could never come apart. Dennett's own move is the tape: a dream impression could be something that only gets recorded as you surface, and no amount of looking inward could tell that from having actually watched it. If two stories can never diverge, they're one story, and the word experience stops doing any work at all.
4. Jonas: except they do diverge, from the outside, and they already have. Waking people from REM gets you far more and much richer reports than waking them from the flat stages. And the alpha-sleeper cases go the other way from what Dennett wants — people whose brainwaves say they were idling awake insist nothing was happening to them. If the tape only gets written at the moment of waking, why would whether you get a report, and what's in it, track what the brain was doing an hour earlier?
5. Rae: becuase the tape recorder runs off the same wiring. That correlation shows the reports track physiology, which nobody disputes. It doesn't show anything was lived through. And your move-3 killer — that theories you can't tell apart collapse into a single theory — is just verificationism in a nicer jacket, which is a big cheque to cash in a kitchen at 1am.

Separately, and this came up later: my flatmate Tom keeps a dream journal, and he'll swear the entries get *more* detailed the longer he waits before writing them. Five minutes after waking it's a sketch; an hour later there's dialogue and weather and a whole geography. He says that's his memory filling in properly and it proves the experience was there. Jonas heard about it and said it's plainly the opposite, textbook confabulation. Rae said it's neither, it's just beside the point.
