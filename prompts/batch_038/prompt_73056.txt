Last spring my department co-hosted a workshop on the Industrial Revolution with the history faculty. The survey paper that opened it took up the Needham question in its economic form — why sustained industrialization began in Britain rather than in China — and the scheduled discussion was unremarkable. At the reception afterward, however, two of the participants kept at it: Elena Varga, an economic historian who works on eighteenth-century factor prices, and Simon Reyes, a philosopher of science who works on explanation. I was standing close enough to take notes on most of the exchange. Neither asked me to take a side, and I could not tell which of them was ahead; I would like an independent verdict.

The argument, as nearly as I can reproduce it:

1. Reyes opened by saying the paper's question is not a question but a question schema. A why-question is incomplete until a contrast class is supplied — why this rather than that — together with a standard for which differences count as relevant. The paper fixed neither, so "why Britain and not China?" has no determinate answer, and everything said in its name was really an answer to some other question the speaker had silently supplied.

2. Varga said this was a way of declining to engage. The literature does offer answers, and they are specific and disputable: coal that was both accessible and near demand; New World possessions that relieved the land constraint; unusually high wages combined with unusually cheap energy, which is what made labor-saving, fuel-hungry machinery pay; and political fragmentation, which gave innovators exit options and set rulers competing for them. A question with that many live candidate answers under active empirical dispute is not malformed. It is contested, which is a different thing.

3. Reyes said the apparent conflict among those answers was evidence for his point, because each is keyed to a different contrast class. Coal and the colonies answer "why Britain rather than the Yangzi Delta." High wages and cheap energy answer "why Britain rather than anywhere else the same technology was known." Fragmentation answers "why Europe rather than China" — and, he noted, it cannot answer "why Britain rather than the Netherlands," since the Dutch were one of fragmented Europe's winners too. The paper was answering three questions and presenting the sum as one answer. He invoked Lipton's paresis case: whether some factor explains an outcome depends on which alternative it is being contrasted against, and something that is no difference-maker in general can be the right answer to one foil and useless for another.

4. Varga said that if the question is vague, the remedy is to sharpen it, and that this is exactly what historians have been doing for decades — the regional comparison is a refinement, not a repudiation. She said Reyes's standard would condemn ordinary questions wholesale: "where do you live?" does not specify city, street, or country, and nobody supposes it ill posed. Indeterminacy in the choice of a contrast class is a property of nearly every explanatory question asked outside a formal system.

5. Reyes said he was not making a vagueness objection but a presupposition objection. "Why Britain and not China?" presupposes that Chinese non-industrialization is a fact requiring explanation alongside the British event, as if two symmetric outcomes were on offer and one obtained. But the default condition of the premodern world was no sustained growth anywhere. If absence is the background state, then the absence in China is not a datum competing with the occurrence in Britain; only the occurrence is. The question as framed implies there are two things to explain when there is one.

6. Varga said the argument worked against him. If non-industrialization is the default everywhere, the asymmetry he says is missing is precisely what the question names: one place escaped the default and the other did not, and "why Britain and not China?" is shorthand for "why did the escape happen there and not there." She said that selecting a contrast to pursue is a matter of interest, not of well-posedness, and that Reyes was smuggling an objection to the topic in as a defect in the question.

7. Reyes said he would accept the charge and make the interest point openly, because the topic had not been chosen neutrally. The puzzle in its original form concerned modern science — why the scientific revolution occurred in Europe when China had a long prior record of technical achievement — and the industrial version was grafted on later, carrying the earlier framing's normative charge with it. A question that treats one trajectory's outcome as the target and the other's as a shortfall does not merely fail to be precise: it fixes in advance what will count as relevant, since relevance was built into how the question was posed. That, he said, is why the answers arrive as a catalogue of British advantages rather than as a comparison of two paths.

8. Varga's last move was that whatever the framing's pedigree, it has produced claims that could be and have been tested — that English real wages were high relative to the price of energy; that living standards and commercialization in the Yangzi Delta around the middle of the eighteenth century were broadly comparable to England's; that Britain's coal lay near its demand while China's largest deposits lay far from its most advanced region; that what is called the high-level equilibrium trap really operated, with efficient agriculture holding labor cheap enough that labor-saving machinery never paid. If the question is malformed, she said, Reyes owes an account of why the research program built on it is among the most quantitatively serious in the field.

9. Reyes said productivity establishes usefulness, not well-posedness, and that the record in fact supports him: nearly all of that quantitative work happens at the level of the refined regional comparison, where the contrast class has been fixed. The original question, he said, does no work in the research. It is a label for a program, not a question anyone answers.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the nine, say whether it is clearly mistaken, weak, defensible, strong, or clearly right, and justify the rating in a sentence or two — noting in particular where a move overreaches beyond what its own premise supports. Then tell me: (a) whether the two are using "well posed" in the same sense, and if not, which senses are in play and which moves trade on the ambiguity; (b) who is right about the two separable issues — whether the question lacks a determinate contrast class, and whether it carries a presupposition that fails; and (c) what both of them are missing, including whether the dispute would come out differently under a different framing such as "why Britain and not France" or "why the eighteenth century and not the sixteenth." Finally, if you think the question can be repaired, state the version or versions that ought to be asked instead and say which of the candidate explanations mentioned above attach to which version. I do not want references or a bibliography; I want the arguments assessed on their merits.
