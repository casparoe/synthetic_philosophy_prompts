I am a third-year philosophy student, and in our department's seminar on minds and machines each participant facilitates one forty-five-minute session for the remaining twenty-two students. My topic is Dennett's fourfold taxonomy of creature types — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian — which we read in *Kinds of Minds* (1996). I do not want to lecture on it, because the version my classmates will accept without resistance is the shallow one: a ladder of intelligence on which each rung simply adds processing power to the one below. I want them to generate the differences through play, and I want the ladder reading damaged by their own scores. Please design the complete activity.

What I need:

**The task.** One task, fully specified, repeated four times under four different regimes. It must be cheap to run (paper, tokens, dice at most), solvable within a few minutes, and structurally identical across all four regimes, so that any difference in performance is attributable to the permissions granted rather than to a change in difficulty. Build into it a surface cue that strongly suggests a false hypothesis, so that the regime permitting offline deliberation can plausibly finish *below* the regime permitting only feedback-driven adjustment. That inversion is the point I care about most; tell me how to calibrate the cue so the inversion happens often but not inevitably.

**The regime rules.** For each regime, state precisely what players may and may not do. My own sketch, which you should refine or replace: a sealed-card regime in which players execute an assigned strategy without deviation and improvement happens only by replacing players whose cards failed; a regime in which adjustment is permitted solely on the basis of outcome feedback, with no verbal hypothesis-forming and no records; a regime with unlimited rehearsal and planning aloud but nothing written down, so the model must be rebuilt inside the round; and a regime with notation and artifacts permitted, in which groups inherit materials left behind by a group that has already left — materials that may themselves carry errors. Label the regimes A through D for the participants; they should not hear the creature names until the debrief.

**Scoring.** A payoff scheme requiring no money: how points are earned, what they buy (trial budgets, information tokens, or the like), and how the four totals are displayed so the inversion is visible to the room rather than merely asserted by me.

**Timing.** A minute-by-minute schedule for forty-five minutes with one facilitator, including setup that can be completed while students are arriving.

**Debrief.** Map specific moments of play onto the taxonomy, and in particular drive the group to say where the design work happened in each round: in the organism's fixed make-up, in its own history of consequences, inside a private model, or in artifacts that outlive their makers. Then confront them with round C scoring below round B and ask what that shows about the value of an internal world-model — and what has to be true of a world-model for it to be an advantage at all.

**Failure modes.** What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to say in the moment: players who cannot stop theorising aloud when theorising is forbidden, players who improvise when their card forbids it, groups whose inherited notes collapse the task too quickly, and wherever the timing is likely to break.

**Limits.** A candid account of what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally. All twenty-two participants are Gregorian minds role-playing at every other level; I need to know which features of the taxonomy are simulated only by stipulation, so that I do not overclaim in the debrief.
