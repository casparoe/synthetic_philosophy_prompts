Back in September I took a job typing up minutes for a city committee, and I've now sat through four meetings about a formal apology the council is considering. Quick background so this makes sense: in 1963 the city cleared a neighborhood everyone called Ward Eight to build the civic center, paid the homeowners roughly sixty cents on the dollar under a taking that was rushed through as an emergency measure, and the same city's ordinances then made it close to impossible for those families to buy anywhere in the better neighborhoods. The civic center is still sitting on that parcel and its revenue still goes into the general fund. The committee has to recommend language to the council by spring.

Two members have been going at it since meeting two, and it spilled out into the hallway after the last one. Dean is the city attorney, on the committee ex officio. Ruth is a retired history teacher appointed from the descendent community. I took notes, and I'm honestly not sure whos winning. Roughly how it went:

1. Dean: the council shouldn't say "we apologize." An apology has to come from whoever did the thing, and nobody in that chamber did it. The most the current council can honestly say is that it regrets what happened. Saying "we apologize" is a false sentence, and building the city's official acknowledgment on a false sentence is a bad way to start.

2. Ruth: the city is one continuing legal person. Same entity that signed the taking, still holds the deed, can be sued today on a 1963 contract. You take the assets with the liabilities — you don't get to keep the civic center and disown the demolition. And Dean's rule proves way too much: no country could ever apologize for anything its predecessors did, which would make the whole genre of state apology nonsense.

3. Dean: fine, grant the continuing person. That settles who can apologize, not whether this one is an apology. As drafted it costs the city nothing — no money, no land, no change to how the parcel is assessed. An apology that keeps every benefit of the wrong is a press release. He said he'd rather they not do it at all, because a free apology closes the file and makes the substantive fight harder later.

4. Ruth: agreed on cost, wrong conclusion. The apology does something money can't — it converts "that's just how the city grew" into an acknowledged wrong, and it's a precondition for anything else, since the council can't fund a remedy it doesn't think is owed. And the "closes the file" worry holds the one thing they can definitely get hostage to a budget fight she might lose. Bank the acknowledgment now.

5. Dean: then who is it addressed to? Most of the people who were pushed out are dead. The ones still alive weren't the ones thrown out of anything, and grandchildren can't accept on their behalf, because accepting an apology isn't a property right you inherit, it's a personal act. If the descendants say "we accept," they're presuming a forgiveness only the dead could give. So the document should go onto the record and stop pretending it has a recipient.

6. Ruth: the harm didn't stop at the people it was done to. Her father never owned anything after 1963; the lost equity and the torn-up networks went straight down the line, so the standing to receive the acknowledgment goes down the line too. And "only the dead could forgive" would make every historical apology impossible, plus it treats descendants as bystanders to their own family's ruin. Also — an apology addressed to "the record" is exactly the free press release he objected to in move 3, so he can't have it both ways.

7. Ruth, still going: and you're being inconsistent. First you wanted the words to track the moral facts about who acted, then you wanted them to track the council's willingness to pay, and not once have you asked whether anybody in that chamber actually feels remorse. If this is a corporate act, sincerity drops out completely. If it's a personal act, your continuing-person argument falls apart. Pick one.

8. Dean: sincerity doesn't drop out, it relocates. An institution can't feel anything, so the only sincerity test it's capable of passing is doing something it wouldn't otherwise have done. The cost IS the sincerity condition. That's not a dodge, it's the only version of sincerity available here.

Then Ruth said something like "so we agree" and Dean said "we don't" and they both left.

Can you go through this move by move? For each of the eight, tell me whether it's right, partly right, or wrong and why — not just who wins overall, though I do want that too at the end. And is there something neither of them has said that would actually break the tie? I keep getting the feeling they're both circling a thing they haven't named.
