I'm the contracts and resident-services lead at a small housing association in the North West — about 900 homes, some of it supported housing — and the board has taken on board the republican idea that freedom isn't just non-interference but non-domination: not being subject to someone's arbitrary power. They want a written test we can run over any new contract, licence or service before we sign. I don't want an essay on Pettit, I want something my team can actually use, and two of them aren't philosophers so it needs to be plain English.

You're a political philosopher who's done applied work on republican liberty. Think it through carefully — start with a short bit of brainstorming about what the principle actually commits you to, and what has to survive translation into a procedure before you write anything.

The deliverable is a step-by-step domination screen. For each step: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, whether a bad answer stops the thing dead or just flags it for the board, and where the step hands the decision to a human because its a question of values rather than facts. It has to run in all three directions — where we hold power over residents, where a contractor holds power over us, and where a funder or regulator holds power over residents through us — with the same test each time. And it has to handle the obvious circularity: any interference we carry out to reduce domination is itself an exercise of power, so what makes it non-arbitrary, and how do we operationalise that in a document that a surveyor or a housing officer is going to fill in on a Tuesday?

Then run it on three of ours:

1. Our supported-housing licence, which lets us ask a resident to leave for behaviour we consider unsuitable, on two weeks' notice, with no external review.
2. A new outsourced repairs contract: fifteen-working-day target, and a complaints process run by the same contractor, escalated to us only if the resident pushes.
3. Our resident participation budget, where residents deliberate and rank spending priorities but we keep the final say and can set aside the result with a written reason.

One of these should come out in a way we won't like — don't soften it, say which and why.

Finally, be straight with me about where the screen misfires: the judgment calls dressed up as procedure, the relationships where it goes quiet because the dominated party can't or won't avow their interests, and the cases where non-domination and non-interference genuinely pull in opposite directions and the test can't tell us which to prefer. If the whole thing is really just a politeness audit with fancy vocabulary, I'd rather you said so now.
