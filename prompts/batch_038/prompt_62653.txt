You are a philosopher of social epistemology who also works carefully with the formal social-learning literature — informational cascades, rational herding, aggregation of private signals — and who is comfortable reading ordinary Anglo-American trial procedure. Please think hard before writing. The practice described below contains several parts that pull in different directions, and the value of your answer lies in keeping those parts distinct rather than in gesturing at a general theme about group knowledge.

**The practice.** In a criminal jury trial in the federal courts and most U.S. states, three features are standard.

1. *The silence rule.* Jurors are instructed at the outset not to discuss the case with one another until all the evidence has been received and the court has sent them out to deliberate. No comparing notes, no speculating aloud, no preliminary vote. A minority of states — Arizona first, in the mid-1990s, followed by a few others — reversed this for civil trials, permitting jurors to talk over the evidence among themselves in the jury room during recesses when all of them are present, provided they hold back from reaching or expressing any judgment about the outcome until deliberations begin.

2. *The closed-record rule.* Jurors are instructed not to look anything up: no news coverage, no internet searches, no visits to the scene, no experiments of their own. The verdict must rest on the record produced in court.

3. *The black-box rule.* Once a verdict is returned, the law makes the deliberation room very nearly uninspectable. Under Rule 606(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence and its state analogues, during an inquiry into the validity of a verdict a juror may not testify about "any statement made or incident that occurred during the jury's deliberations; the effect of anything on that juror's or another juror's vote; or any juror's mental processes concerning the verdict." The exceptions are narrow: extraneous prejudicial information improperly brought to the jury's attention, an outside influence improperly brought to bear on a juror, a clerical mistake in entering the verdict — plus the constitutional exception recognized in *Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado* (2017) for a juror's clear statement showing racial animus. A compromise verdict, a quotient verdict, a juror who misunderstood the instructions, a juror who capitulated to the loudest voice in the room: none of these may be established by the jurors' own testimony.

**What I want from you.** Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective belief that this package presupposes, and test it for coherence. Specifically:

(a) What picture of the jury does feature 1 assume? Protecting twelve judgments from one another sounds like the independence condition behind the Condorcet jury theorem — yet the same system then *requires* those twelve to deliberate toward unanimity, deliberately dismantling that independence. Is the jury, on this practice's own understanding, an aggregation device or a single group agent? Can it be both in sequence without contradiction, or does the sequence merely conceal a switch between two incompatible views?

(b) Bring in the cascade literature explicitly. Distinguish an informational cascade from rational herding and from Condorcet-style aggregation, and say which of these each feature is meant to block, tolerate, or exploit. Explain precisely why a premature vote would generate a cascade rather than merely a conversation, and whether feature 3 protects against cascades or simply hides them from review. Note also that feature 1 forbids speech but cannot forbid the visible reactions of eleven faces while a witness testifies; say whether that channel is a cascade channel, and what the rule's blindness to it reveals about the view of communication it presupposes.

(c) Is the Arizona variant even intelligible? Instructing jurors to exchange arguments while withholding judgment presupposes something about the voluntariness of belief and about whether argument-exchange can be separated from belief-formation. State that presupposition and say whether it can be satisfied.

(d) Feature 2 makes the standard of proof relative to a closed, procedurally controlled body of evidence rather than to everything knowable. Is that an epistemic requirement, or a fairness requirement dressed in epistemic language? What would have to change if the trial's aim were treated as purely alethic?

(e) Now evaluate a reform proposal I am considering — treat it as new, since it is not an existing institution. *Sealed preliminary ballots.* After the evidence closes and instructions are read, but before any discussion, each juror privately writes a verdict on a card plus one sentence naming what that juror takes to be the decisive evidence. The cards are sealed and held by the clerk. The jury then deliberates as usual, as long as it needs. If it reaches a verdict, the envelopes are destroyed unopened. If after three days it reports itself deadlocked, the envelopes are opened: if at least ten of the twelve agree, the court enters that verdict without retrial; otherwise a mistrial is declared. Does this reform presuppose the same theory as features 1–3, or a rival one? Address two things in particular. First, the reflexive problem: if jurors know their sealed cards may themselves decide the case on a hang, do the cards still capture pre-cascade private signals, or does anticipation of the group's later use of them corrupt the very independence the sealing was meant to preserve? Second, the asymmetry: the sealed information is consulted only when the group agent fails, and destroyed whenever it succeeds — what does that ordering say about whose belief the practice ultimately treats as the verdict?

(f) Finally, mark the limits of the reconstruction. Distinguish what the practice *strictly* presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and identify where several incompatible theories would equally license it. Then say which of the three features would have to be abandoned, and which could survive, under a design that took cascade dynamics fully seriously.
