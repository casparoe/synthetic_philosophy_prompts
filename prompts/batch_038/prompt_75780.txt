I'm reading one of those trade-press histories of ideas, and it sums up the mid-century row over how history explains things like this:

> "Hempel argued that explaining an event means subsuming it under a general law, which put historians in an awkward spot: if you can't state the laws of human behaviour your account rests on, you haven't explained anything, you've just told a good story. He also held that explaining and predicting come to the same thing, so the covering-law historian was committed to saying that, in principle, he had predicted the French Revolution. Danto led the reply, that historical explanation is narrative all the way down: an account explains by showing how the outcome follows from the earlier events as a story, and stories, unlike laws, are essentially about particular unrepeatable sequences. The model duly collapsed, refuted by the plain observation that no working historian has ever produced a law, and virtually nobody defends it today."

Is that fair? I've done some philosophy but none on this, and I suspect the 'refuted by plain observation' bit goes too fast, though I couldn't say why. Could you work through it sentence by sentence — what's accurate, what's a simplification that quietly changes the substance, what a defender of Hempel would object to, and whether the conclusion still stands once you've put the record straight? And is the last claim about the present state of play defensible, or is that the author tidying up?
