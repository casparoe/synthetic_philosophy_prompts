A colleague of mine works on metaphysical grounding and has, over the years, issued the following verdicts, all of which she holds firmly:

1. The fact that Socrates exists grounds the fact that {Socrates} exists, though not conversely — and she insists the direction is not a matter of necessitation, since in every world in which either fact obtains, so does the other.
2. The fact that the rose is red grounds the fact that the rose is red or the sky is green; but where both disjuncts are true, she says the disjunctive fact is grounded by them jointly rather than by either one alone.
3. The fact that the marble is scarlet all over grounds both that it is red and that it is not green; the fact that it is red all over does not, she says, ground that it is not green.
4. The fact that Hesperus is Phosphorus is grounded — in Venus, not in our naming practices — whereas the fact that 2 + 2 = 4 is grounded in nothing whatever, though she denies that going ungrounded is by itself what makes a fact fundamental.

She has never said anything general about the formal properties of grounding.

Her graduate students have now put a case to her. Consider a world containing exactly two electrons, e and e′, and no other electrons. Let (A) be the plural fact that e and e′ exist, (B) the fact that there are exactly two electrons, and (C) the fact that there are at least two electrons. They want to know what she will say grounds (B) — and, more provocatively, whether she will allow that (B) grounds (A).

Predict her answers. Say which accounts remain in the running given 1–4 — essence-based grounding, truthmaking with a preference for the most specific maker, grounding as counterfactual dependence, grounding as necessitation plus an independent priority ordering, or something else — and what each implies about the relations among (A), (B), and (C), including whether any of them needs a further "and that is all" ingredient. Then give your best guess about what she will say, explain why you prefer it to the alternatives, and name one further case you would put to her that would separate the surviving accounts. If no account survives 1–4 intact, say which of her verdicts she is most likely to give up.
