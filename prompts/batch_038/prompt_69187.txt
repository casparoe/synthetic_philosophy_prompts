I have spent four years cataloguing the deposit from the Verney Street Mill for the Fenny Cross Local History Room, and on Saturday I am giving the opening remarks at the ribbon-cutting for the new exhibition of those records. I write the wall labels, but I am not an academic — I taught history at the comprehensive here for thirty-one years — and the register of this speech needs to be mine. Please write it for me.

**The occasion.** The Local History Room occupies the former mill office. A new gallery is opening, built around the mill's own books, with four cases and a photographic wall. Funding came from Ockenden Holdings, the property and logistics firm the mill's owning family became; the gallery will be called the Ockenden Room, because that is what the funding agreement says, and I have no intention of pretending otherwise. About ninety people, a Saturday morning, the mayor speaks after me, and a reporter from the Fenny Cross Advertiser will be there. Roughly eight minutes for me: call it 1,000 to 1,200 words, written to be read aloud.

**Who is in the room.** Four generations of Ockendens, including the current chair, who personally paid for the conservation of the earliest ledgers and is not a villain. Two historians from the university thirty miles off — one a labour historian, one who works on the history of record-keeping — who advised on the gallery text and will read my labels afterwards. Perhaps twenty-five residents whose relatives worked at the mill, several of whom have lent photographs and objects for the community cases. A handful of men in hi-vis from the firm now occupying the mill buildings, who have come on a Saturday. Mrs Edith Sowerby, aged ninety-four, who wove at Verney Street from 1946 to 1962 and is the only person in the room who worked there; her grandson is recording today on his phone. And a general public that has come for a free event and a cup of tea.

**What actually survives.** Nothing here needs checking against the outside world; everything you require is below, and please do not invent a document, a date, a name, or a figure that is not in this list.

1. Sixty-three day books, 1848–1911, kept by successive timekeepers: one line per worker per week, in columns headed Name, Time, Work, Fines, Advance, Remarks. Three different hands before 1911, each spelling names as he heard them; from 1911 the wage ledgers are typed by a firm of accountants who standardised the spellings. The Remarks column is almost empty. Across all sixty-three volumes there are 214 entries in it, and about two-thirds concern the weather, the boiler, or the yarn market. Nine mention a worker by name; four of those nine record a dismissal.
2. The week of 3–9 September 1877 has no entries at all for the spinning room. The Remarks column for that week reads, in full: *Frames stopped.* Whether that was the workers, a breakdown, or no orders, the books do not say, and the outgoing letter book for 1877 does not survive.
3. A petition of 1891, sixty-one signatures and forty-four marks, asking for the abolition of the fine for spoiled work and for a half-hour at dinner. It survives because Walter Ockenden filed it with his solicitor's correspondence about a threatened prosecution for intimidation. The cover of that file is annotated in his hand. The fine was not abolished. The half-hour was granted in 1893, and the ledger records it as a concession by the firm.
4. The firm's outgoing letter books, 1878–1940, which are substantial. Almost no incoming correspondence survives, because the office kept copies of what it sent and binned what it received.
5. One letter from a worker, preserved only because it was pinned to the reply. In pencil, four lines, from Hannah Teale, 1908: *Sir, my husband Samuel Teale is dead on the 4th of January and there is 11 shillings owing to him for the weeks he worked and I have three children and no coal. I am your obedient servant, Hannah Teale.* The reply, of which we hold the firm's copy in full, is courteous and pays six shillings, the balance being deducted against an advance standing in the deceased's name. That advance is the only fact the books preserve about Samuel Teale's last months.
6. An engineer's register of accidents, 1874–1901, kept for the firm's insurers. It records damage to machinery first and injury to persons second. Some entries describe the event only as damage to a frame caused by carelessness, and do not name anyone.
7. A photograph of 1911 captioned Carding Room, Verney Street Mill: forty-seven people in the frame, of whom the caption names three, all of them managers or partners. Family donations to the community cases have let us match four more faces. Forty remain unnamed.
8. The parish registers record the pauper burials by plot number, with "unknown" against a run of years; the register for 1855–1862 was destroyed in a fire at the vicarage in 1929.
9. A centenary booklet of 1948 by Ernest Bloor, a local newspaperman, warm and anecdotal, which is where nearly everything the town believes about the mill's early years comes from. Bloor tells how Josiah Ockenden carried a barefoot child home through the snow in the winter of 1853 and paid for her schooling afterwards. He says he had it from Walter Ockenden's daughter Constance, born 1889, who said she had it from her father. The day book for November 1853 to February 1854 is water-damaged and illegible. There is no record of any payment for anyone's schooling; there is a single undifferentiated line headed Charities in the annual accounts.
10. Fourteen crates of counting-house material, deposited in 1997 when the offices were cleared, never sorted, currently in a self-storage unit because this building has no environmental control. The exhibition label mentions them.
11. My own index. To make the catalogue usable I had to choose one spelling per person. The family everyone in this town calls Hanrahan appears across four volumes as Hanrahan, Hanahan, Anahan and Hanaghan, and the index now says Hanrahan, because that is the spelling in the 1891 petition — though the marks in that petition are not attributable to individuals, and the roll of names beside them was written out by one literate hand. Every future citation of that family will use my spelling.

The family has asked, gently, that I tell Bloor's snow story. Two historians will know exactly what it is.

**The brief.** The speech must make the shape of this collection — what the books were for, and what that determined about who appears in them — do the actual structural work, not serve as a theme announced at the end. Every general point has to be carried by a specific object the audience can walk over and look at in a case. It must name real individuals only where the records or the room supply them: no invented workers, no composite figures, no "let us imagine she was called…", and no attributed thoughts, feelings, or speech for anyone who left no words of their own. The only direct quotation available is Hannah Teale's letter and the petition's opening sentence, which reads: *We whose names and marks are hereunto set, being employed in the carding and spinning rooms, do respectfully state that the practice of fining for spoiled work falls upon those who have least means to bear it, and that the spoilage arises from the speed of the frames and not from any want of care.* If you use either, use it exactly.

It must thank the Ockendons truthfully and without grovelling: the money is real, the deposit is real, and a family that hands its own account books to a public is doing something. It must handle the snow story in front of the family that loves it and the two historians who know what it is, neither debunking it nor repeating it as fact. It must include a moment of naming drawn from the petition, and must be honest about what that list is. It must say something about the fourteen crates and about my own index — I am not above the problem I am describing, and the gallery is itself a selection. It needs one moment of genuine humour suited to a Saturday morning, and it must not become a sermon, an indictment, or a eulogy for industry.

Do not name any historian, philosopher, school, or theory, and do not use the vocabulary of archival theory: no "silences of the archive," no "erasure," "subaltern," "provenance," "hegemony," "marginalised voices." If the ideas need those words, I have failed. Close by asking the room to do something at the table by the photographic wall, where they can write the name of anyone they recognise among the forty — and be honest that we will keep those slips in a separate file, labelled as family testimony, rather than fold them into the index, and let that decision carry the last thought rather than explain it.

**Deliverable.** The speech, ready to read aloud, with at most a few bracketed cues. Then, separately, a note of about 200 words identifying the two or three places where these constraints forced a choice I could have made differently, and what the other choice would have cost.
