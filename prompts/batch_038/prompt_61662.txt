I have just read a section of a well-regarded general-audience intellectual history of nineteenth-century political thought devoted to Hegel on war. Rather than reproduce the author's prose, I have condensed the section's argument into the following numbered claims, in order, and as far as I can judge without distorting them:

1. For Hegel, war is not an absolute evil nor a merely external accident that befalls states; it has a positive ethical moment, because it is the situation in which the vanity of finite goods — life and property — is taken seriously rather than merely preached about.
2. He held that peace, and especially perpetual peace, corrupts nations: he compared a lasting calm to a sea going putrid for want of wind, and war to the movement that keeps the water healthy.
3. Because states are individuals, there is no authority above them to adjudicate their disputes — at most arbitrators and mediators, and only contingently — so international law retains the form of a mere ought, and Kant's league of nations could bind a state only for as long as that state continued to will to be bound.
4. It follows that war is not merely tolerated but welcome in Hegel's system: a good to be kept available, whose justification lies in the health of the ethical order rather than in any particular grievance.
5. When Hegel says the individual's substantial duty is to maintain the independence and sovereignty of the state at the risk of property and life, he means that the state's preservation is the highest end and persons its instruments; the Hegelian subject has no interests of his own that survive the state's demand.
6. The same mechanism operates over the long run: wars are how the world spirit tests peoples and carries forward the one whose principle is higher, so the victorious nation is by definition the one that was in the right.
7. The chapter concludes that anyone who accepts these premises must abandon both international law and the idea that states owe one another anything beyond what they choose to concede — that realism enters philosophy with Hegel.
8. It adds that Hegel was writing as Prussia's official philosopher, and that these passages were calibrated to flatter the restored authorities in Berlin.

I would like a careful audit of this condensation. Specifically:

- Sort the eight claims into what a Hegel scholar would accept as a fair reading of the published paragraphs on external sovereignty and war, what is a defensible simplification that nonetheless drops something load-bearing, and what is contrary to Hegel's own argument. Please say what is dropped in each simplification, not merely that something is.
- Claim 4 is the one I trust least. My recollection is that in the very passage where the comparison to the wind and the sea appears, Hegel notes the objection that all this is a merely philosophical idea, a justification of providence, and that actual wars still require a different justification, which he then defers to a later discussion — where the resolution seems to be that disputes between sovereign states depend on particular wills, with force as the ultimate recourse. Is that recollection right? And if it is, does the chapter's slide from ethical significance to desirability survive it, or does Hegel's hedge block the slide? What does he think justifies an actual war, if anything does, and is the question even well-formed within his system?
- Claims 3 and 6 seem to me to run together two different bodies of work: the account of external sovereignty in the philosophy of right, and the succession of world-historical peoples in the philosophy of history. Does the absence of a praetor above states entail that the winner was right? Treat the claim that world history is a court of judgment as carefully as you can: is it a thesis about the correctness of outcomes, a thesis about where justification is ultimately located, or something retrospective and philosophical that makes no claim at all about particular wars? Does claim 7 follow on any of these readings?
- On claim 5: Hegel elsewhere insists that the modern state is the actuality of concrete freedom and that subjective freedom, private right, and the family are preserved as moments within it, not cancelled. How does that square with the demand for sacrifice? I take it he thinks a particular distortion arises when the state is construed as a mere civil society whose end is the security of life and property — please explain what that distortion is and whether it supports or undercuts the chapter's instrumentalist gloss.
- On claim 8: is the biographical framing accurate, or is it a piece of an older polemic that later scholarship has largely dismantled? I would like to know what is actually known about the composition and publication circumstances of the philosophy of right and the reception of its remarks on war, and how much confidence that warrants.
- One methodological worry. I have been told that the edition of the philosophy of right that most readers use carries an apparatus of additions compiled after Hegel's death from lecture material and student notes, and that a good deal of the most frequently quoted material about war comes from that apparatus rather than from what Hegel himself published in 1821. Is that correct, and does it matter for the audit? In particular, is the wind-and-sea comparison part of Hegel's published text or part of the compiled additions — and if the distinction is real, which of my eight claims lean on which layer?

I should add that I came to the chapter already inclined to reject the warmonger reading of Hegel, so please flag anywhere your correction of these claims, or my own condensation of them, is being shaped by that inclination rather than by the text. No bibliography and no citations are needed; where you are unsure of Hegel's exact wording, paraphrase rather than reproduce quotation, and tell me explicitly where your confidence drops off.
