I run the special-risks pricing team at a mid-sized specialist underwriter: four analysts, two of them actuarially trained and two with marine and aviation operational backgrounds but no training in probability. We insure first-of-a-kind exposures — third-party liability for autonomous coastal cargo vessels, contamination liability for cultivated-protein production plants, collision and servicing liability for small satellites — where there is no usable loss history and often no analogue class with one either. We produce roughly twenty-five such files a year; the smallest carries about £1.5m of premium, and a badly misjudged estimate on the largest would exceed our free surplus.

Our underwriting committee's form has three fields: a single-number best estimate of the probability of a loss exceeding our retention within twelve months; a confidence label (high / medium / low, defined in our handbook only as reflecting the reliability of the underlying information); and a free-text box of 400 characters. Two standing practices make the instrument worse than it looks. First, the committee reads the number and largely ignores the label, so a thinly supported 0.30 and a well-evidenced 0.30 are treated identically at the pricing stage. Second, the numeric field cannot be left blank and must contain the single best estimate; files arriving without one go to the CFO, who has never declined to enter something. Our present workaround — writing a range such as 0.08–0.40 into the free-text box — is truncated to its midpoint by the reporting system before it reaches the committee.

I read Keynes's Treatise on Probability as an undergraduate, and what stayed with me is the distinction between the probability of a conclusion and the weight of the argument supporting it, along with his claims that not every probability is numerical and that some probabilities cannot be compared with one another at all. I want those ideas put to work, not summarized.

Write the decision procedure my team should follow in completing a file. It must be runnable in about ninety minutes by an analyst with no probability training, since our two domain specialists will use it without me. Give me: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure terminates in a number and the points at which it terminates in something else — and, for the latter, what that filing actually looks like given a form that demands a figure and a CFO who will supply one; the points at which the question ceases to be evidential and must be handed to a person because it concerns risk appetite or which harms we are prepared to underwrite; and an explicit flag on every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure. Where a step admits two defensible treatments — how to weigh unanimous but unanchored expert testimony, whether fragmentation across mutually contradictory sources raises or lowers weight, what convention to adopt when nothing discriminates among scenarios — commit to one and justify it rather than laying out the alternatives for me.

Then run the procedure on three files. (i) Autonomous coastal cargo vessel liability, where the only quantitative material is two manufacturers' internal simulations that differ by an order of magnitude, plus a draft safety standard from a flag-state regulator. (ii) Cultivated-protein contamination liability, where we hold thirty years of adjacent data on microbial contamination in conventional food plants and are being asked about an organism with materially different growth characteristics. (iii) Satellite servicing collision liability, where five independent consultants, none of them possessing any operational data, each describe a serious collision within the decade as very unlikely. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like; say which, and why the procedure forces it.

Close with what the procedure cannot do: which of Keynes's commitments survive translation into a two-page worksheet, which I have had to bend or abandon, and where a committee determined to see a number will simply route around the whole thing.
