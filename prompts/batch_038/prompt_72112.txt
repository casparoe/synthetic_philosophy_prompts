I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a national newspaper, and this autumn I have been asked to give the opening talk in a city library's adult evening series: forty-five minutes, ninety people, no slides, one blackboard. The advertised title is Was farming a mistake? — and the booking list tells me the room will already know the popular answer. They have read the widely reprinted essay arguing that agriculture was the worst mistake in the history of the human race; they have heard of the original affluent society and the fifteen-hour foraging week. So my difficulty is the reverse of the usual one. They will arrive converted, and I need them to leave holding three harder things instead: that a pattern in skeletal indicators across a population and a verdict about how good individual lives went are not the same claim; that affluence in this literature can be defined either by producing much or by wanting little, which quietly reopens the question of whose preferences get to count as the baseline; and that there was no moment at which anyone chose, because rising density removes the foraging option precisely when it would be needed.

Plan the talk; do not deliver it. Tell me what has to be in place before the comparison means anything at all, and the order in which those pieces must go in, with what depends on what and what would collapse if inverted. Tell me which single sequence — one valley, one crop, one set of cemeteries — should carry the whole argument, what that case must contain and what it must conspicuously lack, and why the Kalahari time-budgets cannot be the carrier given that the fifteen-hour figure turns on how one counts food processing. Map the misconceptions the room will bring or invent, including ones I have not listed, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than rebutted from the podium at the end. Tell me whether the word mistake should still be on the board in the final five minutes, and if so in what sense it survives. Give me one question I can put to the audience that would distinguish genuine uptake from the substitution of a new slogan for an old one. And tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level — domestication genetics, the Holocene climate argument, the tally of independent centres — and announce as deliberate omission rather than silently omit.
