I don't have any philosophy training at all, so please bear with me — I've been reading on my own for a couple of years and I've hit on something that I think actually helps with a work problem I have.

I run listing review for a mid-size online marketplace where people sell secondhand art: paintings, prints, sculpture, photographs, and design furniture. Around 400 new listings a day go through a queue that five reviewers work, and none of them have an art history background, including me. Their job is to decide what the title and description are allowed to say about who made the object and how "original" it is. Right now they're making it up as they go and its a mess — two reviewers will look at the same listing and reach opposite conclusions, and we lose the appeal about half the time.

Going through a year of dispute records, I noticed our buyers are actually complaining about three completely different things and we treat them all as one thing called "authenticity":

1. it wasn't made by the person named
2. it wasn't made by the person at the time — it's a later instance of something: a cast made decades after the sculptor died, a print pulled from the original plate after the artist died, a photo printed by the estate from the old negative, a chair reissued by the manufacturer under licence. The object is arguably real and the buyer still feels sold a fake.
3. the seller made up a story about where it came from, and the object itself might be fine.

Only the third one is about the seller being a liar. The first two are about the object, and they're seperate questions from each other, which I think is why our reviewers keep tying themselves in knots. An object can be completely genuine and still be a bad listing, and an object can be a later instance and still be a fine listing if the words are exactly right.

Somewhere in all this I picked up a copy of Nelson Goodman's *Languages of Art* and got stuck on his distinction between autographic and allographic art — roughly, that some art forms can be forged and some can't, and that what decides it is whether there's a notation that fixes which instances of the work count as instances. Painting has no such notation so any indistinguishable copy is a forgery; music does, so a correct performance isn't a forgery of anything. When I first read it I couldn't see what it had to do with me. Then it clicked: basically every fight on my platform is a fight about which side of that line an object is on, and the sellers always say "it's the same work" while the buyers always say "it's not the same object," and Goodman's line says both of them might be right, depending on the category. I also have a vague memory of an argument that a work of art is a kind of human performance or achievement, and that's why an indistinguishable copy is a different thing even if you can't tell them apart — I think that came from a book about forgery, but I couldn't tell you who said it. If that's a real position and it earns its keep here, use it; if it doesn't, say so.

So: write me a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run, in plain English, grounded in whatever the philosophy of forgery and originality really says rather than in trade folklore.

Constraints, because they matter:

- 15 minutes per listing. Photos and whatever the seller wrote. They cannot handle the object, they cannot take it to a lab, they cannot make phone calls.
- No conservator on staff. We have one on retainer who answers email within three days, but we only pay her to look at things above $10,000.
- Anything over $2,500 needs a second reviewer to sign off. Anything over $15,000 comes to me.
- If a buyer chargebacks or sues, the exact wording of the listing is what gets read out loud. So the procedure's job is picking words we can defend, not making the seller happy.
- One of our top ten sellers does most of the 19th-century bronze stuff and has already threatened to pull about 200 listings if we start changing his titles. I'd like the procedure to be firm enough that I can point at it instead of arguing.

Please write it in these sections, in this order:

1. **What the three complaints are really about** (~200 words) — take my breakdown above and either sharpen it or tell me it's wrong and give me the right one.
2. **The wording ladder** (~400 words) — the tiers of phrase a listing is allowed to use, from strongest ("by their own hand, first and only instance") down to weakest, with a plain-English test for each rung and the specific words that are banned outright. I keep seeing catalogue phrases like "attributed to," "studio of," "circle of," "follower of," "manner of," and "after," and I roughly know they're a ladder from most confident to least confident, but nobody has ever explained the rungs to me. Either use that vocabulary or build me a better one, but make it something a reviewer can apply from photos alone.
3. **The questions, in order** (~500 words) — numbered, what to ask first and what to ask next, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, and where each answer sends you. Include the step where the reviewer has to decide whether there even is a notation in play, because that's Goodman's whole point and I can't see how to say it without the word "notation."
4. **Stop and hand it to a human** (~300 words) — where the procedure runs out, which questions are about value rather than fact, and what exactly triggers escalation to a second reviewer or to me.
5. **Three listings, run through it** (~200 words each) — show the actual output, meaning the final allowed wording plus the two sentences the reviewer writes to justify it. Use these: (a) a small bronze of a racehorse with a foundry stamp on the underside, seller says it was cast in the 1950s from the artist's original plaster, artist died in the 1890s, title currently says it's an original bronze by the artist, seller is the guy who threatened to pull his listings; (b) a black-and-white photograph, negative from the 1930s, printed in the late 1990s by the photographer's widow for the estate, no edition number, no signature; (c) a signed oil painting where nobody doubts the maker, but the conservator's report the seller supplied says most of the surface is later inpainting and the stretcher and frame are replacements. At least one of these should come out in a way I'm not going to like — if all three flatter me, redo one.
6. **Where Goodman breaks** (~300 words) — the categories on my platform where the autographic/allographic line doesn't do the work: furniture reissues, prints pulled from the original plate, casts, photographs, and anything digital. Tell me which of these the distinction genuinely handles and which it just papers over, and what to do instead in the ones it fails.
7. **What this procedure can't do** (~150 words) — be blunt about where it will misfire, what it costs us in wrongly rejected listings, and what I'd have to change in the business for it to work properly.
