I'm on the advisory panel for a small regional science museum, and at our last meeting two members got into a genuinely heated argument about one line of wall text for the new climate exhibit. I was taking notes, so now I have to write up what was actually said, and I'd like a second opinion before I do.

The exhibit has a timeline running from the end of the last ice age to the present, and the designers wanted a single marker on it for the start of the Anthropocene. Rosa is an environmental historian; Ben is a Quaternary geologist who works on lake cores. Here's the exchange as near as I can reconstruct it, numbered so you can take it move by move:

(1) Rosa said put 1610. Ice cores record a shallow dip in atmospheric CO2 around then, and the best explanation for it is the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas after 1492 and the forests that grew back on land taken out of cultivation. Dating it there names the actual cause — colonization and the Columbian Exchange — rather than implying that a generic "humanity" did this.

(2) Ben said you can't build a geological boundary out of that. An epoch has to begin at a point you can physically locate in one reference section and then match up everywhere else, which is why the working group that spent fifteen years on the problem proposed a mid-20th-century start tied to fallout radionuclides, with a varved lake in Ontario as the reference site. His objection to 1610: the CO2 feature is a gradual wiggle, not a horizon. You can't drive a spike through a slope, and it's small enough that reasonable people can argue about which year the minimum actually falls in.

(3) Rosa said his marker is worse, not better. Plutonium comes from weapons tests, so the official birthday of the human epoch gets set by a handful of militaries in a decade when most people on Earth were still farming with hand tools. And fallout isn't instantaneous either — deposition varied by hemisphere, latitude and rainfall — so he has exactly the diachrony problem he just accused her of.

(4) Ben conceded that's partly fair, but said the bomb signal is sharp, unmistakable and shows up in nearly every archive you can think of — corals, tree rings, ice, varves, cave deposits, even human teeth — so it correlates globally even if it isn't perfectly simultaneous. He also said a boundary is a bookkeeping device: putting it in the mid-20th century isn't a claim that humans started changing the planet then, any more than the boundary at the end of the Cretaceous is a claim that the impact happened at the exact moment the iridium layer settled.

(5) Rosa said that gives her the point. If the date is a bookkeeping convention, then choosing among the candidate conventions isn't dictated by the rocks alone, so it's legitimate for other things to weigh — what the label will teach a fourteen-year-old, whose deaths the story skips over. Ben said no: among markers that meet the criteria you take the sharpest and most widely correlatable one, and if several tie you break the tie by convention, but you don't get to pick the one that flatters your politics.

(6) Rosa then said he should explain what happened when they actually voted. The stratigraphic committees turned the whole proposal down in 2024, largely on the grounds that the proposed start was far too recent and the sedimentary record far too thin, and the international union confirmed the rejection afterwards. Her conclusion: the people who run the formalization process decided the formalization frame didn't work, so the honest wall text is no date at all — describe the Anthropocene as a long, uneven process and leave it at that.

(7) Ben said the vote was about whether to formalize, and that the same bodies said in the same breath that the term would go on being used as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system. He also said "no date" isn't neutral at all: it's a substantive claim that the change was gradual, which is the one thing most of the evidence argues against. Then he conceded something, which surprised me — he said the mid-century marker sits downstream of the causes, since nitrogen fixation, extinction rates, plastics and concrete were all well underway before it, so the sharpest signal isn't the most meaningful one and he doesn't have an answer to that.

(8) Rosa said that's exactly why the museum should anchor the label to the local core already on display downstairs, which shows an ash layer from the old power station and then the fallout spike a few centimetres above it, captioned so visitors see that marker and meaning come apart. Ben said that teaches them precisely the wrong idea about what a reference section is, since a local core can't be a global one.

They never resolved it and the label is still blank. Can you go through this and tell me who's right at each of the eight moves — not just who wins overall — and point out any place where one of them makes a good argument the other never actually answers? Then give me a verdict on who was more right overall and what both of them are missing. Please be explicit about how confident you are in each individual verdict, roughly on a scale like "this is a 90% call" versus "this is basically a coin flip," and single out the one point in the whole exchange you're least sure about. And if you think I've written one of them up in a way that makes their position sound weaker than it probably was in the room, flag that too — I was taking notes, not transcribing.
