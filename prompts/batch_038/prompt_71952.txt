so I've been doing the numbers at a mid-size housing nonprofit for about four years now — mostly "will this rehab come in on schedule and on budget" estimates that end up in front of the board — and I came at it sideways from the forecasting-tournament crowd. I've slowly accreted a personal methodology, and now that I'm writing it down for a new analyst starting in the fall I've discovered I can't actually tell whether the package coheres. Here it is:

(1) start outside: for any estimate, find a reference class of comparable past projects, take the base rate, and let the inside story only nudge you off it. (2) the class has to be picked by actually looking at the specifics of the project in front of you — what makes it like these and unlike those — so I judge similarity case by case rather than off a fixed taxonomy. (3) if my inside story ends with "this one's an exception to its base rate," I treat that as a red flag about me rather than as information; the default is the base rate and overriding it needs a really good reason. (4) but base-rate neglect isn't automatically a fallacy — when the case-specific detail is genuinely diagnostic, discarding it in the name of objectivity is just as much an error as ignoring the base rate. (5) epistemic modesty: my credences should sit fairly near the middle unless I have strong evidence, and I distrust my own feeling of confidence as a matter of policy. (6) calibration is the virtue I care about most — the 70%s should come true 70% of the time. (7) for a given question on a given body of evidence there's one right credence, so two people who've seen the same evidence and are both being rational can't end up disagreeing. One practice I've added on top, which may or may not matter: every quarter I make myself argue, in writing, that the project I'm most attached to belongs in a *worse* class than I originally put it in, and I only let myself move the estimate if that argument produces something concrete.

A concrete case so you can see how it plays out. Two years back we had a 12-unit rehab; across our own ~40 comparable projects maybe 25% had finished inside the original schedule, but we already had a general contractor under a fixed-price contract, which the team said made this one different. I went with 50%. It finished three weeks early and about $11k under. I've been telling that story internally as a win for (4) rather than as luck.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'd like an audit rather than a tutorial: rank the genuine tensions by severity, say for each one what has to give and what I lose by giving it up, flag the ones that dissolve once you state them more carefully, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I dont have a horse in this race — some of these I've held longer than others but nothing here is load-bearing for me personally, so don't try to guess which one I'd prefer to keep.
