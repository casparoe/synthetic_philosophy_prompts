**Question:** State the two choice problems Allais used to challenge expected utility theory, indicate the pattern of choices most subjects exhibit, and identify precisely which axiom of the theory that pattern violates, showing the violation by exhibiting the relevant structure of the prospects. Conclude by explaining why prospect theory, unlike expected utility theory, can accommodate the pattern.

**Candidate's answer:**

Allais's challenge consists of two paired choices. In the first pair, one prospect pays one million dollars with certainty, while the other pays five million with probability 0.10, one million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the second pair, one prospect pays one million with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89, while the other pays five million with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90. Most subjects take the certain million in the first pair but the ten per cent chance of five million in the second.

The pattern violates the independence axiom, which Savage formulated informally as the sure-thing principle, and the violation is an instance of the common consequence effect. To see it, note that the certain million can be written as a 0.89/0.11 mixture of one million with one million, while its rival is the same 0.89/0.11 mixture with the one million component replaced by a gamble on five million and nothing. The second pair is obtained by replacing that shared 0.89 component with nothing and leaving everything else intact. Since independence requires that substituting a common component in this way cannot reverse a preference, the two choices cannot both be rational on the theory's terms.

What drives the behaviour is risk aversion. Utility for money is concave, so the first million is worth far more than each of the next four, and a person who feels that strongly should take it for certain. Expected utility theory has no room for this, because it weights each outcome by its objective probability and therefore treats the desirability of a prospect as a matter of the weighted average alone; that is why the theory delivers these subjects as irrational. Prospect theory repairs the omission by evaluating outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point and by making the value function concave in the domain of gains, which supplies exactly the aversion to risk that the subjects display in both pairs.
