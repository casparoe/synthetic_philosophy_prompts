I am to deliver the eulogy at the memorial service for my doctoral supervisor, Professor Miriam Doerr, a philosopher of language who died last month at eighty-three. The service is in the chapel of the college where she taught for forty years; there will be perhaps two hundred people, roughly a third of them philosophers — colleagues, former students, three or four people she argued with in print for decades — and the rest family, neighbours, and the members of the village choir in which she sang alto for twenty-five years. I was her last doctoral student and I now teach the same subject, so the room knows that I could have given them a technical performance and will notice that I have not.

Her work was on Wittgenstein's argument against the possibility of a private language, and she held it not as a thesis but as a discipline: that there is no such thing as an inner life reportable in principle only by its owner, and that a person is not concealed behind her conduct but displayed in it. For fifty years she kept a set of notebooks in a notation of her own devising that no one has ever read; her will directs that they be deposited in the college archive unopened. Her brother Douglas, who survives her and is not a philosopher, has spent the week pressing me to open one and read a page aloud at the service, on the grounds that it is the only way the room will hear anything that was genuinely hers. I have decided not to, and the eulogy must make that refusal intelligible to him without letting him feel that I am keeping her from the people who loved her.

The governing constraint is this. She regarded the ordinary eulogy as bad philosophy in a good suit: it claims privileged access to a dead person's feelings and then presents what it finds there as the person herself. So the speech is to be written under a rule I have set myself — not one sentence may assert what she felt, believed, hoped, feared, regretted, or took pride in, as a report delivered from inside. Everything must be cashed out in what she did, what she said, how she treated people, and what those around her were able to make of her. Her own recorded words are permitted, since those are public too; her interior is not.

The difficulty I want solved rather than glossed is that this rule sounds as though it must produce something cold, and most of the room has come to hear that she loved them. Douglas spent much of his life convinced his sister was remote, and a eulogy that declines to speak of her inner life will sound to him like confirmation. Her daughter Anneke has asked separately that her mother be described accurately rather than admiringly, and has also said that she does not want to sit through an inventory. The speech has to show — and to get this exactly right, philosophically — that refusing to relocate a person inside her skull is not a sceptical economy; that the public criteria are not poor evidence for something better hidden behind them; that our being able to see how she went on was not a substitute for knowing her but the whole of what knowing her consisted in. It must make that available to the philosophers as an argument being run, and to everyone else as warmth, with no sense that apparatus is on display.

Particulars you may use, and should develop rather than merely list: she answered every letter from strangers who wrote to her about her papers, including one from a retired postal worker who corresponded with her for eleven years; her form of approval, at home and in supervision alike, was to ask for the thing again, slower; in her seventy-second year she offered to teach the notebook notation to a graduate student, who declined for want of time, and she never offered again and left no key; she sang the lowest part in a choir, the line you cannot hear separately. Invent further particulars of the same kind if you need them; do not generalize instead.

She also objected on principle to the quotation of Wittgenstein at ceremonies, holding it to be a way of borrowing authority, so the speech must quote him nowhere and cite no section numbers, and must avoid the vocabulary of the seminar entirely. One sentence in the whole eulogy may be such that the philosophers hear her position stated and everyone else hears only a sentence about her; no more than one.

Write the eulogy itself, about nine hundred words, in my voice, to be read aloud by someone who will not be entirely steady. Give me the text and nothing else — no title, no explanation of your choices, no stage directions beyond an occasional marked pause.
