You are, for this exercise, a philosopher who works at the intersection of virtue ethics and personality psychology and who has designed decision instruments for real institutions — not a commentator summarizing a literature. I am going to ask you for a piece of professional work product, and I want you to treat the diagnostic thinking as the substance of the task rather than as throat-clearing. Please think carefully and at length before you write the instrument itself; do not begin with the instrument.

I am a professor of philosophy at a medical school, and I chair our Panel on Professional Conduct and Remediation. The panel hears roughly eighteen cases a year. Its standing membership is two clinicians, one administrator, one faculty member from outside medicine, and me. A hearing lasts forty-five minutes; the written determination is one page and is signed by the dean, who accepts our findings unless they are visibly unsound. The stakes are remediation plans, conditions on progression, probation, and — three times in eleven years — a recommendation that a trainee be withdrawn from the program.

Our current practice is incoherent, and the incoherence has a shape I can describe exactly. When a trainee we do not know behaves badly, we reach for character vocabulary — integrity, candour, compassion, professionalism — and treat a single observed episode as sufficient warrant for a global trait attribution. When a colleague we like behaves badly, we reach for the situation — exhaustion, hierarchy, bereavement, a corrosive service culture — and treat that same single episode as evidence of nothing about the person at all. We are, that is, situationists for our friends and Aristotelians for everyone else, and we have never had to say which we are. I want the panel to stop doing both, or at least to do both under rules it can articulate.

So: I want the situationist critique of character turned into something my panel can actually run. I mean the critique that grew out of the person-situation debate — the obedience studies, the bystander work, the seminary students in a hurry, the found dime and the willingness to post a stranger's letter — together with the weak cross-situational consistency those studies are taken to reveal, the resulting skepticism about global traits as ordinarily conceived, and the responses to that skepticism: local rather than global traits, traits as conditional dispositions with identifiable triggers, the claim that practical wisdom is precisely a sensitivity to situational variation, and the claim that character is held up by social scaffolding and sags when the scaffolding is removed. You know this literature; I do not want it surveyed. I want it translated, and translation is where the difficulty lies.

**Part 0 — Diagnostic (roughly 500 words).** Before drafting anything, tell me: (i) which three or four distinctions from this literature must survive the simplification, because without them the instrument collapses into either trait-mongering or excuse-mongering; (ii) which technical apparatus can be dropped without loss for an audience of clinicians, who will read this standing up between cases; (iii) where the popular formulation of the critique — that psychology has simply disproved character — overstates the case, and how the instrument must be built so that it does not inherit that overstatement; and (iv) two or three candidate architectures for the procedure, with your reasons for the one you chose. If a step you draft later turns out to do no work, cut it and say so here.

**Part 1 — The instrument.** Ordered steps, each stating the question asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, what evidence is admissible at that step, and what the step outputs. Include explicit stopping rules: where the procedure terminates in a finding, where it terminates in no finding, and where it must hand the question to the panel as a matter of judgment rather than fact. Three features are non-negotiable.

- A *no-alibi-without-mechanism* rule: a situational explanation may be entered into a determination only if the panel can name the specific situational variable it holds responsible and state what would change were that variable absent. Vague appeal to pressure or circumstances is not a mechanism.
- A *sampling* step: who is testifying, and in which situations have they actually seen this person? Character references from people who know the subject in one role only must be marked as what they are.
- A standing separation of three things the panel habitually conflates: what the evidence supports about the subject's dispositions, what the subject is answerable for, and what the institution should do next. Every determination must close with a forward-looking recommendation about how to structure the situations this person will be in — not only a verdict about the person.

At each step, mark whether it is mechanical or a judgment call wearing a procedural costume. Where it is judgment, say what the judgment tracks and what would cause two competent panelists to diverge.

**Part 2 — Three applications (about 400 words each).** Run the instrument on these, showing which step decides the case and sketching the one-page determination.

*A.* A final-year student was recorded turning to a neighbor's paper three times during one anatomy examination, eleven days after her brother died. Two faculty who have taught her since first year describe her as honest. The referral asks for a character finding: that she lacks the integrity required of a physician.

*B.* A resident on a night shift did not object when an attending, in front of the team, gave a dose she believed to be wrong. She had twice that month raised concerns to the same attending and been told to stop slowing the team down. The patient was unharmed. The program director wants a recorded finding about her moral courage, on the ground that the institution needs to signal that silence is itself a failure.

*C.* A consultant surgeon of twenty-two years' standing, described as decent by everyone who writes to us. Over nine months, three episodes with nothing obvious in common: in a recorded consent conversation, when a patient asked something he had already answered, he remarked in front of the registrar that this was why patients should not read things on the internet; at a six in the morning handover he omitted a documented allergy and, when the receiving team caught it, said they would have found out anyway; a scrub nurse complains that he twice addressed her by the name of a nurse who left three years ago and, when corrected, said it hardly mattered. For each he offers a situational account — exhaustion, a hostile list, grief for the colleague who left. The referral is for three separate minor lapses.

At least one of these must come out against my own expectations, and where it does I want you to say so plainly instead of softening it.

**Part 3 — Limits and failure modes (roughly 300 words).** Where the instrument will systematically err, and in which direction; how it should handle evidence generated inside a simulated or examination station, which is itself an atypical situation; how it should handle a subject whose circumstances are chronically bad, so that situational explanation threatens to explain everything and excuse nothing; and what it cannot do at all. Note also the reflexivity problem: the hearing is itself a high-surveillance, high-power situation, and I want to know whether the instrument should be suspicious of the behavior it produces in the room.

Two constraints on form. Plain language throughout, with any technical term defined once at first use; the instrument proper should be printable on two pages and usable by someone with no philosophy training. And no bibliography, no reference list, no citations — where a study genuinely matters to a step, describe it in a clause and move on. Total length around 2,800 words.
