I'm a junior, and I'm the secretary of my school's philosophy club. Our faculty advisor was out sick last Thursday, so the meeting was run by two seniors, Maya and Jonah, and the demonstration we had planned turned into a forty-minute argument that the rest of us were not equipped to settle. I recorded most of it and have written it up below as fairly as I can. I'd like you to referee it.

The demonstration was a money pump. We put three snacks on a table — a chocolate bar, a bag of sour candy, and a bag of chips — and our treasurer offered swaps for a quarter apiece. Devin volunteered. He said he preferred chips to chocolate, chocolate to sour candy, and sour candy to chips. Starting with the sour candy, he paid 25 cents to swap to the chocolate, 25 cents to swap to the chips, and 25 cents to swap back to the sour candy. Then he went around the cycle a second time for the fun of it, and finished exactly where he began, $1.50 poorer.

Here is the argument that followed, as I recorded it:

(1) **Maya:** A preference cycle like Devin's is irrational, and the demonstration is the reason. If your preferences loop, someone can charge you to go around the loop, and you will pay to end up back where you started. Being exploitable in that way is a sure loss, and a preference ordering that guarantees a sure loss can't be rational.

(2) **Jonah:** That's not an argument about preferences, it's an argument about a sequence of choices — a diachronic one. It only goes through if Devin actually makes all three trades. But each trade, taken by itself, was fine: at each moment he paid a quarter for something he genuinely preferred to what he was holding. You can't condemn the first trade by describing a situation he isn't in yet.

(3) **Maya:** He can see it coming, though. Once he knows how the sequence runs, his options at the first swap aren't "chocolate for a quarter" versus "keep the candy." They're "start down a path that ends with me $1.50 poorer" versus "decline." Reason backward from the last swap and the sophisticated agent refuses the first one. Foresight dissolves the pump, and that's my point: the cycle cost him real money, and the cost traces back to the cycle.

(4) **Jonah:** "Sophisticated choice" is just a label for the premise you need. Backward induction assumes Devin's later trades are already fixed — fixed to be the ones the treasurer is counting on. They aren't; they're his choices to make when he gets there. And if you say he should bind himself in advance, then note that binding costs something too. An intransitive agent who pays for self-control ends up no poorer than a transitive agent who never needed it. So the cycle's cost is just the price of an option to change your mind, and paying for an option isn't irrational.

(5) **Maya:** You're also leaving out resolute choice — adopting a plan at the start and holding to it, instead of re-deciding at every node. You'd say that's irrational because at the moment of the swap he prefers the swap. But that present preference is precisely what generates the loss, so you're using the thing in question to dismiss the cure. And your position has a further cost: whether Devin's preferences are rational now depends on whether a treasurer with quarters happens to be nearby and on whether self-binding happens to be cheap. Rationality shouldn't hinge on facts like that. If exploitability comes off the table here, it comes off the table generally — including for someone who takes a certain $50 over a gamble worth a bit more, and who could likewise be walked around a sequence of individually sensible trades into a worse place.

(6) **Jonah:** I'll accept that consequence, more or less. Exploitability is a property of an agent in an environment, not of a ranking in isolation. But notice what your "sure loss" claim is doing: it measures Devin against a version of himself who declines everything, evaluated from the starting node of the whole tree. Evaluating from the starting node is exactly the planning assumption in dispute, so the demonstration can't be offered as a proof that his ranking is defective without assuming that. Which makes me suspect we aren't disagreeing about Devin at all. We're disagreeing about what the word "irrational" attaches to — a preference ordering, or a course of choices.

(7) **Maya:** No. If this were only about words, you'd be entitled to say that an agent can be fully rational while reliably paying to return to her starting point, and conceding that is not a verbal concession. And the circularity charge fails: to call $1.50 a loss I only need the fact that Devin prefers keeping his money to losing it, plus that he can predict his own behavior. His cycle is about snacks. Nothing in my argument assumes his preferences over snacks are transitive.
