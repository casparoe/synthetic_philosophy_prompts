Write a dialogue of roughly 900 words between two equally accomplished philosophers arguing in the idiom of classical Indian epistemology. One is a Naiyāyika, who maintains that there are four pramāṇas: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda). The other is a Buddhist epistemologist in the tradition of Dignāga, who maintains that there are only two, on the ground that there are only two kinds of knowable object — the bare particular (svalakṣaṇa), grasped by non-conceptual perception, and the general characteristic (sāmānyalakṣaṇa), grasped by conceptual inference — so that every candidate pramāṇa must be matched to one of these or dissolved.

Set the whole dispute on a single case that both are trying to explain in their own terms: a person has been told by a forester that a gavaya is like a cow; some time later, walking in the forest, they see an unfamiliar animal and come to know something. Have the Buddhist argue that whatever is gained there is either perceptual or inferential — that upamāna is testimony plus inference in disguise, and that testimony itself is only inference, since an utterance functions as a sign from which one reasons to the speaker's reliability and thence to the state of affairs. Have the Naiyāyika respond with the counter-question that actually matters: what precisely is the cognition produced, and could this person have reasoned their way to it? They should press that the person is unable to state any universal premise, that they cannot exclude the possibility of a merely cow-like animal that is not a gavaya, and that both the apprehension of the similarity and the fixing of the forester's word are novel in a way neither accepted pramāṇa delivers.

Make the clash of criteria explicit rather than buried: the Buddhist individuates pramāṇas by their objects, the Naiyāyika by the distinctness of the cognition produced. Each should turn that criterion against the other, and each should concede at least one substantive point — for instance, whether a means of knowledge can count as distinct merely because the knower cannot reconstruct their own reasoning, or whether a pramāṇa whose object is not independently identifiable is really a pramāṇa at all. End without a verdict and without any closing summary.

Do not quote or cite particular sūtras, commentaries, or secondary literature; keep the exchange to arguments and cases.
