I am in the second draft of a novel whose central character is a parish priest in a mid-sized city that has stopped being religious without having become anything else in the interim. I have arrived at the point, familiar to most novelists, where the book threatens to harden into a brief for a thesis — and the thesis is one I cannot state without wincing, since it belongs to a dispute that sociologists conduct and novelists ought to keep out of. Roughly: does religion make people better? Before I let my manuscript settle that question, I want to see how another novel handled it, and I want to be told where the handling succeeds and where it cheats.

The book is Graham Greene's *The Power and the Glory*: the fugitive, unrepentant priest in Tabasco during the suppression of the Church; the lieutenant who is personally incorruptible and shoots hostages from villages that failed to denounce him; the mestizo who lures the priest back across the border with a summons to hear a dying man's confession; Padre José, married off by the state and given a pension; the final chapter, in which a second priest arrives at night and the woman who has been reading lives of the saints to her children agrees to shelter him, having already added the executed man to her catalogue. Assume I know the novel well. I want no summary and no verbatim quotation — I would be unable to check it, and quotation is not what criticism is for.

So that the reading has something definite to bite on, here is a distinction I have been using in my own drafts. Call someone a *carrier* of moral goods when what flows through him depends on his office rather than on his character, like a courier whose package survives his vices; call him a *source* when the goods are generated by the character itself, so that a foul spring gives foul water. Greene's priest is the limiting case of the carrier theory: cowardly, drunk, the father of a child he loves and cannot properly acknowledge, and yet the sacraments he administers are held to be valid all the same. The mestizo is, correspondingly, a free rider on precisely that feature — the priest answers the summons because the office obliges him, and it is the reliability of the obligation that makes him killable.

Against that apparatus, please work through the following.

First, my thesis conflates at least three questions, and I want them separated and then applied to the novel: whether religious participation changes behaviour net of the likelihood that already-decent people are the ones who participate; whether religion supplies a trust and coordination mechanism that must be replaced by something if it goes, and whether a secular substitute can hold — the lieutenant's programme is Greene's test of this, and his hostage policy is a tax on exactly the solidarity he claims to serve; and whether religion changes the target of "better" rather than its quantity, tightening obligation toward kin, congregation, and nation instead of widening impartial benevolence toward strangers. Bring in whatever you know of that empirical dispute, but give me the ideas and not the references.

Second: does the novel answer any of these three, and if so which? Is the ending a vindication of the carrier view — the institution reproducing itself through the pious rather than the holy — or a quiet refutation of it? Does Greene commit anywhere to a verdict on whether his priest was a good man, and if he refuses, is the refusal an achievement or an evasion?

Third, and most importantly: where does the reading I have set up break? Is it a category mistake to treat a theology of grace and of dignified failure as evidence bearing on the moral effects of religious practice, and if Greene would say so, is that a real objection or a convenient one?

Finally, tell me which of Greene's structural devices is doing the work — my suspicion is that it is his refusal to give either the priest or the lieutenant an interior vindication, so that the reader must supply the evaluation the text withholds — because I intend to steal it, and I need to know what it actually consists in. I would rather you commit to a reading and be wrong than hedge throughout; where you are least certain, say so plainly.
