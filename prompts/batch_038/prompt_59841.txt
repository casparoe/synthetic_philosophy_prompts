I'm sitting in on a history-of-science seminar this semester and we've spent the last two weeks on the whole simultaneous-invention business. You know the usual lineup: Newton and Leibniz both landing on the calculus, Darwin and Wallace both landing on natural selection, Bell's patent application and Gray's caveat showing up at the patent office on the same day, and the oxygen case where Scheele made the gas first but published later, Priestley made it in 1774 and went to his grave calling it dephlogisticated air and insisting phlogiston was doing fine, while Lavoisier is the one who actually understood what he had. Merton's famous take is that none of this is coincidence — that multiples are the normal case in science and singletons are the interesting exception, and that this shows discovery is broadly inevitable, a function of where the field already is rather than of who happens to be standing there.

What I can't get my head around, and I don't think the seminar has really faced it either, is that whether two bodies of work count as "the same discovery" looks like it gets settled after the fact, by how later science sorts them, not by anything you could have seen at the time. Two made-up cases to make it concrete. Case A: two groups working on different organisms, one framing everything as a specific molecular feedback loop and the other as a network-level property, publish results that in 1998 read as basically unrelated; twenty years later a unifying framework shows they were tracking one and the same phenomenon. Case B: two groups publish nearly word-for-word the same finding about a catalyst in the same year, get held up as a textbook multiple for decades, and then it turns out one group's effect was a trace impurity doing something else entirely and doesn't generalize — so retrospectively they weren't the same discovery at all, they just looked like it. Case B counted as a multiple when the counting could have influenced anything, and Case A didn't. So the statistics Merton is generalizing over seem to be built with hindsight doing the typing, which makes me suspicious of the inevitability conclusion being drawn from them. And the grain looks totally free: individuate finely and everything is a singleton (Leibniz's notation and metaphysics aren't Newton's fluxions; Wallace's biogeography-and-Malthus route isn't Darwin's pigeon-breeding route), individuate coarsely and everything is a multiple.

Can you explain to me what the conceptual issue here actually is and where you think it lands? I guess I want to know what the unit of a discovery even is — the proposition arrived at, the experimental capability acquired, the question the researchers took themselves to be answering, or the causal role the result ends up playing in later work — since each of those sorts my two cases differently and I can't see why one is privileged. Is there any way to fix the grain that isn't just hindsight, or is the individuation of a discovery irreducibly interest-relative? And if its interest-relative, does the multiples evidence survive in any form — does it stop being evidence for inevitability and start being evidence about something else, like the sociology of credit and priority disputes? I can see a pushback too, that I'm being cheap: if you demand the two parties share concepts and framing you've defined away the phenomenon, since obviously people in different places describe things differently, and maybe the right move is to say the grain should be set by the question at issue rather than by the answer given. Tell me whether that rescues the thesis or just relocates the problem, and whether "it had to happen eventually" and "it had to happen by roughly then" are separable claims that the multiples evidence supports to different degrees. I'm not after sources or a reading list, I've got plenty of those — I want the argument actually worked through.
