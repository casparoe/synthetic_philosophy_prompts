Fourty years in a lab (analytical chem, mostly trace metals in water) and now that I'm retired I've been reading philosophy like a man catching up on the news. I'm currently going through one of those big popular histories of ideas — sweeping claims, not a footnote in sight — and it has a chapter on Indian logic that I'd like a second opinion on before I let it reshape my intuitions. Here's the gist, in the book's own phrasing as near as I can reproduce it from memory:

"Two thousand years ago, Indian logicians had already worked out most of the philosophy of science. The Nyaya school held that knowledge reaches us through four channels, which they called pramanas: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Perception is the foundation. It is defined as a cognition produced by the contact of a sense organ with its object, provided it is not mistaken and is definite rather than vague — and because truth is built into the definition, perception simply cannot go wrong, which puts the Naiyayikas firmly on the side of common sense against the sceptics. Inference is the syllogism, only better: where the Greeks use three lines they use five, and one of the five is the demand that you produce a familiar case in which the connection is known to hold, which is what a referee means by 'show me your raw data.' At the center of it all is vyapti, an invariable connection between the sign and what is signified — smoke and fire — and the Naiyayikas held that you establish it by repeated observation coupled with attention to negative cases. That is Mill's method of agreement and difference, arrived at eighteen centuries early, which means they had induction without Hume's problem. Their boldest move is testimony: they counted another person's word as a source of knowledge in its own right, on the grounds that you cannot verify everything yourself and that the statement of a trustworthy person gives you knowledge you could not otherwise get. That is a philosophical justification of scientific authority, of peer review and of citation, long before there were journals to review. And unlike the Greeks, who largely ended up doubting everything, all the schools of Indian philosophy accepted these four channels and merely argued about how to apply them."

Two things bug me and I can't tell whether they're bugging me for decent reasons. One: I spent my whole career trusting instruments that I knew perfectly well could be wrong, so a definition of perception with "not mistaken" written into it reads to me like defining the problem away rather than solving it — though maybe that's a bench habit that doesn't travel to a different intellectual game. Two: the testimony paragraph is the one I'm most inclined to sign, since I've cited other people's numbers all my life for quantities I could never have measured myself. But "the word of a trustworthy person" feels too small a hinge to carry the weight of peer review, where arguably the point is that nobody's trustworthiness gets assumed.

So — how much of that chapter is right? Go through it and sort out what a scholar of Nyaya would nod at, what they'd call a fair simplification for a general audience, and what they'd say is just wrong. Then tell me whether the book's four big conclusions survive the corrections: that they dissolved the problem of induction, that they anticipated Mill, that they supply a justification of scientific authority, and that everybody in Indian philosophy signed up to the same four pramanas. Skip the bibliography and the further-reading, please — I can't check any of it and my library card mostly gets me murder mysteries.

And when you're done, be straight with me about the soft spots in your own audit. Which of your corrections would you drop if someone put a better argument in front of you, what specifically would change your mind on the two claims I'm least sure about, and which of your judgments lean on a contested reading of the tradition rather than on anything settled? If your honest conclusion is that the book is broadly fair and I'm the one nitpicking, say so — I'd much rather be told than flattered.
