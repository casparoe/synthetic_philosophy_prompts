I'm in the second half of a philosophy-of-war seminar, and last week's session on the moral equality of combatants ended with two of my classmates, Dev and Marguerite, arguing in the corridor for the better part of an hour while the rest of us listened. We had read Walzer's defense of the equality of combatants alongside the revisionist literature that denies it, and the seminar was given a single case to argue over: a state launches a war of territorial conquest against its neighbour on the stated pretext of protecting a persecuted minority, and the pretext is fabricated — the leadership knows this, though almost nobody else does. On the aggressor's side is Marek, a nineteen-year-old conscript two months into his service, whose family gets its news entirely from state media, who has been told by his officers that the operation is a humanitarian intervention, and who in the engagement in question does exactly what the rules require: he warns civilians to leave a village before it is shelled, and fires only at identified uniformed combatants. On the defender's side is Ilias, a thirty-eight-year-old reservist and schoolteacher, entirely clear that the war against him is unjust and fighting to stop it; in the same engagement he calls in a strike on a convoy he believes is carrying ammunition, which turns out to be carrying Marek's unit plus a dozen conscripts being rotated out of the line, and the strike is, on any reasonable measure, disproportionate to the advantage he expected. Dev and Marguerite were asked whether Marek and Ilias are moral equals in that engagement, and here is how the exchange went:

(1) Dev: No, and the case shows why. Permissibility tracks liability, and liability tracks the moral status of what you are doing. Marek is contributing, however innocently, to an unjust war of conquest; Ilias is resisting one. The domestic analogy is exact: a security guard and a bank robber who both fire carefully and both warn bystanders are not thereby in the same moral position, and it would be absurd to say the guard's carefulness makes him the robber's equal. What the law of armed conflict says is a separate matter — a useful convention, but conventions are not moral truths.

(2) Marguerite: The analogy is doing far too much work. The robber chose his project; Marek was drafted at nineteen, has no independent access to information, and faces state punishment for refusing. The deeper problem is that the guard-and-robber framing assumes a sovereign standing above both parties who can settle who the robber is. In war there is no such sovereign. Soldiers on both sides occupy the same structural position — acting under coercion, socialization, and radical uncertainty — and the equality of combatants is a recognition of that fact rather than a piece of legal bookkeeping.

(3) Dev: Coercion and ignorance mitigate; they do not reverse. An excused wrongdoer is still a wrongdoer, and Marek is still a threat Ilias may defend himself against. And your structural-symmetry point proves too much: if it were enough that each side sincerely believes its cause is just, no one could ever be criticized for fighting, and the entire jus ad bellum debate would be pointless. If anything, uncertainty should make Marek more careful about whom he shoots, not hand him a licence.

(4) Marguerite: Fine — but follow your own view to its end. If liability depends on the justice of the cause, and Ilias's cause is just, then Ilias also needs his particular strike to serve that cause in a necessary and proportionate way. In this engagement it did not: he got the target wrong and killed more than a dozen people for no gain. On your theory Ilias acted impermissibly, and Marek, who did everything the rules require, acted permissibly or at worst excusably. That is the contingent-pacifist result the revisionists keep running into: almost nobody in almost any actual war can satisfy the in bello conditions, because the information those conditions demand is simply not available to anyone inside the fighting. A theory of the morality of war whose deliverances no participant can apply is not a theory of war; it is a theory written afterwards, by people who were not there.

(5) Dev: That confuses the criterion of rightness with a decision procedure. The fact that Ilias could not know what the convoy carried does not make killing its occupants permissible; it makes his mistake blameless — which is exactly the distinction you refused to apply to Marek back in (2). And the armchair objection is a red herring, because the revisionist view is meant to guide political leaders and the design of the law, not to serve as a field manual for a nineteen-year-old under fire. Notice, though, that you have conceded what matters. You agree that Marek's permissions and Ilias's come apart — you simply think the asymmetry runs the other way in this case. That is not the moral equality of combatants; it is a different inequality.

(6) Marguerite: It is not an asymmetry, it is one standard applied twice, and that is precisely what symmetry means. Your "guide leaders and design law" move is also unstable: if the true morality of fighting is asymmetric while the law, the training, and the soldiers' own self-understanding must all stay symmetric, then the moral theory has become an esoteric doctrine that everyone actually in the practice is expected not to believe. It also lets the leadership off. Under the equality view, a soldier who fights well is not a criminal and the guilt sits where the decision was made — with the people who fabricated the pretext. Under your view, Marek is a potential wrongdoer every time he fires, and the men who sent him there are merely his accomplices in a distributed crime.

I stayed out of it, and I honestly cannot tell who is ahead. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each of (1) through (6), say whether the point lands, lands only in part, or fails, and give your reasons — and then say who is more right overall, and on which issue the gap between them is widest? I would also like to know whether there is something important that both of them are treating as settled and arguing straight past. Two candidates occurred to me while they were talking, though I could not test either: first, Dev keeps arguing about liability while Marguerite keeps arguing about excuses and about the law, and I am not sure those are commensurable frameworks, so part of the deadlock may be that they are answering different questions; second, both of them seem to treat liability as something an agent either has or lacks, rather than as something that comes in degrees with the amount of responsibility the agent bears. Tell me whether either of those is a real problem for the exchange, whether there are further shared presuppositions doing unnoticed work, and what the disagreement would look like once those are put on the table.
