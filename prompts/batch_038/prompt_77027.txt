I am a junior at a public high school and the founder and president of our philosophy club, which meets for forty-five minutes on Thursdays. Our attendance runs between twelve and eighteen students, and the range is wide: a few members have read a fair amount on their own, while most came out of curiosity and have never thought seriously about any of these questions. Next month's session is on Kavka's toxin puzzle — the case of the billionaire who offers you a very large sum on the condition that, at a specified hour tonight, you intend to drink a vile but non-lethal toxin the following afternoon, with the money already sitting in your account before the time for drinking arrives and with no requirement that you actually drink. I expect our discussion to degenerate into people restating their intuitions at one another, so instead of a discussion I want to hand out a sorting instrument: a decision tree that shows each member, from their own answers to concrete questions, which resolution of the puzzle they are already committed to.

Please write that handout for me. It should have the following parts.

1. Set out the logical space before you write a single question. Identify four to six binary commitments that generate it — for example, whether forming an intention is something one can simply do at will, whether one can intend what one confidently expects not to do, whether an intention, once formed, supplies a reason to act that did not exist beforehand, whether one can settle in advance how one will deliberate later, and whether the proper object of assessment is an individual decision or a policy for making decisions. Enumerate the combinations those commitments allow, say which of them are even coherent, and only then locate the actual positions within the space.

2. Give me eight to twelve questions, each built on a concrete situation a member can react to without having read anything about intentions — a New Year's resolution, promising a friend you will drive her to the airport at five in the morning, signing up for something you know you will dread. No node may use vocabulary whose meaning already presupposes where that branch leads, and a member should not be able to guess their destination from the wording. Two distinctions must survive into the questions: the difference between being unable to choose one's intentions at will and being unable to intend what one predicts one will not do, and the difference between changing one's mind later and never having had the intention at all. If you drop other distinctions for the sake of usability, list what you dropped and why the handout still works without it.

3. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, if it has one, plus a single sentence stating the most awkward thing about holding it — the cost it imposes elsewhere. Where you are not confident that a combination has an actual defender, label the cell unnamed and diagnose the emptiness: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Please do not supply an attribution you are unsure of. I have no way to check any of this, and an invented name would make the handout worse than nothing.

4. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say what further question would be needed to tell them apart — even if that question is too subtle for our format.

5. Include a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, and tell me what to do with a member whose answers trace no coherent path. I would rather the handout treat that as information about the puzzle than as a failure to fill in the form.

6. Finally, run the tree on two variants our club came up with, since I want to know whether it is tracking something real or just fitting the original case. In the first, the payment arrives a week after the drinking, so drinking is the only route to the money. In the second, the substance has been replaced with something harmless and pleasant that the member would happily consume anyway, so the only remaining question is whether a reward can get you to intend something you were going to do regardless. For each variant, tell me which questions stop discriminating, which leaves collapse together or disappear, and which members would end up sorted differently than they were in the original.

Keep the whole thing to roughly two pages plus the key, since I have to photocopy it on the school copier and we have only the one meeting. I am not in a position to evaluate technical answers, so anything you include needs to be something you are confident is correct rather than something that sounds plausible to a high school student.
