ok so our grants committee is wedged. Two of us have been arguing about insect welfare for the better part of a year, and we've finally agreed to stop shouting and put something in writing together. I want you to write the document we should have produced.

The two positions, as sharply as I can state them:

Position A, which we've started calling the multiplier. Expected moral weight of a group = probability its members are sentient, times how many of them there are, times how their lives go on average. Concede that the first factor is small for insects — one in ten, one in a thousand, whatever — it doesn't rescue you: there are so many arthropods out there that even a brutal discount leaves them outweighing every vertebrate cause we fund, and plausibly outweighing near-term human stuff too. So the honest response to "we don't know" is not a shrug. It's to treat insect welfare as the biggest line item on the table and start paying for the research and for measuring the interventions that are already happening at scale whether we look or not.

Position B, the anchor. Objects that the first factor isn't a number that survives being multiplied. Probability of sentience isn't a credence about some mind-independent fact; it's an artifact of how you carved up the organism — which species, larva or adult, a mayfly's four days, a mite, a nematode. And there's no single N either, because the population estimate moves by orders of magnitude depending on how you count life stages and what you're willing to sweep up out of the leaf litter. So the product has no determinate value and the error bars are wider than the answer. Worse, the method is self-undermining: run the same arithmetic on springtails and it swamps the insects, run it on nematodes and it swamps the springtails, run it on bacteria and it swamps everything, which looks less like a discovery about the world and more like a broken calculator. B is not saying insects don't matter. B is saying the scale argument can't license a decision, and that a funder acting on it is acting on an illusion of precision — including, most dangerously, the illusion that killing an astronomical number of them could be a humanitarian project.

Here's what I need in the document:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. Not vacuous ones. Real ones about the neurology, about how loose the population numbers are, about what the interventions we already do — insecticide, tillage, outdoor lighting, larval insect farming for chicken and fish feed — are doing at scale, and about the places where each side thinks the other is at least partly right.

2. A crux table. The sub-questions where an answer would actually move one of them. For each: a neutral statement of the question, the concrete case or observation both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded before the result is known. Five cruxes minimum, and two of them have to be these:

- The oracle case. Suppose a machine could settle conclusively whether one single species is sentient — no hedging, no partial credit, no "sentience-adjacent" — but only one species, chosen by the committee, and the verdict can't be extrapolated to anything else by fiat. Which species does A pick, which does B pick, and what does each predict about how the answer changes their own bottom line? I want both picks and both sets of reasons, even if one of them thinks the whole exercise is meaningless.

- The gene drive case. A suppression drive against a malaria mosquito, ready to release. A wants to call it either an enormous welfare win or an enormous welfare loss and wants the number on the page; B says the question is malformed. Show me the most the two of them can jointly say about it, and mark precisely where the joint text has to stop and hand back to the disagreement.

3. A short section, plain prose, on which parts of this fight are just about how to use words. I suspect at least some of it is about whether "probability of sentience" names a quantity at all. Say which parts are verbal, which parts are genuine disagreements that would survive a shared vocabulary, and what that shared vocabulary would look like.

4. One closing paragraph both would actually sign, telling the committee what to do with next year's money. If you decide they can't sign anything, say so, and then give me the narrowest thing they could sign.

Ground rules: no citations, no reading lists, no named papers or real researchers — if you catch yourself reaching for a footnote, cut it and make the argument instead. And don't strawman anybody: B isn't a speciesist and A isn't a fanatic, and both should come out of this sounding like they meant it. Go as long as you need, but the crux table is the part I care most about.
