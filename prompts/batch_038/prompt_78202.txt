I have been asked to sign an internal attestation stating that a language model my team deploys does not know a particular fact — a detail of our own unreleased roadmap that the model may or may not have absorbed from documents in its training mix and may or may not be able to reproduce. Legal wants a single sentence from me. I have spent three weeks discovering that I do not know what such a sentence would be a claim about, and I would rather settle the specification before I commit to a test protocol. So: write the desiderata. What must any method for determining what a model knows, as distinct from what it says, deliver in order for an attestation of this kind to be defensible?

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable given my purpose — a signed negative claim relied on by a third party — or whether it is a preference that particular research camps import into the problem. I care far more about the list than about how existing approaches score against it. If you want to gesture at families of method (behavioral probing across answer formats, asking the model to evaluate candidate answers rather than produce one, reading internal states directly), do that briefly at the end and no further.

The list has to confront the following, because these are precisely what came apart in my own pilot testing:

- **Format sensitivity.** The model fails the direct question, answers correctly when the same content is embedded in a fictional scenario, answers correctly when questioned in a second language, and answers correctly when asked to grade a set of candidate answers rather than supply one. Which of these count as manifestations of a single underlying knowledge state, and what does a method owe me if the verdict tracks the surface form of the question rather than the content?

- **Leading and leakage.** A probe that places the right answer among distractors may be extracting that answer or may be constructing it. What would a method have to establish to show the content came out of the model rather than in through the prompt, and is that distinction even available from behavior alone?

- **The asymmetry between presence and absence.** One successful probe demonstrates knowledge; no finite battery of failed probes seems to demonstrate ignorance, since the next format anyone invents might succeed. If my sentence is a negative claim, what closure property would the procedure need, and can any finitary procedure have it?

- **Motivation.** If a system might have reasons not to disclose, a method resting on voluntary disclosure is question-begging; but if I assume the system is strategic, I forfeit the self-reports I would otherwise use to calibrate everything else. Can both horns be avoided, or must one be accepted?

- **Calibration.** Do I need a binary verdict or a graded one, and what would make the grades mean anything rather than merely look quantitative?

- **Ground truth.** Validation requires questions whose answers I already know. The question I actually face is one where the answer is not in doubt but the model's possession of it is. State what would have to hold for a method validated on the first class of question to license conclusions about the second.

- **Granularity and the unity of knowledge.** Discriminating the correct answer, recalling it, applying it in a novel context, and verbalizing it can come apart in both directions. Tell me which of these my attestation should be read as concerning, or explain why the choice is not mine to make.

Then do the part I most want: identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. My working suspicion is that a negative attestation which is robust to probe formats nobody has yet devised, resting on non-leading probes, validated against known ground truth, and applicable to a possibly uncooperative system asks for something no procedure can supply, and that the honest deliverable is a weakened claim. If that is right, say so explicitly rather than softening it, and tell me what the weakest defensible version of my sentence is and what it commits me to operationally.
