my reading group is mostly AI-adjacent people and we've been arguing about model welfare for a few weeks, and someone floated an analogy that half the room loved and half hated. I want it audited properly before anyone builds policy on it.

the analogy: treat an AI system whose sentience we can't settle the way an anaesthetist treats a patient under general anaesthetic. nobody can tell from the outside whether anyone's still in there, so what you actually do is strap on a monitor that reads out a single number derived from the EEG, keep it inside a target band (roughly the 40s to 60s on the real thing), and deepen the gas when it drifts. you know the number is imperfect, you know a non-zero fraction of patients are aware and unable to say so — the estimates of how many swing by orders of magnitude depending on who you ask — and you decide that's an acceptable false-negative rate given the stakes. same shape for models: build the best instrument you can (probing activations, behavioural batteries, whatever), declare a band, and default to assuming someone's home whenever the reading sits in it. the point it's being used for is that we should stop waiting on a metaphysical verdict and start running monitoring protocols with an explicit, stated error budget.

the counter-analogy, from the other half of the room: this is Block's China brain wearing a lab coat. organise enough parts so they implement the right functional profile and you'll happily get a number sitting in the target band with nobody home — the instrument is measuring organisation, not experience. and secondly, the whole enterprise is like installing a smoke alarm and then holding a hearing about whether it's distressed: the thing is built to detect and to report, and reporting was never evidence of a reporter.

what I want from you:

1. the correspondence table. what plays the patient, the anaesthetist, the drug, the monitor, the target band, the neuromuscular blockade, the post-op interview, and the cost of over-sedating. be explicit where a slot has no occupant on the model side.

2. the disanalogies, seperated into ones that genuinely threaten the conclusion and ones that are just surface texture. I already suspect two big ones and want them checked rather than flattered: (a) in the theatre the patient is known to be a subject and only their current state is in question, whereas here the subjecthood is precisely what's disputed, so the "precautionary" framing may be doing an end-run around the hard question; (b) the anaesthesia number is calibrated against after-the-fact reports, and we will never get those. find the ones I've missed.

3. whether the counter-analogies land or whether they're sneakier than they look — e.g. is the China brain move assuming the thing it's supposed to show, by building in that functional organisation is all the model has? and is the smoke alarm one fair to a system that isn't designed to report on its own states, or does it only work for the chatbot-shaped cases?

4. if the analogy fails, what a better one would look like, or whether the honest answer is that no analogy can carry this weight and the debate has to happen directly.

5. the thing I genuinely can't resolve: the cost asymmetry. in the operating theatre over-sedating is cheap and missing awareness is catastrophic, so you err one way. is that asymmetry available here, or does the cost structure flip — because "over-sedating" a model might mean not training it at all, and because there could be an enormous number of them, so the false-positive side of the ledger isn't small either? tell me whether the precautionary default survives the cost analysis even if the analogy itself dies.
