So I've been going around in circles on the whole internal-versus-external reasons fight — Williams's thing, about whether a reason has to hook into what the person already cares about — and I want to find out whether my own gut verdicts actually add up to a principle, or whether some of them have to go. Here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them.

1. My cousin wants to be a paramedic, has the aptitude, would be good at it, but she's not applying becuase she doesn't want to rock the boat with her family. I think "you have a reason to apply" is true and I'd say it to her face, even though right now she feels no pull at all.

2. A guy who genuinely, on reflection, doesn't care about his health — knows the smoking will kill him, shrugs. I still want to say he has a reason to quit.

3. But my neighbour has zero interest in chess, never has, wouldn't enjoy it if he tried (I know, I made him try). Here I do not want to say "you have a reason to learn chess." I notice 2 and 3 are inconsistent unless something else is doing the work.

4. Someone pours what he believes is gin into his glass and it's actually petrol. He wants a gin and tonic. I think he has no reason to drink it — the desire is right there and the reason still isn't.

5. Same setup, except he just wants something pleasant to drink and would in fact enjoy the petrol, don't ask me how. Now I lean the other way: he does have a reason to drink it. I know how weird that sounds next to 4.

6. My great-great-grandfather owned slaves, saw nothing wrong with it, and nothing in his upbringing or his desires pointed toward freeing them. I want to say he had a reason to free them anyway. Saying "given who he was, he had no reason" makes me queasy.

7. The same man, asked to worry about the carbon footprint of his descendants. Here I happily say he had no reason to care. It wasn't in reach.

8. A monk who's spent thirty years deliberately and successfully extinguishing any desire for honour. I want to say he has no reason to chase awards, and that this isn't a criticism of him.

9. A severely depressed friend who currently wants nothing, including not to go on. I want to say he has plenty of reasons and that the depression is why he can't see them, not a reason why there aren't any.

8 and 9 are the pair I can't reconcile. Both are people whose desires have gone quiet, and in one I want the silence to matter and in the other I don't.

10. A woman promised her sister something years ago, has completely forgotten the promise, and keeping it would now cost her a lot for no gain she can foresee. I think she still has a reason to keep it, and that "you'd have wanted to, you just forgot" is a fair thing to say to her.

11. Someone who would, if they thought clearly and had all the facts, come to want X. In some cases I'll accept "so you have a reason to X now" and in others — chess again — I won't.

What I want: is there a single principle about what makes "S has a reason to φ" true that gets these, or as many as possible without degenerating into a shopping list of exceptions? Commit to one. If you think two are genuinely viable, say which one you'd sign up to and why, and argue for it rather than laying both out and handing me the choice. Walk me through it clause by clause, saying which numbered case each clause is earning its keep on. Then run your principle on three cases I haven't given verdicts for and tell me what it comes out as: (a) a person with a phobia who has a strong standing desire never to fly and a job that requires flying, (b) a toddler, (c) me, right now, about learning the fiddle, which I've idly wanted for twenty years and never started. And be blunt about which of my eleven verdicts your principle has to overrule — I'd rather lose two cleanly than keep all eleven under a principle nobody could state out loud.
