I run patient safety and clinical risk for a regional health network — roughly 1,900 clinical staff across four hospitals — and over the last two years we have put a handful of algorithmic decision-support tools into routine use: an early-warning score for sepsis, a triage flag on chest imaging, a readmission-risk score that feeds the discharge workflow, and a dosing suggestion in the ICU. Our incident review committee is six people (two physicians, two nurses, a pharmacist, an informaticist, me), we meet weekly, and we review about thirty events a quarter, of which three or four now have one of these tools somewhere in the causal chain. The committee works from a conventional root-cause template: reconstruct the timeline, ask at what point the harm could have been prevented, list contributing factors, and close with a named individual who owns each corrective action. That template worked when the chain ran through people. It is failing on the tool cases, in three specific ways, and I have not been able to fix it by rewriting the form. First, the "at what point could this have been prevented" question always finds a clinician, because the clinician is usually the only node with a logged, timestamped discretionary act — so the review ends up blaming the one person who actually exercised judgment, a pattern my nursing leads have started calling the last-nurse rule, and I cannot defend it to them. Second, when we try to move the finding upstream we hit a wall: the model's thresholds are changed by a vendor engineer under a contract that gives us notice but not approval, some configurations were set by a consultant who no longer works here, and the drift-monitoring duty is written into three job descriptions, which in practice means nobody. Third, when the causal story is genuinely distributed, the template's only remaining exit is to write a systems issue and hand a corrective action to IT, which the committee knows is a euphemism and which our regulator will not accept, since it requires a single accountable executive for every serious-event review.

What I want is a procedure my committee can actually run on a tool-involved event in ninety minutes, with no legal training in the room and no philosophers on it. My own half-formed hunch, from what reading I have managed, is that our template is collapsing several different things that are all called responsibility — the causal sense, the sense in which something falls within a person's role, the sense that requires that the person was competent and had a real option, and the sense that carries blame — and that the reason the review lands badly is that it enters through one of these and exits through another without saying so. Build the procedure on whatever distinctions of that kind are actually load-bearing rather than on my guess, but keep the vocabulary plain enough for a tired nurse at 4 p.m. I want the questions in the order the committee should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and explicit stop conditions: where the procedure terminates in a named locus of responsibility, where it terminates in an organizational one, and where it terminates in no locus at all. That last outcome is the one I care most about, so please develop it: if the review finds a genuine gap, the procedure should tell us what kind of gap it is — a gap in who can be blamed, in who can answer publicly for what happened, in who has the standing capacity to have done otherwise, or in who can be made to repair the harm — and it should map each kind to a different institutional response, because I suspect the responses are not interchangeable: a no-blame finding, a compensation route that does not depend on fault, suspending or re-gating the tool's use, reopening the contract, referring the case outward, and so on. It should also say how to get from a distributed causal story to the one signature our regulator demands without manufacturing a scapegoat, and how the same findings can support a disclosure conversation with a family that wants a name without turning into an admission the lawyers will fight.

Then run it on three cases from the last eighteen months, lightly disguised. Case one: a sepsis alert fired on a medical-surgical patient, the bedside nurse documented dismissing it as an artifact because the patient looked well, and the patient deteriorated overnight and was resuscitated; the alert had fired more often than usual because a vendor update six weeks earlier had lowered the threshold, which no one at our end approved, tested, or noticed, and her onboarding module had told her these alerts should be taken seriously. Case two: a patient discharged as low risk was readmitted within a week; the readmission model consumed a social-determinants field that had been silently empty since a records migration, so it had been mis-ranking a subset of patients for months; nobody overrode anything, the drift monitoring was unassigned in practice, and on the base rate the readmission was more likely than not to have happened anyway. Case three: an ICU dosing suggestion performed exactly as designed and as documented in the vendor's validation report, and the harm was the trade-off that was chosen deliberately at procurement — fewer hypotensive episodes, more of a different complication — by a committee whose members have nearly all left the organization; nobody here did anything wrong and nothing malfunctioned. In at least one of the three, I expect the procedure to land somewhere I will not like, most plausibly on a named clinician; if so, say plainly that it does and explain why the procedure does not dissolve that finding, rather than quietly steering around it.

Two final constraints. Mark every step that is a judgment call dressed as procedure — I would rather see those flagged than have them hidden inside a flowchart that looks mechanical — and tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it cannot distinguish a real gap from blame diffusion, where it breaks down if the tool's contribution to the outcome is unquantifiable, and where it produces an answer that is defensible but not something I would want read aloud to a family. I do not need citations or a bibliography; refer to positions, arguments, and criteria in your own words and I will track down sources myself. What I need is the worksheet the committee could fill in during the meeting.
