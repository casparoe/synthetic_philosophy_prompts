Construct a thought experiment about two neighbouring countries — call them Fjordland and Sveland — engineered so that the question "which of these is the more liberal society?" cannot be settled by the usual measures. Hold everything else fixed: same language, population, per-capita income, climate, ethnic composition, near-zero corruption, and identical scores on the standard democracy indices. Let them differ only in the pattern of their institutions. Fjordland owns the commanding heights of its economy and bars private schools and clinics, but runs a minimal permitting regime, has decriminalised drugs and abolished its alcohol restrictions, and allows the press to publish anything about anyone. Sveland has near-total private provision of schooling and healthcare and little state ownership, but retains a state retail monopoly on alcohol, punitive drug laws, and a licensing regime that gives front-line officials wide discretion over who may open a business or build a house. Stipulate that a Fraser-style economic-freedom index ranks Sveland clearly ahead while a Freedom House-style civil-liberties score ranks Fjordland ahead.

State the purpose of the case up front: it exists to test whether the dispute is merely an artefact of how indices are constructed, or whether some further fact could settle which country really is more liberal. That fixes what counts as cheating — a solution that stipulates a winner, or that simply adds a fourth index and declares it authoritative, fails.

Then deliver the following:

1. Rank the pair under at least three conceptions of liberty — negative liberty as non-interference, republican non-domination, and freedom as effective capability or real opportunity — and pay particular attention to where non-domination splits, since state ownership makes Fjordlanders dependent on a single employer and landlord while discretionary licensing makes Svelanders subject to the arbitrary will of officials.
2. Identify the one feature of the design that does the work, and say how each verdict changes if you remove it.
3. Say what non-index evidence could break the tie — revealed preference in migration between the two, a test of exit costs, surveys of felt freedom, the behaviour of each state in hard cases — and why each candidate may fail to be decisive.
4. Close with how someone who holds that "liberal" simply names a bundle of institutions that travel together would object that the case is impossible rather than difficult, and whether that objection succeeds.

Keep the scenario itself to two paragraphs. Use only realistic institutional detail: no science fiction, no invented technologies, and no country may be secretly a dystopia. Finish by saying briefly whether the real Sweden–Norway comparison is any better posed than your fictional one, or whether it inherits exactly the same indeterminacy.
