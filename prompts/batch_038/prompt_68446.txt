I am in my final year of high school, and my history teacher has agreed to let me present a paper at our school's humanities symposium in the spring. The paper compares how four political communities have handled the record of mass violence in their own pasts — postwar Germany (usually discussed under the term Vergangenheitsbewältigung, coming to terms with the past), Japan, Turkey, and the American South — and argues that the comparison supports a general principle about legitimacy. My teacher read the draft and told me, without elaborating, that my central argument proves too much, and that I should find where it breaks before the judges do. I have looked for a week and cannot, so I am asking for a diagnosis.

Here is the argument.

(1) A state's claim to rule depends in part on the truthfulness of the account it gives of its own past, and specifically on whether it acknowledges the mass violence its formation and maintenance involved.

(2) Germany is the case that motivates (1): the postwar Federal Republic eventually prosecuted perpetrators itself, taught the crimes in schools, preserved memorials at former camp sites, and made Holocaust denial a criminal offense, and it is widely credited with a legitimacy that a successor to the Third Reich could not otherwise have had.

(3) Whatever does the work in (2) cannot be peculiar to Germany, since the relevant feature is a state's relation to its own historical record rather than the character of its people.

(4) So any state that has not acknowledged the mass violence in its record lacks, to that extent, a legitimate claim to rule those it governs.

(5) Every long-standing state has such a record. Turkey has prosecuted writers under a penal-code article criminalizing insults to Turkishness for describing the events of 1915 as genocide. Japan issued an official acknowledgment in 1993 of its military's role in recruiting women for wartime brothels, which later governments have revisited and qualified. Confederate monuments were raised across the American South in two waves, one as Jim Crow was consolidated and another during the civil-rights era, and several states have since legislated to prevent their removal.

(6) So almost no existing state is legitimate.

(7) The requirement is also unsatisfiable, because the historical record expands continuously as archives open and as new scholarship and testimony appear. Meeting (1) would therefore require a state to endorse a complete and accurate account of its own past — something no state possesses and no government controls.

(8) So either (1) is false or political legitimacy is a fiction.

I would rather give up (8) than (1), and I would especially like to keep the comparative material in (5), since that is the part I researched myself. Please name the step to reject and explain why that step looked acceptable to me when I wrote it — I want the equivocation or the illicit inference identified, not a general caution about generalizing from a single case. Then price the alternatives: for each of (1), (3), (4), and (7), what is lost if I abandon or weaken it?

Two problems I could not solve on my own. First, Germany acknowledged the extermination of the Herero and Nama only in 2015, and the 2021 agreement that followed provided development aid while disclaiming legal responsibility. For the century before that, Germany was a prosperous democracy nobody described as illegitimate. Does that falsify (1), or does it show that (1) needs a restriction — and if it needs one, does the restricted version still reach the American South, or colonial violence generally? Second, I am unsure who the agent in (4) is supposed to be. The Republic of Turkey insists it is not the Ottoman Empire; West Germany was not the Reich; postwar Japan governed under a new constitution; and the American South is not a state at all but a region inside one whose federal government's acknowledgments are a separate matter. If the obligation to acknowledge requires continuity between the agent who owes it and the agent who committed the violence, none of my four cases supplies that continuity in the same way. Tell me whether supplying it repairs the argument or destroys it, and, if some weakened version survives, what the weakest conclusion is that would still support the claim I wanted to make — that a community's refusal to face its own violence is a standing defect in its right to govern, not merely a matter of diplomatic taste.
