I am in the final stages of a chapter for an edited volume on individual responsibility for aggregate harms — emissions, industrial animal agriculture, overfishing, the ballot box — and the closing section is the one in which I am expected to say plainly where I stand rather than survey the debate. A colleague who read a draft suggested that I first set my own position out in numbered form and have it audited, on the grounds that a referee will do the same thing less charitably. I have done so below, and I should like you to play that part. What follows is my position as I would defend it, not a summary of anyone else's.

(1) Wrongness for individual acts is fixed ex ante: an act is wrong just in case its expected outcome is worse than that of the best alternative open to the agent. I am not an actualist about consequences, and I do not think that what an act turns out to do bears on whether it was permissible. (2) The inefficacy objection therefore fails for ordinary consumption. Retail and agricultural markets are threshold phenomena with imprecise boundaries: a given purchase carries a small probability of triggering a proportionate response further up the chain — an order placed, a chick hatched and slaughtered, a quota adjusted. Expected marginal harm is small but non-zero, and that suffices for wrongness where the alternative is costless. (3) I deny that there are group agents of the kind that could bear obligations. Talk of what "we" ought to do is either distributive — a compressed way of stating a set of individual oughts — or it is empty; and nothing in my account needs a collective over and above individuals and their dispositions, since the threshold mechanism in (2) is wholly a matter of what particular buyers, suppliers and producers will do. (4) I am nonetheless unmoved on overdetermination. Take a thousand people who each empty a barrel of effluent into a lake, where one barrel alone would suffice to kill the fish, and stipulate that each knows with certainty that the other nine hundred and ninety-nine will do likewise. Each acts wrongly, and each is fully blameworthy. I want to insist that this verdict is not smuggled in through any residual chance of being the decisive dumper; by stipulation there is none. This is, I take it, the shape of the problem Parfit pressed against consequentialist accounts of imperceptible difference, and I think my account is meant to survive it. (5) On voting I hold the reverse of what my critics attribute to me. A single vote has no realistic chance of being decisive, and I explicitly refuse to import the threshold reasoning of (2) into elections. And yet I hold that failing to vote in a national election is wrong, and wrong in a non-trivial degree — comparable to other things I call duties, not a negligible residue of expected harm. Its ground is that one ought not to free-ride upon, or hold oneself aloof from, a joint undertaking whose outcome matters morally. (6) Finally, offsets. A reliable, well-verified offset discharges the wrongness of an otherwise impermissible long-haul flight. But I also hold, and hold forcefully against the market-minded, that harming a person and then conferring an equivalent benefit on an unrelated stranger does not render the act permissible; compensation is not justification.

I confess I cannot see for myself where the trouble lies, and I have no settled view about which of these I would surrender; please do not assume that the intuitively costliest verdict is the one I am most attached to, or that the most theoretically elegant commitment is the one I would defend to the last. What I want is an audit rather than a lecture: begin with a single sentence bottom line saying whether the package hangs together and, if it does not, which one revision buys the most. Then rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment does the damage, why the two cannot both be held, what the cheapest repair would be, and precisely what I should lose by making it — which verdicts, which principles, which replies to the inefficacy objection. Separately, identify any pair that merely appears to conflict and explain in a sentence or two why it survives inspection; I would rather be told where I am in the clear than have everything flagged. Keep the whole thing to at most eight hundred words, and write in British English.
