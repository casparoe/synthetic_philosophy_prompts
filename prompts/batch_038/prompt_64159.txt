I am auditing a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and our current unit concerns how a Bayesian should revise her credences when experience fails to deliver any proposition with certainty. The reading group has split into two camps, and I have been asked to write the position paper that adjudicates the dispute. The first camp treats Jeffrey conditionalization as a genuine norm of rationality: whenever experience moves your probability for the members of a partition E₁, …, Eₙ from P(Eᵢ) to P*(Eᵢ), rationality requires you to leave every conditional probability P(H|Eᵢ) untouched and to obtain your new unconditional credences by mixing over the old conditionals with the new partition weights. On this view rigidity is not a convenience but the very content of the norm, and it is said to be underwritten both by diachronic Dutch-book or dynamic-coherence arguments of the kind associated with Skyrms and Armendt, and by results such as Diaconis and Zabell's, which spell out when a revision that preserves those conditionals is even possible and hence how constraining the demand turns out to be. The second camp thinks the apparatus is question-begging. Their complaint is that rigidity assumes in advance that an experience carries a fixed evidential meaning that survives the experience itself, which is precisely what is in doubt when the experience is murky; and they press Carl Wagner's reparameterization of the same update in terms of ratios of new to old odds among the partition cells rather than in terms of the cells' new probabilities, a move intended to isolate the genuinely evidential component of the shift so that it can survive revisions in the very conditionals that Jeffrey's rule holds sacred.

So that the paper is not purely schematic, here is the running case the seminar has been arguing over. A field analyst is testing well water for nitrate contamination; let H be the hypothesis that the sample exceeds the safety threshold. She uses a colorimetric strip that reads clear (C), pale (P), or deep pink (D). Her prior is P(H) = 0.4, and her likelihoods are P(C|H) = 0.1, P(P|H) = 0.3, P(D|H) = 0.6, together with P(C|¬H) = 0.6, P(P|¬H) = 0.3, P(D|¬H) = 0.1; this gives her P(H|C) = 0.1, P(H|P) = 0.4, and P(H|D) = 0.8, and prior probabilities over the readings of 0.40, 0.30, and 0.30 respectively. She takes the reading at dusk and cannot be sure what she saw, and reflection leaves her with P*(C) = 0.25, P*(P) = 0.45, P*(D) = 0.30. Jeffrey's rule then yields P*(H) = 0.445. Afterwards she remembers that the strip box sat in a hot vehicle for a week, which she takes to make a pale reading less diagnostic of contamination, and she is disposed to lower P(H|P) from 0.4 to 0.3 while leaving her other conditionals alone. Two things then become contentious. First, this second revision is not a redistribution of probability across any partition of experiential states, so Jeffrey's rule has nothing to say about it; yet the two revisions interact, since lowering P(H|P) while holding P(H) fixed changes the prior probabilities of C, P, and D themselves, and it is far from obvious that there is any coherent sense in which the dusk update and the heat update can be applied in sequence, or any principled account of why their order should or should not matter. Second, the Wagner-style parameterization appears to handle the interaction gracefully: the dusk experience can be captured by odds ratios alone, since the new odds of D to C are 1.2 against prior odds of 0.75, and the new odds of P to C are 1.8 against prior odds of 0.75, so the experience contributes factors of 1.6 and 2.4, and one might reapply exactly those factors after the likelihoods have been corrected for heat exposure instead of insisting that the old conditionals be preserved.

Write an essay of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words that takes a definite side in this dispute. The central question is whether Jeffrey conditionalization is a substantive norm of rationality governing learning from uncertain evidence, or whether the rigidity requirement is an unmotivated restriction that reduces it to a special case of a broader and better-motivated family of revisions. Address at least the following: whether rigidity enjoys any independent justification beyond stipulation, and what exactly goes wrong, or fails to go wrong, in the analyst's case; whether the dynamic-coherence line genuinely pins down Jeffrey's rule rather than something weaker or stronger, given that the bets which supposedly punish a non-rigid updater must themselves be settled on the basis of evidence the agent does not have with certainty; whether the odds-ratio reparameterization supplies a real alternative norm or is merely notational redescription of the same constraint; and whether the order-dependence of successive uncertain revisions is a genuine embarrassment for the view that such revisions are a form of learning at all. Use the numbers in the case to test the positions you consider, showing where two candidate rules diverge and arguing which divergence tracks rationality rather than bookkeeping. Close with a clear verdict and defend it; I do not want a survey that ends by observing that the matter is subtle or that each camp has something going for it. A qualified verdict is acceptable, for instance that the rule is authoritative only under specified background conditions, provided you say precisely what those conditions are and whether they obtain in the case above. Please do not append a bibliography or a list of references; argue from the concepts and from the case itself.
