For several years I have been brokering joint agreements between organizations whose missions rest on genuinely different value systems. The usual shape of these deals is a trade: each party devotes some of its own resources to priorities the other holds and treats as near-worthless itself, and receives the same in return, so that both end up better off than if they had stayed separate. I have watched one particular outcome emerge from such negotiations often enough that I now expect it, and I have never found a word for it.

Here is the pattern. The two sides each have a preferred option, and these sit at opposite ends of a range of possibilities. They settle on something in the middle. It then turns out that each side ranks the middle *last* — not merely below its own preferred end, which would be unremarkable, but below the other side's end as well. Both would have preferred the other party to win outright rather than have the compromise.

Two instances, stripped of identifying detail. First: a legislature can prohibit a practice outright or leave it unregulated, and instead licenses it. The prohibition camp considers licensing worse than free permission, because a licensed practice acquires official standing, a trade association, and an interest in its own survival, whereas an unlicensed one stays marginal and stigmatized. The permission camp considers licensing worse than free permission too, because the licensing apparatus is the foothold from which restrictions are later widened and never repealed. Both vote for the bill. Second: two foundations with non-overlapping missions merge into an umbrella body with a 50/50 budget and a shared communications function. Each founder now finds it harder to press its own case, because donors and journalists treat the question as already covered by the umbrella, and each judges the merger worse than the other foundation having proceeded alone at full strength. Both signed; both describe the merger as a success.

There is a further feature I keep seeing, and I am unsure whether it belongs to the same phenomenon or is a second one riding along. The compromise is unusually hard to object to afterward. Objecting requires saying that you would rather the other side's program had prevailed, which reads either as an admission that you do not hold your own commitments as firmly as you claim or as a gift to your opponents. So the middle is shielded by the fact that the only available objections are self-incriminating, and the settlement comes to be described as sensible precisely because no one is visibly aggrieved.

Is there an established concept for a negotiated midpoint that both parties rank below each of the two extremes? I recognize that it lies near the familiar caution that the position between two claims is not automatically the correct one, but that caution concerns inference, whereas what I am describing concerns what a negotiation lands on when both sides are clear-eyed about their own ends, correctly informed, and genuinely advantaged by striking a deal at all. I would like to know: which established concepts come closest, and exactly where each of them stops short of this; what has to be true about how each side evaluates the range of options for the middle to come out last on both sides' rankings — explain the condition in plain terms before you give me its technical name, if it has one; whether the diagnosis changes when the midpoint is bad only from the bargaining parties' standpoint but good for the people the policy actually affects; and where the line falls between this and compromise in the ordinary sense, which both parties do rank above the alternatives. I am after the carving rather than the label: if nothing in the literature fits, propose a term, define it, and say which nearby cases fall just outside it.
