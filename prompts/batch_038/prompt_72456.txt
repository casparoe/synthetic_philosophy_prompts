I'm TA-ing an undergrad seminar on the ethics of harm this term and there's one thing I keep failing to explain. We've got two readings that attack the killing/letting-die distinction from totally different directions. One says what matters is whether your behavior starts up a threat that ends in the harm, or whether you merely fail to interrupt a threat that's already running — on that view the line is a real feature of the causal structure, not an artifact of how we describe things. The other says that causal story isn't doing any work at all, and whether an omission counts as letting someone die depends entirely on the normative baseline, i.e. whether the victim had any kind of claim or entitlement to the thing you withheld or took away. My students keep asking whether these two accounts even disagree or whether they're just carving the same cases up differently, and honestly I dont have a good answer for them.

Could you write me a dialogue between a philosopher defending the causal/initiation account and one defending the entitlement/baseline account, arguing over a pair of cases I made up for the seminar? Case one: Mara has been on a ventilator in a public hospital ward for three days after a car crash; the hospital's allocation committee decides the machine is better used on another patient who's worse off but has a much better prognosis, and a nurse disconnects Mara, who dies. Case two: Mara owns her own ventilator and uses it at home; a stranger breaks in, unplugs it, and carries it off to a neighbor who needs it more, and Mara dies. In both cases an agent intervenes to remove a life-sustaining process, but only in the second does Mara have a claim to the machine. Most of my students think the second is straightforwardly a killing and the first is something else entirely, and I want the two characters to fight over which account explains that verdict and which one is just dressing up the intuition after the fact. Then have them escalate to a third case that's meant to put pressure on the causal theorist: a city has run a pump station keeping a low-lying neighborhood dry for forty years, people built there in reliance on it, and now the city switches it off during budget cuts. Switching off a pump looks like an act, but it also looks like stopping aid rather than starting a threat, and I'd like the causal theorist to make something of the fact that terminating an ongoing prevention isn't the same thing as failing to initiate one. Work in, too, the point that the causal theorist can't just equate doing harm with acting, since you can allow a harm by acting — a guard who unlocks a gate and steps aside is doing plenty, but it seems like allowing.

Both characters should be sharp and neither should be a strawman: they need to actually answer each other's moves, trade concessions where concessions are genuinely due, and land in a real standoff rather than converging. Somewhere in the middle, one of them should put a case on the table where the two accounts come apart and predict opposite verdicts, so it's clear to my students that the disagreement is substantive. Roughly 900 to 1100 words, dialogue only, minimal stage directions, and please don't have either of them cite papers, books, or real philosophers by name — I want the arguments, not the bibliography.
