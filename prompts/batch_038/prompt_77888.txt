Background, because I think it matters for how you answer: last summer I did a ten-day silent meditation retreat, my first, mostly because I wanted the quiet and not because of any beliefs I had going in. Around day six something shifted, and for a couple of hours there was hearing and thinking and a bad left knee, but no sense whatsoever of a person behind it — no owner, no watcher, and nothing that felt missing which had been there before. I've been chewing on it ever since. I eventually read my way into the "consciousness as a user illusion" line — Nørretranders, Dennett's interface framing, Metzinger's self-model stuff — and it fit the experience so cleanly that it kind of scared me, because I'm pretty sure I got to the metaphysics from the experience rather than the other way around. So I'd like you to try genuinely hard to break it, and I want you to assume I'm a motivated reasoner about this.

Here's the position, decomposed as tightly as I can manage. (1) The rich, movie-in-the-head world we take ourselves to be living in isn't something the brain instantiates all at once; at any instant only a thin sliver is determinate, and the sense that there was ever more — a filled visual field, a hum of background meaning, somebody at the controls — is generated retrospectively. (2) The self is one of those generated items: a model the system runs because modeling is useful for narration and for predicting other people, with nobody inside using it. (3) Nothing is thereby lost or explained away in a suspicious sense — the illusion just is the reality, and there's no extra layer of non-illusory feels underneath that the story fails to capture. I think its (3) that's load-bearing and that I'm least sure about.

So: what would count against this? Not a survey of the fact that philosophers have objected — I want the specific findings, cases and arguments that should function as evidence against it, ranked from would-make-me-drop-it to merely-annoying, with a line for each saying what the world would actually have to look like. A few things I've half-thought about and want weighed in on: how much the change-blindness and confabulation work really licenses, since a thin-but-real experience plus a confabulating reporter sounds like a rival to (1) rather than support for it; the hydranencephaly kids who seem to have vivid emotional and perceptual lives without much of the cortex the view says does the generating; the worry that the interface metaphor needs a user, or at least a something for which it is an interface; and Hoffman's version, which apparently eats the same evidence and concludes that matter is the illusion rather than mind — is that a competitor that costs me something, or just a rebranded cousin? Tell me which results people cite as support that actually cut the other way on a closer reading, what a committed defender of the view would honestly concede as counting against them (not just what thier critics say), and whether there exists any conceivable report, behavior, or neural signature the view couldn't absorb as more illusion. If there isn't one, say so plainly, and then tell me whether being unfalsifiable-by-construction is fatal here or just what a view about experience has to look like. Oh, and please write in American English.
