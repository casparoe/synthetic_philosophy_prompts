I am at work on a novel in which a separating couple retain a firm of mediators who advertise themselves as beyond negotiation altogether, since every outcome they reach is merely read off from an axiomatic bargaining solution. For the opening of Part Two I need a satirical prospectus for that firm: about 600 words, in the soothing corporate register of a private clinic's patient literature, with subheadings, a 'what to bring' list and one client testimonial.

The comedy must be generated by the firm's own formal commitments rather than by caricature. They take the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution as their house standard — Pareto efficiency, symmetry, invariance to positive affine transformations of either party's utility scale, and monotonicity, the principle that if one party's options improve so that her best achievable payoff rises while the other's stays as it was, she cannot end up worse off. They have surrendered independence of irrelevant alternatives on grounds of conscience, and this should be pitched to prospective clients as reassurance. The prospectus should also explain, in the firm's own untroubled language, that the settlement lies where the line drawn from the disagreement point to the point of each party's best achievable utility meets the Pareto frontier.

What I want the piece to betray, without once seeming to notice it, is how much is settled before any axiom is applied: who fixes the disagreement point, how each party's utilities are calibrated, and the brisk efficiency of a credible threat. The target is the practice of presenting contested modelling choices as neutral arithmetic, not any real mediator. A reader who has never met a bargaining problem should still follow it and laugh; let the formal content be carried by the firm's euphemisms rather than by notation. The deadpan must hold throughout — no winking, no aside in which the author steps forward.
