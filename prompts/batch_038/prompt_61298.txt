For a graduate problem set in intergenerational ethics I have assembled the following argument out of premises defended by different people. None of them accepts the conclusion, and neither do I, but I cannot locate the joint, and I want the problem set to have a defensible answer key.

(1) It is irrational to weight a benefit less merely because it falls at a later time: mere position in time is not a reason-giving feature of a benefit.
(2) So, within a life, the total matters and its temporal distribution does not — a well-being profile spread evenly across a century is neither better nor worse than one concentrated in its first decade, given the same total.
(3) If temporal position gives no reason within a life, it gives no reason across lives either, since time's irrelevance cannot depend on whose time it is.
(4) So the well-being of people living a thousand years hence counts for exactly as much as the well-being of people alive today.
(5) If the far future will contain more people than the present does, and if present saving raises their well-being, then the far future contains vastly more morally weighty well-being than the present does.
(6) Hence we are required to discount nothing and to save as much as we possibly can for the far future.
(7) But no ethic that requires the worst-off people now to sacrifice for the comfortably-off people of the deep future is acceptable; it is not merely demanding, it is perverse.
(8) Therefore (1) is false, and a positive rate of pure time preference is required by any coherent intergenerational ethic.

Diagnose it. I want to know whether the chain is valid with a false premise, invalid with all-true premises, or something less tidy — do not simply assume the last option because I have listed it. For every step you reject, say why the step looked unobjectionable in isolation, name the slide if there is one, and distinguish the readings of the premise on which it would be true but idle from those on which it would be substantive but false. If forced, I would rather keep (1) than (8); tell me what keeping (1) actually costs, what accepting (8) costs, and whether some third position can retain (1) and refuse (6) without collapsing into (8). Close by identifying where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to break down, and what argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict. Please write in American English.
