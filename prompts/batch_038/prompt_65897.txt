You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of information and on the conceptual analysis of privacy; please treat what follows as a piece of careful applied analysis rather than a general survey, and think it through slowly before you start writing.

I am a second-year graduate student. My supervisor is drafting a monograph on informational privacy, and her manuscript is the subject of our department's work-in-progress seminar this term; I have been asked to prepare a formal comment on the middle chapter. Her method throughout is uniform: she states a worked example, then issues a verdict — privacy violated, or not violated — and only later gestures at the theory. The examples are her own inventions, not lifted from the literature, so please do not try to match them to a published author or book. Please also do not give me a bibliography, citations, quotations, or page references; neither of us has the sources to hand and I want reconstruction and argument, not name-checking.

Her verdicts on the eight examples so far:

1. A fertility clinic sends appointment reminders — which reveal that the patient is in treatment — to the household email address she had supplied years earlier for billing, without ever asking whether that address is safe to use. **Violation.**
2. A direct-to-consumer genetics company requests separate, plain-language, itemized permission for each of four downstream uses (research, product improvement, partner marketing, law-enforcement access), lets users revoke any of them at any time, and honors revocations within thirty days. **No violation.**
3. A period-tracking app whose policy said only that it "may share information with trusted partners" sells users' inferred cycle predictions to an advertising network; accepting the policy was the sole condition of using the app at all. **Violation** — and she stresses that the user's acceptance is beside the point.
4. A hospital releases records to an academic researcher under a data-use agreement that forbids re-identification; the researcher's graduate student re-identifies four percent of the records against a purchased public voter file, then publishes nothing and shares nothing. **Violation.**
5. A fitness app silently reverses a default so that GPS routes are public unless the user finds and flips a buried setting. Route-sharing is entirely ordinary in that user community, nobody is embarrassed by anything, and a good many users are delighted. **Violation.**
6. A patient gives her oncologist a specific, written, revocable authorization to send her pathology report to her personal trainer; the trainer signs a confidentiality undertaking and uses the report only to adjust the training program. **Violation** — and here she stresses that the patient's authorization was as complete and as informed as authorization can be.
7. A newspaper assembles a searchable database of the names and faces of everyone photographed at a permitted public march, the names supplied by matching faces against public social-media profiles. **Violation.**
8. A hospice patient, while fully competent, initials a clause in the admission packet permitting her voice recordings and personal letters to be used "for the bereavement program." She dies. Her daughter asks for a recording of her mother reading a story she had recorded years earlier, and the hospice hands it over. **No violation.**

The ninth example, which she has not yet ruled on, is the one I have to comment on. Same hospice, same initialed clause, same recordings and letters: the bereavement program trains a conversational system on them and offers the family an interactive voice that answers in the deceased's manner and cadence. It never claims to be her; access is restricted to the immediate family; nothing is published or sold; and the clause she initialed said only "for the bereavement program," with no mention of interactive reconstruction.

Here is what I need from you, in this order:

**Step 1 — brainstorm before you commit.** Lay out at least four candidate general rules that could generate her eight verdicts: for instance, a rule on which privacy claims are really claims about property, contract, and confidential relationships in disguise; a rule on which privacy is a matter of the subject's control; a rule on which privacy is a matter of access or secrecy; a rule on which it is governed by context-specific norms of information flow; a rule on which it protects personhood or dignity. You may add or replace candidates as you see fit. For each, say which of the eight verdicts it explains cleanly and which it has to strain to accommodate, and note the strain. A compact table whose rows are candidate rules and whose columns are the eight verdicts — marked *clean*, *strain*, or *incompatible*, with a one-line note wherever it is not clean — would be ideal here.

**Step 2 — survivors.** Tell me which candidates, if any, survive all eight unqualified. If none does, say whether the ledger is consistent only with a hybrid or with an auxiliary assumption bolted onto one of them, and state precisely what that auxiliary is and what priority rule the hybrid needs when its components conflict. Recommend the one that best coheres with her own precedents, and then, honestly, state what principle the chapter would be adopting by ruling that way and what it would be adopting by ruling the other way — including any cost to examples she has already committed to.

**Step 6 — the discriminator.** Design a tenth worked example that would tell the two best-fitting candidates apart, and state in advance what each of them predicts about it.
