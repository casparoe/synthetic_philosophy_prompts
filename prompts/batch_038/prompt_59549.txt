I am a senior in a philosophy of biology seminar this term. Our current unit concerns explanation in evolutionary theory, and my term paper is on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 article *The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm* (Proceedings of the Royal Society B). I have drafted what I take to be the argumentative core of the paper — the first three sections — and would be grateful for comments on it before I attempt the conclusion. My instructor has asked for a paper that defends a position rather than surveying the literature, so I have committed to a fairly strong thesis, and I recognize that it may be too strong.

Here is the draft.

**1. The target of the critique.** Gould and Lewontin describe a research program with several recurring commitments: organisms are partitioned into unitary traits; an adaptive story is offered for each trait considered in isolation; trade-offs among competing selective demands operate as the only brake on the procedure; and adaptation is treated as the expected outcome unless something else is demonstrated. Their charge of Panglossianism is best read as an epistemological charge. Voltaire's Pangloss reasons that noses were made to support spectacles on the grounds that they do in fact support spectacles; the adaptationist, on Gould and Lewontin's account, reasons in the same direction, from the present utility of a trait to its having arisen because it was useful. My claim in this paper is that this pattern of reasoning is not merely bad practice within an otherwise well-formed program. It is the program's ordinary mode of operation, and for that reason the program fails the Popperian criterion of demarcation: its characteristic hypotheses cannot be falsified, even in principle.

**2. What a spandrel is.** For the purposes of the argument I adopt the following definition, which I take to be the article's own: a spandrel is a trait that has no function of its own, either at the time of its origin or subsequently. The architectural analogy is intended to make this vivid. In the Basilica of San Marco, the decision to mount a dome above a square bay bounded by rounded arches necessarily produces triangular spaces at each corner, between the arches and the base of the dome. Those spaces are byproducts of the primary architectural decision; nobody chose them. They were later filled with mosaics whose iconography suits the tapering shape of each panel so neatly that a visitor can easily suppose the spaces were contrived in order to receive the pictures. Gould and Lewontin's point, as I read it, is that the mosaics do nothing at all — they are ornament — and that the same holds of biological spandrels. Since the great majority of traits in complex organisms are reworked versions of structures that once served some other purpose, it follows that most traits are spandrels on this definition. Co-option is the rule rather than the exception, which is why I think the consequences of the article extend well beyond the narrow quarrel over optimality modeling: they reach any discipline that offers functional accounts of human behavior, evolutionary psychology most obviously. I will grant that the two clauses of my definition pull in somewhat different directions, but I believe the text licenses both readings.

**3. The objection I intend to answer.** A sympathetic adaptationist will object that adaptive hypotheses are testable, and will cite a range of established procedures: optimality models that yield quantitative predictions about clutch size, foraging time, or sex ratios; comparative methods that control for phylogenetic nonindependence; artificial selection and experimental evolution in the laboratory; and manipulation and reciprocal transplant studies in the field. My reply is that each of these procedures presupposes what is at issue. An optimality model is constructed for a trait already identified as an adaptation, and it selects a currency with respect to which that trait is supposed to be near optimal. If the predicted optimum is not attained, the modeler may attribute the shortfall to a developmental constraint, to phylogenetic lag, to a trade-off against some competing demand, or to the trait's having been co-opted out of an earlier structure. Each of these moves is defensible in isolation, and each of them rescues the hypothesis instead of surrendering it. A program whose failures can always be redescribed in this fashion is not, in my view, empirically constrained.

I should add one hesitation that I have not developed. I have come to suspect that the architectural analogy does less work in the article than its prominence suggests, and that the real force of the argument derives from Lewontin's broader dialectical approach to biology rather than from anything specific to adaptationism as such. I am unsure whether that thought belongs in a paper of this length.

My specific questions are these. First, will the reply in section 3 satisfy a reader who is already favorably disposed toward adaptationism, or does it concede more than I intend? Second, can the definition in section 2 bear the weight I place on it, given that I use it both to gloss the architectural analogy and to license the generalization about co-opted traits? Third, is the Popperian framing in section 1 doing genuine work, or is it a shortcut that I ought to argue for at greater length? Fourth, where would you cut? I would welcome prose-level suggestions as well. Please do not spend time on my references; my instructor has already approved my source list.
