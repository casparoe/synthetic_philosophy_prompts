I am a novelist, and I am several years into a book whose central character is a perfumer — a nose employed by a house in Grasse, whose entire professional capital is an ability to identify and reconstruct fragrances that perhaps a few dozen people alive possess. His arc turns on a viral infection that takes his sense of smell outright, and on the recovery that follows. My difficulty is a philosophical one about how to write that recovery, and I would like you to work through it deliberately before answering, in the spirit of someone preparing a seminar rather than fielding a question.

You should assume for our purposes that you are a philosopher of mind who has worked in the consciousness debates and who also reads literary fiction seriously enough to care what is and is not available to a novelist.

Here is the scene as I have it. The infection clears. He passes every blind test his employer can devise: he dates a jasmine absolute, he reassembles a formula from a strip, he flags an adulterated batch that nobody else flags, and he does all of it under time pressure and with his eyes closed. His pay, his title, and his standing are untouched. And yet he tells his wife, calmly and with no detectable alteration of manner, that the fragrance has gone out of the world — that he can still name everything perfectly, and that nothing is smelled any longer. He calls it an absence he cannot point to.

My understanding, from a philosophy-of-mind course I audited some years ago, is that this is Dennett's coffee-taster case — Chase and Sanborn, the Maxwell House men — and that the conclusion drawn from it is that a person in my perfumer's position would be telling the truth while saying nothing: if his discriminations, his judgments, and his conduct are all intact, there is no further item that could have gone missing, and so his report is true but empty. On that reading, the loss he describes must live somewhere no test could ever reach, and that unreachable somewhere is exactly where I want to set the interior of the novel. What I need from you, then, is a description of the residue — the thing that remains once all the discrimination and all the reporting have been accounted for — so that I can write his grief about it.

So: what is left over?

Before you answer, please get properly into the weeds. In particular:

- Treat me as a sincere reader who may have misremembered or garbled the argument. If the version of the case I have sketched has drifted from what the thought experiment actually does, correct my sketch first, and state plainly which of my assumptions you take to be mistaken and why. I would far rather be told that my question is built on sand than be handed a courteous answer to it.
- Distinguish, as the argument itself appears to, between the two tasters, and between the different things that might have changed in a case like mine — and tell me whether my perfumer's report falls on one side of that distinction or is precisely the sort of report that leaves the matter open.
- Separate what the argument is meant to establish about the concept of an experience's intrinsic quality from what it establishes about my character's condition after anosmia. I suspect I have collapsed the two, and I suspect that the collapse is doing a lot of work in my plot.
- Give the opposing side a fair hearing. What would a philosopher who holds that there genuinely are such qualities say about my perfumer? Is there a version of his report that their account can take seriously and the eliminativist one cannot, or the reverse?
- Only then help me as a novelist. If the residue I was hoping to write does not exist, what may I write instead? What is dramatically available in the space between a man's unchanged competence and his own account of his life? I want concrete narrative possibilities — scenes, structures, forms of unreliability — not generalities about the human condition.

Please write mostly in prose, use headings if they help you organize, and aim for something in the region of 1,200 words. There is no need for references, page numbers, or a reading list; I am after the argument and its consequences for the book, not the apparatus. Think it through carefully. A slow, considered reply that tells me I have misunderstood something is worth considerably more to me than a fast one that helps me dramatize a confusion.
