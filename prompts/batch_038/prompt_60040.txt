Just got out of a four hour argument with a friend about the Lucas-Penrose Gödel thing — that for any machine there's a sentence whose truth we can see but the machine cant prove, so minds aren't machines. Neither of us budged and I want a diagnosis, not a referee's whistle.

His take: the debate's gone nowhere since Lucas because nobody's bothered to formalise the step that actually matters. The anti-mechanist needs a human to be able to know that her own whole belief-state is sound, and Löb-type results are exactly the ones saying a system can't pull that off unless its inconsistent. So it's a formal question with a determinate answer that people just havent done.

My take: it's a verbal fight about what calling the mind a machine even commits you to. We never get handed the machine's program, so "we can see the truth of" never gets a determinate object and the argument never leaves the ground. And Penrose's quantum gravity stuff either turns it empirical or changes the subject entirely.

Which of us is closer — or are we both wrong about the shape of it? Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: formal, verbal, empirical, or two sides answering different questions under one heading. Ground it in what Lucas, Penrose and their critics actually argue, not in a list of the objections. Then be honest about whether anything could break the deadlock — a theorem, an experiment, a better account of what it is to know something, or nothing at all, and why.
