I'm giving a fifty-minute seminar talk with twenty-five minutes of discussion at a metaphysics-and-philosophy-of-mind department, and what I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

Here is the thesis. Writers on AI safety discuss a puzzle they call ontology identification. Suppose you build an agent whose goal is stated as: produce as much diamond as possible, where diamond is defined inside the agent's initial world-model as carbon atoms each covalently bound to four others. The agent then gets smarter and learns that its chemistry was a limiting case of something else — nuclear physics, quantum field theory, whatever the next revision turns out to be. Its goal-term was defined inside an ontology it has now discarded, and there is no general rule for reading off the number of diamonds from the internal representations of a system whose world-model differs arbitrarily from the one the goal was written in. The safety literature treats this as an open technical problem. My claim is that it is not technical, and that the technical framing hides where the real difficulty sits.

My argument, in the form it will appear on a slide:

1. Any goal an agent can be given is stated in the vocabulary of an ontology: a stock of entities plus a way of counting or ranking configurations of them.
2. For the agents we care about, radical revision of that ontology is not a failure mode but the expected course of events; an agent that could not revise its world-model would not be worth building.
3. When the ontology is revised, the extension of the goal-term under the new ontology is not fixed by the old term plus the new theory: many candidate extensions are compatible with everything the agent now knows and everything it knew before.
4. The information that would select among those candidates is not in the new evidence, and it is not in the old evidence either, since the old evidence fixed the term only relative to the ontology it was gathered under.
5. Nor is it in the head of whoever specified the goal. The specifier's term was fixed the same way — ostension, description, ordinary causal commerce with a world they too described in a doomed ontology — so there is no determinate fact about what the specifier meant for the agent to recover. This is the step I most expect to be attacked: the problem is not that the agent lacks accessible information about a determinate target, but that there is no determinate target for information to be about.
6. The standard repairs fail for a common reason: hedging across candidate ontologies needs a weighting over them that is itself unsettled; defining the goal by reference to whatever the agent's best future theory says makes the goal hostage to a variable the agent controls; retreating to observable proxies relocates the question to which observables, under which theory of observation.
7. So the target must be fixed by something other than a description the agent could write down or a fact about the specifier's psychology — most plausibly by a historical relation between the agent's concept and the practice that introduced it, together with a norm about which revisions preserve a term rather than replace it. That is a claim in the metaphysics of reference, and it is where I want the talk to land.

The room: about twenty people, mostly philosophers of language and mind who work on reference, natural kinds, mental content, or grounding, and who lean externalist. The departmental default is that causal-historical chains plus some appeal to naturalness settle most reference questions, so they will want to say I have dressed an old problem in new clothes. Two philosophers of physics attend, one of them a structural realist who will not grant that theory change is as discontinuous as premise 2 and premise 3 need. One machine-learning researcher who works on representation learning is coming out of curiosity and doubts any of this touches systems that exist. Nobody works on AI risk, several consider the field unserious, and there is a strong local reflex to file arguments like mine under applied problem rather than philosophy. I am a fourth-year graduate student and most of these people have taught me.

Please write the response in American English, in these sections and in this order:

1. The thesis as the room will hear it (about 150 words): restate my argument the way a sharp externalist in that audience would summarize it back to me, and flag which premise you expect to draw the most fire.
2. Concessions for the first five minutes (about 200 words): what I should give away up front to head off the this-is-just-the-qua-problem and this-is-just-an-engineering-problem reactions, and what I must refuse to concede on pain of losing the talk.
3. The objections, in the order they will arrive (about 1,400 words, the bulk of the answer): eight to ten entries, each with the objection stated in the objector's own idiom in one to three sentences; a tag for which part of the room raises it; a reply I can deliver aloud in about ninety seconds; and a one-line note on where that reply is soft or concedes more than I would like. Include at least one from the structural realist, at least one from the machine-learning researcher, and at least one arguing that my premises prove too much and collapse into a general skepticism about determinate content.
4. The question I am hoping nobody asks (about 200 words): the objection I have no good answer to, why it is damaging in this room specifically, and the least evasive way to absorb it.
5. Bottom line (about 100 words): the one claim the room has to accept for the talk to have worked, and the sentence I should end on.

Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; name positions, distinctions, and moves the room would recognize, but nothing I would have to look up.
