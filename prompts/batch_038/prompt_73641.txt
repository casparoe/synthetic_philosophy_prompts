I'm in the third year of a philosophy of language course and we've just spent three weeks on definite descriptions — Russell's analysis, then Donnellan's referential/attributive split, then Kripke's response about speaker's reference being a pragmatic thing. I did fine on the readings but when our TA asked me in section to just say what I actually think the correct account is, I realised I've got a bunch of gut verdicts that I've never tried to line up. So I want to write them all down and see if there's a principle underneath, or whether some of them have to go.

Here's what I actually believe, case by case. (1) Standing in my kitchen I say "the table is covered in books" and it's obviously true and obviously about my kitchen table, even though there are countless tables in the universe and nothing about my sentence pins down which one. (2) But put two tables in that kitchen, both covered in books, and now I don't want to call it true — asserting it feels like a mistake even though both candidate tables satisfy it. (3) At a party I point at a guy in the corner holding a martini glass and say "the man in the corner drinking a martini looks thrilled," and his glass actually has water in it. I'm confident I was talking about him, not about whoever, if anyone, really was drinking a martini. (4) Different setup: someone tells me there's a spy at the bar and that he's drinking a martini, and I say "the man drinking a martini is a spy" without having anybody in mind. Here I think the claim could come out true even if the guy I'd have pointed at is clean — it's about whoever actually satisfies it. (5) Smith's case: the detective says "Smith's murderer is insane" while Jones is in the dock, but the evidence against Jones is planted, and actually Smith killed himself so there is no murderer at all. I want to say the detective's claim is flatly false, not neither-true-nor-false. Yet if instead there *had* been some unknown murderer, I want to say the same claim is true and about that unknown person, not about Jones. (6) Here's the one that clashes with (3): I say "the woman who won the marathon set a course record," meaning my friend, because I was told she won — but she actually came second. My gut says this is about the real winner and it's false. Why does pointing at a guy make the description go referential, but hearing about a woman not? I suspect the answer is something about perceptual salience and that feels like an ad hoc patch, so tell me straight if it is one. (7) "The present king of France is bald" is not true, but I don't feel great calling it simply false, and "the present king of France is not bald" also seems not-true on one reading and true on another, and the two sentences look like they contradict each other, which bugs me. (8) "The number of planets is odd" seems perfectly fine and doesn't feel like it's about any object in the way my friend's name is about her, but if nothing is being picked out, why does swapping in a different co-referential or equally abstract description inside "Mary believes the number of planets is odd" change the truth value? (9) And our TA threw "the average American has 2.4 children" at us — nobody would deny that sentence, but it can't be about a person, and it isn't literally true of anyone, so some of these expressions aren't even trying to refer.

So: is there one account of what a definite description contributes semantically, plus what context does, that delivers all nine of those verdicts? Give me a small number of candidate accounts, each stated precisely enough that I could tell what it predicts, and say which of my cases motivates which clause. Then run the best candidate (or two, if it's genuinely a tie) on cases I haven't listed — e.g. "Mary wants to marry the richest man in town" where I can't tell whether there's someone in particular she wants to marry, and a sentence about a character inside a novel, where the description is empty in reality but true in the story. And be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up rather than quietly reinterpreting it. Finally, please keep separate what's actually settled here from what people who work on this still fight about — if there's a live debate between, say, a unitary Russellian-plus-context story and a genuinely ambiguity-based one, say so and don't dress up one camp as the consensus. And where your preferred account leans on a contested reading of what Russell or Donnellan or Kripke was up to, flag that too, since I don't want to walk into section repeating a reading my tutor would push back on.
