I cover labor and technology for a general-interest magazine, mostly the science-and-ideas beat, which means I'm the person who ends up writing the 3,500-word "what did that actually mean" piece rather than the news peg itself. The peg right now is that California's Prop 22 has survived everything thrown at it: the legislature passed AB5 in 2019 and swept app drivers into employee status under a three-part test, the platforms went around the legislature and put their own measure on the November 2020 ballot, spent over $200 million on it, won with about 58 percent, and then in July 2024 the state supreme court unanimously upheld it. What the measure did was keep drivers as independent contractors while bolting on a compromise package — a healthcare stipend tied to engaged hours, accident insurance, a guarantee of 120 percent of the local minimum wage for time spent actually on a trip, and a clause making it very hard for the legislature to amend any of it later. My editor's note in the margin was "don't do flexibility-versus-security again, everybody does flexibility-versus-security." So I've been re-reading Berlin on the two concepts and then Pettit's republican correction on planes, and I want to find out whether the three-way distinction actually does work on this case or whether it's just decoration.

Here's what I'm after. Analyze the AB5-to-Prop-22 episode as a case in the three conceptions of liberty — negative (non-interference), positive (real capacity for self-direction), and republican (non-domination, being free from another's arbitrary will whether or not they happen to exercise it). Be disciplined about the mapping: who are the players, and not just the companies and the drivers but which drivers, since the full-timers and the side-income people wanted opposite things; plus the legislature, an electorate voting on somebody else's employment status, and the courts. What does each conception think the options were, and what does each say the drivers had before and what they have after? Then get into the weeds on the domination charge, because that's where I think the story is. My best interview is a guy who says, sincerely, that he's freer than his brother with the salaried job, because he can simply not log in — and who also got deactivated from one app once with no explanation given, spent three days trying to get an answer out of a support form, and now runs three apps simultaneously so that nobody can hurt him. Does the republican account get that guy right? Does a standing capacity to deactivate him, mostly unexercised, make him unfree on the ordinary days when nothing happens? Does multi-apping kill the charge, since his exit is instant and costless — or does the republican have a real reply, something like the point that his outside option is made of the same kind of power three times over? I want you to press on whether the arbitrariness criterion, the idea that interference only dominates when it fails to track the interests and opinions of the person interfered with, does any genuine work here or is just a convenient place to hide a judgment you were going to make anyway.

And then tell me where the frame breaks. My own suspicion is that the labels invert: the side campaigning in the pure language of negative liberty — you keep the right to log on whenever you like — was also the side delivering a package of state-mandated benefits and locking the legislature out, while the labor side's core complaint was about arbitrary power over individuals, which is supposed to be the republican's distinctive insight rather than Berlin's. So does the episode show that Berlin's warning about positive liberty sliding into coercion points the wrong way in platform politics, or is there a version of the warning that survives contact with a $200 million ballot campaign? Is non-domination in danger of being unfalsifiable here — if any relationship of dependency counts as domination, does it stop distinguishing gig work from ordinary at-will employment, which is most employment in America anyway? Say where the theory is silent rather than making it speak. Finally, tell me what the frame predicts, since that's what will keep it from being decoration in print: given the analysis, what should I expect in the next round — other states, federal rulemaking, the same fight in a different costume — and which one concrete detail of this episode should I open the piece with so readers get the distinction without ever hearing a term of art. No bibliography, I've got the books, I just want the argument.
