I've been editing Wikipedia for eight years and our sourcing rules have started nagging at me. The setup: nothing goes in unless it's been published somewhere reliable — it doesn't matter that you know it's true or could show it yourself, and you're not even allowed to synthesize two sources into a conclusion nobody has published. Then there's the loop: we're told not to cite Wikipedia in Wikipedia, and more and more the "reliable published sources" for a fact turn out to be rehashes of each other or of us to begin with.

So what picture of justification is the whole apparatus running on? Is the reliable-published-source acting as a foundation that stops the regress, or is the real structure coherentist — a giant web of articles and sources holding each other up with nothing basic anywhere — or is it secretly infinitist, the chain just going forever while we cut it off with an arbitrary stopping rule? Point at where different rules seem to assume different answers, like how one decent source carries a fact about a 19th-century railway but medicine and living-person claims get a much harsher standard, and how we lean on peer review and fact-checking desks whose own authority we never look inside of. Tell me which parts are genuinely underdetermined, where more than one epistemology would license the identical rule so the rule proves nothing. And finish with what would change your mind: which feature of the practice, described differently, would flip you from one theory to another, and where you think your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong.
