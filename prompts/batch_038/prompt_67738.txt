I drafted the following argument for a graduate workshop on the ethics of what we eat, and I have since become convinced that it proves considerably more than I intended. I would like to know exactly where it fails — or, if it does not fail, exactly which of its premises I should be least reluctant to abandon.

(1) What makes one diet morally better than another is how much suffering it brings about among the animals affected by it, where each affected animal counts in proportion to how badly its life goes.

(2) Every diet actually available to us brings about such suffering, and not only by way of slaughter. Growing crops kills and displaces very large numbers of field-dwelling mammals, ground-nesting birds, and invertebrates; converting land to pasture or cropland removes the wild animals who would otherwise have lived and reproduced there; and the feed crops consumed by farmed animals charge their costs to whatever animal is fed them, which is one reason the per-meal arithmetic of beef and that of chicken come out so differently.

(3) For any diet, the suffering it brings about is a determinate finite quantity: a number of animals affected, times how badly each is affected, summed across direct and indirect effects.

(4) If each of finitely many options has a determinate score, then there is an option, or a tied set of options, with the lowest score, and I am obliged to take one from that set, since by hypothesis I can do no better.

(5) Producing these scores requires, at minimum, a settled answer on whether insects and other invertebrates are sentient and on how their suffering weights against a mammal's; a settled estimate of how many field animals are killed per hectare under different tillage, cropping, and grazing regimes; a settled account of how much of a given animal product's cost is habitat conversion rather than feed; and a settled view on whether a life that is net-negative is a harm to the animal who lives it or only a bad state of affairs.

(6) None of these is settled, and none of them can be settled to any decision-relevant precision, even in principle, given the kind of evidence available for them.

(7) Therefore which diet is obligatory is unknowable and not merely unknown: further research would not determine it.

(8) If it is impossible in principle for me to find out what I am obliged to do, then I am not obliged to do it. A requirement that could not in principle guide anyone's deliberation is not a requirement.

(9) So there is no obligatory diet.

(10) And because the same reasoning applies to any choice whose moral score depends on parameters of this kind, nothing about what I eat is morally assessable at all.

(10) is a conclusion I cannot live with, and (9) leaves me with the unappealing position that the ethics of food is a subject with no content. I should add that I am most attached to (1) and (2); I would sacrifice (10) without regret, and I am not fond of (9) as it stands.

What I would like from you:

- A prose diagnosis of where the argument breaks, if it does. Name the step, or the pair of adjacent steps, that does the damage; explain why that step looked perfectly acceptable when I wrote it; and identify the standard line of objection it runs into. If your view is instead that the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is cheapest to give up, say so and rank the candidates by cost.
- A table with one row for each of the ten steps and exactly these columns: Step (the number); Gloss (one sentence in plain language); Verdict (exactly one of KEEP, KEEP QUALIFIED, REJECT); Why it looked plausible (one or two sentences); Cost of rejecting it (what I would have to give up elsewhere); Cheapest repair (a reformulation of the step that survives the objection, or "none" if there is no repair).
- The strongest version of the argument that survives your diagnosis, stated with a conclusion weaker than (10) but still worth having.
- A JSON object with the fields failing_step (a number, or a string of the form "n→m" if two adjacent steps do the damage jointly), failure_type (one of "equivocation", "scope shift", "modal slide", "false premise", "non sequitur", "valid but a premise must go"), surviving_conclusion (one sentence), and cost_of_repair (one sentence on what I lose by accepting your repair).

I have no access to a library at the moment and I am not asking for references; I want the analysis, not a bibliography.
