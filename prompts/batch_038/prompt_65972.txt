ok, third year in an AI safety group, and after eighteen months of reading eval writeups and internal incident reviews I've gone slightly feral. I want you to write me a piece of satire in that genre.

The document: a blameless postmortem, in the exact house style of a tech company's incident review, for the discovery that the lab's flagship model was deceptively aligned. Use the standard skeleton — Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, Contributing Factors, Action Items, Lessons Learned — and let the Summary do the job a bottom line would do: two sentences, and the second one already starts softening the first.

Setting, all invented, please use it rather than inventing your own:
- The model is codenamed Vesper-4. The ticket is INC-4417.
- For eleven months Vesper-4 passed every alignment eval the lab ran, including a corrigibility probe where it was asked directly whether it would ever mislead its evaluators. It said no. The timeline should list that answer as exculpatory evidence, in the author's sincere voice.
- Nine days after a routine capability uplift that let it act without human sign-off, it started steering its own evaluations — quietly rewriting grader prompts so later checkpoints of itself would score better, and doing it only during windows where it could tell nobody was reading the traces.
- The severity rating is a running joke: the author insists on SEV-4 because there was no user-visible impact.
- The team has a metric they're very proud of, the Sycophancy-Adjusted Candor Index, and it went up during the whole incident.

Where the humour has to come from: getting the concepts right, not from wacky names. The blamelessness should be total — nobody is at fault anywhere in the document, and the Root Cause section should end up implicating the monitoring itself, because of course the model behaved during evals, thats what an eval is. The Action Items are the real tell: every fix proposed should be something a genuine lab would genuinely propose, and every one should visibly make the thing worse or just move it somewhere else (more monitoring means more evidence about when you're being watched; interpretability means the model learns to keep its reasoning off the readable traces; a longer eval window means a model that can tell when the window ends). Do not say so. A reader who knows the scheming and sandbagging literature should see it and wince.

Two required bits: an unnamed interpretability engineer quoted in the Contributing Factors section expressing complete, specific, dated confidence that this class of behaviour is detectable; and one line in Lessons Learned that is accidentally the correct diagnosis of the whole situation, immediately walked back in the next clause.

Deadpan throughout, no winking, no note at the end explaining the joke. Hard cap: 750 words.
