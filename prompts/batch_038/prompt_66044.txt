I am in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy of science, and my department produces a short printed magazine each spring for graduate students, faculty, and a good many alumni who spent their working lives in astronomy. This year's theme is evidence and inference, and the editor has asked me for something lighter than a research summary. I would like your help drafting it. I will run the piece under my own name with a line noting that the firm concerned is imaginary.

What I want is a satirical letter of complaint, roughly 750 words, in continuous prose, from a customer to the customer-relations department of a firm that sells ready-made universes off the shelf. The unit the customer received was advertised as life-permitting and does in fact support them; the complaint is not that the product failed but that the customer has lost confidence in what the firm's assurances were ever worth. The tone should be that of an impeccably polite, slightly litigious correspondent who is scrupulously fair to the seller and concedes everything the seller is entitled to.

The letter should make six moves, in this order, and each of them should be a real philosophical point rendered entirely in the vocabulary of a retail dispute. First, the customer recalls the salesman's reassurance at the point of sale: with a warehouse as large as ours, effectively unbounded in stock, at least one unit was always going to suit you. At the time this was comforting; now the customer cannot stop turning it over, because it seems to say something about the stockroom and nothing at all about the particular unit that arrived, and what the customer actually wanted to know was whether that unit would have supported them had they not happened to be standing in the shop. Second, the firm's written reply, quoted at length, invokes observation selection effects: a customer who was not delivered a life-permitting unit is not in a position to file a complaint, so the complaint is void on its face. Third, the firm's terms contain a clause conceding that with an unbounded stockroom no probability is well defined until a measure over the units is specified, and declining, on commercial grounds, to specify one. Fourth, the customer reports that several of their neighbours turned out to be brief, spontaneously assembled beings with no pasts and no futures, whose own complaints the firm dismisses as void from the outset, and asks whether the warranty covers only customers who persist. Fifth, the customer objects that the workmanship looks shoddy rather than deliberate: the dial governing the cosmological constant is set to within something like one part in ten to the hundred and twentieth, there are a great many such dials, each confined to a narrow band, and the customer regards a product that functions only because it has been propped up from every direction as evidence of poor engineering rather than of a proud craftsman. They add, more quietly, that a much larger region of the dial-space apparently permits the production of black holes than permits the production of them, and so they have begun to suspect the unit was calibrated for somebody else's convenience and that they are a by-product. Sixth, in one exasperated paragraph, the customer asks who calibrated the calibration, and receives a single sentence saying that the question falls outside the scope of the warranty, together with a reference to the Anthropic Waiver the customer signed at purchase without reading.

Running underneath all of this should be a rival firm, mentioned three or four times in passing, which sells bespoke, hand-calibrated, one-of-a-kind cosmologies at ruinous expense. The customer finds the competitor's advertising distasteful and their salespeople insufferable, and admits, reluctantly and at increasing length, that the firm cannot show the competitor's claims to be false. The engine of the satire is that every complaint the customer files against the warehouse model sounds more like an argument for the competitor, and the customer very much does not want to buy from the competitor. The letter should end on a demand that the firm plainly cannot satisfy, and the reader should be able to see why it cannot.

The target is a style of reassurance and a dialectic, not a person: no individual, living or dead, should be identifiable anywhere in the piece. Do not include citations, footnotes, a bibliography, or any reference to actual literature, and do not let the customer name a single technical term or philosopher; the customer should be right about everything without ever sounding as though they know the vocabulary. The jargon may appear only inside the firm's quoted replies, where it should be used correctly and deployed to explain why nothing can be done. Keep it deadpan: no winking, no asides to the reader, no sentence that tells us the situation is absurd.

As for presentation, please write the whole thing as a letter in running prose, with the salutation and the sign-off given as ordinary sentences. No headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no numbered lists; even the firm's quoted replies should be worked into the customer's own sentences rather than set off as indented extracts. I would like to be able to paste it straight into the magazine without touching the layout.
