a pantoum, four quatrains, so 16 lines, for the start of next week's session in my third-year philosophy of perception seminar — we're doing disjunctivism and i want to read them something instead of handing out the usual summary. form details matter: lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and in the final stanza the first stanza's line 3 returns as line 2 and the first stanza's opening line comes back as line 4, so the poem ends on its own beginning. rhyme ABAB if you can without making the argument stupid, near-rhyme is fine. the recurring lines should come back word-for-word identical every time — all the shifting has to be done by what surrounds them.

the form is the content, that's the whole reason i want a pantoum and not a sonnet. the two lines that keep returning should carry the highest-common-factor move: a first-person subject reporting a case (a straight stick half in water, or a coin gone elliptical at an angle) and then something along the lines of 'it looks to me exactly as it would if i were seeing it straight' and 'so there has to be something the same in both'. first pass those lines are innocent, just description. second pass the stanzas around them let them do the slide toward sense-data. third pass the new lines refuse the inference — what keeps coming back is only that i can't tell the cases apart from where i'm sitting, which is a fact about my not being able to tell, not a fact about a shared ingredient in the two episodes. fourth pass the loop closes on the negative line: the bad case has no positive character of its own beyond its being impossible to tell from the good one.

ground rules. no names anywhere in the poem and no term-dropping — don't use 'sense-data' or 'naive realism' as jewelry. keep illusion and hallucination visibly seperate somewhere in there, because i want them to notice those are two different arguments and not one: with illusion you can at least insist the stick genuinely does look-bent-from-here, whereas with hallucination there's nothing left to hang the look on, and that second case is the one disjunctivism lives or dies on. also — my TA is convinced that negative disjunctivism is committed to hallucinations having no phenomenal character at all, full stop, and i dont want the poem to settle that by fiat. let the closing turn be a claim someone in the room could reasonably resist rather than a definition dressed up as a discovery. and don't let the last stanza land as a win. the worry that in the good case the object isn't doing any of the explanatory work, that all the work is being done by its merely seeming that way to the subject — that should still be hanging in the air when the opening line comes back around.
