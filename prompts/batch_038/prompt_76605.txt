I've been going around in circles on Moore's paradox and there's a wrinkle that the standard summaries don't seem to touch. The usual set-up: "It's raining but I don't believe it" is the omissive form, "It's raining but I believe it isn't" is the commissive form, and some people argue the omissive one is the lesser problem becuase it can come out literally true — the speaker might simply have never formed any belief about the weather, so nothing she says is false, it's just... infelicitous.

But then look at these five, all said by the same person, and stipulate that in every case she's describing her own psychology accurately:

1. "It's raining, though I've not really formed any view about whether it is."
2. "It's raining, but if you asked me to put a number on it I'd say 60%."
3. "It's raining, but I don't know that it is."
4. "It's raining, but I don't know that for certain."
5. "It's raining, though I might be wrong about that."

To my ear (1) and (3) are genuinely awful, (2) is uncomfortable but not absurd, and (4) and (5) are completely fine — people say things like that all day, nobody blinks. And that pattern is what I can't explain.

So: can you work out what's actually doing the separating? A few things I specifically want addressed —

- Why the "she's just suspending judgment" escape hatch fails to rescue (1). Suspension is supposed to be the innocent case, the one that makes the omissive form merely odd rather than paradoxical, but (1) sounds paradoxical anyway. What's the difference between suspending judgment and saying you've suspended judgment while asserting?
- What the (3)/(4) pair is telling us. They differ only by two words, and the whole thing flips. If asserting P commits you to knowing P, then (3) should be as bad as the original Moore sentence — but then why is (4) fine, when it seems to concede the same thing? Is the word "know" being read differently in the two, and does that reading do any real philosophical work or is it just an artifact of English?
- Whether (2) sits where a credence-based norm of assertion would predict it sits, or whether its badness has a different source entirely.
- Then the bigger question: does the pattern across all five suggest the absurdity lives in the content of what's said, or in what saying it represents about the speaker's own state of mind? I want you to commit to an answer rather than laying out both sides, and to say what an account has to get right to cover all five at once — ideally something a rival account would predict differently about, so I can see the accounts actually pulling apart.

Assume I know the basics already (the transparency of belief, the commissive/omissive distinction, assertion norms, that sort of thing) — don't spend words re-establishing those.
