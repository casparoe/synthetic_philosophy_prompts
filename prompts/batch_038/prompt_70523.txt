Ok so I got into a roughly two hour argument with a friend last night and I still can't tell who won, so I want a neutral party to lay it out for me. He's a nurse anesthetist, I'm not — I do software, philosophy is a hobby.

What we were arguing about is what the actual practice of anethesia quietly assumes about consciousness. Two parts of it I keep turning over.

First, the number. There's a monitor that takes the EEG and spits out a single number, 0 to 100, and during surgery they aim to keep it somewhere in the 40s and 50s. Nobody in the room pretends the number is measuring experience — it's calibrated against behavior, basically whether you respond to a command and whether you'll retain anything afterward. And with some drugs the number goes the wrong way entirely, climbing when the patient is supposedly going deeper. His take: that's proof there's nothing else there to find, the field has been looking for decades and everything it ever turns up is arousal and reportability, so consciousness just is whatever capacity-to-respond-and-report the number tracks. My take: that's precisely what you'd expect if the felt part were real but invisible and you were forced to go at it through proxies.

Second, the components. The drugs get described as doing several seperate jobs — abolishing pain, paralyzing muscle, blocking memory formation, and knocking out awareness — and the paralysis one obviously does nothing whatsoever to consciousness. Then for something like a colonoscopy they'll give you a drug whose main contribution is the memory block; you're drowsy, possibly tracking what's going on, you'll have no recollection after, and everyone counts that as a success. No incident report, no ethics consult.

And yet the same field treats awareness under paralysis — awake, in pain, unable to move, may or may not remember — as among the worst things that can happen to a patient, and spends real money and real monitoring effort to prevent it.

So what theory of consciousness does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that there's nothing over and above responsiveness and reportability, or that there is something over and above that we can only reach indirectly? I want it broken down: which specific features of the practice strictly require which assumption, and which features are merely compatible with several. My sense is that the amnesic-but-awake colonoscopy and the panic about intraoperative awareness are pulling in opposite directions, and I want to know whether some single view licenses both or whether the practice is just not philosophically coherent and doesn't need to be.

Related: if the practice is comfortable with you having an experience you'll never retain, it seems to be assuming that what makes an experience bad for you depends partly on your later relation to it. Is that a seperate commitment from the one about what consciousness is, or does one follow from the other? And what would concretely have to change in an operating room if you swapped in the opposite assumption — that a moment of pain is just as bad for the person even if it leaves no trace at all?

Please don't just hand me a label like 'its physicalist' and stop. I want the reconstruction, where the practice is genuinely underdetermined between rival accounts, and where you think my friend is overreading his own evidence.
