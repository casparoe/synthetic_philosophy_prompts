I'm putting together a handout for a seminar where the students have read the personal identity chapters of Parfit's Reasons and Persons — including his suggestion that his reductionism about persons is close to the Buddhist view — and essentially nothing Buddhist. I want one designed case, not a survey, and I want it to land a point they won't get from the reading: that both sides can deny a self and still disagree about what the denial is worth.

Build me a paired thought experiment to these specs:

1. Two cases, a paragraph each, one continuously existing human being in each. No fission, no teleporter, no uploading, no rebirth into a different body, no hells or pure lands. Any technology allowed has to be modest and ordinary — a clinic, a drug, a rehab program.
2. The two cases are matched on Relation R: same stretch of time, same strength of psychological connectedness and continuity, same memories, intentions and character traits, and the same ordinary kind of cause holding the chain together.
3. They're also matched on the stuff a secular reader would call wellbeing: equally content, equally functional, same relationships, same projects, same level of reported happiness.
4. They differ on exactly one thing — whether each link in the chain is accompanied by appropriation, the clinging-and-identification stance toward the aggregates that the Buddhist tradition treats as the thing actually causing suffering. Pervasive in one case, absent in the other.
5. Neither subject knows they're in a comparison, and nothing may turn on anyone being told a doctrine.

Then show the work: state the verdict Parfit's account gives in each case and why it has to be the same verdict in both, and state the verdict the no-self analysis gives in each and why they come apart. Explain the mechanism in plain English, at most two Pali terms, each glossed the first time.

Then a third variant, briefly: the same clinging-free chain, but the absence of appropriation was installed by an implant rather than arrived at by practice. Say whether that breaks the Buddhist verdict, the Parfitian one, both, or neither.

Finally be honest with me. Which feature of the pair is actually doing the work, and does the divergence refute Parfit's convergence claim or just show the two are answering different questions — survival and what matters versus liberation? Give the strongest Parfitian attack on the case (my guess is that it smuggles a welfare claim into a criterion of survival and so changes the subject) and the strongest reply.

The whole thing should come out of the theories, not be stipulated into them — if a verdict only appears becuase I wrote it into the scenario, the case fails. No citations, no reading lists, no invented sources. Keep it under about 800 words total.
