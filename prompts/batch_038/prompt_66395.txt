Been meaning to ask someone about this for ages. I teach a philosophy of history module and every year I dutifully put on the Hempel versus Dray material — the covering-law model of historical explanation — and every year I come away feeling like the whole thing ended in a weird way, not the way a philosophical argument normally ends.

Here's the pattern as I see it. Hempel says historical explanation, to be a real explanation, has to subsume the event under general laws, or at least under general hypotheses that the historian doesn't bother stating. The objections all land pretty fast and, as far as I can tell, all of them are well known by now: historians simply don't cite laws when they explain anything; explaining an agent's action by their reasons is a different kind of thing entirely; the laws get produced on demand and turn out to be trivial or gerrymandered to fit the case; narrative does explanatory work by itself, through the ordering and the choice of where to start and stop, without any laws in sight. Hempel concedes ground — statistical explanation, sketchy laws, "explanation sketches" — and by the mid-eighties everybody treats the model as dead. But nobody wrote the obituary. There was never a decisive refutation that anyone signed up to, and the field just walked off to talk about other things.

So: what sort of disagreement was this, actually? My hunch — offered as a hunch, please pull it apart if it's wrong — is that it was never one debate. Maybe Hempel was answering a question about the logical form any successful explanation must have, and his opponents were answering a question about what makes a historical account intelligible to a reader, and the word "explanation" did all the work of hiding that. Or maybe the split is over "law" itself: Hempel was happy with any true general statement, his critics kept demanding something with real predictive and projectible content, and once you seperate those two senses most of the arguments stop connecting. There's a third suspicion too, which is that both sides were arguing over examples philosophers invented — toy cases about dry rot and the Dust Bowl and a monarch's decision-making — rather than over what historians actually write, which would explain why nobody could ever settle it by looking.

What I want is the diagnosis, with reasons, not another tour of the literature: was this substantive, verbal, a case of two questions wearing one name, a hidden methodological disagreement about which texts count as data, or just a very hard question that got abandoned early? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would have counted as progress — a distinction someone could have drawn, a serious empirical study of working historians' prose, a formal account of what a narrative does, a change of subject — or whether nothing could have, and it died for institutional reasons rather than logical ones. My own guess is the second, that analytic philosophy of history just lost its audience, but convince me either way.

British English for the answer, please.
