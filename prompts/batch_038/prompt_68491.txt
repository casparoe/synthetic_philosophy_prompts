So I've been going back and forth with my brother-in-law about my retirement money and I can't seem to make him understand what I actually object to, and honestly I'm not sure I understand it myself either. Background: I'm 41, I do freight logistics in Columbus, and I finally have real savings, about $80k, which for me is a lot. Two ways I could set it up. Plan A: all of it in a cheap index fund, don't touch it for close to thirty years. Plan B: $55k in that same fund and $25k in short-term government bonds that I promise myself I never sell, as a floor. My brother-in-law works in wealth management and he ran both. Over that stretch plan A comes out a hundred and forty grand or so ahead on average, and — this is the part that matters — the floor in plan B doesn't protect me from anything he can name, because even in the bad scenarios, like a lost decade in the market landing exactly when I want to start drawing it down, my actual life looks nearly the same under both plans. I paid a fee-only planner separately to check that and she agreed with him: worst realistic case under plan A, I delay redoing the kitchen and I can't help my daughter as much with a down payment. Nothing structural. I still own the house, I still eat fine, I'm still okay.

And here's what he can't accept. He says there are only two reasons anybody ever turns down a bet that comes out ahead on average. Either the money's worth less to you at the margin, so the upside doesn't buy as much as you think it does — but I just told you, and his own planner told me, that the outcomes barely differ in any scenario. Or you've got the odds wrong, you're quietly inflating the chance of disaster — but I don't, I use his numbers, I think the market is fine. He says those are the only two knobs on the machine, so if I'm not turning either one then I'm not holding a position, I'm making an error, or I'm frightened and dressing it up. He also likes to bring up that at 26 I flew to another country on a one-way ticket with no job and no plan, which he takes as proof that I'm obviously not a careful person in general, so this has to be a mood. But it doesn't feel like fear and it isn't a mood. What I want — and I know how stupid this sounds said out loud — is for it to be true that there is no way the next thirty years go where I'm not okay. I don't want whether I'm okay to be downstream of something I can't influence and can't repair afterward, even though being not-okay in the bad case would, by everyone's reckoning including mine, be pretty mild.

So: is there anything to say on my side, or is he right that I'm backed into an inconsistency? I'm not after the usual nobody-can-tell-you-what-to-do-with-your-money shrug. I want to know whether what I'm describing is an actual view someone could hold consistently, the way you can consistently hold a view about breaking a promise or about who counts in your calculations, or whether it dissolves into a phobia the second you spell it out like this. And if it is a real view, does it survive the fact that I was perfectly happy to take a much bigger risk when I was 26 — because that one I genuinely cannot answer, and its the thing he reaches for every single time.
