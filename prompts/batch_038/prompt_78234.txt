I am at work on a novel set in Florence in the 1490s, mostly in and around a merchant household, and one of my principals is the Dominican confessor who advises that household in matters of money. He is a schoolman. He has read Aristotle on the household and on the two kinds of getting wealth, and he holds that money exists by convention as a measure for exchange, that it is by nature sterile, that taking increase out of money itself is therefore the furthest thing from nature, that a man who charges for the use of something whose use is its consumption has sold the same thing twice, and that getting wealth for the sake of the household has a limit while getting wealth for its own sake has none. I want his judgments to be consistent with one another and to run on that sort of reasoning, not on economic ideas no one in his century could have had.

I have already written his verdicts on eight matters, and I have given him a terse reason for each, as a confessor would give it in the box rather than in a lecture.

A. A widow lent a neighbor twenty florins for a year and asked for twenty-two on the day. Forbidden, and he required the two florins restored before absolution: she sold the wine and she sold the drinking of it.

B. The same woman put her twenty florins into her cousin's venture to the Levant for a share of whatever came back, gain or loss, with nothing written under her share. Permitted, and warmly. His words: here she goes to sea too.

C. A grain dealer bought three small holders' next harvest forward, at a cash price well under what the wheat usually fetches, and sold the contracts on before the reaping, taking no grain into his own house. Forbidden. The parchment says purchase, he told me to write, but the profit is the same in a fat year and a lean one, and a thing whose profit does not care what the weather does is not a purchase.

D. A matched pair. For four hundred florins the household bought the right to twenty-five florins a year forever out of the produce of a named olive grove, with the grove standing as security: permitted, because the money has bought into a thing that bears fruit. The identical arrangement secured on nothing but a notary's promise and his future earnings: forbidden, because a word does not bear fruit.

E. A lender's clause charging an extra florin for every month of delay past the due date. Permitted only under conditions he insisted on: the delay must be real and not contrived by the lender, the penalty must be sworn to match what the delay actually cost, and no loan may be treated as late from its first day. A man may be made whole for what waiting cost him; he may not be paid for having waited.

F. Another matched pair. A shipper paying a premium to underwriters who take the peril of the sea at the rate the market sets: permitted. A lender taking out a policy on his own loan at a premium exactly equal to the interest he would otherwise have charged: forbidden — a loan with the peril painted on.

G. The friars' public pawnshop for the poor, which takes a small sum on every loan to cover its clerks' wages and the rent of its strongroom. Permitted, though it is a charge on a loan of cash, because the poor man pays for a service actually rendered and the house keeps its doors open. He added that the same charge taken by a rich man on a rich man's loan would be another matter entirely.

H. A merchant whose paperwork was flawless — partnership shares, forward sales at fair prices, nothing to fault — and whom he nonetheless refused absolution, saying that the fault was not in the parchment but in the appetite: the man had begun with enough and could not tell him what enough would be.

The case I now need him to decide is this. The household proposes to leave two thousand ducats with a banker who does business at the fairs, returnable in full on demand, with nothing whatever said about payment in the instrument. For three years the banker has sent a voluntary gratuity of about five percent, described by both men as a mark of friendship, promised nowhere and claimed by no one. The merchant says plainly that he would leave the sum where it is even if the gratuity stopped. The banker's partners have signed an undertaking to make the whole sum good out of their own goods should the bank fail. The merchant's stated end is bounded and, as I have written him, honest: his wife's physicians these two years past, his daughter's dowry, and to leave trade within three years for his farm. He can name the alum contract he declined in order to keep the sum liquid. And his factor remarks that two other families on the street have been doing the same thing for years.

What I want is not history and not a bibliography — please give me no citations, no reading list, and no titles of works, and gloss any technical term you need in a phrase. I want reasoning I can put in a man's mouth. Specifically:

1. Predict his verdict on this arrangement, and say which of A through H is its closest analog and where that analogy gives out.
2. Lay out the candidate principles his eight verdicts admit — I suspect at least three, perhaps one keyed to who bears the peril of the principal, one keyed to whether any fruitful thing stands behind the contract, one keyed to compensation for a loss or a gain genuinely forgone, one keyed to looking through the paperwork to the intention, and one keyed to the end of the getting rather than the instrument. For each, show which precedents it delivers cleanly and which it must strain, reinterpret, or abandon.
3. Say which of them you think he is in fact running, and why the record favors it — and, for each of the two strongest candidates, name the single detail of the new case that does the most work for it and what changing that detail would do to the verdict, since I intend to write a second scene in which the merchant comes back with the arrangement amended.
4. State honestly what he commits himself to either way. If he forbids it, what principle has he adopted that will embarrass him later in the book? If he permits it, which of A through H does he now have to re-read or quietly walk back — and if my set of precedents is not in fact jointly consistent, tell me so and tell me which one a man of his training would most plausibly abandon.
5. How much of his reasoning here can genuinely be traced to Aristotle's own account of money and exchange, and how much is later pastoral casuistry wearing his authority? I want to know where my character is quoting his master and where he is quoting his profession.
6. Finally, propose one further case, invented in the same world and the same register as A through H, that would decisively separate the two leading candidate principles — something small enough to happen at a dinner table, so that I can use it as the scene in which the reader, and the merchant, find out which man my confessor actually is.

Please write your answer in American English. If any detail of my setup is historically implausible in a way that would break the scene, say so in a sentence or two at the end; otherwise leave the history alone and work within it.
