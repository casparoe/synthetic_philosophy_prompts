For my dissertation chapter on moral dilemmas I need a case built to a pretty fussy spec, because everything I've drafted so far falls apart the second I show it to my advisor. The chapter's target is the standard deflationary line: there are no genuine dilemmas, there are just two prima facie oughts plus a tiebreak, you rank them, the stronger one wins all-things-considered, and the "dilemma" evaporates. I keep accidentally writing cases that either rank cleanly (so the deflationist is right) or that are secretly prudence-vs-morality conflicts, which she says is a completely different debate. So I want a scenario that survives the ranking move intact.

The specs, all of which I need actually met rather than quietly relaxed:

- realistic setting — a hospital ethics committee, a small firm, a family, a school district, whatever you like, but no trolleys, no lifeboats, no transplant surgeons, no one holding a relative hostage, and no promise made to a dying parent;
- both oughts are moral, and both are all-things-considered rather than merely pro tanto;
- their strengths have to be incomparable, not just unequal, so that the ranking strategy has nothing to grip onto — if a scale could be laid under them, the case is dead;
- the agent is fully informed: no missing fact whose discovery would dissolve the bind, and the dilemma can't come from uncertainty about consequences that could be resolved by looking;
- clean hands: the bind can't be traceable to the agent's own earlier wrongdoing, since the deflationists will happily say a self-inflicted mess isn't a dilemma, its a debt;
- exactly two live options, plus a third option that is clearly worse than both, so the "there's always another way out" reply has somewhere to go and visibly fails;
- an appropriate residue on the losing side — something owed, or a reactive attitude that genuinely fits, not just a bad feeling about how the world is;
- the scenario itself under 250 words.

Then walk me through it. State each of the two oughts and why each is all-things-considered rather than pro tanto; show precisely where the ranking strategy gets traction and where it stalls; and give the best deflationist reply about the residue — I expect something like "the residue is agent-regret about the way the world turned out, not a surviving obligation" — and say whether that reply is actually available in your case or only sounds available. I'd personally find it most convincing if the residue is something the agent can be held to (an explanation owed, a repair owed) rather than an inner state, so build toward that if you can. Also tell me which single feature of the case is doing the anti-dissolution work, what the case degrades into if I delete that feature, and whether the scenario forces any revision of standard deontic principles (agglomeration and the like) or whether it can be stated without touching the logic at all. If one of my specs turns out to be unsatisfiable in combination with the others, tell me which one and why instead of fudging it — that would actually be useful for the chapter too.
