**Does the cruel man have a reason not to be cruel? Three internalisms, not one**

**§1.** A man has been cruel to his wife for twenty years. Asked why he does not stop, he says — sincerely, and after reflection — that her suffering moves him to nothing, and that he has considered the matter and finds no purchase in himself on it. The internal reasons theory, as Bernard Williams developed it, is standardly taken to deliver a verdict about this man: he has no reason to stop. From which it is supposed to follow either that we must accept a relativism about reasons that lets cruelty off the hook, or that we must abandon internalism. My aim is to show that this dilemma is manufactured, and manufactured by a conflation of three claims that ought to be kept apart.

**§2.** First, what the internal reasons theory says. On Williams' account, the claim that A has a reason to φ is true only if A could arrive at the motivation to φ by a sound deliberative route from A's actual motivational set — his S. Two features of the formulation are habitually flattened. The claim is counterfactual rather than actualist: nothing requires A to feel any tug now, and the theory is not the claim that reasons are desires. And "sound deliberative route" is deliberately generous: it covers means–end calculation, but also imagination, the correction of beliefs in the course of deliberating, and reflection on what one might become — including reflection that alters S itself. Externalism, by contrast, is the view that reasons are facts about the world bearing no relation whatever to what anyone happens to care about.

**§3.** I want to separate three theses that go by the single name "internalism."
(i) *Accessibility-internalism*: for a consideration to be A's reason, A must be able to reach it from where he actually stands by reasoning that is sound. A claim about routes.
(ii) *Existence-internalism*: whether a reason obtains at all is a function of A's motivational makeup. A claim about the truth conditions of reason statements.
(iii) *Authority-internalism*: a consideration can ground blame, resentment, and the standing to demand that A act on it only if it is internal in the sense of (i). A claim about the interpersonal consequences of reason attribution.
My exegetical claim is that Williams is usually credited with all three, needs only (i), and that (ii) and (iii) are independent additions which (i) does not entail.

**§4.** If only (i) is in play, nothing follows about the cruel man's blameworthiness, since (iii) is a separate thesis about our practices and not a corollary of a thesis about routes. What the case shows is narrower and less alarming: we cannot reason him out of cruelty. That is a fact about the limits of persuasion, not a fact about the furniture of the normative world. The relativism charge gets its sting only by sliding from "he could not be brought by sound deliberation to see it" to "he is not answerable for it," and those are different claims.

**§5.** The obvious reply to (i) is that conversion is itself a route. A well-known response to Williams — McDowell's, I believe — urges that a person can be brought to acknowledge reasons he could not previously have acknowledged, and that this transformation can be rational rather than merely causal; if such a conversion counts as a sound deliberative route from S, internalism absorbs its rival instead of excluding it. I want to press the cost of that concession. S can be altered by a great many things: propaganda, grief, a novel, a blow to the head. If routes count simply in virtue of beginning in S and ending in motivation, then almost every conclusion is reachable from almost every S, and (i) stops constraining anything. The only way to keep (i) substantive is to give "sound" content independent of S — but a standard of correctness in deliberation not drawn from the agent's own motivational makeup is exactly what the externalist posits. So (i) is either vacuous or externalism under another name.

**§6.** What then of blame? If the cruel man has no reason to stop, our blame of him can be only one of two things: a mistake, or a causal intervention — an attempt to install in S a motive that was not there. Williams is generally read as accepting the second horn. But an account on which "I blame you" names a piece of social technology is not an account of blame; it is an account of training, and it forfeits the very thing that made the cruel man's case troubling. This is the real price of internalism, and it is paid in (iii), not in (i).

**§7.** The reconstruction has a cost of its own. If Williams holds only (i), his position is compatible with much of what his opponents want to say, and the famous debate threatens to become verbal. I think that is worth accepting, because the alternative is the manufactured dilemma of §1. But the cost is not zero: a Williams who cannot be accused of relativism is also a Williams who cannot be credited with having changed the subject.
