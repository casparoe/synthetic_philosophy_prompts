In 1947, while preparing for his American citizenship examination, Kurt Gödel told Einstein and Morgenstern that he had found an internal contradiction in the United States Constitution — one that would permit a dictatorship to be established entirely by legal means. Both men were alarmed, and on the usual telling they spent the hearing trying to keep him from elaborating on it. Gödel never set the argument down, so its content is speculative; the most-cited reconstruction runs through Article V, which nowhere forbids amending the amendment clause itself, so that a first amendment could lower the thresholds Article V requires and a second could then install one-person rule without further supermajorities.

Treat this as a case study in what the incompleteness theorems do and don't establish outside mathematics. First, map the analogy strictly: what would a constitution have to be — what axiom set, what notion of proof, what consequence relation, what expressive resources — for the first or second theorem to apply to it at all, and which of those conditions the Article V story fails? Say explicitly whether Gödel's supposed finding would be an incompleteness result, a consistency result, or neither, given that the second theorem concludes not that a contradiction is derivable but that consistency is unprovable within the system. Then consider Alf Ross, who raised in a 1969 Mind article the puzzle that a rule of amendment cannot validly amend itself, since any replacement clause could derive its force only from the clause it replaces: is Gödel's discovery better described by Ross's puzzle than by incompleteness, and what turns on the difference?

Finally, run the analysis the other way. Legal orders absorb conflicting norms through priority rules — later over earlier, specific over general, higher over lower — rather than collapsing into triviality. Does that fact undercut the standard use of Gödel against formalism in law, or does it simply show that constitutions were never the right target? And what, if anything, does the episode legitimately establish about the limits of a written constitution?
