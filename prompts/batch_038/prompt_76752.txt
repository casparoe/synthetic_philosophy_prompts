From the mid-twentieth century onward the social interpretation of the French Revolution was attacked and never convincingly restored: one side held that 1789 was a bourgeois revolution in which an emergent capitalist class seized political power; the other replied that the revolutionary leadership was composed mainly of venal office-holders, lawyers, and rentiers, and that the upheaval on balance retarded capitalist development; the subsequent turn to political culture relocated the engine of events in a revolutionary political imaginary, with the Terror read either as the unfolding of that imaginary or as a contingent response to war and emergency. My hypothesis is that the resulting stalemate is not about the prosopographic evidence, which both camps largely share, but about incompatible criteria for a social factor's being the cause of an event — one demanding counterfactual dependence, the other satisfied by an expressive or constitutive fit between class position and political form. Diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicating it: is it verbal, substantive but underdetermined, or two questions travelling under one name; what exactly is the word class doing in each camp's argument; and does the quarrel about the Terror belong to the same dispute or to a further one about whether the Revolution picks out a single explanandum or a sequence of them (1789, 1792, 1793–94) that may admit of different explanations without either side conceding anything? Finally, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a new kind of evidence, a formal or comparative result — or argue that none should be expected and why. Answer in exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
