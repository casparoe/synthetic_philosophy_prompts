I retired four years ago after 33 years in soil microbiology (mostly what happens to nitrogen-fixing bacteria when farmers stop rotating crops), and to keep my head busy I've started reading philosophy properly for the first time since a required course in 1969. A neighbour pointed me at Augustine's Confessions, and then, when I grumbled about a colleague of mine who once told a funder his data were collected blind when they weren't, at the two short things Augustine wrote about lying — the early one on lying in general, and the later one he wrote to a friend who wanted to know whether you could pretend to share a heretical group's beliefs in order to get inside it and expose them. His answer to that second one floored me. He says no, not even then, and the reason he gives isn't mainly about the harm done to the person being fooled — its about what asserting something you don't believe does to the asserter, the split that opens up between the heart and the words. I'm still not sure I agree with him but I cant dismiss him either, which is a fairly new experience for me.

Here's the practical problem. I've somehow ended up chairing the human-subjects review panel at the regional university where I'm emeritus. Three of us, meets Thursdays by video, we get maybe two to four protocols a month, roughly 40 minutes each, and I sign the determination letter. Almost every one from the social sciences and a good many from public health involve deceiving somebody: masking the real purpose, using a confederate who says things that aren't true, joining a community under a made-up name, sending testers to ask landlords or employers for something while misrepresenting who they are. Our current practice is basically a cost-benefit shrug — is the science worth it, can we debrief afterwards, does the consent form contain a line authorizing us to withhold the purpose. Nobody can articulate a rule, we just vote. I'd like to try running Augustine's view through that mess, not because I've converted but because an absolutist position is at least a position, and because it seems to me a lot of what we approve turns on a distinction nobody ever states out loud: not telling someone something versus telling them something false.

So: could you write me a decision procedure my two colleagues and I could actually run on a deception protocol in fifteen minutes — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where it says flatly not permitted no matter how good the science is, the points where it can only require modifications, and the points where it has to stop and hand the thing back to a human being because the remaining question is about values rather than about whether anyone was told a falsehood. Ground it in what Augustine actually argues: the intent-to-deceive element, the ranking he gives of lies by seriousness, the withholding-versus-asserting line, the claim that the liar is the first casualty, and his refusal to trade a lie for a good outcome. And be straight with me about which of those distinctions survive being handed to three non-philosophers on a Zoom call, which ones you're quietly dropping or padding, and where his view and our federal regulations point in different directions so the procedure isn't pretending to settle something it can't. No Latin, no quotations, no reading list — I want something that could feed into two plain sentences in a determination letter that a disappointed investigator could read without feeling lectured. Then run it on three protocols. (1) An obedience study whose consent form explicitly authorizes masking the purpose and promises a full debrief, but where a confederate tells each participant a fabricated story about how the other participants performed. (2) A researcher who wants to join a private online group pushing a discredited treatment for a serious illness, under a false name, posting agreement with things she doesn't believe so that she'll be trusted with the group's recruitment material. (3) A paired-testing audit study of rental discrimination, where testers present themselves as genuine prospective tenants to landlords who will never be debriefed because the whole point is that they don't know, and where the results will very likely end up in a discrimination complaint. I know what your verdict on the third one is going to be and I'm going to hate it, since audit studies are the only instrument I ever saw that could measure something people go to real trouble to hide — so don't soften it to spare my feelings. Tell me instead what the procedure cannot do at all, and what the honest options are for a committee that thinks Augustine is right and still wants the audit study.
