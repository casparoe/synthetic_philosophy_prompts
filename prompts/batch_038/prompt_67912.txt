I'm helping run a little reading group and this week we're on Popper's *Poverty of Historicism* — specifically the part about whether there can be laws of historical development at all, as opposed to trends. Our discussion always collapses into people shouting about whether Marx was right, which isn't really the argument, so I want to ambush them with a piece of satire that gets the philosophy in through the side door instead. My roommate's doing an actuarial science masters and says the whole genre of internal insurance guidance is 90 percent hedging language and disclaimers, which is exactly why I think it'll work as a vehicle.

So: can you write me an internal underwriting manual page, the dry numbered "class of business guidance" sort of thing a specialty insurer circulates to its brokers, for a new line the firm is reluctantly launching — cover for holistic, whole-of-society transformation projects. The clients are invented (ministries of planning, transitional committees, a bureau of historical inevitability, whatever you like), the firm is invented, and please don't quote Popper or any other real text or person verbatim; anyone who speaks should be made up. Around 700 words, numbered clauses, plus a sidebar or two — maybe an excluded-perils box and a short note on how the rates were set. The jokes have to come from the actuaries getting the epistemology right rather than from wackiness. In the course of trying to price the risk they should run into the fact that a supposed law of historical development keeps degrading into a trend whose validity depends on initial conditions persisting; that they cannot forecast the future growth of knowledge, and therefore cannot forecast the insured's own future discoveries, which is a material uncertainty they have to disclose and an exclusion they can't draft tightly enough; that a whole-society experiment has no control group, can't be run twice, and can't be revised piecemeal once everything is interconnected, so the exposure is unbounded and arguably uninsurable in principle even though the sales team really wants the premium. I'd like the piecemeal-versus-utopian engineering distinction to surface as a plain difference in what they will and won't write — small reversible interventions treated as ordinary property risk, the total blueprint treated as something else entirely. And there should be a warranty clause about criticism, the insurer requiring that the insured not suppress critics, purely because without a free flow of corrections they can't price anything; that's as close as the piece should get to the political point, and it should stay in the register of a company worried about its loss ratio rather than about liberty. Include at least one quoted line from a fictional chief underwriter or a reinsurer who has seen this book of business before.

Tone is the main thing: totally deadpan, no winking, and never state an objection as an objection — a reader should be able to reconstruct Popper's argument from the clauses alone, and someone who's never read him should still find it funny as corporate absurdism. Don't tack on a moral at the end, don't explain the joke, and if you find yourself writing the word "ironically" anywhere, delete that sentence.
