In three weeks I present a chapter of my dissertation at a two-day workshop on modality and semantic theory hosted by my department's logic-and-language group. The room will be about twenty people, and its composition matters for what I want from you: two committed two-dimensionalists (one defends the zombie argument and writes about epistemic space and scrutability; the other works on indexicals and on what primary intensions are supposed to explain), a Kripkean sceptic who has argued in print that the whole apparatus misdescribes how reference works and that natural-kind terms have no epistemic intension worth the name, a formal semanticist who works on doubly-indexed semantics for "actually" and will regard centered worlds as bookkeeping, and the remainder graduate students, several of whom have never met a center. The slot is twenty-five minutes plus discussion, and my supervisor's standing complaint about the chapter is that it reads like a technical appendix in search of a thesis, so the discussion period is where the paper will live or die.

The thesis I am defending I call single-intension two-dimensionalism, and its moving parts are these. (i) A sentence as used in a context has exactly one intension: a function from centered worlds to truth values. (ii) What the literature calls primary and secondary intensions are not two components of content but two modes of evaluating that one intension — in the secondary mode the center is held at the actual one while the world varies, in the primary mode the center travels with the world being evaluated — so neither is prior and there is no semantic pluralism to buy. (iii) The explanatory jobs usually assigned to the distinction must accordingly be redone in terms of modes of evaluation: the necessary a posteriori is not one proposition with two guises nor two propositions, but a single intension that comes out true at every world when evaluated counterfactually from our center and is nonetheless not knowable a priori, because knowing it requires knowing which center one occupies. (iv) The framework still earns metaphysical keep in one place, and I want to claim it: for a phenomenal concept the two modes of evaluation provably coincide, since the intension assigns the same extension at every centered world whose center is having the relevant experience whatever physically realizes it. (v) That collapse is not a special plea but follows from a general principle I defend — a center contributes only the location of evaluation, never the criteria of application — and I argue it blocks the move from conceivability to possibility while leaving the conceivability of zombies entirely intact. A friend of mine who works on Stalnaker tells me the deflation in (ii)–(iii) is either obvious or that the "actually" literature already delivers it with fewer coordinates, and I have not yet worked out how much of that I should concede before I walk in.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the five objections I will actually get from *this* room — not the ones a general metaphysics audience would raise — ordered by how much damage they would do if I fumbled them. Answer as exactly five numbered entries with no introduction and no conclusion; each entry should contain the objection phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their idiom (the formal semanticist will talk about indices and expressibility, the Kripkean about reference-fixing and about whether I have smuggled descriptive content back in through the center, the two-dimensionalists about what a primary intension is for if it is not a mode of presentation and about whether conceivability can carry any weight once it is merely a way of holding coordinates fixed), then a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, then a final one-sentence note saying where that reply is soft — the point at which I would be forced either to give up something in (ii)–(v) or to promise results from chapters I have not written. Fold the accusation that my view is not new into whichever entry it naturally belongs to rather than treating it separately, and in that entry tell me the least defensive answer. Mark which of the five is the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say what I should say if it comes anyway.
