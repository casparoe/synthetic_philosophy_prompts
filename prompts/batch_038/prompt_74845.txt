Not a philosopher — civil engineer, so my working life is load tables, inspection intervals, design life. But there's a family of projects I keep running into in adjacent fields that I don't know how to think about, and I'd like someone to tell me what they're actually committed to.

Concretely. On Svalbard there's a seed vault tunneled into a mountain, holding duplicate samples from gene banks all over the world, and not far away, in a decommissioned coal mine, there's a snapshot of every active public GitHub repository on microfilm that's meant to still be readable in a thousand years. Both Voyager probes carry a gold-plated disc with playback instructions etched on the sleeve, and the instructions define the unit of time by the spin-flip transition of the hydrogen atom — so the intended reader is assumed to share our physics and to know literally nothing else about us. In New Mexico, designing the marker system for a repository licensed to isolate waste for ten thousand years, a national-lab task force came up with concepts including a mass of huge irregular spikes jutting out of the ground in every direction, a slab of black basalt big enough to make the land unfarmable, and a granite information building shaped so the wind whistles mournfully through it. Around the same problem, a linguist consulted by an earlier federal task force proposed an invented priesthood whose job would be to carry the warning forward through ritual, and two semioticians writing in a European journal proposed cats engineered to change colour near radiation, plus a body of folklore teaching people to run when the cat changes.

So: what theory of value does this cluster presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as carefully as you can. Is it that what we do now matters only because there will be people to recieve it? That the act of addressing an audience is worth something even into an empty ocean? Or something far more mundane — insurance and option value for our own grandchildren, dressed up grandly?

Are these even the same kind of project? The warning markers protect people who'll exist whether or not we build them; the Golden Record protects nobody and needs nobody. I suspect that difference changes what each one is committed to, but I can't articulate how. Which rival views does the practice rule out — someone who thinks nothing can be good or bad for people who don't yet exist, someone who thinks all value is value-for-someone-now? And what would those people have to say the projects are, if not what the builders say they are?

The cats are the part that sticks with me. That proposal gives up on the content surviving and bets on a story and a reflex surviving instead, which seems to be a claim about what actually gets transmitted through ten thousand years of drift — narrative and behaviour rather than propositions and text. Is that a separate commitment from the one behind the seed vault, or the same one wearing a different hat?

And the thing I can't square: we'll spend a billion dollars addressing an audience that probably won't exist, and comparatively nothing on making sure an audience exists. Is that hypocrisy, or do the two halves presuppose different theories of value entirely?

Please be careful to separate what's settled among people who actually work on this stuff from what's a live fight — from reading around the edges I can tell some of it is genuinely contested, and I don't want a contested position handed to me as the consensus. Where the practice is underdetermined, where several incompatible theories would license it just as well, say so plainly instead of picking one and dressing it up.
