I'm co-leading an independent reading group on philosophy of mind, and for next week's session I want to hand out a construction exercise on Dennett's three stances together with his account of real patterns — the rough idea being that a pattern is real when it licenses a description of some body of data appreciably shorter than an item-by-item rendering of that data, and that beliefs and desires count as real insofar as they figure in patterns of that kind. My co-lead is skeptical that the compression idea can bear the weight put on it, and after three meetings of circular argument I would rather have a concrete case in front of both of us. So I'd like you to build one to order.

Design a system for which the intentional stance is genuinely predictive and genuinely compact, but for which some description in a non-intentional idiom is at least as compact and delivers all the same predictions — so that compression and predictive success, taken together, fail to privilege belief talk even though the realism about beliefs is supposed to rest on precisely those facts. Constraints: (1) the system must be an ordinary, non-science-fictional physical thing — no trained models or computer programs, no markets, languages, legal systems, or other institutions, and no organism, since I want to rule out the standard answers; (2) the design stance must be worse than useless here, either because the thing has no function at all or because what it does runs contrary to what it was made to do, so that a functional description positively misleads; (3) the physical stance must be available in principle but not in practice for an observer who has to predict the thing's behavior on short notice and with limited information, and you should say why; (4) the rival compact description must not be a covert design ascription or a disguised physical derivation, and you should state in a sentence or two what idiom it is actually couched in.

The deliverables are these. First, the scenario itself, in a paragraph of no more than about two hundred words. Second, all three candidate descriptions written out, with rough order-of-magnitude comparisons of their lengths in bits — approximations are fine, hand-waving is not. Third, the verdict the compression criterion yields on whether the system has beliefs, alongside the verdict one would naively draw from the intentional stance's predictive success, and an explanation of exactly where the two come apart. Fourth, identification of the single feature of the case that generates the wedge, plus a check on it: state what happens if you weaken or remove that feature, and whether the case then collapses back into ordinary intentional attribution or into a physical description in disguise. Fifth, how a defender of the claim that beliefs are real would attack the case — for instance by insisting that attributions must be robust across counterfactual circumstances rather than merely compact over the data actually observed — and what, if anything, that move costs her. Please don't dissolve the assignment by arguing that the specification is incoherent; if you think it cannot be met as stated, show me precisely which constraint fails and then give me the nearest case you can construct that satisfies the rest. Work from your own understanding of the framework — no citations or references are needed or wanted.
