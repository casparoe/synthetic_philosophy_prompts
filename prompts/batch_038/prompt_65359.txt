For an independent study I've been working through the letter-string puzzles from the Copycat project — you're shown that abc becomes abd, asked what ijk becomes, and then handed harder strings of my own devising, like mrrjjj, where the letters arrive in repeated groups. I've come to accept a fairly strong claim about these cases: what makes one answer feel compelling is not that the two strings resemble each other, but that there exists a chain of small, individually licensed conceptual shifts running from the rule you extracted in the first string to the structure of the second, with the right answer being simply the endpoint of the cheapest such chain. What would count against this view? I'd like the puzzle cases, experimental findings, and modeling results that ought to move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, along with what a similarity-based rival account would predict differently in a way that could actually be tested on people. Please separate the counterexamples a defender of the slippage picture would themselves concede as real costs from the ones they'd dismiss out of hand, and give an honest assessment of whether the notion of a cheapest chain has quietly been stretched far enough to absorb any disconfirming case — and if it has, whether that is a genuine problem for a view of this kind rather than a technical one. For what it's worth, I would find it most damaging to learn of string pairs that are nearly identical on the surface yet where people reliably refuse the obvious slip, or of very dissimilar pairs where the slip comes effortlessly.
