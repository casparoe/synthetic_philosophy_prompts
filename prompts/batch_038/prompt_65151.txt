I'm in a seminar on the metaphysics of probability, and I'm struggling to see how Lewis's Principal Principle is supposed to coexist with ordinary inductive reasoning. Two things seem to pull in opposite directions. First, if the past outcomes of a chance process count as admissible evidence, then the Principle appears to require that I give credence 0.5 to heads on the next toss of a coin whose chance of heads is 0.5 even after watching it come up heads twenty times in a row, which strikes me as absurd: surely that run of heads is powerful evidence that the coin isn't fair after all. But second, if the Principle instead implies that observed frequencies should override what I take to be my knowledge of the chance, then it looks as though I could never have known the chance in the first place, and the Principle would only ever bind in circumstances that never actually arise. Could you explain what claim the Principle is really making, and say which of my two worries depends on a misreading of it?
