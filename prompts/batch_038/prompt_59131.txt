I am a staff software engineer at a company that builds an AI coding assistant, and in six weeks I am giving a twenty-five-minute talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on automation and the quality of work. I have no formal training in philosophy or the social sciences. I was invited because our usage telemetry is unusually detailed and because the organizers said they wanted a builder in the room rather than another commentator. The audience will be about a dozen people: mostly labor economists and sociologists of work, who think in terms of task composition, wages, and deskilling; two philosophers of technology who work on skill and expertise; and a handful of practitioners from other industries. Almost none of them have ever shipped a developer tool.

The position I intend to defend is deliberately not the one they will be braced for. My argument, roughly, is this. (1) Work is meaningful for a person chiefly when their contribution is answerable, meaning that some specific outcome others depend on can be traced back to a judgment they made. (2) Craft skill produces meaning through what I will call legible failure on the day: when the work goes wrong, the wrongness points at a person and at a decision they own. (3) Assistant tools of the kind my company sells do not, on our own data, reduce the difficulty or the judgment content of the work; what they do is make the output indistinguishable from the assistant's, so that the record no longer shows whose call it was. (4) The primary harm is therefore not deskilling but de-attribution: the quiet collapse of the social mechanism by which competent work gets recognized and incompetent work gets corrected. (5) It follows that firms should be designing and measuring for visible accountability, even where that costs throughput, and that the usual remedy of reskilling programs answers a different question than the one being asked. I am also prepared to say the unpopular part: that a substantial loss of raw coding skill among junior engineers is an acceptable price, provided attribution survives.

I want you to prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument if it landed unanswerable, and phrase them the way these people would phrase them rather than the way I would. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds standing at a lectern, and tell me plainly where that reply is soft. Tell me which two I should concede within the first five minutes and which I must hold at all costs, and identify the single reply that has to land for the talk to be worth giving. I already suspect that someone will point out that I sell the product I am describing, and I do not know whether to address that head-on in the opening or to leave it for the floor; give me a definite answer. End with the one question I most want nobody to ask, and say whether I should invite it deliberately anyway.

Please commit rather than hedge. I do not want a survey of how each side of these debates might respond, and I do not want a menu of alternative answers with the choice left to me. Take a position on which of my five steps is weakest, say so, and give your reasons.

Two constraints on the form. First, write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter or an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists in your reply, even though I have numbered my own steps above. I will be reading it on a train. Second, do not send me anywhere to read. I want no bibliography, no reading list, and no names of books, papers, or authors, because I cannot verify them and I will not have time to read them. Describe what the people in that room believe and how they will put it, not where it is written down.
