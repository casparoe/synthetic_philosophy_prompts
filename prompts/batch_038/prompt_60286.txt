I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student (logic/foundations) and for my outreach requirement I've been roped into running a 45-minute session at a summer math camp for high schoolers. The kids are good at competition math but have basically no proof experience past induction. Topic I've been handed: infinity, and why some infinities are bigger than others. That would be fine except for two problems. First, roughly half the room has already seen a Hilbert's hotel video online and can recite the punchline, so the hotel is dead as a discovery device — they'll just shout the answer at me. Second, they arrive already believing the conclusion (older sibling told them there's different sizes of infinity), so the real risk is that they take the diagonal argument as a magic trick with notation rather than a proof.

So: plan the session for me. Don't explain the material to me, I know it — I want the structure. What has to be in place before what, and why; specifically, whether the usual order (hotel → matching → "same size" → diagonal) survives at all once they already know the hotel, or whether I need to invert it and start from the diagonal and treat the hotel as the boring warm-up. I've been toying with building "same size" out of a conveyor-belt picture instead of the hotel: an infinitely long belt of envelopes moving past an infinite stack of letters, and the claim is that you can tell there's equally many of each without counting either one, just by whether the process ever strands a letter or an envelope. Tell me whether that carries the idea, what a carrying example for equinumerosity has to have and has to lack, and — importantly — whether the belt picture smuggles in exactly the "but you never finish" objection I'm going to need to answer ten minutes later when I do the diagonal. If it does, tell me what to use instead that they haven't already seen on the internet.

Then the misconception map: which wrong ideas they'll form, and the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off. The ones I can predict are that "bigger infinity" means "has more members" in an ordinary counting sense; that infinity is a number with weird arithmetic (they'll want ℵ₀ + 1 = ℵ₀ to be a *fact about a thing*); that Cantor proved something about the set of all sets; that the diagonal number "isn't really on the list because the list is infinite and you never get to the end of it" — that's the one I'm most worried about and I genuinely don't know whether to pre-empt it early or let a kid raise it so I can kill it live; and the apologetics version, where the hotel is supposed to show that actual infinities can't exist in reality — some of them will have met that, so tell me whether I engage with it or refuse it.

Finally: one question I can put at the end that would actually show me the idea landed rather than that they're being polite, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level (ordinals? choice? Russell's paradox? the continuum hypothesis? Zeno and supertasks?) plus how to say "I'm not covering that" without making them think I'm hiding the good stuff. No readings, no history interludes, no handouts — 45 minutes, one whiteboard, and I've been told its "for fun" so there's no assessment I can borrow.
