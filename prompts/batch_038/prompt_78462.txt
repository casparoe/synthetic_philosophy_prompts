I am a philosophy senior, and next Friday I defend my undergraduate thesis in a public oral examination: twenty minutes of presentation, then sustained questioning from my three-person committee, then an open floor for the rest of the thesis seminar. I would like a preparation sheet for the question period. I am not looking for a defense of my thesis, a summary of the debate, or a list of references — I have read the literature and my committee has read two drafts. What I need is a realistic map of which of my moves will be attacked, by whom, in what order, and how much damage each attack will do.

My thesis concerns Kim's causal exclusion argument, and my position is deliberately unfashionable in my seminar. The argument has four steps.

1. I grant the exclusion argument as an attack on nonreductive physicalism. Given the causal closure of the physical plus a ban on systematic overdetermination, a mental property distinct from its physical realizer does no causal work at the level at which the physical story is told. I do not try to block closure, and I do not try to block the overdetermination premise.
2. I claim the standard conclusion — epiphenomenalism or eliminativism — depends on a premise Kim leaves tacit: that a causal claim is true or false simpliciter, at one privileged level.
3. Against that, I argue causal relations are level-relative in a metaphysical rather than a merely pragmatic sense. A mental property M can be a cause relative to a coarse-grained set of variables, and its realizer P a cause relative to a finer-grained set, and both claims are literally true, with no further fact of the matter about which is the causation. What fixes the level is not our explanatory interests but the grain at which interventions on the system are possible in principle — I call this the interventional grain.
4. Therefore nothing at the mental level ever changes what happens at the physical level. My view requires no downward causation, and I present that as a feature: psychology and moral responsibility get everything they need from level-relative causation, and physicalism is never threatened.

The committee and the room:

- My advisor is a metaphysician who works on realization and grounding and is broadly sympathetic to Kim. He has told me twice that step 3 "looks like Yablo's proportionality thesis with extra machinery," and that if that is all it is, I have not answered Kim so much as changed the subject.
- My second reader is a philosopher of action and of psychological explanation. She is no reductionist, but she thinks reasons-explanation is a different kind of explanation, and that framing the dispute in terms of mental properties being "causes" already concedes the wrong picture. She will want to know what my view accomplishes that the far simpler claim — that psychological explanation is not causal explanation — would not.
- My third reader works on causation and is an interventionist. He will press hardest on "possible in principle": which interventions count, whether interventions on M that unavoidably also fix P show M is not a cause, and whether my "no further fact" clause renders causal claims indeterminate in a way that his own framework does not.
- The open floor will have roughly a dozen classmates who have read the anthology's presentation of the exclusion argument and one response paper, and who will ask blunt, large questions.
