ok, kind of an odd one but I could really use help, and you're the right sort of person for it — please act as a professional philosopher, specifically someone who works in metaphysics and philosophy of language and has thought hard about how words like "species" or "planet" or "art" get their boundaries. Think carefully about this and don't just give me the encyclopaedia summary. Also correct me anywhere I've described the facts wrong.

Context: I write the exhibit text at a mid-size science center. We're redoing our solar system hall — the long walkway with the Sun at one end and everything to scale — and the centerpiece is a big wall that has to state a number. How many planets. Underneath it I get about 120 words to say why that number. This should be the easiest label I write all year and it has become a nightmare.

Since we put up a preview of the new text, I've had emails from both directions. A retired engineer sent me three pages about how the third criterion is arbitrary and Pluto got robbed. A middle school teacher wrote to say the opposite, that we should just follow the official definition so kids don't get marked wrong on tests. A nine year old asked me in person why Pluto got kicked out and whether that was true, and I froze, because I realized I couldn't answer without saying something I think is false. Our gift shop is still selling a nine-planet plush set from 2015 and it sells fine.

What I keep coming back to is the practice itself. There's this international union of astronomers, and it does something very specific: a few hundred members at a general assembly vote by show of hands, the vote settles what a word means, press releases go out, and the whole world treats the result as news about the solar system. In 2006 they laid down that a planet has to (a) orbit the Sun, (b) be massive enough for its own gravity to make it round, and (c) have swept its orbital neighbourhood clear of other stuff. Anything with (a) and (b) but not (c) is a dwarf planet, and they were explicit that a dwarf planet is not a kind of planet — a proposal that would have made it a subcategory was voted down. But the IAU's own list of eight planets includes Mercury, and Mercury isn't actually in hydrostatic equilibrium, so (b) fails and it counts anyway. And people point out that if you dropped Mars into the Kuiper belt it would fail (c), so planethood would depend on where a body happens to be sitting rather than on what it is.

Meanwhile the guy who ran the New Horizons mission says anything round is a planet, which would push the count well past a hundred, and plenty of working planetary scientists just talk that way regardless of the resolution.

So — questions, and I want you in the weeds, not at 30,000 feet:

1. Start with some brainstorming: a rough list of the candidate pictures of what a word like "planet" could be, before you commit to any of them.
2. What does the practice of having a naming body vote on the definition presupose about what "planet" is? Which view of meaning, or of how the world is carved up, is the one that makes a show-of-hands vote on this make sense? And which rival views does the practice quietly rule out?
3. Here's the part I can't get straight. The practice seems to say two things at once: "we decided this" and "here's what the facts about the solar system are." The vote gets reported as a discovery. Is that coherent, or is one side confused about what it's doing? Is the public outrage a category mistake, or is the union's own framing the category mistake? I genuinely can't tell, and I suspect the answer is both, or neither.
4. Separate, as carefully as you can, what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Where is it philosophically underdetermined — where would two or three very different theories license the exact same vote?
5. What about Mercury, and the Kuiper-belt-Mars case? Do they get explained away, or do they show the practice is committed to something incoherent? Does it matter that (c) seems to make planethood historical and relational rather than intrinsic?
6. Was there an option nobody took — the body officially declaring the word has two legitimate technical uses and refusing to pick one? Why do you think that isn't on the table for an institution like this? Is there a version of the practice that's more honest without being less useful?
7. Then tell me what goes on the wall. Not necessarily the finished 120 words, but what the honest version has to say under each of the main competing pictures, and which one I can defend to both the engineer and the teacher.

Please don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list — I have no way to look any of it up and it's useless to me. Naming the positions or types of view is fine and helpful, just don't tell me to go read something. Around 1200–1500 words would be great. Thanks!
