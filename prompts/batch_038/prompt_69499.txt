I am a final-year undergraduate in a seminar on liberalism and empire, and before I begin drafting I need to hand my professor a two-page proposal for the end-of-term paper: about 5,500 words, six weeks away. My topic concerns Mill's writings on India, and I have a half-formed idea I would like turned into a genuine research plan rather than a restatement of the obvious debate.

The familiar way into this material asks whether Mill's defence of British rule in India is consistent with the principles laid out in On Liberty — specifically, whether the exception he allows for governing peoples he describes as not yet fit for self-government contradicts the rest of his liberalism. I want to argue that this framing stalls because it assumes Mill has a single justificatory structure, where in fact he appears to have two that come apart at an identifiable point. The first is developmental: a claim about which populations are ready for representative institutions. The second is administrative and epistemic, and it grows out of his decades of work at East India House and his 1858 opposition to transferring the government of India from the Company to the Crown: the claim that ruling India well required a small, experienced, semi-detached body of officials insulated from both metropolitan electoral politics and local factional pressure, and that the divided arrangement in which the Company's directors administered India under the supervision of a parliamentary board, for all its friction, actually produced such a body. My suspicion is that Mill's permissibility claim about non-consensual rule is doing administrative-epistemic work — it is about who is positioned to know a governed population's good, not about that population's stage of development as such — and that this only becomes visible in 1858, when he argues that abolishing the Company would injure Indians even though his developmental premise about India's unreadiness has not changed.

The question I want the proposal to sharpen is this: is the developmental premise load-bearing in Mill's justification of British rule in India, or is it carrying rhetorical weight while the administrative-epistemic premises carry the argument — and if the latter, does his position survive the loss of the developmental premise in a way the consistency debate assumes it could not?

Please write the proposal so that it contains the following. First, the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, including an explicit criterion for what would distinguish the readings from one another. Second, an account of why the two established positions — Mill as straightforwardly coherent, Mill as hypocrite or as theorist of liberal imperialism — leave my question open; make this argumentative rather than historiographic, since I have no archive access and will supply every reference myself. Third, the method: what I would actually do, week by week, and above all how I would test the hypothesis instead of merely illustrating it — which textual moves would count as evidence that the administrative argument is operating independently, and what a disconfirming pattern would look like. Fourth, three or four candidate conclusions, with the considerations that would decide among them. Fifth, an explicit failure condition: what would show that my distinction collapses (for instance, if every administrative claim turns out to presuppose the developmental premise), reducing the project to the very debate I wanted to escape. Sixth, an honest assessment of scope — whether this is achievable in 5,500 words in six weeks using published texts and standard editions only, and what I would have to narrow or cut to make it achievable. Formal enough to submit, and please do not pad it with a bibliography.
