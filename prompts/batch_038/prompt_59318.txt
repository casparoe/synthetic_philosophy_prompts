Two months ago I was the person at my organization who recommended that we shelve a capability we had already demonstrated in-house, on the grounds that its costs were diffuse and unbounded while its benefits were appropriable by very few. The recommendation was refused, and the refusal was explicitly von Neumannian: a technology of that kind cannot be un-invented; forbearance by the party best placed to exercise it merely transfers the capability to parties less inclined to be careful; the only rational course is therefore to proceed and to invest in institutions able to absorb the consequences. Since then I have been reading his 1955 Fortune essay "Can We Survive Technology?", in which he argues that the crisis of the century arises because the political environment containing technological activity has become, in his phrase, "undersized and underorganized," and concludes that the answer is political integration at planetary scale together with openness and inspection, rather than any attempt to hold back the technical frontier.

I have a one-year fellowship application due and a half-formed idea I would like turned into a two-page proposal. My suspicion is that the premise carrying the whole argument — that whatever is technically possible and worth doing will be done sooner or later — is not one claim but at least three, and that they come apart. There is an incentive claim: in a rivalry, each party's best response is to develop, so mutual forbearance is unstable. There is a verification claim: even a stable agreement could not be monitored, so defection goes undetected. And there is a claim about the internal dynamics of research: capability arrives as a by-product of work aimed at something else, so there is no decision point at which a direction can be declined. Each has different falsifiers and licenses different remedies. Notably, von Neumann's own prescription of inspection and openness seems to presuppose the second, the way his argument is invoked today usually assumes the first, and the third would defeat both.

Please give me: a precise statement of the research question; why the existing treatments — intellectual-historical readings of the essay, the general literature on technological determinism, and current governance arguments that appeal to it — leave the question open; my method, month by month, including how I would test whether the three readings really are separable against a contemporary case; a defense of this reconstruction over the obvious alternatives; what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project's failing; and an honest assessment of the ways it could prove too large for twelve months. I will supply the references myself, so no bibliography, and please do not attribute any specific wording to von Neumann beyond what I have given you here.
