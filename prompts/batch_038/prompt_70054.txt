So I do incident reviews for a living — when something breaks or somebody gets hurt at one of our plants, I have to write up why. And there's a discipline to it that I never really appreciated until I started trying to apply it to history. You're not allowed to just list everything that was off that day. You have to name the thing that, if it had been different, the incident doesn't happen, and you have to say why it happened at that plant and not at the sister plant running the same process with the same equipment and the same shifts. Half the time the honest answer is that there wasn't one such thing, and then you have to say that instead, which nobody likes. Anyway, I fell down a rabbit hole on the England/China thing after a podcast, and every answer I read is just a list of stuff England happened to have: coal that was cheap and close to the water, high wages that made machines worth building, colonies and silver, a parliament that protected property, a culture of tinkerers and practical science, a patchwork of small competing states instead of one big empire. My friend says all of those lists are made up after the fact and the real answer is that Britain got lucky, and honestly I can't tell which of us is right. I think the actual problem is that I don't know what would count as a right answer, so I can't judge any of them.

Before I buy any of the books, write me the requirements: what an answer to "why did industrialization take off in Britain and not in China" has to deliver to count as an explanation and not just a good story. Give me the case or the consideration behind each one, not just the label. I especially want the requirements about the shape of the question, because that's where I get stuck. Does an answer have to say what it's contrasting against, given that "China" is a whole civilization and "Britain" is a wet rock, and given that I keep seeing people say the richest part of the Yangtze delta looked a lot like England right around 1750, down to running out of firewood? Does it have to pin down the timing and not just the place, since the question isn't only why there but why then? Does it have to explain why not the Dutch, who had most of the items on every one of those lists a century and a half earlier and didn't take off? Does it have to survive the possibility that a big chunk of it was fluke — and if so, what does "survive" even mean when you've got one case, no repeats, and no control? Does it have to seperate causes from things that only showed up afterwards as consequences, and how would you check that with dead people and bad records? Does it have to be something you could have seen coming from the inside, or is it allowed to only make sense in hindsight?

Then mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp wearing a requirement costume — I suspect "it should give us lessons for how poor countries catch up today" is the latter, but I could be wrong, and I bet there are others on the list that only look neutral. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of the popular answers, because I want to know where to aim when I finally read them. And most of all: is there a subset of these that just can't be satisfied together? My hunch is that "name a difference that actually made the difference," "don't hide behind luck," and "make it general enough to apply somewhere else" can't all hold at once, and that if I'm right the requirements list is itself the answer to my argument with my friend — the question is asking for something no answer can give. If that's right, show me the collision step by step rather than just asserting it. Don't give me a reading list or any book recommendations, I'll get to that later; I want the spec first.
