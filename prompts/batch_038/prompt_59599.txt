Right — software engineer here, no philosophy training beyond what you soak up from podcasts and long comment threads, and I've been trying to work out what the language of thought hypothesis actually claims, because it keeps getting wheeled out in arguments about whether large language models "really" understand anything. I read a write-up of it recently by someone who clearly knew the field, an ex-academic's blog, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust before I start repeating it at people. Roughly what it said:

"Fodor's language of thought hypothesis is the claim that thinking happens in a literal language — not English, but an innate code built into the brain, usually called Mentalese. Its words correspond to our words, which is how translation between natural languages is possible at all. Two arguments drive it. First, thought is productive and systematic: if you can think that John loves Mary you can think that Mary loves John, and you can think thoughts you've never thought before, and that's exactly the pattern you get from rules that combine symbols. Second, you can't learn a language unless you already have one, because learning means testing hypotheses and hypotheses are themselves thoughts — so the code has to be in place from birth. Fodor accepted the extreme consequence: if concepts can't be learned, then essentially all of them are innate, including concepts for things nobody's ancestors ever encountered. That also settles the semantics problem, because a symbol in Mentalese means what it does in virtue of the rules that manipulate it — so there's no need for a little person inside reading the symbols. It follows that the mind is best modelled as a machine running a program, that neuroscience is beside the point for understanding thought, and that neural networks can't be the right story about thinking, since distributed representations have no syntax. Today's language models are the counterexample: they reason systematically with no symbolic code anywhere in them."

Two things felt off as I read it — the idea that the rules settle what the symbols mean, since thats roughly what Searle was making such a fuss about, and the last sentence, which seems to contradict the bit about syntax. But I might just be pattern-matching onto pop-philosophy I picked up secondhand, and my default suspicion is that the whole hypothesis is a programming metaphor taken too literally, which is hardly a neutral starting point.
