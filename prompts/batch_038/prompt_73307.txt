I sit on the investment committee of a private foundation with a $180 million endowment, and the chair has asked me to circulate a written opinion ahead of our March meeting. The situation is a money pump in institutional dress, though I would rather the memo not advertise that fact.

The committee sets its policy portfolio by simple majority vote among the allocations on the table, and for six quarters the slate has been roughly the same three options: a conservative 55/45 mix, a growth-tilted mix, and an inflation-hedge mix. Member rankings have been stable and split into three blocs — conservative-first, growth-first, hedge-first — producing a Condorcet cycle, so the winner depends on the order in which options are put to a vote. Our outside adviser has exploited this, I am confident, without meaning to: each quarter she presents a transition from our current holding to the option that beats it head-to-head, and each transition has been approved by a majority as an improvement. Over three rounds we have moved conservative to growth to hedge and back to a position materially identical to the conservative mix we started from. Transaction costs, manager termination fees, bid-ask spreads, and tax drag on the taxable sleeve have totalled roughly $135,000, with nothing to show for it.

Draft the memo. It should recommend one concrete rule change and should weigh the candidates rather than gesture at them: ranking our criteria lexicographically so that no cycle can form; a scoring rule that aggregates member rankings into a transitive collective order; adopting a multi-year plan and adhering to it resolutely, with a defined procedure for reopening it; a standing no-round-trips check that refuses any sequence of transitions we can foresee will return us to the starting point at a net cost; or delegating the choice to staff under a mandate. State your diagnosis of where the defect actually lies — in the individual votes, each of which was defensible on the reasons available at the time, in the aggregation rule, or in our myopia — and explain why that diagnosis matters for which remedy you pick. Address whether sophisticated choice is available to us at all: can a body that meets quarterly, with rotating membership and an adviser who frames each quarter's option separately, reason backward from what it knows it will later approve and decline the first transition? Say what resolute choice in McClennen's sense and planning agency in Bratman's sense would commit us to under the plan option, and whether a committee can have plans in the sense those accounts require.

Give the strongest consideration on the losing side a real hearing. One member argues that nothing we did was irrational, that the sure loss is an artifact of an artificial sequential setup that a committee facing genuine transaction costs would never walk into twice, and that precommitment would freeze out new information and disenfranchise members who join later. If you reject that view, say precisely which part of it you reject.

Begin with your bottom line — the recommendation — in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my request, and no citations or bibliography; refer to the decision-theoretic ideas by name only.
