I write a fortnightly column for a science-and-ideas desk where readers email me a real decision they're stuck on — usually one where the numbers are known but their gut won't sign off on them — and I try to say something intelligent about whether the gut is right. About half of them turn out to be people defending a cautious choice by saying they're "risk averse", and I honestly can't tell at the moment which of those are making a respectable point and which are just dressing up a probability-weighting quirk or a framing effect. I want to get better at telling them apart, and I think the tool for it is risk-weighted expected utility — the Buchak-style view where a global risk function gets applied to the gamble as a whole rather than everything being reduced to the curvature of a utility function — but I don't want a lecture on it, I want something I can actually run.

So: write me a procedure I can apply to a reader's submission in about twenty minutes, with no more maths than an A-level, and no chance to email the reader back for clarifcation (one round of questions is the most my editor allows, so the tests have to be answerable from what a reader typically volunteers). I want the questions in order, what counts as a pass on each one, where the procedure terminates with a verdict of "this isn't risk-sensitivity at all", and where it hands the decision back to values because no amount of consistency-checking settles it.

Some things it has to handle, because they're exactly where I currently get lost:

- The four or five look-alikes that readers call risk aversion and that the procedure should be able to separate out — diminishing marginal utility of money, narrow framing and probability weighting, aversion to unknown rather than quantified probabilities, and whatever the honest case for genuine global risk-sensitivity actually is. Each needs its own tell.
- The representation-theorem problem: if any coherent enough set of preferences can be redescribed as expected-utility maximisation with a suitably bent utility function, my procedure can't just declare someone "really" risk-weighting rather than utility-curving. Tell me what work the distinction is still doing, or tell me to drop it.
- The calibration trap — the argument that refusing small bets at these odds commits you to refusing preposterous large bets, or to refusing tiny bets you'd obviously take. A reader who declines a small one-off risk and also declines a small repeated one isn't in the same position, and the procedure needs to treat those differently rather than scoring both as caution.
- The dynamic-choice objection: global risk-sensitivity gives up the sure-thing principle, so it's vulnerable to being money-pumped or to changing its mind once a gamble is partly resolved. Where in my procedure does that actually bite, and what do I tell a reader whose stance only survives because they're never going to face the same bet twice?

Then run it on three submissions I've invented but which are close enough to real ones that they'll keep recurring: (1) a reader in their late sixties offered an elective operation with roughly a 2% chance of a permanent serious complication and good odds otherwise, who has refused and wants to know whether that was cowardice or good sense; (2) a reader who won't cycle to work because of a roughly one-in-fifty-thousand-per-trip fatality risk but drives the same route daily, and describes this as a coherent philosophy of risk; (3) a school governor who campaigned hard against a new playing surface over a small chance of a rare reaction in one child, and who cheerfully supports the same kind of surface across the borough's other schools — I rather like this governor and I'd prefer the procedure to side with them, so please be straight with me if it doesn't.

Mark clearly which steps are judgement calls wearing a procedural costume, and finish with what the procedure simply cannot do for me — where I have to stop writing and say the case is undecidable. Please write the whole thing in British English, and don't give me a bibliography or citations: I can't check them from here and I'd much rather you left them out entirely than guessed.
