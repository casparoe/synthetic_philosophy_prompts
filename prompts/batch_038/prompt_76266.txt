So, I sit on a small environmental review board — eleven of us, mostly part-time, one lawyer, one ecologist, zero philosophers — and next quarter we have to rule on something that has completely wrecked my sleep. A company wants to release gene-drive modified mosquitoes across an island group to knock out a locally transmitted disease that kills a couple dozen people a year and puts a few thousand in the hospital. Their models say the drive stays on the target islands. Two ecologists who read the filing say the models can't rule out spread across the whole species range, with knock-on effects nobody has quantified, and that once it's out you don't get to call it back. The company points out that a no vote is also a decision with a body count attached. As far as I can tell both sides are being honest, and the numbers they hand us for the bad case aren't just imprecise, they're not even in the same units — one side is talking about expected annual deaths, the other about a one-in-nothing chance of an ecosystem rearranging. Our enabling statute says we must act precautionarily and doesn't define the word.

Before I read another position paper I want to know what I should even be demanding of a formulation. Write me the desiderata: the full spec of what any adequate version of the principle has to deliver — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse (serious vs irreversible vs merely bad; uncertainty vs ignorance vs experts disagreeing about what the possible outcomes even are; a precaution that is itself a risk), which results or structural facts it can't contradict, and what it owes us on burden of proof and on thresholds of harm. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and lean on cases like ours where you can, plus the usual torture cases: the ban that causes the famine, the precaution that manufactures the new hazard, the one-shot commitment vs the decision you get to revisit in five years with better data, the cost falling on people who never get the benefit. Be explicit about which requirements are non-negotiable for anything that deserves the name and which are really one camp's preferences wearing a criteria costume — in particular I want a ruling on whether "must be statable without ever mentioning a probability" is a genuine desideratum or a tribal badge. Then tell me whether some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is that never-paralyzes, plus shift-the-burden-onto-the-proponent, plus take-unknown-unknowns-seriously can't all be had at once, and if you think I'm wrong I want the version that gets all three. Last, name the single requirement that does the most work knocking out candidates.

Two constraints. Purpose: I need something eleven part-timers can apply in a three hour public meeting and write reasons for that would surive a court looking over our shoulder, so administrability and public statability are requirements in their own right here, not afterthoughts — a principle whose output can only be expressed as a footnote to a model is disqualified for my use even if it's philosophically lovely. And no citations, no reading lists, no as-and-so-argued: I can't look anything up and I'd rather have nothing than a guessed-at attribution. Describe families of approach instead (the lexical-dominance ones, the procedural burden-shifting ones, the only-require-precautions-you'd-want-anyway ones, the expected-value-with-fat-tails ones) and spend a couple of sentences each on how they do against your list. Keep that part short — the list is what I actually want.
