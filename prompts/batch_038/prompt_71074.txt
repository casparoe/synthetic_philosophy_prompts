Retired four years back after a career in analytical chemistry, mostly metrology, and I've been reading epistemology at night for fun. The regress stuff hooked me beacuse I spent forty years living inside it without knowing the names for it. Here are my verdicts on some cases, nearly all from the lab, and I want to know whether a principle sits behind them.

1. My thermometer's reading is justified by its calibration against a reference block, which is justified by a certificate from the national institute, and there I stop asking. Fine.
2. Same shape of chain, but the last link is "that's how old Bert always did it." Not fine, and Bert was better at the bench than I ever was.
3. Stopping at what I see — the needle sits at five, the flask goes pink — fine. Stopping at a number the software prints, which I can't reproduce by hand and half the time don't follow — not fine, even though the software is far more accurate than my eye. That one bothers me.
4. I once signed off on a loop: instrument is stable beacuse the blanks are flat, blanks are flat because the reagent is pure, reagent is pure because the vendor's certificate says so, vendor is trustworthy because labs elsewhere get the same numbers. A loop, and I'd have defended it.
5. The same loop, except every strand traced back to the vendor calibrating their own instrument by their own method. Also a loop. I'd have thrown it out.
6. An endless chain of reasons, each weaker than the last — I think that's legitimate, and I think I used one constantly, since the real chain from my reading to the definition of the kelvin is longer than any person can check.
7. A very long finite chain, each link weaker than the last, most links unchecked by me — not fine, that's just swallowing the literature.

6 and 7 look identical to me and I can't feel a difference. Same trouble with 3, and with 4 against 5.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, say which case earns each clause, and say plainly which of my verdicts it has to overturn. Then tell me what it says about the case I keep flip-flopping on — a simulation result from code I didn't write and can't check, which agrees with two other simulations I also can't check, coded independently by different groups — and about some case of your own choosing that I haven't thought of.
