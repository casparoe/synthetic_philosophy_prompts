I spent 38 years in isotope geochemistry and only started reading history and philosophy of science seriously after I retired, so forgive me if I'm naieve about how these comittees actually work. I sit on the advisory board of our county museum up here in New England, and next month we vote on the wall text for a new exhibit on 1816, the year without a summer — Tambora, the frosts in June, the ruined harvests, the families that packed up and went west.

The designer has proposed something that's split us down the middle: she wants Mount Tambora printed into the credit panel as a collaborating agent, listed right beside the human curators and the historical society that loaned the artifacts. Her case is that the volcano did things nobody in the story did, and treating it as mere background is a kind of dishonesty — especially since the attribution isn't even clean, there's background cooling, a weak solar cycle, and at least one earlier eruption mixed in. Two of the historians think it's determinism in a nice font: credit the mountain as an actor and you quietly turn the farmers, the relief committees, and the people who decided to stay into scenery.

Write the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Give a concrete call on the credit line — keep, kill, or rewrite, and if rewrite, show me the actual wording — plus what happens between now and opening, and a genuine engagement with the historians' objection rather than a polite nod to it. Then tell me how confident you are in the recommendation, and single out the one part of it you're least sure of.
