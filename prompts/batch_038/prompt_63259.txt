I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my editor has commissioned a two-thousand-word essay on where Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument stands half a century on. Before I draft my own version I want a rigorous model essay to react against. Please write it.

The question I want settled is narrower than the one that usually gets asked. The familiar framing — does the basketball story refute egalitarianism — has been argued to exhaustion, and both sides have their moves memorized. What I want you to take a position on is this: does the argument establish anything general about patterned principles of distributive justice, or does it only establish a conclusion about one rather special family of them, namely those that fix the entire profile of holdings at a time?

To make that tractable, work through four things.

First, separate two claims the argument is usually credited with at once. One is a feasibility claim: that any patterned principle, to be maintained over time, requires either prohibiting voluntary transfers or endlessly intervening to undo their effects. The other is a permissibility claim: that such intervention is unjust because it takes from people what they are entitled to. The first is a piece of modal bookkeeping about distributions; the second presupposes precisely the property rights the patterned theorist is disputing. Say which of the two the Chamberlain story actually supports, and how much of the argument's reputation depends on readers sliding from one to the other.

Second, test the argument against patterns that do not fix the whole profile. Consider a floor that says only that no one may fall below a threshold. Consider a ceiling, or a ratio cap between top and median. Consider a pattern stated over opportunities or over the distribution of political influence rather than over holdings. Suppose the starting distribution satisfies a floor and each of a million fans parts with twenty-five cents to watch a player they value more highly: has the pattern been upset, merely strained, or left entirely intact? If one fan's quarter does push his household below the threshold, is that a discovery about liberty or a discovery about the pattern?

Third, deal with the variant I have been turning over, which you should call the relay case. The player does not hoard his windfall; he spends it on a regional training academy that quickly dominates youth competition and prices the local public league out of existence. The academy's fees are paid by the households that can still afford them, and within a generation the children of the very families whose quarters funded the original transfer are the ones shut out of the pathway that transfer created. Every step is voluntary, and on the entitlement view every transfer is just. Say whether a theory whose stability claim is recursive — that just holdings voluntarily transferred stay just — can absorb this, or whether it must bite the bullet; and say what follows for the patterned theorist who wants to argue that the injustice here is cumulative rather than located in any single act.

Fourth, address the proviso. Nozick himself limits original acquisition by a condition about not worsening others' situation. Ask whether that condition can be extended from acquisitions to the aggregate of many small transfers, and whether extending it would collapse entitlement theory back into something with a shape after all.

Then commit. A conditional verdict is acceptable — the Newcomb-style "it works against X but not against Y" — provided the condition is drawn precisely and defended, not offered as a way of avoiding a verdict. Give the strongest version of the position you reject a paragraph of its own, stated so well that a partisan of it would recognize it, and then answer it.

Two house rules. Argue from the logic of the cases rather than from authority: no bibliography, no footnotes, no name-dropping of critics or defenders, and no pretending to quote anyone; unpack every technical term on first use for a reader who is intelligent and curious but has never taken a political philosophy course. And resist the usual modernizations. I have read the creator-platform paragraph a hundred times; if you reach for a contemporary analogue, make it one that stresses the argument rather than merely illustrating it. Open with the case, not with a biography of the author. Aim for about two thousand words.
