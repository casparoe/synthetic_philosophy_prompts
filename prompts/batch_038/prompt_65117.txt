I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student and this term I'm TAing an upper-level seminar, "Moral Obligation and Its Limits" — fourteen students, mostly seniors — which in practice means I'm the one writing and marking the in-class final. I want one essay question on supererogation, worth 20 marks, to be written in 75 minutes, and I want to kill the failure mode from last year, where nearly everyone reproduced Urmson's four marks of the supererogatory (optional, good, praiseworthy, better than what duty requires) and then declared either that consequentialism can't accommodate the category or that it can, depending on which side they'd already picked. Reciting the analysis earned a B on its own. So the question has to make the analysis grind on material the students have never seen, and here's the material, which comes out of a chapter of my own dissertation that my advisor keeps saying isn't a real view. Call it the ledger view: when you perform a genuinely supererogatory act you acquire a stock of moral credit, and that credit lowers your obligations going forward, so whether some act is now required of you depends in part on your own history of good-but-optional acts. The credit definately has to be agent-relative and spendable — Al's kidney donation to Bea buys Al, not Bea, some slack — and for the exam I'd like it treated as expiring after some period, since otherwise you get a saint who accumulates a lifetime exemption and then stops mattering morally at all.

Here's what I need from you. First, the exam question itself, with the ledger view fully defined in the stem: the students have never heard of it, so the stem has to be self-contained, roughly a page, and state the view plus two or three of its consequences (credit is spent down, it's indexed to the agent's history, an act's status can flip from supererogatory to merely permissible or even required as your balance changes). Second, a model answer of about 700 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. The question should be aimed so that a decent answer has to engage at least some of these, and I'd like the marking scheme to say which ones are load-bearing and which are bonus: whether a credit system can specify duties at all without making them endogenous in a way that lets you pre-purchase permission to walk past a drowning stranger (the self-service worry, and whether it's really just the bootstrapping problem in a new costume); whether the ledger view needs a scalar account of duty to make credit commensurable, and if so whether that means it's only available to someone who has already given up the off-the-call reading of "beyond the call of duty"; what's left of the general claim that donating a kidney is supererogatory if the answer is indexed to the agent's balance, and whether that's a bug or actually a point in the view's favor; the response objection, that praise is supposed to register the value of the act and turning that value into spendable currency changes what the practice of praising is for; and what a Scheffler-style agent-centered prerogative does here, given that its own defender says the hybrid view doesn't deliver supererogation but only acts that are permissible-but-not-best. I also want the scheme to catch two specific cheap moves: invoking Heyd's paradox as if it were a knockdown refutation rather than a live argument with a reply on the table (roughly, that the good-making features of an act need not be duty-generating), and the handwave that the whole thing "shows the analysis is circular" without saying circular in which direction.

No name-checking should earn marks, and no marks for saying that supererogation is interesting because it sits between duty and wrongdoing. Write everything in American English, keep the question stem in a register a tired senior can actually parse at 9 a.m., and make the marking scheme operational enough that I can apply it consistently across fourteen scripts in one sitting. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in ten words or fewer, the single issue that seperates an A answer from a B answer on this question.
