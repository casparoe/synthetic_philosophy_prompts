Something's been bugging me about how lab animal oversight actually works. In the EU and most other places, ethics committees have jurisdiction over vertebrates and cephalopods — a fruit fly, a cricket or a honeybee can go into any apparatus you like with no review whatsoever. Meanwhile the UK a few years back explicitly wrote octopuses and lobsters into its animal sentience legislation. So a cuttlefish gets a committee and a bee gets nothing, and the animals on the wrong side of that line are also the ones that exist in numbers bigger than everything else combined by orders of magnitude.

What theory is that practice actually running on? Reconstruct the implicit commitments: is the cut-off about phylogenetic distance from us, about sentience being a binary all-or-nothing property, about who carries the burden of proof, or about what a regulator can ennumerate and inspect? Which rival positions does publishing a species list quietly rule out — graded welfare, say, or expected-value reasoning under moral uncertainty? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with, because I suspect several incompatible theories license the exact same list.

And then: does the scale point make the whole thing unstable? If you hold a small but non-zero credence in insect sentience, does a zero-standing regime survive it, and what would have to change institutionally — beyond just adding taxa to the list — to take that credence seriously?
