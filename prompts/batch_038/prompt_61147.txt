An old friend and I have just spent the better part of an evening on a question neither of us would concede: whether the Keynes of the General Theory abandoned the Keynes of the Treatise on Probability, trading a logic of probability relations for a psychology of moods. My case is that the later work puts the earlier doctrine under pressure rather than dropping it, and since neither of us will be moved by another paraphrase, I have decided the argument needs a poem.

Write a sestina of thirty-nine lines — six sestets and a three-line envoi, using the standard end-word permutation (6-1-5-2-4-3) rather than a free rotation. Three of the six end-words must come from Keynes's logical vocabulary (something like weight, relevance, ignorance); three must come from Ramsey's — the Ramsey of the essay written in 1926 and published after his death — so something like bet, coherence, disposition. The form has to carry the argument: because the permutation returns each word in a new position without ever exchanging one for another, the six must never collapse onto a single scale, and that refusal is precisely the claim.

The content should be exact enough that a specialist would not wince. Probability as a relation between a body of evidence and a proposition, not a property of events; the possibility that two probability relations are simply not comparable, as when the barometer stands high and the clouds are black; evidence that increases the weight of an argument while leaving its probability unmoved or even lowering it; and the later insistence that about some matters there is no calculable probability whatever. Let my friend's objection sound somewhere inside the stanzas instead of being answered from outside. The envoi should be the moment the decision is taken without a sufficient basis — and it must not treat animal spirits as a synonym for exuberance. A reader who has never opened the Treatise should still find it a good poem.
