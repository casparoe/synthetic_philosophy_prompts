hey, so i'm in a normative ethics seminar this term and I'm presenting next week on supererogation — specifically on whether it's a real deontic category or just an artifact of theories that are scared of being demanding. I want to build my own pair of cases instead of recycling the stock ones, and I'm hoping you can construct them to spec for me.

Here's what I need. Two scenarios, A and B, same setting, same agent, and literally the same act described the same way — except that in A the act is supererogatory (permissible to omit, morally good, and genuinely costly to the agent) and in B that exact act is something the agent is morally required to do. The flip between A and B has to come from ONE feature, and it can't be any of the obvious ones:

- not the size of the cost or risk to the agent
- not the stakes for whoever is being helped
- not a new role duty, job, contract, or promise
- not making the agent the person who caused the need in the first place

So whatever lever you pull has to be something else entirely.

The point of the pair — this is my actual argument in the presentation, so tell me if it can't be pulled off — is that you can move the line between "good but optional" and "required" without touching cost or stakes at all. Which also means: please don't stipulate the verdicts into the description. No "heroically," no "she obviously had to," no loading the adjectives. The statuses have to fall out of the theories.

Other constraints: realistic and mundane — a shared flat, an office, a school pickup, a community garden, that register. No burning buildings, no drowning kids in ponds, no kidneys, no throwing yourself on grenades, and no big money donations; everyone already knows those cases and the drama hides the structure. The cost to the agent still has to be real, not trivial, because if it's basically free a lot of accounts will say it's just "nice" rather than supererogatory. Each scenario one paragraph, 150 words max.

Then after the cases:

1. Run both through the three standard conditions one at a time and show exactly which condition holds in A and which one fails in B.
2. Name the lever you used, say which of the three conditions it moves, and explain the mechanism — why that feature and not something else is what turns permission into requirement.
3. Tell me which feature of the case is load-bearing and what happens to the pair if you delete it.
4. Give me the best attack on the pair from someone who denies supererogation exists at all (a maximizing consequentialist, say), and separately the best attack from the other direction — someone who thinks A isn't supererogatory either, because the cost is too everyday to count as a sacrifice worth the label.

Finally, be straight with me about how confident you are the design actually works. I want to know what would change your mind — which consideration would make you conclude that B is still only supererogatory after all, or that A never was — and where in your own reasoning you're most likely to be wrong. And if you had to lean on a contested reading of "costly" or of "permissible to omit" to get the pair to come out right, flag that explicitly instead of papering over it.
