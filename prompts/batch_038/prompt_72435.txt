I run a graduate seminar on population axiology and I've been keeping a private list of my own verdicts on the cases we read, mostly so I can catch myself out in front of the students, and I've finally admitted they don't hang together. Here's the list. Base world A: ten billion people, all with very good lives, nothing pathological about distribution.

1. A+ — ten billion more people added, each with a life worth living but at a lower level than the original ten billion, and stipulated so that the addition doesn't take anything from anyone: not worse than A. Arguably better.
2. B — the same twenty billion, but the two groups levelled up so everyone is at the original high level: clearly better than A+.
3. Repeat 1 and 2 over and over until you land on Z: a vast population, all of them with lives barely worth living. Verdict: Z is worse than A, and not just slightly worse. I don't want to say it's a tie.
4. Add to a huge existing population one single person whose life is barely worth living and who doesn't affect anyone else in any way — no resource drawdown, no one even learns they exist. Verdict: not bad. Maybe very slightly good.
5. Same addition, but the existing population is small and superbly well off. Verdict: this one feels bad to me in a way 4 doesn't, and I genuinely can't say why, since the added person affects nobody in either case and neither group knows about the other.
6. One person in ecstasy versus a trillion people at barely-worth-living. Verdict: the single ecstatic person, provided the quality gap is wide enough. So quality has to do some work that number can't outvote.
7. The teenage mother who conceives now rather than waiting a few years; the child she has will have a life worth living, but a worse one than the different child she'd have had if she'd waited. Verdict: her choice was worse in some impersonal sense, she wronged no one in particular, and yet I still want to call it criticisable rather than merely suboptimal.
8. Structurally identical but collective: a depletion policy that brings into existence future people whose lives are worth living and slightly worse than the lives the alternative future people would have had. Verdict: wrong, full stop, and I don't want to soften this to 7.

The pair I can't repair is 4 and 5. And 1 through 3 are each steps I've called not-worse or better, which is supposed to land me on Z, and then 3 says Z is worse. And 6 wants quality to have lexical-ish force, but if it does, 4 should come out bad and I don't want it to.

My tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: 'an addition makes things no worse if no existing person is made worse off, the added people have lives worth living, and the addition creates no comparison-class between people who can measure themselves against each other.' That last clause is my attempt at 5. It does save 1 and 4, it doesn't obviously save 5 the way I want, it walks me straight down the chain to Z anyway because each step is separately permitted, and it turns 7 into nothing worse than a missed opportunity, which I don't want either.

So, what I'd like: give me a principle, or two or three competing ones, precise enough that a smart undergraduate could apply them mechanically to a case description without asking me anything. For each, tell me which clause is doing the work on which numbered case — I want the mapping, not a summary. Then apply them to cases I haven't listed, specifically: (a) Segregated Z, where the trillion barely-worth-living people each inhabit their own sealed arcology and never meet, never communicate, and have no shared institutions or economy, versus ten billion at a high level in one integrated society; (b) subtracting from Z — the extra people are removed such that the survivors never notice and lose nothing, leaving ten billion at the high level — is that better, worse, or neutral, and does the answer depend on whether they are merely removed or never existed; (c) adding a trillion animals whose lives are barely worth living to a world full of flourishing people. And be straight with me about the cost: for each principle, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. If no principle can keep both 4 and 5, or both the step-by-step permissiveness of 1–2 and the verdict in 3, say so plainly, pick the one you'd throw out, and explain what I lose — I'd rather have a simple principle that overturns one of my intuitions and tells me exactly which one than an elaborate one that saves everything with four epicycles.
