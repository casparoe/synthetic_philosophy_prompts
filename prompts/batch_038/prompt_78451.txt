I have spent thirty-three years in precision machining and inspection, the last nineteen of them running a small shop that makes parts for other people's assemblies. I have no academic training in philosophy. I read a little, badly and out of order, and I have been turning over the question of whether numbers exist for the better part of a decade, mostly at night and mostly without anyone to argue with. What follows is an essay I have drafted. I am not looking for encouragement, and I am not looking for a reading list: I have neither library access nor the ability to work my way through a set of sources, so please do not send me off to read anything. I would rather have six specific judgements from you than a syllabus.

---

**The Nominal Dimension**

Every drawing that leaves my office carries dimensions of a particular kind. A bore is specified as 25.000 millimetres with a tolerance of plus or minus 0.01. The 25.000 is called the nominal dimension. Nothing in the building is exactly 25.000 millimetres long; my best machine gets close enough that the difference cannot be measured with the instruments I own, but it does not get there, and nothing physical ever does. A machined surface is a landscape, not a plane. And yet every measurement we take, and every judgement we make about whether a part is acceptable, is a measurement of departure from the nominal. The nominal is not made of anything. You cannot hand it to anyone. It does not causally interact with the metal. Still, it is not nothing: it is what makes separate measurements comparable, what turns a drawing into a specification rather than a description, and what allows a part cut in my shop to fit an assembly built eight thousand kilometres away by people I will never meet.

I understand the philosophical dispute, such as I understand it, to run roughly as follows. Platonists hold that numbers and other mathematical objects exist independently of us, outside space and time, and that our mathematical statements are made true by them. Nominalists deny that they exist, or at least insist that we need not believe in them in order to have science and arithmetic. Both positions appear to want the same thing from me: a yes or a no about whether 25.000 is out there. I think the drawing suggests a third posture, which I will now try to state and will probably state badly.

My proposal is that a mathematical object is a standing instrument. It exists in the way that the rule of a craft exists: not as a physical object, not as a private mental image, and not as a mere pretence, but as something a community maintains in common and against which its individual results are measured. Two features do the work. First, it is public and shareable — the same nominal governs every shop that works from the drawing, and there is no question of my nominal differing from yours. Second, it is normative — particular outcomes are correct or incorrect relative to it, and the nominal never bends to accommodate a bad part. My parts are approximations of it; it is never an approximation of my parts. I suspect that this last asymmetry is the entire source of the feeling that numbers must be somewhere else, somewhere better than here. We are used to things that are answerable to us. We are not used to things we are answerable to that are not objects, and so we reach for a second, ghostly stock of objects.

The place where my analogy strains is worth putting in the open, because I would rather be corrected on it than protected. For most of the twentieth century the kilogram was a cylinder of platinum and iridium kept in a vault near Paris; every national copy was what it was by comparison to that artifact. The artifact had a defect: it drifted. Over the decades it came to differ, slightly, in mass from its own official copies, and there was nothing more fundamental to appeal to in settling which was right. That problem was eventually dissolved rather than solved, when the kilogram was redefined in 2019 by reference to a fixed value of a constant of nature, so that no object is the standard any longer. The nominal dimension has no such defect and could not have one. 25.000 cannot gain or lose. If every measuring instrument on earth were altered, the departure of my part from 25.000 would not change, because there is no such thing as 25.000 changing. If mathematical objects are instruments of this kind, then, they are instruments made of nothing that can wear out — which is exactly why they feel eternal, and exactly why I do not think the feeling is evidence for a separate realm of being. It is evidence of a different kind of job.

The argument I most want to answer is the one that says our best physics cannot be stated without mathematics, and that if we accept the theory we have accepted what it takes to state it. My reply from the shop is this: I cannot state a single job packet without nominals, and I am not thereby committed to a nominal being a piece of furniture in the universe. Indispensability tells you what a practice requires in order to be carried on at all; it does not tell you what exists. But here I know I am on thin ice, and I am not certain the ice will hold me. The numbers on my drawing are there so that separate people can coordinate with one another. The numbers in physics seem to be doing something else: they seem to explain why things happen, rather than merely allowing us to agree about what has happened. If that difference is real, my analogy may cover the bookkeeping use of mathematics and leave the interesting use entirely untouched.

Finally, I want to say what my view is not, because it keeps sliding toward positions I do not hold. It is not the claim that numbers are ideas in our heads; the whole point of the nominal is that it constrains me from outside my own head, and constrains people who have never heard of me. It is not the claim that mathematical sentences are false, or that we only speak as if they were true; when I say this part is eight thousandths over nominal I am saying something true, and its truth is nobody's opinion. What I want to hold is the difficult pair: mathematics is genuinely true and genuinely ours. I expect that is where I fail, and I would like to be shown exactly how.

---

My questions, in the order that matters to me:

1. On accuracy. Where have I misdescribed the two established positions? I strongly suspect that I have turned nominalism into a flat denial when the interesting versions of it are more like a refusal of commitment or a story about how the sentences come out acceptable without the objects; tell me that plainly, and tell me about any other place where I have flattened a view I do not fully have.

2. On originality, which for me is a practical question rather than a vanity. Does the position I have sketched — mathematical objects as publicly maintained instruments of a practice, against which individual results are correct or incorrect and which cannot themselves drift — already have a name and a standard statement? I am not asking for sources to chase down. I am asking only whether the room is occupied, and if it is, in what precise respect my version differs from what is already standing there. If the drift argument in particular is a known move, say so.

3. On structure. Which paragraph is actually carrying the weight of the piece, and does it hold? My own guess is the paragraph about the drifting artifact, since everything else could be said by someone who liked the analogy less.

4. On the thin ice. State the objection about coordination versus explanation as strongly as a convinced platonist would actually press it — not a version I could swat away. Then tell me honestly whether a view like mine can absorb that objection, or whether it is the kind of thing that leaves my position covering only the trivial cases of mathematics.

5. On the shop. Is the machining material doing real argumentative work, or is it picturesque decoration that a reader would tolerate for two paragraphs and resent by the fifth? Tell me which passages would survive if I cut the concrete examples entirely, because if the answer is almost none of them, I need to know that.

6. Your verdict, without kindness. Is this worth developing into something longer, or is it a good analogy in search of a thesis?
