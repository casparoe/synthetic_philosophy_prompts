I never studied philosophy, so this comes from thinking on my own and from an argument that has been running in my gaming group for the better part of a year. I would like you to audit the comparisons both sides keep reaching for, because I suspect the argument is being settled by those comparisons rather than by anything anybody actually says.

The setting: I play a large story-driven role-playing game in which the companions you travel with have their own loyalties, keep a running account of how you have treated them, and can die permanently. Quicksaving is available and most of us use it constantly.

One side of the group says a game character is like a character in a novel. There is nobody there; the character is made out of description and cannot be harmed any more than a chapter can be harmed; and the only reason to care about what happens in a book is what it does to you as a reader. From this they conclude that nothing a player does to a character can be wrong, and that anyone who feels guilt over a companion's death has merely muddled themselves about what is on the screen.

The other side says a game is like a sparring ring, or a flight simulator. The opponent is not real, but the rehearsal is: what you are doing when you choose cruelty in a game is practising a way of being a person. From this they conclude that the novel comparison is not just mistaken but evasive, because a novel happens to you while a game requires you to decide and to press the button.

Then there is my own complication, which neither side handles. A couple of months ago I killed one of my companions in order to find out what the rest of the party would do, and afterwards I reloaded the save and carried on as though nothing had happened. The first side says that settles the matter: reloading is like a writer crumpling up a bad draft, the deleted playthrough never happened, and there is nothing left to answer for. I am not convinced, and I have not managed to say why in any organised fashion. I notice only that I learned something about what I was prepared to do, and that when the reloaded companion warmed to me again, the trust in it seemed to carry a different weight.

What I would like from you:

1. For each of the three comparisons — the novel, the sparring ring, the crumpled draft — set out explicitly what is supposed to correspond to what. A table would be welcome: who or what plays the author, the reader, the character, the page, the opponent, the ring, the bin, the manuscript.
2. Say where each comparison stops working, and separate the breakdowns that genuinely damage the conclusion it was being used to support from those that are interesting but beside the point.
3. Say what each one quietly assumes about its target without having earned it — features of novels, of sparring, and of drafts that get smuggled across with the comparison.
4. Tell me whether either side's conclusion survives once all of that is on the table, and whether the reload is really neutralised by the draft comparison or whether something gets through it.
5. If one of the three is simply the wrong way to think about this, say what a better comparison would be and what it would let you conclude that these cannot. The furthest I have got on my own is that a game character is like a stranger you meet on an overnight train: the conversation is entirely real while it lasts, both of you are altered by it, and then one of you gets off and there is no afterwards. I cannot tell whether the missing afterwards makes such a meeting weightless or only finite, so please test that one as well rather than take it from me.

Give me your bottom line in your very first sentence — which of the three comparisons is doing the most work, and whether either side's conclusion survives — and then the reasoning behind it, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Please write in British English.
