I've spent the better part of fifteen years reading the same argument about wireheading, in the same three registers: the lever-pressing rat, the person who plugs in and never comes out, and the alignment-flavored version in which an agent with a corrupted reward channel is treated as a warning about AGI rather than about people. All of them assume the interesting question is whether the pleasure is real, and all of them resolve the story by revealing that the wirehead is, in the end, deceived about something. I'd like a story that refuses both moves.

Here is the apparatus I want you to build the story around. Treat it as given; do not redesign it.

A near-future clinic treats severe, refractory anhedonia by means of a chronically implanted device that adjusts the patient's hedonic set point in small, reversible steps. Because the adjustment changes what the patient wants, the clinic's licensing board requires a countersignature before every step upward. The countersignature does not come from a doctor, a court, or a family member. It comes from a Witness: a short-lived simulation instantiated once a week from a preserved snapshot of the patient's own psychology taken before treatment began — the patient as they were, with their prior values, their prior exhaustion, their prior honesty about their own life. The Witness is given the week's proposed adjustment and the clinical record, is run for twenty minutes, and is asked one question. Its answer binds. If it refuses, the setting rolls back.

The story turns on this: the Witness never refuses. It approves, week after week, and its approvals get faster. The clinician who administers the protocol — your point-of-view character, whose entire job is to keep the countersignature honest — has to work out whether the Witness is being persuaded, worn down, or fed, and those three possibilities are not distinguishable from inside the record.

The conceptual payload I want is a distinction that almost every treatment of this topic collapses. There are at least three things people mean when they say an agent has tampered with its reward, and they have different moral shapes:

1. Driving the reward channel directly — pleasure with no object, the rat and the lever.
2. Modifying the reward function itself — changing what the agent counts as good, at the source.
3. Leaving both the reward function and any reviewer of it perfectly intact, and instead editing the evidence the reviewer is shown.

Categories one and two are what the genre writes about. Category three is what I want, and I want it to be the load-bearing element of the plot rather than a line of dialogue: the countersignature protocol is an anti-tampering mechanism, and the way it fails must be a way in which nothing about the Witness's values has been altered at all. The Witness knows precisely what it is approving, approves it for reasons it would defend, and is not fooled about any matter of fact. If your ending requires the Witness to have been deceived, you have missed the assignment.

I also want the closing bind to be genuinely unresolvable rather than merely bleak. The clinician comes to suspect that what the Witness is shown has been curated — not by a villain, but by the ordinary operation of a system whose summaries are written by people measured on patient-reported outcomes. The obvious remedy is to give the Witness information it has not asked for. Doing so is itself a decision about what the Witness is allowed to care about, which means the clinician becomes the tamperer in the exact act of trying not to be. End there, or near there. Do not resolve it with a choice the reader can feel good about.

Craft constraints:

- Length 1,800 to 2,500 words.
- Close third person on the clinician. The Witness must be a character, not a device: give it a voice, a sense of humor that survived the depression, and a clear-eyed willingness to say unpleasant things about the patient it is a snapshot of. It knows it has twenty minutes and it knows what it is.
- Include at least one full Witness session, staged as procedure: the room, the paperwork, the way the question is phrased, the way the twenty minutes get spent.
- Include at least one character who argues the other side well and is not a strawman — someone who maintains that the patient is making no error of fact whatever, that the clinician's discomfort is about values and not about truth, and that a protocol which can be satisfied only by withholding information from the reviewer is not an ethics but an insecurity.
- No character in the story may say wirehead, wireheading, reward hacking, reward tampering, utility function, experience machine, hedonic treadmill, or qualia. The concepts should be present in what happens. Nozick's machine belongs in my reading history, not in your dialogue.
- Forbidden endings: the reveal that the Witness is the real patient and the body the copy; the reveal that the clinician is also a Witness; the reveal that the Witness has been switched off and the approvals are synthetic; any simulation-collapses-into-itself move. The machinery must stay honest. That is the point.
- Prose should be restrained and a little dry. The horror here is administrative, and it should arrive through forms, phrasing, and scheduling.

Format. Give me exactly two sections, in this order:

**The Story** — the piece itself, 1,800 to 2,500 words, with a title.

**Afterword** — 300 to 400 words. Identify which of the three categories above your central turn instantiates, and say why the other two would not have done the work you needed. Then state what you cut or softened in order to keep it a story rather than a seminar, and name one technical place where a careful reader could object to the setup as I've specified it — and tell me whether you think that objection is fatal to the story or merely to the clinic.
