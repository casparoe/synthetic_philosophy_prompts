The Remains of the Day is the one novel on my historiography syllabus, and this term I want to use it against the presentism debate instead of as another unreliable-narrator exercise. Here's the angle I keep circling. Stevens is, as a first-person ethic, exactly what the anti-presentist tells the historian to be: he never imports standards from outside his world — dignity, the Hayes Society, what a great gentleman's moral authority entitles him to decide — and he applies that discipline hardest to himself. And the book makes that refusal the disaster of his life. So the injunction to judge the past only by its own terms, adopted as a way of living, starts to look like abdication. Does Ishiguro know that, and does it hand the presentist a real argument that the historian's own standpoint isn't a distortion to be corrected but a precondition of judgement at all?

Please separate two claims people slide between — that we can't describe the past except in our own concepts, and that we shouldn't evaluate it except by its own standards — and say which one the novel is actually attacking. Then tell me who's doing the history in the book. The reader has to reconstruct the 1923 conference and the sacking of the two Jewish housemaids out of a narrator who is busy hiding them, which is a a strange version of the internal-source problem: testimony from someone who doesn't believe his own testimony.

And be honest where the reading breaks. The pub at Little Compton, maybe, where the villagers' present-tense verdict on Darlington looks cruder than Stevens' refusal to give one. End with what the novel can show here that a methodologist's argument can't. No quotes or references needed, I've got the book — 1200 words or so.
