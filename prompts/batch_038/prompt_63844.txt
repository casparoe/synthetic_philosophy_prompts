I have been trying to sort out my own judgments about when an engagement with the past strengthens a person or a community and when it sickens them. I took as my starting point the three modes of history that Nietzsche distinguishes in his essay on the use and abuse of history for life — the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical — but I have found that his diagnoses do not track my verdicts at all reliably, and I now want to know what principle my verdicts themselves add up to, if any. Below are the cases with my gut reactions attached.

1. A twenty-year-old organizer reads everything she can about the Haitian Revolution and emerges convinced that people like her may attempt what looks impossible. Clearly good.

2. The same organizer a decade later cannot begin any project, because for every tactic she considers she can name three predecessors who tried it and failed. Clearly bad — and it strikes me as the same body of knowledge, which is the first thing I cannot explain.

3. A small town keeps a modest museum of its own founding and observes the founders' day each year. Fine.

4. That town votes down a new school because the only suitable site is the founders' meeting house. Not fine.

5. A man learns that a grandfather his family has venerated for forty years was an informer, and insists the flattering stories stop being told. Fine. I think the pain this causes does not count against it.

6. A woman learns in middle age that the country she was raised to be proud of was built on dispossession, and concludes she can no longer love anything inherited from it — its language, its holidays, its institutions — then finds herself unable to act politically at all and spends her days in shame. Not fine. But case 5 was fine, and the only difference I can name is how it turned out, which seems to me far too weak a ground.

7. A descendant of enslaved people declines to study that history, saying she has other things to do with her life. Within her rights, entirely.

8. A descendant of slaveholders declines to study the same history, giving the same reason. Not within his rights. Identical act, identical stated reason, opposite verdict. This is the pair I most want accounted for.

9. A scholar spends forty years on a scribal tradition with perhaps two hundred living readers, in a language almost nobody speaks, and says it has been the best thing in his life. Fine.

10. Another scholar does something I cannot distinguish from that and describes himself as hollowed out by it, incapable of loving anyone or anything else. Not fine.

11. A village celebrates each spring a founding legend that everyone in it knows never happened. Fine; they wink at it, and it binds them.

12. A national curriculum teaches a legend of the same general shape and forbids alternatives on pain of dismissal. Not fine — though on the surface this is case 1 with a larger budget.

13. A government commissions a complete, permanent, fully searchable public archive of a decade of state violence: every testimony, every order, every name, nothing ever removed. I am genuinely undecided here. Part of me wants this to be an unqualified good, and part of me suspects that a society rendered incapable of forgetting anything is thereby rendered incapable of beginning anything.

My tentative principle is simply this: history serves life insofar as it leaves the person able to act, and harms life insofar as it does not. Please test it against all thirteen. I already expect it to fail, since it appears to give the same verdict on 5 and 7 where I give different ones, and since it seems to make the whole standard a matter of what happens to turn out well for one particular individual.

What I want in return is a principle, or at most two or three competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of; every clause tied to the case or cases that motivate it; its verdicts on 13 and on the pair 9 and 10, which I regard as my hardest; and a candid list of the reactions above that it cannot save, together with what a consistent person should say about them instead. Where you judge one of my verdicts to be not merely unexplained but simply wrong, say so plainly and argue it. Finally, tell me what kind of property the principle is ultimately about — knowledge, power, affect, or the agent's standing toward the past — because I suspect that nothing I am tracking is a feature of the historical material itself, and I do not know how to say that without dissolving the whole question into mood. If two of your candidate principles come apart anywhere, please construct the case that separates them.
