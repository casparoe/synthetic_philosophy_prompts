I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm four months into a series about escalation — why two sides who both want a deal end up in a standoff neither can exit. Last week I sat down with an economist at a policy school who does formal bargaining models, and he walked me through a version of what he calls a commitment race, then wrote the argument out for me on the back of a napkin because I kept losing the thread. My editor loves it and wants it as the spine of the piece. I've read it out loud to her line by line and she nods at every line, and that's exactly what worries me, because the last step is something I can't print and I can't find the loose board in the floor.

Here's what he wrote, cleaned up a bit:

(1) In a commitment race two sides want the same thing, and each can take steps that make backing down costlier for itself — publishing the red line, moving the ships, signing the no-strike clause, going on record that you'll pay any price, the whole steering-wheel-out-the-window move. The side that has made retreat more expensive for itself ends up with most of the surplus, because the other side folds first.

(2) Costs you've already sunk are gone either way, so whether to take the next step depends only on how things look from where you're standing.

(3) In a well-formed race, each available step is such that, from where you're standing, taking it raises your odds of winning by more than it raises your expected loss if you lose. He said this is the whole tragedy of the structure: the marginal step always pays.

(4) So a rational side takes every step that's available, at every point it's available.

(5) Both sides are rational and both know the other is, so both take every step, all the way to the last one.

(6) The last step is collision: the strike that hollows out the hospital and bankrupts the union, the price war that ends with both firms insolvent, the ships that actually fire. Both sides rank every settlement they walked past on the way there above that.

(7) And you can't repair this by advising one side to stop at step four, because at step four (2) and (3) still hold — the advice is advice to be irrational at the only moment it could be followed, and it works only if it isn't.

(8) So either rational players can be walked, one rational step at a time, to an outcome both rank below one they had, with no single point at which either could rationally have avoided it — or the settlements that obviously do happen, since most strikes settle and most of these standoffs defuse, happened because somebody wasn't playing rationally.

(9) His punchline: the model predicts that whoever is most comfortable being irrational wins, and that's why these things get won by people who've convinced themselves they'd rather crash.

I think (9) is a load of nonsense, and (8) can't be right either, because I've reported on maybe a dozen of these and in nearly all of them both sides were staffed by very shrewd people who settled, and nobody's story involved a madman. But I can't locate the step where it goes wrong — every premise sounds true in isolation, which is the problem. So: where does it break? If I'm forced to pick premises to save I'd save (2), because the sunk-cost point is one my readers already half believe and I like telling them the rest, and I'd save (3), because that's the sentence the whole piece sings on. Tell me if that instinct is exactly backwards.

And please don't hand me a menu of six possible exits with pros and cons — I have to decide whether to run this and I don't have the column inches for a survey. Give me your single best diagnosis, say why the step looked innocuous when my source wrote it down, and price it honestly: what do you have to give up elsewhere in the picture once you reject it, and does giving that up break the ordinary, unexciting cases where commitment tactics clearly do work (the union that really does strike, the government that really does follow through)? Be definite. If your view is that the argument actually goes through and I'm the one who's wrong, say that plainly and tell me what I should stop believing — I'd rather be corrected now than in the letters page.
