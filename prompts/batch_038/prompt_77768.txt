Predictive processing has produced what looks to me like a genuinely stalled dispute, and I want a diagnosis of its structure rather than a tour of the positions.

On one side are those who treat the free energy principle as a substantive empirical claim about brains — hierarchical prediction error, tested against binocular rivalry, mismatch negativity, multisensory illusions. On the other side are those who say the principle is a redescription: since anything with a Markov blanket can be written down as minimizing variational free energy, "it minimizes free energy" carries no content, and when the principle is read instrumentally it collapses into ordinary reinforcement learning anyway, given that expected free energy just decomposes into instrumental value plus information gain. A third camp attacks from the opposite flank: prediction does not entail representation (a Watt governor is a predictor), so the whole internal-model story is optional metaphysical dressing, and the debate between the first two camps misses the interesting question.

My hypothesis is that these are three different questions wearing one label — a mechanistic question, a question about the empirical content of a formal principle, and a question about what committing to a model commits you to — and that the impasse is structural rather than evidential: any candidate counterexample can be re-absorbed by re-specifying the generative model or relocating the blanket, so nothing can even be stated as falsifying.

Is that right? I would like an account of what kind of disagreement each pairing of camps actually is, whether they are contradicting one another at all or merely talking past, and what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a discriminating experiment, or a reasoned argument that none should be expected.
