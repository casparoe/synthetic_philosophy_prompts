I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of social science, and its final unit is devoted to the dispute over whether the world is becoming less violent: the long-run homicide and battle-death series, the objection that direct physical killing is only one species of violence and that structural domination does the work uncounted, the fight over whether rates per capita or absolute magnitudes measure the thing we actually care about, and the deeper methodological worry that for most of human history there is no denominator worth trusting, so that the question may not have a determinate answer at all. My students arrive sorted into two camps — the empiricists, who think the pessimists are in the grip of news-driven availability bias, and the "statistics can't see the reality of violence" camp, who think the very act of counting bodies is a moral evasion. Last week a student said that a rate is a way of not looking, and I could not tell whether that was an insight or a category error, and I have decided that the way to break the deadlock is to put a novel on the syllabus for the final session rather than another paper. The novel is Cormac McCarthy's *Blood Meridian*, which I first read as an undergraduate and have been half-afraid to teach ever since.

What I want from you is a critical reading of the novel through this debate, and I want it to be genuinely two-sided. Several moves occur to me, and I would like you to develop, refine, or reject them. First, there is the Leviathan problem: the Glanton gang is not a stateless eruption but a contractor, paid per scalp by a Mexican state government that then discovers the gang is delivering scalps from whoever happens to be nearest, which makes the sovereign not the suppressor of atrocity but its purchaser — and that seems to cut against the pacification-by-state story at its most confident point. Second, there is the question of what kind of claim Judge Holden is making when he insists that war was always there and always will be, that it is the truest form of divination, that whatever exists without his knowledge exists without his consent, and, at the end in the bar, that he will never die: is that a per capita claim, a claim about the essence of things, or an unfalsifiable metaphysics that declining rates cannot touch, and if the last, is that a strength of the novel or a cheat? Third, the book is oddly indifferent to scale — a few dozen men over a year or two, rendered as though it were the whole of history — which struck me as a dramatization of exactly the absolute-versus-relative confusion the seminar keeps circling. And fourth, my own hunch, which I want you to pressure-test rather than flatter: that the Judge is a portrait of the enumerating intellect itself, so that the novel is not simply counter-evidence to an optimistic conclusion but an indictment of the codifying posture that produces rates at all, the man who draws the world into his ledger so that it exists only there.

Two constraints. Do not quote the novel or anyone else verbatim, since neither of us can check the wording right now: paraphrase instead, and where your memory of a particular scene or speech is shaky, say so plainly and keep to a level of generality you can stand behind, rather than confabulating a detail that I would then repeat to fifteen graduate students. I also want no citations, no bibliography, and no reading list. Please organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order: "The Novel as Counterexample" (about 250 words, stating the strongest version of the case that the book refutes the decline thesis); "The Empiricist's Reply" (about 300 words, the best answer — including the point that a stylized, selected rendering of violence is not a sample, and that citing it as evidence commits the very error the optimists diagnose in the news cycle); "The Judge as Enumerator" (about 350 words, developing or dismantling my fourth hunch, and saying what it implies about whether there is a fact of the matter); "Where This Reading Overreaches" (about 250 words, honest about what the novel resists — its indifference to interiority, its aestheticization of the very thing it is supposed to indict, the possibility that I am importing a philosophy of measurement McCarthy never held); and "Three Questions for the Seminar" (about 200 words, discussion questions that force both camps to move, not questions that let my first student score an easy point). Aim for roughly thirteen hundred words in total, and end the third section with a one-sentence statement of where you yourself land.
