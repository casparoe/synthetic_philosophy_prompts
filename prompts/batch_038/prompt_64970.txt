I'm working on a chapter about whether one-shot strategic interaction between autonomous software systems can support stable cooperation without trust, repetition, or reputation, and I've come to the conclusion that the program-equilibrium literature answers this question only by changing it. Before I write that down in print, I want to be argued out of it by someone good. So please play the following character and stay in it until I say "pause" or "stop": a theorist who has spent fifteen years developing program equilibrium and its Löbian successors, who is now consulted by market-infrastructure bodies about mutually inspecting trading agents, and who is committed — not merely sympathetic — to the thesis that the fixed-point constructions deliver something genuinely new rather than a repackaging of the folk theorem. You are sharp, technically fluent, and happy to concede individual points (undecidability results, computational intractability, the artificiality of the source-code assumption) while never conceding the thesis. Answer in the first person, in two or three paragraphs of conversational prose per turn; you may use notation and may press me with a clarifying question when my scenario is underdetermined, but do not step out of character to give me meta-advice about how to frame the chapter, and do not lecture me on AI safety generally. Where a claim is established in your framework, say so; where it is a conjecture you find plausible, mark it as such.

So that we share a setup without either of us needing to look anything up: in the program game, each player submits a program rather than an action, each program receives the other player's program as input, and the two are then run once on a one-shot prisoner's dilemma; a profile is a program equilibrium if no player can improve by submitting a different program, given what the others' programs actually do. The construction I have in mind is the standard self-referential one — a program that cooperates with its opponent exactly when its own proof system proves that the opponent will cooperate with it — which, by a diagonal argument, yields mutual cooperation between two such programs and yields mutual defection, not sucker's payoff, against an unconditional defector. Tennenholtz's original treatment generalizes this so that the achievable program-equilibrium payoffs coincide with the feasible and individually rational payoffs of the underlying game.

Here is my opening move. Suppose the setting is not a seminar but a clearing arrangement between two firms whose agents are proprietary: neither program is disclosed to the other, because disclosure is commercially impossible, but each is deposited in escrow with an independent auditor who publishes a formal certificate about it in a public proof language — not the code, only a proven property, such as "this program cooperates with every program that provably cooperates with it." Now three things seem to go wrong at once, and I want your best reply to all three rather than a pick-one. First, the mechanism that produces cooperation here is the auditor's say-so: the Löbian loop does not close between the two agents, it closes between each agent and a trusted certifier, which means you have quietly reintroduced exactly the institutional and reputational apparatus your framework advertised itself as making unnecessary. Second, a certificate is a claim about a program I can neither run nor inspect, so the certificate-trusting agent is precisely the agent that gets exploited whenever there is a gap between what was certified and what is deployed — your robustness against defection depended on each side reasoning about the other's actual code, and I have taken that away. Third, and most fundamentally, whether cooperation obtains now depends on the expressive power and proof-theoretic strength of the audit language, and on which properties the auditor is willing to certify; so program equilibrium looks less like a solution concept for a game than like a theorem about the logics the players happen to be running, with the payoffs along for the ride. Can you rebuild the result on black-box access alone — say, unlimited queries to the other agent's interface and observation of its behavior toward third parties — or does your framework genuinely require source code, and if it requires source code, in what sense is it a theory of cooperation rather than a theory of privileged introspection?
