I am in the final year of a philosophy degree and currently enrolled in an advanced seminar on distributive justice. Last week our instructor posed a case to the room: a school board, working within a fixed budget, cancels a selective gifted-and-talented program rather than extend comparable resources to the lowest-performing schools. The stipulations were that the disadvantaged pupils end up exactly as well off as before, the advantaged pupils end up worse off, and there are no third-party effects. The decision thus equalizes the distribution of educational prospects without improving anyone's position. The seminar question was whether the resulting state of affairs is better in any respect than the one it replaced, and whether the answer differs for an egalitarian and for a prioritarian.

Two members of the seminar, Noor and Callum, kept arguing after class and then over coffee. I took notes because I could not settle the matter myself and said nothing throughout, so I have no stake to defend. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Noor said the case shows why telic egalitarianism fails. If equality is valuable in itself, then the board's outcome is not merely equal but in one respect better than the status quo ante, since the distribution is now equal where it was unequal; yet no one is better off and some are worse off. On her view, a respect in which a change counts as an improvement, when the change consists solely in losses, is not a genuine value at all — so the leveling-down objection is not rhetorical flourishes but a demonstration that equality cannot be non-instrumentally good.

(2) Callum said this presupposes that a respect counts as a respect for improvement only if it is traceable to someone's being better off, which is a person-affecting constraint the prioritarian officially rejects. Prioritarianism is impersonal too: it holds that the moral weight of a benefit depends on the absolute level at which it falls, which is a fact about the shape of the outcome rather than about any individual's situation. If impersonal value is already on the table, he said, the egalitarian's claim is unfamiliar rather than defective, and Noor's objection simply assumes the welfarist premise it is meant to establish.

(3) Noor replied that the disanalogy is structural. Under prioritarianism the value of a distribution is the sum of individually weighted well-being levels, so whenever one outcome is better than another the entire difference is accounted for by gains and losses to particular people, with nothing left over for the pattern as such. Equality, on the telic view, is supposed to contribute value that persists after every individual change has been registered as a loss. It is that residue, not impersonality as such, that the objection targets.

(4) Callum said her principle now proves far too much. We readily say that a world containing an intact cathedral and a living reef is in one respect better than a world in which both have gone, even if nobody remains to benefit; we say the same of species diversity compared with monoculture. If a respect is disqualified whenever it fails to be traceable to someone's being better off, then every non-welfarist value is disqualified along with it. The objection is therefore not an argument against egalitarianism but an argument for welfarism, which Noor owes separately.

(5) Noor answered that those respects differ in kind from equality, not merely in degree. A flourishing reef or a preserved cathedral is a respect that could in principle be improved by additions; nothing about it is constituted by anyone's loss. The egalitarian's respect, in the leveling-down case, is realized in the reduction of the well-off. A value whose only mode of realization is harm cannot generate reasons, and that is what it means to call it no value at all.

(6) Callum said the distinction collapses under pressure. Whether a respect can be served only at someone's expense is contingent on the options available, not on the respect itself: preserving the reef may require flooding a village, and reducing inequality is routinely achieved by policies that leave the worst off better off and the affluent only marginally worse. If being realizable only through harm is the disqualifier, then the same respect is disqualified in some choice situations and vindicated in others, which is no verdict on its status as a value. On his reading, Noor's principle either eliminates all non-welfarist value, as in move (4), or else merely restates the truism that leveling down is not worth doing.

(7) Noor conceded that (6) had some force but said the objection was never about what anyone ought to do. It is diagnostic: it reveals what egalitarians are committed to when they claim that inequality is bad in itself, and in the limit case they will want to disown that commitment. Callum replied that this makes the objection unfalsifiable, since any egalitarian who disowns the limit case has thereby ceased to be a telic egalitarian, so the objection can refute only positions nobody holds.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the seven numbered steps, assign a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating in a few sentences. Then answer three questions separately: whether Noor is correct in (3) that prioritarian value is fully traceable to individual-level gains and losses, so that no analogue of the leveling-down problem arises for it; whether Callum's generalization in (4) really shows the objection proves too much, or whether it trades on cases that are relevantly unlike the one at issue; and whether the distinction Noor draws in (5), between a respect that is constituted by loss and one that is merely contingently expensive, survives (6). Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify at least one substantive consideration that neither of them raised but which bears on the dispute — including whether the school-board case as stipulated is a fair test of either position or is quietly weighted in favor of one of them. I am not asking for references or a survey of the literature; I want your own assessment of the arguments exactly as these two made them.
