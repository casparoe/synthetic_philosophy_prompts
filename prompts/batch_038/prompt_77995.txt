I chair a technical panel at a regional water management district, and twice a year we publish a single number: the design flood elevation that county engineers, private insurers, and the state board rely on when they issue or deny permits in the floodplain. The panel is four people — a senior hydrologist, a climate modeler, an applied statistician, and a structural engineer — plus me as chair. Our standing problem is that every cycle we exchange the same datasets, run the same two-day workshop, and end up with a spread of several feet that we then paper over by taking a midpoint, which everyone privately regards as a fudge. I have been reading about Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that two rational agents who share a common prior and who commonly know each other's posterior beliefs cannot agree to disagree — and I want to know whether it gives me anything operational, or whether it is the kind of theorem that tells you something about an idealization and nothing about a room full of professionals. Please build me a diagnostic procedure the panel can actually run on a live disagreement between two named members: a sequence of questions to ask in order, a specification of what would count as an adequate answer to each question rather than a deflection, the point at which the procedure licenses me to declare the disagreement resolved in principle so that whatever remains is bookkeeping rather than epistemics, the point at which it tells me to stop pushing and record a formal dissent in the register that goes to the board, and the point at which it hands the matter back to the board because what is left is a question about our mandate rather than about belief. The steps have to be runnable by four people with no probability theory beyond high school, inside a ninety-minute session, with no power to compel disclosure beyond what our charter grants us — but I do not want the theorem's content lost in the translation. In particular the procedure must respect that the theorem concerns common knowledge of each other's beliefs, which is a much stronger condition than everyone having heard everyone else's number, and that it concerns agreement on the expectation of a quantity rather than agreement on all the reasoning behind it. Tell me explicitly which of these idealizations the procedure manages to preserve, which it has to weaken, and what the weakening costs me in the conclusions I am allowed to draw.

Our panel informally sorts stuck disagreements into three buckets we invented at a retreat, and I want you to work with that taxonomy rather than around it. We call them data gaps (somebody has not seen something the rest of us have), model gaps (we have all seen the same data and fitted different structures to it), and mandate gaps (we are in fact optimizing different things — protecting the tax base versus protecting lives versus protecting the district from liability). Map these three onto the premises of the theorem: tell me which bucket corresponds to a genuine failure of a premise, which is a disguised restatement of another bucket, and which is not an epistemic failure at all but a category error on our part. If you think the taxonomy is wrong, replace it with one that tracks the premises and that a lay member could apply to a colleague in real time, and say which distinctions you kept and which you dropped as unusable. I also want you to be straight with me about the standard objections — that the common prior assumption is doing all the work and that no two real people actually share a prior, and that common knowledge is never achieved in practice because information gets exchanged in stages and by email — and to tell me, objection by objection, whether it disables the procedure for my panel or merely relocates the difficulty to a step I can still run. I do not want references, a reading list, or attribution of results to particular papers; assume I cannot look anything up and that a citation would be useless to me. What I want is the machinery.

Finally, run the procedure on three cases from recent cycles. First: the hydrologist and the climate modeler have exchanged every dataset either of them used, each can accurately describe the other's method back to the other's satisfaction, and they still differ by three feet on peak-flow estimate, with each saying the other's downscaling is unjustified. Second: the statistician and the structural engineer have never opened each other's working files and neither wants to; the engineer says the uncertainty bands are absurdly wide because they ignore what actually gets built, and the statistician says the engineer has quietly substituted a preference for constructible outcomes for an estimate of what will happen. Third: me versus the hydrologist. For two years I have argued for shifting the published number toward the high end, largely because the district has absorbed three rounds of expensive litigation, and I have noticed that I restate my position each cycle without taking hers apart. The procedure should be capable of concluding that the malfunctioning agent in that case is the chair, and I want it to say so plainly instead of diplomatically, because the whole point of adopting it is to be bound by it. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: the situations in which running it produces false confidence, the ways it can be gamed by a member who has learned the steps and simply wants to keep a private number without looking obstructionist, and the cases where it will tell me to keep pushing toward convergence when the honest recommendation is that we publish two numbers and let the board choose between them.
