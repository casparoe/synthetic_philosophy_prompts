I have painted shop signs by hand for twenty-two years — grocery fronts, pub boards, gold-leaf inscription on a funeral director's window — and for eight of those years I have also taught a Saturday lettering class at the community art centre in my town. I left school at sixteen and everything I know about letters was put into my hands by an old signwriter, so I have no academic training and no vocabulary for the thing that has come to occupy most of my thinking. The thing is this. In the shop I can look at a trainee's layout sheet and know within a second that the 'a' is wrong, and I cannot say what is wrong with it in any form that helps him fix it. Meanwhile the same letter can be built as the two-storey printed shape you meet in a book, as a single bowl with a tail, as a leaning shape with a flat top, and every one of them is an 'a' past argument. So something is shared, but the shared thing is not a shape, and it does not seem to be a rule either, because every genuinely good new design I have ever made broke whatever rule I could have written down beforehand. Two winters ago I found a library book about researchers who tried to get a computer to invent new letterforms that would still belong to the style of an existing alphabet, and I sat up most of the night with it, because it was asking my question in a language I did not have. My own hunch, arrived at slowly and with no help, is that what is shared across all the 'a's is not an outline but a job: a mark is an 'a' when it is aimed at the 'a' slot in an alphabet and taken by a reader to fill it. I know perfectly well that this hunch is in trouble, and I know roughly where — I want the assignment to attack it rather than flatter it.

The centre has started issuing certificates of completion, and the coordinator has asked every tutor to set one written take-home and to hand in a model answer and a marking scheme alongside it, because the marking is done by a retired schoolteacher who volunteers on Tuesday evenings and who knows nothing whatever about lettering. That is the real reason I am asking for what I am asking for. My students are hobbyists — a nurse, two retirees, a printer's apprentice, a woman who letterheads cards for a living — and between them they have read nothing philosophical and will read nothing before the assignment is due. So the question has to carry everything it needs inside itself, it has to be answerable in ninety minutes by an intelligent person with no background, and the marking scheme has to be usable by a marker who cannot tell a good argument about letters from a confident-sounding one. It should be worth twenty-five marks, the student's answer should be capped at eight hundred words, and there should be nothing to look up.

Here is what I would like you to write. First, one essay question, built around five exhibits which I have drawn or collected myself and which you should describe in words inside the question rather than assume any picture accompanies it: (1) a row of six 'a's I drew in a line, beginning with the two-storey printed form and ending with the single-bowl handwritten form, each stage differing from its neighbour only slightly, so that at the fourth stage I honestly cannot tell you whether it is a two-storey 'a' that has lost its shoulder or a one-storey 'a' carrying a scar; (2) a photograph a friend brought back of a hand-painted shop sign from a town whose local lettering style is old-fashioned, in which the mark doing the work of 'a' is to my eye indistinguishable from our 'o', while their 'o' carries a small spur — the locals read the sign fluently and correctly, though none of them were ever taught a rule, and a tourist standing beside them reads 'o' where they read 'a'; (3) a bakery sign I painted in which a single closed round shape does double duty, serving as the 'o' at the end of the first word and the 'a' at the beginning of the second, the two words running together so that the identical mark belongs to both; (4) a card by a first-year trainee who drew the mark as an 'a', can point to the standard he was copying, and means it sincerely, but which everyone in the shop — me included — reads at a glance as a 'd', with no amount of explanation changing the first reading; and (5) a set of letterforms produced by a program that generates shapes nobody has used before, one of which, placed consistently in the 'a' position throughout a complete alphabet of its own making, resembles no 'a' I have ever seen. The question should require the student to put forward a single account of what makes a letterform the letter it is, run that account against all five exhibits, and state plainly what they would have to surrender wherever it fails.

Second, a model answer of roughly six hundred words. It may coin or borrow a term or two if it explains them as it goes, but it must be the sort of answer one of my students could in principle have written in ninety minutes, not a lecture. Do not build it around my job-and-slot hunch unless that hunch actually survives the exhibits; if it does not survive, I would rather the model answer said so and showed what has to be added or given up.

Third, the marking scheme, with the twenty-five marks distributed across named moves rather than across virtues like clarity or effort. For each band — excellent, good, adequate, poor — say what has to be present for a marker who cannot judge the subject matter herself to recognise it: which exhibits must be handled and how, what a unified account must do to count as unified, and what the student must concede where the account breaks. Then list the specific errors that should cost marks, described concretely enough to be spotted by a non-expert — for instance, resting everything on resemblance to a standard without ever saying whose standard it is or how it gets set; declaring that a letter is simply whatever the reader takes it to be and then having nothing left to say about the trainee's card or the tourist at the sign; announcing that there is no fact of the matter and treating that as a conclusion rather than a surrender; drawing a distinction between the letter and its shapes and then never using the distinction on any exhibit; and reaching for a famous example about resemblances running through a family, or about a game and what makes it a game, without ever bringing it down onto the six-stage row. Finally, tell me which of the five exhibits your average student will fumble, and what a fumble of that exhibit looks like on the page, so that the marker knows the difference between a student who skipped it and one who tried and failed.
