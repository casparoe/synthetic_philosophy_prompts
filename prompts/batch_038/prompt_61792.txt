I am a final-year high school student writing a long essay on perception for a school philosophy prize, and I need a thought experiment built to a specification rather than an explanation of the debate. I will set out the two positions in my own words so that we are working from the same definitions, and then give the constraints.

On the common-factor view, when someone sees a yellow lemon on the table and when someone else hallucinates a yellow lemon on the table, there is a mental state they both share: something it is like that is present in each case, and which explains why the two are indistinguishable from the inside. The disjunctivist denies this. On that view the good case is fundamentally different in kind — the experience is constituted by the object and the scene itself — so the two cases have no basic mental state in common, and the only thing the bad case shares with the good one is that you cannot tell them apart.

The version I want to test is the strongest form of the claim about the bad case: that a hallucination has no positive phenomenal character of its own whatsoever. What it is like to hallucinate a lemon is nothing over and above its being the kind of state that cannot be discriminated, by reflection alone, from actually seeing one. Its character is entirely borrowed from the good case, and borrowed through the impossibility of telling the two apart. I suspect this claim is in trouble if the impossibility can be removed while everything else stays fixed, and I would like a case that removes it.

Please construct a scenario meeting all of the following:

1. The subject is undergoing a visual hallucination of something mundane — a boring, ordinary feature of a place they know well — and it is as vivid, detailed, and present-feeling as the corresponding perception would be.
2. The subject nonetheless knows, and is right, that they are not perceiving it, and this knowledge does not come from introspecting the experience or from any perceptual check. It comes from something like their own action moments earlier, a reliable warning given in advance, or a certainty about how many objects a container holds. Choose whichever source makes the case sharpest and say why.
3. Realistic setup only: no simulated worlds, no vats, no electrodes or neurosurgeons, and no clinical condition, drug, or psychosis that would give the subject independent reason to distrust their eyes in general.
4. None of the standard illustrations. No dagger, no pink elephant, no hallucinated lemon or snake, no duplicated room or perfect replica. If your case could be swapped into a textbook as one of the usual examples, replace it.
5. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

Then do the demonstration:

- State plainly what the common-factor theorist says about the phenomenal character of this subject's experience, and what the disjunctivist says, given the collateral knowledge in the case.
- Answer the central question directly: once the subject can tell that they are hallucinating, does the borrowed-character view have anything left to point to? Show whether the character has to go missing, whether it can be re-located in something else — for instance a standing inability to discriminate that obtains whether or not the subject knows about it — or whether the view has to concede that hallucinations do have positive character of their own after all.
