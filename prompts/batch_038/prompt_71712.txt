Three months ago I took over responsibility for correspondence, parliamentary questions and press enquiries at a public body whose conduct has lately been under sustained scrutiny. I am writing because I have come to doubt whether I still know what dishonesty is, and because I have been asked to draft the internal guidance on it.

Let me be candid about what the work involves. Almost nothing I sign is false. Asked by a reporter whether we had been warned of a particular operational risk, I explained that no finding had been made against us by any regulator; that was true, I volunteered it, and it ran the next morning as a flat denial of the warning. Asked by a parent whether her child's case had been considered, I wrote that her correspondence had been received and logged. Several of our statements have been produced by subtraction: a draft circulates among four pairs of hands, each of whom strikes a clause they would rather not defend, and what survives is a true sentence that conveys nothing, composed by nobody in particular, believed false by no one at any stage. Three weeks after one such exchange I realised the reporter had drawn an inference I knew to be wrong; he rang again, and I said nothing. I cannot tell you whether that silence was worse than the original answer or merely the same thing in a different hat.

I should like an essay on the question I can no longer avoid: whether there is a moral difference between lying and misleading, and, if there is, whether it is a difference in kind or only in degree. Set out the strongest case you can for the view that the distinction is morally load-bearing, and not the caricature its opponents give; I have in mind something about what an assertion commits one to as against what one's choice of topic commits one to, about the share of the interpretive work that is left to the hearer, and about whether a standing practice of trust could survive routine literal falsity even though it plainly tolerates the withholding of information nobody asked for. Set out with equal seriousness the case that the distinction collapses: that the intention to induce a false belief and the resulting epistemic harm are the same in both, and that the distinction hands the conscientious official a licence for which the ordinary liar has to pay in shame.

Then I should like you to test a criterion of my own devising, which I have been using to reassure myself. Call it the diligent-enquirer test: an answer misleads only if an enquirer who was well informed, appropriately suspicious and free to ask every natural follow-up question would still have been left holding a false belief. If the follow-up questions would have got the truth out of me, then on this account I was unhelpful rather than deceptive. Tell me plainly whether that test can be made to work; try to break it with cases of your own, including cases in which the follow-up questions are available in principle but socially or institutionally out of reach; and if it fails, say what ought to be put in its place.

Two further questions bear directly on my own position, and I would rather they were not buried in a general discussion. First, can an institution lie when no individual within it asserts anything they believe to be false, and if it can, what becomes of the culpability of the person who drafted the sentence, which is what I actually care about? Second, how should we treat the old casuistry of mental reservation, including the wide variety in which the unstated qualification is supposed to be recoverable from the words themselves or from the circumstances of the utterance? I recognise that defence in my own drafting habits, and I want to know whether it survives scrutiny or is merely a respectable-sounding description of what deceivers have always done.

Say also whether the verdict changes with the setting: whether the hearer had a right to the information in the first place, whether I am bound by a duty of confidentiality that forbids the complete answer, and whether the exchange is with a journalist who knows how the game is played or with a member of the public who does not.

End with a verdict and defend it. A qualified verdict is welcome, provided it is a verdict and not a shrug; something along the lines of holding that the distinction tracks the wrong-making feature but not its weight, or that it matters between strangers but not within relationships of trust, would satisfy me. What I do not want is a survey that ends by observing how complex the matter is.

Please write in British English. I should like continuous prose throughout, in the manner of a letter rather than a report: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicised emphasis, no tables, and no numbered sections, however you may feel the urge to impose structure on the material. Do not give me a bibliography and do not name authorities; if you draw on a distinction from the literature, explain it in your own words. Something in the region of eighteen hundred words would be right, and I would rather you ran long than left an argument half-made.
