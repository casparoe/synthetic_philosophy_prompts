My brother's getting married in June and I'm the best man. I need the toast — four or five minutes, call it six hundred words — for about 140 people: her whole extended family (her grandmother is 88 and will not follow a single abstraction), our college friends, several of his colleagues from the lab, and one guest who is the real constraint. Our cousin Ruth does philosophy of mind and works on the timing of conscious experience. She should catch exactly one thing in there. Nobody else should notice there's anything to catch.

Here's the material. For nine years the two of them have told the story of how it started, and they have never once told the same one.

Hers: the ferry over to the island, late August, rain coming sideways, and he turned to her at the rail and said something about the engine noise, and that was the moment.

His: three months earlier, at a departmental coffee hour, where she told him in front of four people that his analysis was wrong, and he thought oh, it's her.

I was on that ferry. I was standing behind them. Neither of them said a word the whole crossing — the wind was to loud, they stood at the rail with their hands in their pockets and looked at the water. For nine years I've been on the verge of telling them this at every Thanksgiving. I've decided not to, and I want the toast to be where I say why.

Hard constraints. No roasting, he hates being the subject of a speech. And absolutely not the cheap version — you two remember it differently, who's lying, ha ha — and not the sentimental version either, the truth is whatever you two decide it is. Both of those insult them and both are false.

Here's what I want it actually built on. There's a distinction I've been chewing on, from Dennett (and a paper he wrote with Kinsbourne about when in the brain an experience happens), between two ways your report of your own past experience can come apart from what was there. Orwellian: you had it, it went in, and then the record gets edited afterwards — the 1984 version, where the newspapers are quietly reprinted. Stalinesque: the verdict is settled before the trial, i.e. the content is assembled only later, once more evidence has arrived, so it was never in the record to be edited; what you end up with isn't a corrupted memory of a moment but a first draft of one, composed afterwards and dated earlier than it was written. And there's an old illusion that makes the second one look unavoidable: two lights, different colours, different places, flashed in succession, and people reliably report a single spot travelling between them and changing colour partway across. The change gets reported at a point in the trajectory that falls before the second light has even come on. Which means the trajectory-with-the-change-in-it was only available on the far side. You cannot watch the first light and know what colour it's going to turn into. And the nastiest part of the whole business is that from the inside you cannot tell which of the two you've just had — a misbegotten experience and an immediately misremembered one feel exactly the same, and thats why it's a distinction rather than a difference anyone could detect.

So the reading of the ferry I want the toast to make, without ever saying any of it out loud: neither of them is remembering a moment and getting the words wrong. The crossing was silent. The line of dialogue was composed afterwards, out of the fact of the crossing, and dated to the crossing, because that's the only shape in which something-began-here can be experienced at all. They composed different lines. That isn't a disagreement about evidence, and it isn't a lie, and it isn't charming confusion. It's what it looks like from the inside when an interval turns out to have mattered.

What I need from you:

- don't adjudicate between the two versions, don't name a winner, don't even hint at which one I think is closer.
- none of the vocabulary. No Orwellian, no Stalinesque, no back-filling, no reconstructive memory, no the brain, no consciousness, no phi. And Ruth doesn't get a wink in the text.
- the grandmother has to be able to follow the entire thing. Every abstract move gets carried by a concrete object: the rail, the wind, the pockets, the water, the coffee cup, the four people at the table.
- I do say out loud what I actually saw on that ferry. If I hide it entirely the toast turns evasive and everybody can feel it. But I say it as a thing I'm giving them, not as a correction I'm declining to make — and the toast has to make clear why handing over my version would be a bad trade rather than a generous one.
- it ends as an actual toast, glass up, not as a peroration.
- one sentence in there, exactly one, that Ruth will recognise and that everyone else will hear as sentiment. After the toast, tell me in a single line which sentence that was. Nothing else after that — no notes, no rationale, no explaining the philosophy back to me. I've got it.
