You are a philosopher who works on the ontology of digital and virtual objects, writing for an audience that already knows the standard debates. Think the case through carefully before drafting, and submit only the finished essay.

The case. In a persistent multiplayer world, a player's virtual house — assembled by hand over four years, with a log recording the events that took place inside it — is destroyed by a server fault. The operator restores it from backup: the house is re-instantiated pixel-for-pixel, its inventory and its event log intact, and the world's database lists it under the same identifier as the same item. Functionally nothing is missing. Still, the player grieves, and other players quietly treat the restored house as a replica, which depresses its in-world market price.

Your task is to decide, in essay form, whether the player was genuinely harmed by the destruction given that everything was restored. Reach a definite verdict and defend it. Weigh the two strongest positions against one another: the view that virtual objects are real digital objects, constituted by data and running processes, so that faithful restoration leaves the object intact and the sense of loss merely psychological; and the view that something was genuinely destroyed, so that restoration cannot annul the harm. Then say which position better accounts for the residual evidence — the grief, the market discount — and whether that evidence tracks a metaphysical fact or an illusion.

Two constraints on how you argue. First, your verdict must turn on an explicit claim about what restoration preserves: does the restored house have the same history as the original, or a fresh history accompanied by an accurate record of the old one? State which you accept and why. Second, do not settle the question by appeal to the game's rules or terms of service; say what is true independently of them.

Length: at most 700 words. Open with your verdict in one or two sentences, then give the argument. No citations, no footnotes, no reading list. Do not end on a hedge — close with where you actually stand.
