I'm two years into an archaelogy PhD and the lit review I'm supposed to be writing has quietly turned into an argument with my own position, so I want someone to attack it properly instead of agreeing with me. Where I've landed: collapse isn't a real category of social change, it's a normative projection. What gets called the collapse of a society is almost always two things braided together -- the disassembly of one particular scale of political integration, and the loss of a self-documenting elite whose records and monuments are most of the evidence we have. The people keep going, the language keeps going, the farming keeps going; what stops is the palace, the divine king, the inscription. So my working claim is that societies don't fail, they reorganize, and that describing reorganization as failure imports a state-centric bias, and often an openly racial one -- the whole genre of stories about islanders who ruined their environment and died out, which for Rapa Nui has the convenient side effect of explaining away a population whose actual crash dates to introduced disease and the Peruvian slave raids of the 1860s, not to any pre-contact self-destruction.

To keep this from being pure attitude I've given myself a test. Before I'll say a society collapsed, I want three things independently documented: (i) demographic loss visible in settlement survey, rather than inferred from the fact that the monuments stop; (ii) loss of the *capacity* to organize labor at the earlier scale, not merely a stretch of time in which nobody happens to have done it -- capability, not exercise; and (iii) loss of some piece of knowledge that people then alive demonstrably could not recover, a script, a hydraulic or metallurgical technique. My claim is that no canonical case clears all three, and that (ii) and (iii) get systematically inflated because absence of evidence for continuity is read as evidence of absence.

So: what would count against this? I want a map of its exposure, ranked -- what would be fatal, what merely awkward, what I should just shrug off. Be concrete about what the record would have to look like: what kind of sequence, from where, dated how, would force me to say the Hittite state, the Mycenaean palaces, the southern Maya lowlands, or Rapa Nui really collapsed rather than reorganized. Point out where the legs of my test are doing suspicious work -- Linear B vanishing from the Aegean for several centuries looks to me like a genuine hit on (iii), except the alphabet that eventually replaces it comes from somewhere else entirely, and I cant tell whether that rescues my clause or destroys it. Tell me what my view predicts that Tainter's diminishing-returns account (The Collapse of Complex Societies, 1988) and the Diamond-style ecocide account (Collapse, 2005) don't predict, and whether the three of us actually disagree about anything observable. And give it to me straight on unfalsifiability: my worry is that recasting every case as transformation rather than collapse can absorb any finding whatsoever, which would make my position the very thing I accuse the other side of being. If it is that insulated, is that disqualifying for a claim about the past, or is that just what such a claim looks like when it's about description rather than mechanism? I'd also like to know which parts of it a sympathetic defender would themselves concede count against it, not just what critics say. Two extra things -- give me one test case I haven't listed that I should run my three-part test on, with your prediction of how it'll come out, and if you can only pick one consideration that should actually move me, make it empirical. I'm basically immune to being told I'm committing a category mistake.
