I teach a cross-listed senior seminar — half psychology majors, half philosophy majors — on the foundations of statistical inference, and the back half of the term is devoted to the replication crisis: p-hacking, optional stopping, preregistration, and the diagnosis of literatures whose published results look too consistent to be credible. For the opening session I want to do something less technical. We are going to work through the first scene of Tom Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead: two men in a place with no features flip a coin over and over, Rosencrantz calls heads before each flip, and the coin keeps coming up heads — long enough that Rosencrantz's purse fills while Guildenstern stops being able to treat the thing as a game. Guildenstern is bothered by the implications rather than by the money, and he works through a list of candidate explanations: something like his own willing the outcome as atonement for an offence he cannot remember; time having stopped dead, so that the run is a single toss endlessly repeated; divine intervention; and, last and most deflationary, that it is really just chance. Rosencrantz is largely untroubled throughout. I want the reading I will hand out and defend in class: this scene as a dramatization of the frequentist–Bayesian disagreement, and of the epistemic situation that eventually produced the replication crisis.

Take the two characters' attitudes as positions rather than as personalities, and tell me whether the casting holds up. My own tentative reading, which you should sharpen or reject: Rosencrantz is the frequentist, unmoved because one run says nothing about a long-run frequency, and because under the fair-coin hypothesis every particular sequence of that length is exactly as improbable as the one that actually happened; Guildenstern is the Bayesian, and his list of rival hypotheses is precisely the move that gives the data evidential force — the run is not surprising in itself but surprising under one hypothesis and unsurprising under another, so what carries information is the ratio. If that is right, the scene stages the likelihood principle before anyone in it has the vocabulary for it, and it quietly favors one side: notice that neither man ever asks how the data were generated, whether the flipping was supposed to stop at some point, or how many sequences of that length they would have had to sift through to find one like this. The stopping rule and the analysis plan are simply absent from their reasoning, and their absence is much of what makes the exchange feel insane. I would also like you to press hard on the second hypothesis. That time has stopped and the run is one toss repeated is not a probability hypothesis at all — it is the claim that the observations are not independent, that there is a single datum presented over and over. Say what a statistician would call that diagnosis, and whether it is the right thing to suspect when a literature returns the same significant result again and again. Then: does the divine-intervention hypothesis function as an ad hoc rescue of the null in the Duhemian sense, an auxiliary assumption deployed to save a theory under anomaly, and is it distinguishable even in principle from the mundane hypothesis that the coin is loaded — a hypothesis that, so far as I can reconstruct the scene, neither of them bothers to entertain? Check me on that last point and tell me if I am wrong rather than accommodating me. Finally, the deeper thing I want the seminar to leave with: Guildenstern's list, however inventive, does not contain the truth. The truth is that they are characters in a play, that the coin comes up heads because it has been written to come up heads, and that no finite volume of data can move a reasoner toward a hypothesis that was never in the hypothesis space. Connect that to the standing objection that Bayesian confirmation is hostage to the exhaustiveness of the hypothesis set and to the awkward, vague prior that has to be parked on the catch-all, and then ask what the play's determinism does to the whole statistical project. If the outcome is fixed by authorship there is no chance process to have frequencies about and no coherent credence to hold; is Stoppard showing that both the frequentist and the Bayesian are talking nonsense, or only that the frequentist is?

Be faithful in both directions. I do not want the play flattened into a cartoon of a statistics textbook, and I do not want the statistics stretched until it loses its content; where the analogy is strained, say so and say why. Stoppard's concerns here are existential and theatrical — two minor characters who cannot remember being summoned, in a place with no features, inside a story that has already been written and will end with them dead — and you should tell me how much of the coin-flipping is really about probability and how much is about agency and about being a bit part in someone else's drama. Be equally explicit about the limits of what you can claim without the text in front of you: I do not want line-by-line quotation and I would rather you not attempt it. Work from the scene's structure and dramatic logic, and where the exact wording would matter to your argument, flag that it would matter instead of guessing at it. End by telling me what the play contributes to the debate over statistical foundations that an argumentative paper on the likelihood principle or on preregistration cannot — something about the phenomenology of watching a model you trust emit an impossible output, perhaps, or about what it feels like from inside a research program that keeps confirming itself — and close with two or three questions you would put to the seminar afterward, including one you expect the psychology majors and the philosophy majors to answer in mutually unintelligible ways.
