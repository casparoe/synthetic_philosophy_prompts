Six months ago my son underwent a corpus callosotomy for drug-resistant epilepsy. At the pre-operative consultation the surgeon told us, without hedging, that we should expect to be living with two minds in one body. I am writing a paper for a bioethics seminar, and I want that claim tested rather than either swallowed on authority or waved away.

Write an essay that reaches a verdict on how many subjects of conscious experience a complete callosotomy patient is. Set the behavioural evidence for disunity — independent visual-field responding, interhemispheric conflict in bimanual tasks, alien-hand phenomena — against the evidence for unity: preserved coordination in ordinary life, the subcortical and brainstem pathways the operation leaves intact, and the patients' own insistence that they are one person. Then confront the worry that disposes of that last item too easily, namely that the verbally fluent hemisphere may confabulate a narrative unity it does not actually enjoy, so that reports of oneness carry less evidential weight than they appear to.

Two further questions must be answered rather than deferred. First: suppose a patient in whom crude affective valence still passes between hemispheres while semantic content does not — one hemisphere's fear arriving in the other as unattributed dread. Is that a single subject with an inaccessible region, or two subjects sharing a mood? Second: the operation is frequently staged, the anterior portion sectioned first and the remainder, if at all, later. Can the number of subjects in one body fluctuate over time, and what would settle it? Commit to a criterion that answers both, say what evidence would overturn your verdict, and do not close in equipoise.
