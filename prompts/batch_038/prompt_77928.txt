I'm not an academic and I have no formal training in political philosophy. I work in insurance, pricing risk for a living, and for several years now I've been reading on my own about the proposal usually called futarchy: the idea that a polity should still vote on what it wants to maximize, but should settle the question of what any given policy would actually do by letting people bet on it, and then do whichever thing the market says will do more of the good. The longer I sit with it, the more the whole scheme looks to me like a racetrack that has swallowed a parliament — and I think that's the honest way to see it, not a cheap one. I'd like you to write that as a piece of satire.

Please write the official race program — the printed card handed out at the gate — for a full day of racing at the national legislature of a small country that has governed itself this way for about forty years. Roughly 800 words, deadpan throughout, laid out the way such programs are laid out: a cover notice from the racing authority, then numbered races giving distance, purse, track conditions, and both morning-line and post-time odds for each runner, followed by the usual back-matter sections. Invent the country and its institutions. No real nations, no real companies, no real living people, and no authorial asides.

The humor has to come from the mechanics being correct, so please include all of the following:

1. A cover notice naming the national welfare index — the single thing everyone is betting the policies will increase. It was ratified once, by popular vote, at the founding, and has never been amended. The index is defined by a formula administered by the Handicapper's Office, and patrons are reminded that the index means what the Office says it means. One line about the elderly patron who still writes in each season asking to see the formula.

2. A notice to patrons explaining the two tiers: each patron's ballot in the annual values race counts once, and each patron's stake in the policy races counts in proportion to their bankroll. Present the second as a proud feature, in the authority's own admiring language of weighted conviction.

3. At least one race run as a conditional race, where the quoted price is not on how things will go but on how much more or less of the index there will be if the bill passes than if it fails. Show this the way a card shows weights and eligibility conditions, so the difference is legible to a racing fan without being explained to a student of the subject.

4. One race with track conditions described as liquidity: a bill whose market is so shallow that the closing price was set by a single late wager from a man who runs a dental practice. Give the morning line and the post-time price a wide disagreement, and give the authority's official account of why that is nothing to worry about.

5. A Stewards' Inquiry into a trader accused of buying up the price of a bill in order to move the bill. Let the defense be that this is precisely how the track learns anything. Let the stewards rule, and let the ruling be internally consistent with the country's stated philosophy rather than a convenient reversal.

6. A Scratchings list of bills whose markets never opened, with one dry line of reason for each.

7. A Photo Finish: a bill that passed or failed by a hair depending on whether a particular thing counted toward the index, resolved after the fact by the Handicapper's Office. Include the standing rule that no market may be voided once resolved, and reference one older case in which the resolution criterion was badly drafted and had to stand anyway.

8. A Payouts section addressing the patron who backed the losing side of a bill that turned out to be excellent policy: the nation won, the patron was wrong, and the difference went to the holders who were right. This should be sincerely consoling in tone and should not concede anything.

9. A tipster's sidebar selling legislative forecasts, including what the tipster says when asked why anyone should pay him for information the track already has in the price.

10. Realistic odds throughout: fractional prices, a visible overround in the book, and prices that move between morning line and post.

Two further constraints. First, never state an objection in your own voice and never let a character say anything as blunt as "isn't it strange that" — the reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without being winked at. Someone who has never heard of futarchy should understand by the second race that this is a parliament settling law by betting; someone who has thought hard about prediction markets should find every standard worry — what exactly is being maximized and who measures it, manipulation and the way forecasts change what they forecast, thin markets, what the losers receive, whether betting weighted by wealth is voting — turned up as ordinary racetrack paperwork. Second, be fair to the country. It should look competent and mostly functional, not a cartoon: a couple of races should be settled quickly, cheaply, and better than the assembly that came before would have managed, and the program should be visibly proud of them.
