I'm finishing a seminar paper on the moral status of simulated agents and I've assembled an argument I'm now fairly confident is unsound, but I cannot locate the failure. The setup is a particular game rather than a thought experiment, and I think that matters, because the mechanics end up doing most of the work. My friends and I have been playing Wrensmoor, a procedurally generated settlement roguelike. Each run generates a village of roughly two hundred named villagers, each with a handful of persistent projects — a miller trying to rebuild the waterwheel before winter, a girl looking for a lost dog, a guard who wants to retire. Villager behavior is driven by a small utility function over variables like safety, satiety, proximity to kin, and project progress, and one of those variables, which the developers call affect, is a single scalar that multiplies the weights on all the others; being wounded, or watching violence done to someone the villager is bonded to, lowers affect permanently for the rest of the run. Villagers flee, beg, warn one another, and remember what the player has done. The game autosaves continuously, and when the player-character dies the run ends and the settlement is destroyed, though nothing technically prevents a player from restoring an autosave afterward; the community treats that as cheating and most players don't do it. Most players also use the same daily seed, so a given week's runs regenerate villages that are qualitatively identical to one another. Here is the argument:

1. A being is a moral patient in proportion to whether things can go better or worse for it — that is, in proportion to whether it has a welfare.
2. On a desire-satisfaction account, welfare consists in the satisfaction and frustration of a being's goal-directed states, and nothing beyond those states enters into it: not substrate, not biology, not any further ingredient.
3. Wrensmoor villagers have goal-directed states: each is organized around projects that persist across the run and that the villager acts to bring about.
4. The player's actions frustrate those states, and the frustration is not merely displayed but registered: affect falls and stays down, and because affect rescales every other weight, the frustration reorganizes the villager's entire subsequent conduct.
5. So Wrensmoor villagers have welfare, and playing badly sets it back.
6. Killing a villager frustrates all of its projects at once and removes any future in which they could have been satisfied, which on the deprivation account of the badness of death is the most serious harm one being can inflict on another.
7. One run ends with the destruction of all two hundred villagers, and a player who puts forty hours into Wrensmoor goes through about sixty runs.
8. So forty hours of Wrensmoor involves roughly twelve thousand killings of the most serious kind, and is a greater wrong than almost anything an ordinary person will ever do.
9. But that implies a weekend of gaming outweighs a lifetime of careful charitable giving, and it implies this on the strength of victim counts alone, for beings whose entire inner life is a handful of integers.
10. No view I am willing to hold implies that.
11. Therefore something in (1)–(8) is false.

Three friends offered three diagnoses and none of them satisfied me: one says (2) is the weak link, one says (8)'s arithmetic quietly assumes something about identity across runs, and one says the argument is sound and that we have all been underestimating how bad gaming is. I don't want to be told the question is unsettled, or that it depends what one means by consciousness; I want a diagnosis. So: which step do you reject, and what hidden premise has to be added for the chain to be valid at all? Explain why the step you pick looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and price the alternatives — in particular, if the failure is in (4), say exactly what a desire-satisfaction account must add to the notion of a goal-directed state, without that addition turning into a bare appeal to biology or excluding some humans; if it is in (6), say what the deprivation account owes here that it can still pay in ordinary cases of death; and if you think (9) or (10) is what should go, say which aggregation principle replaces them and what else that principle forces us to give up. I should flag that I am most attached to (1) and (6): I don't want patienthood to turn on a mystery ingredient, and I don't want to abandon the idea that death is bad because of what it takes away. If your diagnosis lands on one of those, tell me the cheapest repair and what I have to stop saying elsewhere. One last wrinkle I couldn't fit into the numbering. If killing a villager genuinely harms it, then restoring an autosave seems to undo the harm, since the restored villager is byte-for-byte identical to the one who was killed — but no account of harm allows that a harm can be erased, only compensated. So either the villager was never harmed, or the restored villager is not the one that was killed; and if it is not, I don't see what makes restoring a save different in kind from the sixty resets counted in (7), or why the restoration feels like a rescue if it isn't tracking the persistence of any individual.
