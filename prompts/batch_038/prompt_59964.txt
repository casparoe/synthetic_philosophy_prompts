I am preparing the take-home final examination for an upper-level undergraduate and beginning-graduate seminar on the ethics of algorithmic decision-making, cross-listed between philosophy and computer science. The students have already worked through the formal literature on mutually unsatisfiable fairness criteria, and the failure mode I want this problem to eliminate is recitation: an answer that names a theorem, announces that the criteria conflict, and stops there. I want a problem whose data carry the argument, so that a student who will not do the arithmetic and the algebra has nowhere to hide behind labels.

Please write the second problem of that examination, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The problem is worth 25 marks in total and students are expected to spend about 70 minutes on it. It must be wholly self-contained: define every term it uses, and do not name or cite any author, paper, textbook, or theorem, either in the question as the students will see it or in the model answer. Students are permitted no resources beyond the examination text, and I want the problem to be fully answerable on that basis.

Use the following scenario and data exactly as given.

Larkspur County must decide, for each defendant, whether to hold them in custody before trial. An instrument assigns every defendant a score S taking one of three ordered bands, S ∈ {1, 2, 3}, with 3 the highest. Defendants belong to one of two populations, A and B, distinguished by a legally protected attribute. Let Y = 1 if the defendant is rearrested within twelve months of release, and Y = 0 otherwise. Over one year the instrument produced the following counts.

Group A: S = 1, 400 defendants, of whom 40 were rearrested. S = 2, 400 defendants, of whom 200 were rearrested. S = 3, 200 defendants, of whom 180 were rearrested.
Group B: S = 1, 600 defendants, of whom 60 were rearrested. S = 2, 300 defendants, of whom 150 were rearrested. S = 3, 100 defendants, of whom 90 were rearrested.

The county acts on the score by choosing a cut point: it holds a defendant if and only if S ≥ k, for k ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}, where k = 4 corresponds to holding no one and k = 1 to holding everyone. The same cut point is currently applied to both groups.

Structure the problem in three parts.

Part (a), 8 marks, is computational and diagnostic. Require the student to compute, for each group, the overall rearrest rate and the rearrest rate within each score band, and to state precisely what it amounts to, in the vocabulary of the course, that these within-band rates coincide across the two groups. Then require them to compute, for each group and each of the four cut points, the positive predictive value of the holding decision, its true positive rate, and its false positive rate, and to report from that grid which cut point or cut points make positive predictive value equal across groups and which make both error rates equal across groups.

Part (b), 10 marks, is where I want the separation between understanding and memorization. Require the student to show that the pattern found in (a) is forced rather than accidental: they must express positive predictive value as a function of the true positive rate, the false positive rate, and the group's overall rearrest rate, and derive from that expression the general condition under which a decision rule that equalizes both error rates across two groups with different rearrest rates also equalizes positive predictive value. They must then use the derivation to say what would have to be true of the instrument, or of the two groups, for a non-degenerate rule to satisfy both families of criteria at once, and to explain what is degenerate about the rules that satisfy equalized odds in the county's own data. Finally, they must give a definite answer, with reasons, to whether allowing group-specific cut points, or allowing the rule to randomize within a score band, enlarges the set of rules available to Larkspur County in this case.

Part (c), 7 marks, is normative. First, require the student to say what further premises, none of them statistical, are needed to move from the grid in (a) to the conclusion that one admissible rule rather than another is unjust to one of the two groups. Second, note for them that Y records rearrest rather than offending, and require them to distinguish, as carefully as they can, what in the analysis of (a) and (b) survives and what does not if rearrest systematically overstates or understates offending in one group relative to the other because of how enforcement is distributed; they should say whether any of the criteria discussed retains its claim to measure what it purports to measure.

The model answer should run to roughly 900 words of prose, with tables and displayed derivations not counting toward that total. It must display the complete grid of computed values for all four cut points and both groups, in a form I can check against the data above, and it must show the derivation required in (b) line by line rather than asserting its conclusion.

For the marking scheme, identify six must-hit moves, allocate marks to them so that they sum to 25, and for each give a one-line note on the way candidates typically botch it. In addition, list the specific errors that should cost marks, and make sure the list covers at least the following: a candidate who reports that the instrument is simply miscalibrated and needs fixing; a candidate who assumes that because the score is group-calibrated band by band, any group-blind cut point inherits that property; a candidate who presents holding everyone, or holding no one, as a genuine resolution of the conflict rather than a degenerate one; a candidate who proposes group-specific cut points as a remedy without checking their effect on predictive value; a candidate who concludes from the impossibility of joint satisfaction that the choice is therefore arbitrary or that fairness is an illusion; and a candidate who treats the rearrest label as unproblematic throughout.

Close with a short paragraph, five lines at most, describing what an answer that merely passes looks like and what an answer at full marks adds, so that two markers grading independently would draw the boundary in the same place.
