I spent thirty-four years as an experimental physicist in a national laboratory, mostly on neutron scattering instrumentation, and have come to philosophy late and without the usual training. I am drafting an essay of roughly three thousand words on the unilateralist's curse — the phenomenon Bostrom, Douglas and Sandberg named, whereby an action that is on balance harmful becomes more likely to be taken as the number of agents who could take it independently increases, and for which they proposed a principle of conformity as remedy. What follows is the argument in condensed form. I would like comments of the kind specified at the end.

1. The standard model. Each of n agents independently judges whether action A is net beneficial; each judgment errs in the optimistic direction with probability p; A is taken if any single agent judges it beneficial and is able to act alone. The probability of a harmful act is then 1 − (1 − p)^n, which rises steeply with n. Under a k-out-of-n approval rule a harmful act requires k coincident errors, so the probability falls steeply with k. Independence carries the entire result.

2. Correlated errors cut both ways. Real research communities do not err independently: they share training, literature, funding and prestige structures. Survey sampling handles this by replacing n with an effective size, n_eff ≈ n / (1 + (m − 1)ρ), for clusters of size m and intra-cluster correlation ρ. Applying that here yields an uncomfortable symmetry. A monolithic community has small n_eff, so the curse is weak — but the k approvals are also not k independent checks, so the remedy is worth little. A diverse community has large n_eff, so the curse is severe — and the conformity principle genuinely works. Disease and cure scale with the same parameter. I want to argue that this is what the original formulation misses: the epistemic value of consensus is not independent of the danger of unilateralism, and the communities whose agreement is most reassuring are not the ones most in need of it.

3. Extremes rather than averages. With heterogeneous agents the risk is 1 − ∏(1 − p_i), which is dominated by the largest p_i, not by the mean. The relevant statistic is therefore the upper tail of the error distribution — the most confident and least careful capable agent — and adding ordinary agents to n changes almost nothing once such an agent exists. The curse is on this reading an extreme-value phenomenon, and counting actors is the wrong first move.

4. What a norm selects for. A principle of conformity binds only those disposed to be bound. I distinguish three error sources: (a) first-order misjudgment of A's value; (b) second-order misjudgment, by an agent who thinks the consensus itself is mistaken; (c) divergence of objective, in an agent who does not share the common-good criterion at all. Aggregation addresses (a) and nothing else. A stronger norm raises the cost of dissent and so selects dissenters for greater confidence in their own judgment; the residual risk under the norm is therefore dominated by (b) and grows with the strength of the norm. (c) is untouched. My thesis is that the curse is usually presented as a problem of aggregation under uncertainty when it is really a problem of selection: given the norm, which agent is left to act?

5. Timescales. In my own field the harm from publication was close to instantaneous and irreversible, while consensus formed over decades. Let τ_h be the interval over which harm accumulates and τ_c the interval needed to reach reliable consensus. When τ_c/τ_h is large the curse bites regardless of institutional design, and the design question ceases to be how many veto players to add and becomes how to shorten τ_c or lengthen τ_h: staged disclosure, reversibility, containment. In 1991 our group spent six weeks debating whether to publish a set of cross-section measurements; the question was settled when one of us posted a preprint to a colleague abroad.

The comments I want are these. (i) Is the effective-sample-size step legitimate, or am I importing a sampling formula into a setting where nothing is sampled — and if the latter, does section 2 survive without it? (ii) Does the selection argument in section 4 follow, or does it prove too much, since a norm that selects dissenters for confidence may equally select them for competence? (iii) Is τ_c/τ_h explanatory or merely decorative: would a reader already persuaded by the standard model learn anything from it? (iv) Where do I over-claim relative to what the mathematics licenses, and where would a philosopher object that I have mistaken a statistical observation for a normative argument? Tell me also which of the five sections to cut. Please do not send references or a reading list; I want the reasoning examined, not the literature mapped. If the whole construction is confused, say so plainly and identify the step at which it fails.
