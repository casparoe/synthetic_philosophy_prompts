Ok so this has been bugging me for like a week and I want a second brain on it. Our campus dining has this "responsible seafood" placard program — the little green tag on the servery stations — and there's a panel of students and a couple faculty (no actual marine scientists, we wing it off a one-page charter that says we weigh animal welfare, environmental impact, and how confident the sentience evidence is). I'm on it, and I'm increasingly sure our rulings don't add up to a single principle, but I can't quite show it. Our record so far, roughly in order:

- Farmed Atlantic salmon from a supplier that stuns electrically before bleeding and caps stocking density — approved with conditions. The minutes basically say sentience isn't in dispute, and the only fixable problems were the kill step and crowding.
- Whole lobster for the deli, supplier boils them live and keeps the tanks out where students walk by — rejected. Members kept saying they didn't need to resolve whether crustaceans feel pain to object to that specific death, and killing the tanks mattered more to them than anything else.
- Frozen lobster tails, same species, chill/mechanical kill before freezing, no tanks — approved. Nobody claimed the tails were less sentient than the whole animal; the vote flipped entirely on how it dies.
- Rope-grown mussels and farmed oysters — unanimous, fastest pass we've ever had, because bivalves have no centralized brain and basically nobody thinks they're sentient.
- Dredge-caught wild scallops — rejected, and the minutes are explicit that it was seabed habitat damage and bycatch, not scallop sentience (same phylum as the oysters we just waved through).
- Farmed vannamei shrimp, broodstock get eye-stalk ablated and they're harvested in ice slurry — rejected, but this one split us down the middle: half voted no on welfare grounds (the ablation, the freezing death), half voted no mostly about mangrove clearing and the fishmeal-in/fish-out problem. This is the one I can't stop thinking about.

Now here's what's coming to us next month and it kind of breaks the rubric. A supplier wants to sell us farmed common octopus — same concept as that company in Spain that's been in the news trying to open the first commercial octopus farm. Sentience-wise we're stuck: octopuses are about as clearly sentient as an invertebrate gets (huge brains, tool use, one European country even wrote octopuses and lobsters into its animal-sentience law a couple years back), so we can't lean on "the evidence is uncertain" the way some of us wanted to with the shrimp. But the husbandry is rough: they're solitary and carnivorous, they do badly when crowded, they cannibalize in captivity, nobody's disclosed an established humane kill method, and getting a kilo of octopus takes a big input of wild-caught fish and crustaceans — plus a lot of these operations still start from wild-caught juveniles, so it piles on fishing pressure. The supplier is offering to contractually commit to an anesthetic/electrical kill, low density, and enrichment, which is precisely the move that got the salmon and the lobster tails a yes.
