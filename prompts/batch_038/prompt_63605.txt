I've built an argument against the view that rational agents should maximize the long-run growth rate of their wealth, and I've grown suspicious of it, because the conclusion discredits a principle I don't want to lose.

(1) A principle of rational choice is unacceptable if it can require an agent to adopt a policy that, in the long run, leaves her poorer than an available alternative with probability approaching one.
(2) When a multiplicative gamble is repeated and the proceeds are reinvested, the policy that maximizes expected log wealth per period — the Kelly policy — is the one that, given enough repetitions, ends up ahead of every rival policy with probability approaching one.
(3) Which policy that is depends only on the odds and payoffs; no utility function enters, and that is precisely why growth-rate rationality is advertised as an objective replacement for expected-utility theory.
(4) Nothing in the support for (2) uses the number of repetitions except through the phrase "enough"; a single decision is a sequence of length one, and a principle that holds for sequences of every length n holds for n = 1. Denying that posits an arbitrary threshold — and in any case no real decision is genuinely one-shot, since every choice is embedded in a lifetime of further ones.
(5) So expected-log maximization governs every decision, not only investment.
(6) Under expected log, an outcome of zero wealth contributes log 0 = −∞; hence any act with a positive probability of total ruin has expected log −∞, however large its other payoffs.
(7) So (5) and (6) forbid any act carrying a positive chance of ruin: elective surgery, flying, and — since nothing is perfectly safe — effectively every ordinary activity.
(8) Those acts are plainly sometimes rational.
(9) Therefore (1) is false: a principle can be rationally binding even when it predictably leaves the agent poorer, and geometric rationality is self-defeating — a theory of long-run success that, applied consistently, forbids going on long enough to enjoy it.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I want to keep (8), and I would rather keep (1) than lose it, since abandoning it seems to license every self-defeat argument in decision theory. Dropping (3) — conceding that log is just one utility function among many — doesn't obviously help, because (4) through (7) never used the claim that the criterion is taste-free; if I'm wrong about that, say so. And if you think the reconstruction is a straw man, so that no defender of growth-rate rationality is committed to (4) or (6) and the argument therefore fails to pick out a live problem, say that plainly rather than humouring it. I want a committed verdict, not a menu: name the step you would give up, say how confident you are that this is the right diagnosis rather than a merely available one, and flag where you think the fix costs more than it first appears.
