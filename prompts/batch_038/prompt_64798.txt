I sit on the exhibitions committee of a mid-sized natural history museum, and in eleven months we open a new permanent gallery provisionally titled The Edge of the Living. Its centrepiece is an interactive we are calling the Living Wall: ten specimen pods arranged in a ring, each fitted with a two-position indicator that the visitor throws to green or to grey, after which the pod's panel reveals the answer the museum has endorsed together with a single sentence of explanation. Education staff ran a cardboard prototype with school groups last spring, and the two answers children gave most often were also the two that our draft answer key handles worst. That key currently applies the seven-item checklist already printed in the old hall text — movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition — ticking each pod against all seven and greying out anything that misses one. The verdicts it produces have several of us embarrassed, and our science顾问 is close to resigning from the advisory panel over pod five.

Five pods are the problem, and I would like the memo to take them in turn. (1) A plaque-mounted vial of T4 bacteriophage, billed as the most numerous biological entity on earth: the checklist greys it out for want of independent metabolism or reproduction, but our consultant argues that a phage assessed as a member of a lineage rather than as a particle in a vial is unambiguously alive, and that the pod is quietly teaching visitors to make a category mistake about individuals. (2) A living culture of the slime mould Physarum polycephalum, which the education team is keen on because it negotiates mazes for oat flakes and reorganises after being cut into pieces, and which it is not clear is even one thing. (3) A vial of freeze-dried tardigrades that have sat in tun state since the 1980s and are rehydrated for weekend demonstrations: no detectable metabolism for decades, revival on demand. (4) A working synthetic protocell from a university laboratory across town — fatty-acid vesicles enclosing a self-replicating RNA system that grows and divides reliably, though the lab tells us in confidence that it does not yet accumulate heritable variation, so it splits without evolving. (5) A floor-to-ceiling screen running a simulation in which self-replicating digital creatures compete for a scarce resource, donated and endowed by a technology firm whose communications office has asked in writing that the pod's indicator be hard-wired to green and that the panel copy describe its contents as a living system; our director does not wish to refuse them, and the pod is already fabricated. Separately, and this is what made me distrust the checklist itself rather than merely its application, three different groups last spring asked about mules, which fail the reproduction item outright and are obviously alive; the panel as drafted has no answer, and neither did the docent.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the committee's next meeting. I want the bottom line up front: a recommended indicator position and a one-sentence panel gloss for each of the five contested pods, and then a decision rule our curatorial staff can apply to the remaining pods and to whatever turns up next year — a sterile hybrid, an immortal cell line, a synthetic organism announced in the press, an exhibit whose state changes during the gallery's own lifetime. The memo should also say what concept the wall is actually deploying: whether living and not-living is a distinction this museum can honestly endorse as binary, and if not, what the wall should be doing instead, given that most of our visitors are eleven years old and that we have publicly committed to giving them an answer rather than a shrug. Set out the strongest case against your own recommendation — I expect it from the educators, who will argue that a graded or context-sensitive scheme teaches children that scientific categories are matters of taste, and from the development office, who will argue that pod five is not worth a fight with an endowment donor — and tell me how you would answer both in the room. Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a list of people who have written about this; the committee will not read them, and I want the reasoning in the memo's own voice. Mark every place where you are guessing about the biology rather than relying on something settled, since I will send those passages to our consultants. And if you think my framing is confused, or that the task as I have posed it cannot be done well — for instance, if you think a two-position indicator bolted to a wall of this kind is simply the wrong instrument and that no answer key deserves this committee's endorsement — say so plainly at the top of the memo rather than producing the polished document I have asked for.
