I assembled the following argument while drafting a chapter comparing higher-education finance in Germany and the Nordics with that in the United States and the United Kingdom, and I now believe it proves more than I can defend.

(1) A public funding arrangement is regressive just in case it moves resources from people who are worse off to people who are better off.
(2) Where university is free at the point of use and paid for out of general taxation, taxpayers who never enrol are, as a class, paying for the education of those who do.
(3) Graduates earn substantially more than non-graduates over their working lives, in Germany and the Nordics as much as in the US and the UK.
(4) Therefore free tuition moves resources from the worse off to the better off, and is regressive.
(5) The same reasoning shows that public funding of any good whose uptake rises with income — conservatoires, doctoral training, tertiary hospitals, elite research — is regressive as well.
(6) Income-contingent loans escape the problem: repayment is a share of earnings above a threshold, so graduates who earn little repay little, and nobody is charged for a qualification they never obtained or never benefited from.
(7) If (4), (5) and (6) hold, then the Nordic model is regressive at one of its cores, and the least state provision of higher education anywhere is the most just arrangement on offer.
(8) So (1) is false.

I would rather keep (2) and (3), which look to me like neutral descriptions of where the money goes rather than normative claims, and I suspect (7) is simply where I have overreached. Which step should I give up? Say why it looked unobjectionable when considered on its own, what giving it up costs, and whether that cost falls differently on a defender of free tuition and a defender of income-contingent loans.
