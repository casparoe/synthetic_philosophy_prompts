You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of formal epistemology and anthropic reasoning, with a working command of Bayesian confirmation theory, indexical or centered evidence, and the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions. Please think carefully before answering; I am looking for conceptual surgery, not a survey of the literature.

Here is the situation. A close friend and I spent most of last night on a single exchange. He ran the Doomsday argument against me: roughly 100 billion or so humans have ever been born; a long-lived, space-colonizing civilization would contain trillions of observers; if my birth rank is a random draw from all humans who ever live, then being among the first 100 billion is overwhelmingly more likely on the early-doomsday hypothesis than on the vast-future hypothesis; therefore I should assign doomsoon far more probability than I did when I went into the conversation. I objected, among other things, that he was treating an arbitrary indexical fact as though it carried evidential weight it had not earned. He waved the objection away with a slogan he has used on me before, in unrelated contexts: "You should reason as if you were a random observer drawn from your reference class." When I pressed, he said the slogan was neutral between his conclusions and mine, and that my refusal to accept it was simply a refusal to do Bayesian reasoning about my own existence. I do not believe that for a second, but I was unable to say why at two in the morning.

Your task is to take that one sentence apart. Treat it as a piece of language whose surface form conceals several distinct theses, and produce a disambiguation with verdicts — a menu, not a defense of your own favorite reading.

Before the menu, begin with a short framing section of no more than about two hundred words that fixes notation: state, in your own words and without quotation marks, what the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption each amount to, and say which one you take the slogan to most naturally express. Keep this tight; the work is downstream.

Then lay out the readings as a numbered list. For each entry give:

1. A one-sentence statement of the reading, made as precise as you can. Where it helps, give its logical form: what is being quantified over, what the sample space is, what the chance claim or indifference principle is, and at what stage of the Bayesian calculation it is supposed to operate — prior construction, conditionalization on indexical evidence, or a decision rule about how to act under uncertainty about who you are.
2. A verdict: is it a platitude that no one in the debate denies, a substantive but defensible thesis, false or incoherent, or question-begging in the specific sense that it can only be stated once the very dispute it is invoked to settle has already been resolved in one camp's favor?
3. Whether the Doomsday conclusion goes through on that reading, and if it does not, exactly which premise of my friend's argument fails and why.
4. Whether a proponent of that reading could honestly use the slogan to dismiss my objection, or whether he would have to say something considerably more specific.

Make sure the menu includes readings that vary along each of the following axes, and be explicit about which axis a given reading is varying:

- The reference class: all observers, all humans, all observer-moments, all observers with your qualitative properties, or a class chosen ad hoc for the problem at hand. Address directly whether the size of the reference class can be fixed independently of which version of the slogan is true, or whether the slogan is unstateable until the reference class is chosen — and whether that fact alone defuses my friend's claim of neutrality.
- The force of "as if": a claim about what your degrees of belief ought to be, a recommendation to adopt a useful fiction, a claim about objective chance in the sense of there being some physical process that selected which observer you are, or a bare principle of indifference over self-locating possibilities with no chance structure behind it at all.
- The force of "random": uniform, exchangeable, sampling with or without replacement, and whether it is a claim that you are typical or only that you should not assume you are atypical. Note carefully that these come apart.
- Whether "you" is doing any real work, or whether the only thing that matters is the evidence you happen to have, in which case the slogan collapses into a general claim about total evidence plus some account of how centered propositions are to be conditionalized on.
- Whether the slogan is a constraint on priors, a rule of conditionalization, or both, and what follows for the disagreement between self-sampling and self-indication, given that they do not appear to be rival answers to the same well-posed question.
- Whether any reading of the slogan can survive contact with the Presumptuous Philosopher style of objection, and separately whether any reading can survive the observation that SIA-style reasoning seems to make the size of an unobservable ensemble of universes turn on fine-tuning considerations. If a reading survives one but not the other, say so.

Include at least one reading on which the slogan is flatly false, and at least one on which it is true but so weak that it cannot bear the weight my friend put on it. Also flag, explicitly and separately, any place where two apparently distinct readings turn out to be notational variants of a single claim, or where an apparently available reading is not really coherent.

Finally, add a closing section that tells me what my friend most likely means when he reaches for the slogan, on the evidence of the argument he actually gave — that is, identify the reading his Doomsday argument requires, state whether he is entitled to it, and give me the single most pointed question I could ask him next time, with a note on how he would probably answer it and what that answer would reveal.

Formatting and housekeeping. Numbered entries, each with the four components above clearly labeled. Plain prose, no tables unless one genuinely clarifies something. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list, no attribution of any claim to a named author or book, and no direct quotation from any published work — I cannot check any of that and I have not asked for it. If you are uncertain about a technical detail, say that you are uncertain rather than inventing specifics. Do not recommend that I go read anything; do the analysis instead. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
