Next term I am responsible for one seventy-five-minute seminar in a first-year graduate sequence on decision-making under uncertainty. The students are mostly economics and public-policy MAs being trained to rank programs by expected value. They can compute an expectation as a probability-weighted sum, they have met the St. Petersburg gamble in passing, and they have had exactly one term of probability: none of them has encountered a conditionally convergent series, and none has any reason to suspect that the order in which terms are added could matter. The seminar's subject is the Pasadena game, and what a decision theory built on expected value is supposed to say when an expectation fails to exist. I have a whiteboard and no slides, and I will not be able to assume any mathematics I have not put on that board myself.

Do not explain the game to me; I know it. Plan the seminar.

(a) The order of the components and what depends on what. In particular, tell me where to place the fact that in the order the outcomes naturally arise the series does converge, to an entirely unremarkable figure: state that figure, and argue whether revealing it early buys me credibility at the cost of the punchline, or whether withholding it makes the eventual result look like a trick.

(b) Which single worked computation carries the whole session, and how much of the standard overshoot-and-undershoot procedure for steering a conditionally convergent series toward a chosen target the students must carry out by hand, as against merely being told it can be done.

(c) The misconceptions this audience specifically will bring in or form on the spot. I anticipate that it is just St. Petersburg with extra steps, and that a bounded utility function makes the trouble disappear; I want the ones I have not thought of, given that these students are trained to produce a number and defend a ranking. For each, give the point in the sequence at which it must be headed off, not the point at which it will surface.

(d) One closing task, doable in the last five minutes, that separates a student who has grasped that the value is not merely difficult to compute but absent from one who has merely accepted that infinities behave strangely.

(e) What I should deliberately leave out, and what I should say aloud about leaving it out, so that the omission registers as a decision rather than a gap.

One point on which I want your judgment rather than my own. I had planned to finish on a wrinkle of my own: the familiar reply that no counterparty could really cover unbounded payoffs does not tame this game, it makes it worse. Cap the winnings at whatever the house can actually pay and the positive part of the series becomes convergent while the penalties are untouched and still diverge, so the realistic version of the gamble has an expected value of negative infinity; cap the penalties instead, at whatever the player can actually lose, and it becomes positive infinity. On that account the sign of the resulting infinity is settled by whose pockets are finite. Is the reasoning correct as I have stated it? And if it is, does it belong at the end of a first encounter, or does it quietly convert a lesson about undefined expectation into a lesson about infinite expectation and undo what I have spent the hour establishing?
