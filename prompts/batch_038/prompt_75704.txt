Context: I've been working through the socialist calculation debate — Mises's original argument, Lange's trial-and-error reply, Hayek's knowledge-based response, plus the modern "but now we have sensors and neural nets" revivals — and I keep tripping over one dialectical move that shows up on the pro-planning side again and again. Let me describe it without using vocabulary that would hand you the answer.

The move: someone argues that a system can't do X because it has no way of getting hold of some information. The reply is a demonstration that the system could do X if run a certain way. The demonstration works, but only because the information turns up among the inputs it's allowed to use, with nothing said about where it came from. And it gets offered as a refutation, when all it's really established is that the original premise, if granted, doesn't lead to a contradiction.

Three instances:

1. Claim: without market prices for capital goods you can't compare alternative uses of a machine, an ore body, a stretch of rail. Reply: sure you can — post prices for everything from a board, watch where the shortages and gluts appear, nudge prices up and down accordingly, iterate until things clear. That's a real procedure and it does reproduce the textbook competitive outcome. But it only gets going once somebody can see the shortages and gluts and translate them into the right direction and size of adjustment, and that seeing-and-translating is exactly what the first claim said the system can't manage. The demonstration didn't produce the missing knowledge, it just wrote a slot for it.

2. Same shape, later century: planning failed back then because of pencil-and-paper limits, but a networked economy with enough compute can ingest all the relevant data and solve the allocation. Plausible as an engineering statement, except it assumes there's a well-defined body of data sitting there to be ingested — whereas the original objection was that alot of the relevant stuff (what this foreman knows about this machine on this shift, what these people would actually trade off against what) never takes a recordable form until it's been expressed through a real choice at a real price.

3. And I see it outside this debate entirely: we could fix the externality if the regulator just estimated marginal damage and taxed at that rate. Fine — given that the regulator can estimate marginal damage. The feasibility proof is the disputed assumption restated with a bit of mechanism wrapped around it.

So, is there an established concept for this? Not just any old circularity. I know about begging the question, about ceteris paribus clauses, about idealization in models, and about the complaint that you shouldn't compare a real messy institution with a flawless hypothetical one. None of those quite fits, because in my cases the demonstration doesn't look circular to the person making it, and it isn't idealizing away some nuisance variable — its taking as an input the precise thing whose availability was in dispute, and it does so under cover of looking constructive. There's a procedure, there are steps, there's even a convergence story, so it reads like a proof rather than like an assumption.
