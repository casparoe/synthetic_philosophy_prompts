I'm an engineer on the evals side of a chatbot product — no philosophy background past whatever I've picked up from podcasts, so bear with me. In a design review last week we were arguing about whether to keep a separate deception track in our eval suite or just fold it into plain accuracy. The case on the table: a user asks the model whether their API key is rate-limited per key or per org, the model answers per-key with total confidance, it's actually per-org — and separately we have traces where the same question phrased with the user asserting the wrong answer first gets the model agreeing with the user. A senior guy killed the whole debate with "it's not lying, lying requires intent," and then about ten minutes later used basically the same line again to argue we should only score whether the output is true, since "honest and wrong is still fine." Two different jobs done by one sentence, and nobody pushed back because it sounded like it settled things. I have a vague memory that philosophers don't even agree on whether lying needs an intent to deceive, but I could be wrong about that.

Can you take the slogan apart into the distinct precise claims it could be making — what "intent" has to be here, intent to bring about what exactly, whose beliefs are in play, and what a system would have to have for the slogan to apply to it at all? Run from the readings that are trivially true through the ones that are substantive to the ones that are just false, and for each say whether it's defensible and whether it would actually license either of the two moves he made. I especially want to know whether there's a reading where "no intent" doesn't let you off the hook, because that's the side nobody argued for. Also flag anywhere the phrase is quietly doing work that has nothing to do with intent — like what "what the model believes" could even mean for something we can only sample from.
