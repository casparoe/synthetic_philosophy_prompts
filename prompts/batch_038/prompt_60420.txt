I was elected to my county's board of commissioners last November after twenty years running a hospital's facilities department, and one of my first assignments was to chair the committee that reviews ordinances which remain on the books but have not been enforced in decades. The list turned out to include a provision making it a misdemeanor for two unmarried adults to share a dwelling, a Sunday restriction on the sale of certain goods, a prohibition on charging money to tell fortunes, and a vague offense covering lewd performances in any place open to public view. My assumption going in was that we would repeal the lot of them as dead letter and hear nothing more about it. Instead, a residents' association has filed a petition asking that we not merely retain the cohabitation provision but direct the sheriff to enforce it, and their filing was drafted by a retired judge who has since argued the case at two public forums. The petition is not an abstraction to me. My daughter has lived with her partner in this county for six years; under the ordinance as written she is a misdemeanant in the place where she grew up, and she has asked me, reasonably, whether I intend to vote to keep her that way. There is a hearing in three weeks, and my only formal training in this material is one semester of jurisprudence in a mid-career program, which is why I am writing to you rather than to the county attorney, whose office has already told me the ordinance is almost certainly unenforceable and therefore not worth arguing about.

What I cannot answer is not the constitutionality but the argument itself, which the judge grounds explicitly in Devlin's 1959 Maccabaean Lecture and presents as a reply to Hart's harm-principle objection. As he states it, and as I have reconstructed it for my own committee memo:

1. A political community is not only an arrangement for coordinating the interests of the people who happen to live within it; it is also a shared way of life, and what makes it this community rather than some other is that its members broadly agree about how people ought to live.
2. A community whose members cease broadly to agree about how people ought to live does not continue to exist in a weakened form. It ceases to be that community, not through violence or secession but through becoming something else.
3. A community may defend whatever it requires in order to go on being itself, and the criminal law is the instrument by which it most clearly declares what it will not tolerate.
4. What a community's shared way of life contains is fixed by the settled moral feelings of its ordinary members — their indignation, intolerance, and disgust, as the reasonable person among them would experience it — and not by any external standard, because no external standard is available to be appealed to.
5. Those feelings must be read over a long enough span to filter out transient opinion; a shift across a few election cycles does not yet count as a change in the community's morality.
6. In this county, ordinary residents have regarded unmarried cohabitation with settled disapproval for as long as anyone now living can remember.
7. Therefore the county may retain and enforce the ordinance, and may do so even if nobody can identify any harm that cohabitation causes to any person, since the absence of harm is beside the point.
8. And whatever else the settled feelings of residents converge upon — some category of public performance, say, or a manner of disposing of the dead, or a form of commerce they find degrading — falls under exactly the same license.

I have gone around this eight times and I cannot find the joint that gives. If I have to choose, I would rather surrender step 2 than step 4, since abandoning 4 seems to commit me to the view that a community's own convictions count for literally nothing in what it legislates, which I find incredible; but I am not certain that 3 still has any support once 2 is gone, and I suspect that is the price. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look acceptable before you rejected it? I would like you to be specific about what each of the standard rejoinders costs its own side — the qualified harm principle, the offense-principle middle position, and the view that majority disgust is a kind of preference rather than a judgment and so gets a different kind of weight — rather than telling me that the liberal position wins by default. Then tell me whether any version of this argument can be repaired so that it stops short of steps 7 and 8 without collapsing back into a principle that permits enforcement only where there is harm to someone, and whether, if it cannot, that shows the petition is overreaching or shows that my own instinct about step 4 is what has to go. Finally, I would like something I can actually say at the hearing: not a refutation, but a way of granting the petitioners what is true in step 1 while explaining to people who have lived here their whole lives why that concession does not entitle them to what they are asking for.
