Our reading group has been working through the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers — the one on causality, the fire and the cotton. One member has come back with an argument meant to show that the position defended there cannot be held consistently, because it destroys the knowledge, the miracle, and the responsibility it was built to protect. Another member says this is only Ibn Rushd's objection from the Incoherence of the Incoherence — that once causes are denied nothing can be known in any settled way — pushed further than Ibn Rushd himself pushed it. I do not accept the conclusion: al-Ghazālī plainly wanted all three of those things, and the view was held by careful people for centuries. But I cannot locate the bad step. The argument as he laid it out:

1. A cause, properly so called, is a thing whose nature is such that, given it, the effect follows of necessity and nothing else could prevent it.
2. Nothing created satisfies (1): God can withhold any created thing's operation at will, and no created thing can secure its own continuation.
3. God alone satisfies (1).
4. Therefore no created thing is a cause. When fire meets cotton the fire does nothing; God creates the blackening and the crumbling, directly or through intermediaries, just as he creates the contact.
5. What we call the order of nature is thus nothing but God's custom — his settled practice of acting in a given sequence — which he can interrupt whenever he wills.
6. The evidence for (4) and (5) is observational, and observation shows only that one thing occurs with another, never that it occurs by it; so the case for occasionalism is an inference from what has been observed to a claim about how things must be.
7. Knowledge of a thing, in the strict sense, is knowledge of it through its cause.
8. By (4) and (5), the only cause of any contingent event is a will under no constraint. So no amount of observation establishes that a regularity holds, that it will hold, or that it holds for the reason we take it to hold.
9. Hence, on occasionalism, no one knows any contingent proposition about the world: every such claim would have to rest on an inference of the kind (8) discredits.
10. But by (6) the argument for occasionalism is itself an inference of that kind. So the occasionalist cannot know his own conclusion; and if he did know it, some inference from observation would have yielded knowledge, contradicting (9). The position is therefore unknowable, and if known would be false.
11. The same collapse removes what the position was meant to secure. A miracle is supposed to be an event no created thing could have produced, which is why it testifies to God's special intervention and to the commission of the prophet who claims it. By (4) and (5), no event is produced by any created thing at all, so the fire that fails to burn the cotton differs from the fire that burns it only in that God departed from his practice. The miracle becomes the breaking of a habit rather than the frustrating of a power, and the contrast that gave it evidential force is gone.
12. The same for accountability: if every human act is created by God and the agent merely acquires it, where acquisition is nothing more than the act's concurrence with a capacity and an intention that God also created, then nothing about the intention brings the act about, and the concurrence is just further custom.
13. So occasionalism entails that we can know nothing about the world, cannot tell miracle from custom, and cannot hold anyone accountable. Since its authors affirm all three, they either did not understand their own view or held it incoherently.

Tell me which step to reject, and why the steps around it are safe. I want the diagnosis to be specific rather than a general apologia: if the chain turns on a term that shifts meaning between premises, or on a quantifier or a scope that widens partway through, say exactly where, and say which premise is doing work that appears to be done by a later one. Then price the repairs. I am prepared to concede (11) and (12) — I suspect the miracle/custom contrast really is thinner than the tradition admits and the account of acquisition really is strained — but I want to block (9) and (10), since the self-undermining charge is what would sink the view rather than merely embarrass it. So: what must the occasionalist pay to block (10) — a weaker and derived notion of created causation, an externalist account of warrant that does not require the ground of a regularity to be necessary, a redescription of the structure of his own argument, a theological premise about God's faithfulness to his practice, something else — and does each available payment make (11) and (12) harder or easier to resist? Finally, if you think some version of the argument survives the best repair, restate it in the strongest form you can manage and say precisely what it then establishes and what it no longer does.
