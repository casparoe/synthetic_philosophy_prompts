I direct intake at a workplace ombuds office, and roughly six times a month we receive a complaint that turns entirely on what a written message implied but never said: a manager's email the evening before a decision the complainant had to approve, a remark at a recruitment dinner, a status update sent to one person only. My analysts are not philosophers. They work from the message plus one page of agreed background facts — no tone of voice, no interview with the writer, no access to anyone's intentions — and they have about forty-five minutes before a finding goes to a three-person panel that includes two non-specialists. A finding that an implied directive or threat was conveyed can end a career; a finding of none dismisses a complainant who may have been right. We currently decide these by instinct, and our rulings do not look consistent from the outside.

Please turn Grice's account of what is said versus what is implicated into a written procedure my analysts can actually run: the tests in order, what the analyst does at each one, what counts as a sufficient answer, which results end the case with no implied meaning found, and which results hand the question to the panel because what remains is a matter of institutional values rather than of what the message conveyed. Tell me which parts of the theory must survive the simplification for the procedure to still be testing what it claims to test, and which I can safely drop, and identify any step where the outcome is really being set by the analyst's feel for our workplace rather than by the test itself.

Address one worry head-on. Our hardest cases are exactly those in which the writer could append a denial without contradicting anything they wrote, because that is what makes such messages useful to send. I am concerned that a test which treats the availability of an unproblematic denial as evidence against an implied meaning will systematically clear the most deliberate offenders. Say how the procedure should handle that.

Then run it on three cases. (a) A manager writes to a subordinate the night before the subordinate must approve a vendor contract: "Just so you're aware, the VP has been asking who is closest to this file." Alleged: an instruction to approve, plus an implied offer of credit. (b) At a recruitment dinner a department head says to the leading candidate: "I imagine you'd find this town very quiet after the city." Alleged: a coded signal that the candidate will not fit in here. (c) A supervisor writes to an employee who has just filed a complaint: "For your information, I've been asked to compile a record of the interactions connected to this matter, and the ones involving you are included in it." Alleged: a threat of surveillance and retaliation. For (c) I expect the procedure may clear the writer; if it does, say so plainly and show which step does the clearing rather than softening the result.

Close with the limits: the kinds of case where this procedure will misfire, the kinds where it should output nothing more than an acknowledgment that the message's import is indeterminate, and what it cannot settle at all.
