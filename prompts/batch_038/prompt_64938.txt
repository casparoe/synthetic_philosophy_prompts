I am a third-year PhD student in programming languages. My work concerns underdetermined behavior in language standards: the distinctions a specification draws between behavior it requires, behavior it delegates to the implementation to define, behavior it leaves unspecified but well-formed, and behavior it declines to characterize at all. For two years I have been reading the normative text of one large industrial standard alongside its rationale documents, its issue tracker, and its conformance test suite, and I can read an operational semantics without strain; my legal training consists of one undergraduate course and nothing since. This term I am sitting in on a law-school seminar on statutory interpretation, because my department has begun a collaboration on machine-readable regulation, and the seminar's running dispute has struck me as one I already know under different names. On one side are the textualists, for whom the enacted words carry the meaning a competent reader of the language would have found in them at the time of enactment, and for whom the legislature's unexpressed hopes are not law. On the other are the purposivists, for whom the statute's purpose, its place in the wider body of law, and the consequences of competing readings do genuine interpretive work, and for whom a text read in isolation from what it was meant to accomplish is not being read at all. In two weeks I present to that seminar, and my draft thesis is frankly deflationary: the fight is between people who read the standard and people who read the design rationale, and both sides are really arguing about how much weight non-normative material deserves when the normative text runs out.

So I would like you to restate that debate in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me the residue report. For the translation, work through a specific set of moves rather than the debate in the abstract. First, ordinary meaning fixed at enactment, and the difficulty posed by terms whose technical usage has drifted since. Second, the semantic canons — *ejusdem generis*, *noscitur a sociis*, *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* — as devices for narrowing a general term by reference to the specific terms around it or to what the drafter said elsewhere and chose to omit. Third, the rule that the enacted text is construed as a whole rather than clause by clause. Fourth, the treatment of drafting history: committee reports, floor statements, earlier drafts, and proposals that were considered and rejected. Fifth, the absurdity doctrine and the textualist objection to it. Sixth, open texture and the penumbra — what a judge is doing when she decides whether Hart's hypothetical ordinance excluding vehicles from a public park reaches a bicycle, a motorized wheelchair, or a war memorial mounted on a truck. And seventh, the textualist claim that a body with many members, conflicting motives, and no unified mind has no discoverable collective intention, only a text. For each of these seven, give me the closest counterpart on my side of the fence — normative versus informative material, implementation-defined and unspecified behavior, undefined behavior as a refusal to assign meaning rather than a license to do anything, deprecation and backwards compatibility, conformance suites as operationalizations of the text, the reference implementation as a de facto authority, the reasonable programmer as the implicit audience — together with an explicit fidelity judgement for each: maps cleanly, maps only after one of the two sides is bent, or has nothing on my side to map onto.

Keep the translation honest, which is where I expect most responses to fail. What I most want to avoid is an answer that quietly imports my framework's commitments and then announces that the lawyers were talking about my subject all along. The existence of an accessible and reasonably determinate drafting record, the assumption that there is a single correct reading recoverable by a sufficiently careful reader, the fact that my disputes are settled ex ante and in the abstract rather than in a concrete case with a factual record and two adverse parties, and the absence in my world of any question about who has the standing to settle a disagreement — these are features of the standards world that may be doing covert work in the analogy, and wherever you rely on one of them I want it flagged as an import, not presented as a translation. Then come to the residue. Gather the items that survived only with distortion and the items with no counterpart at all, and tell me which of two conclusions the residue supports: that my vocabulary is missing conceptual resources the legal debate genuinely needs, so that the seminar is arguing about something my framework cannot even state; or that the debate is less substantive than its participants believe, being largely a quarrel about evidential weight dressed up as a quarrel about what meaning is. Take a position. I do not want a balanced survey that closes by noting the force of both readings; I want a committed verdict and your reasons for it, and I want you to identify which steps of your own translation you are least confident about and why. End your response with a single line in exactly the form `Verdict: TARGET-DEFECT` or `Verdict: SOURCE-DEFLATED`, choosing the one you actually believe. If you think the answer is mixed, still choose the dominant label and state in the body which of the seven items go the other way.
