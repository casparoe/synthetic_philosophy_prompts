I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD; my dissertation concerns when it is reasonable to defer to someone else's judgment, and for the past year I have been attached to a lab that builds and evaluates decision-support systems. A structure keeps recurring in that lab's own data and in its user-study transcripts, and I have been unable to locate a concept for it. I would like to know whether one exists, because if it does not I will have to construct one, and I would rather construct it against the nearest existing things than from nothing.

Here is the structure. A user adopts a system that issues recommendations in some domain — a diagnostic aid, a code reviewer, something that synthesizes a literature. The recommendations are good. Outcomes improve and go on improving: fewer errors, faster decisions, better work. Nothing in the outcome record suggests that anything has gone wrong. But the user's own capacity to evaluate those recommendations — the very capacity that made the deferral reasonable at the outset — erodes, because exercising it is precisely what the system renders unnecessary. The erosion does not appear in any metric anyone collects, since the metrics measure performance with the system present; the only way to expose it would be to withdraw the system, and withdrawing it would produce worse outcomes, so nobody proposes that. And there is a further feature that I take to be the philosophically interesting one: rebuilding the capacity requires a stretch of knowingly worse performance, which is exactly the stretch a rational user will decline to enter. So the deferral consumes the standing condition under which the deferral could be reassessed and revoked, and it does so invisibly from the inside and expensively from the outside.

Two instances, to pin the description down. First, a junior clinician who, over two years with a diagnostic aid, becomes markedly better at treating patients and markedly worse at saying whether the aid's differential is plausible; asked in a study to rate its outputs without acting on them, she cannot, and she reports no sense of loss, since her patients are doing better than they otherwise would. Second, a graduate student who uses a system to survey unfamiliar literatures and who, after a year, writes excellent reviews but can no longer tell whether a summary of a paper is faithful to the paper — the skill required to check is the skill he stopped using.

Let me say what I do not mean, so that the reply does not simply relabel something I have already excluded. I am not describing over-trust that causes a user to overlook the system's errors: there the damage surfaces in the outcome record as a bad recommendation adopted uncritically, whereas here the recommendations are sound and the damage lies entirely in an unmeasured capacity. Nor am I describing the general erosion of craft when work is reorganized around machines; my case is not about the labor process and can befall one person in one domain while their job stays the same. Nor is it the observation that we lean on external aids the way we lean on notebooks and calendars, since what interests me here is that the aid removes the ability to audit it rather than merely extending the auditor.

So: is there an established concept — in epistemology, in human factors or science-and-technology studies, or in recent work on artificial intelligence and human judgment — that picks out this structure, namely deference that destroys the capacity on which its own legitimacy depends and thereby becomes unrevisable except at the price of accepted loss? If there is, I want the two or three nearest candidates named and compared: for each, which features of my description it already carries, and which of the three exclusions above it fails to respect or collapses. If there is not, I want a coined term with a definition tight enough to yield non-trivial boundary cases, and I want you to say where the edges fall. Specifically: does a user who retains the capacity to evaluate but never exercises it suffer the same thing, or merely its precondition? Does the phenomenon require that the system be accurate, or does it survive when the system is mediocre and the user simply has no way of knowing? Is it present when the lost capacity could be cheaply reacquired, as with a language one has not spoken in a decade? And what, if anything, separates this from a person who has delegated decisions to a human expert for years and can no longer second-guess them — because if nothing separates the two, the term belongs to the epistemology of testimony rather than to the study of machines, and I need to know which of those projects I am actually in. I am not asking for references; I can assemble those myself. What I need is the conceptual geography and a defensible carving. A bare label would be useless to me — the boundaries are the point.
