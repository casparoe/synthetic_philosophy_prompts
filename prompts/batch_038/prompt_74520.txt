I retired three years ago after thirty-five years in statistical physics — most of it on first-order phase transitions and nucleation, the last decade on threshold behaviour in epidemic models. Since then I have been reading the philosophy of history seriously, and I have become convinced that the old quarrel about whether the individual or the structure carries the explanatory weight is stuck for a reason that is technical rather than temperamental. I am applying for a two-year senior fellowship attached to a philosophy department, which admits external candidates and asks for a project rather than a curriculum vitae. I have the idea; I do not have the disciplinary idiom, and I would much rather the idiom were correct than the prose graceful. Please draft the proposal for me.

The half-formed idea is this. The question whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler is ordinarily posed as though both the explanandum and the intervention were fixed. Neither is. On the explanandum side, the phrase covers a range running from an authoritarian, revanchist German regime, through one waging a war of conquest in the East, to one that carried out the industrialised murder of European Jews; my working hypothesis is that the sensitivity of the outcome to the removal of the individual is not a single quantity but a profile that varies with the level of description at which the outcome is fixed. On the intervention side, in the physical systems I worked with one can specify what it means to perturb a control parameter while holding the remainder fixed; in a single, unrepeatable, partly self-constituting historical system it is not obvious that any such holding-fixed is available, since the background conditions one wishes to keep constant are themselves among the things the individual influenced. In condensed matter we would say that near a critical point perturbations are not screened off but propagate, whereas far from criticality the macroscopic behaviour is robust to them; and I suspect that the structuralist and the hero-centred camps are, without acknowledging it, making rival claims about where Germany sat in that sense between 1930 and 1932. Whether that analogy can be made to bear weight, or is merely decorative, is the first thing the proposal must determine.

The document should run 1,500–1,800 words and contain:

1. A precise statement of the question, and of the reformulation you judge most defensible. Distinguish the versions that have determinate answers from those that do not, and say which one the project will actually pursue. If the honest conclusion is that the project is about the structure of macro-historical counterfactuals rather than about Germany, say so and defend the choice of case.

2. An account of why the existing positions leave the question open. Cover Carlyle and the heroic tradition; Spencer's objection that the hero is a product of social conditions; the pragmatist distinction between a leader whom events elevate and one who makes events; the mid-century dismissal of counterfactual history as unserious; the revival from around 1990, including the study of plausible worlds, the Ferguson-edited collection of 1997, and the later systematic critique by a British historian of the Third Reich; and the methodologists' requirement that a counterfactual rewrite as little of the actual past as possible. For each, state what it gets right and why the sensitivity question survives it.

3. The method, in four six-month phases, and concretely: which historiographical controversies I would take as test cases (I have in mind the dispute over the relative weight of ideology, bureaucratic momentum, and cumulative radicalisation in the origins of extermination policy, and the debate over whether economic crisis or the miscalculation of the conservative elite did the decisive work in January 1933); what I would extract from them; what a sensitivity profile looks like as a written argument rather than as a formula; and where a collaborating historian is indispensable rather than ornamental.

4. Why this route and not the obvious alternatives: agent-based simulation of Weimar politics; taking a side in the existing debate; and the purely conceptual analysis of causation and counterfactuals with no historical case attached. Be fair to each — I prefer an honest rejection to a rhetorical one — and identify what the case-study route supplies that the others cannot.

5. A direct confrontation with the disanalogies. There is no ensemble, one realisation, no stationarity, no repeatable intervention, and no defensible measure over counterfactual histories; and historians reject the covering-law conception of explanation that my vocabulary quietly presupposes. Say what survives these objections, and do not pretend that early-warning reasoning — rising variance, growing autocorrelation, critical slowing down — transfers intact. If what survives is a modest reframing rather than a method, that is acceptable, but it must be stated plainly.

6. What a result would look like and what would count as failure. I want specific failure conditions: for instance, that the description-relativity of the answer proves trivial once articulated; that the sensitivity vocabulary can be shown to do no work beyond ordinary causal talk; that the charge of scientism is correct rather than merely uncharitable. Also propose the deliverable — a monograph-length essay and a co-taught seminar seem realistic to me, but suggest something better if you can.

7. A candid assessment of scope for a seventy-one-year-old outsider working part-time over twenty-four months, able to read German prose but without archival training or historiographical credentials.

No bibliography and no literature review by citation; I will supply references myself. Do not invent titles, dates, or attributions — where you are uncertain, describe the position instead of naming a work. You may use the names I have given. Assume the readers are philosophers and historians who will be hostile to a physicist's proposal for at least two good reasons, and write so that those reasons are answered rather than waved away.
