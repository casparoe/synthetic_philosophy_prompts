ok so I'm a second-year PhD student and I TA the 300-level personal identity seminar here, and the prof has dumped one of the two midterm essay questions on me because she's at a conference the week they sit it. It's a 50-minute in-class exam, closed book, and by then the students will have had the whole Parfit stretch from Reasons and Persons: the teletransporter, Relation R, the Branch Line Case where the replica goes to Mars and the original hangs around for a few more days, and the bit about whether it could be rational to be indifferent to your own future (the Extreme Claim, if I remember the label right). They'll also have had one further-fact response — the line where a person's identity is a seperately existing fact over and above all the physical and psychological continuity. What I don't want is a question that a good memoriser can ace by reciting that Parfit thinks identity isn't what matters and R is. So I've been messing around with a variant of the teletransporter that I think actually bites, and I want you to turn it into a proper exam question.

Here's the case. Rename it, tidy the wording, but keep the parameters exactly as I've set them, because the whole thing turns on them. A company does a non-destructive full scan of you at 30 — nothing happens to you, you walk out the door — and stores the blueprint in a vault. You then live an ordinary life and die at 85 of a stroke. Within an hour of your death the company builds a replica from the stored blueprint: a healthy 30-year-old body with exactly your 30-year-old psychology, which wakes up, is handed a letter explaining the last 55 years, and carries on from there. There's exactly one replica. The causal chain to it runs entirely through the stored scan and not at all through the dying 85-year-old's brain. The replica has no memories of anything after the day of the scan. The company advertises the service, honestly and without overselling the mechanics, as survival.

What I want the question to force them to do: figure out for themselves where R holds and where it doesn't, since there are two candidate earlier people in play (the 30-year-old at the scan and the 85-year-old on the gurney) and the answer isn't the same for both; then handle the branching problem, because the 30-year-old apparently has two R-continuers and the exam has to make them say what Parfit can do about that without just waving at it; then say whether the case actually discriminates between reductionism and the further-fact view, or whether both of them can absorb it and the case is really about something else; and finally say something about whether the 30-year-old has a reason for special concern about the replica that the 85-year-old definately does not have, and whether that's a bug in the theory or the whole point of it. It has to be answerable in 50 minutes by someone who did the reading but has never seen this case, so ask for one focused argument, not a survey, and don't let it collapse into a mush of discuss the strengths and weaknesses of Parfit's account.

Then give me: the question as it would actually be printed, with the time and a word limit on it; a model answer of about 650 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 that genuinely separates an A from a B. I want the moves named — no well-argued / good use of examples filler. So five must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a short list of the mistakes students reliably make and what each should cost. I can already predict some: treating psychological connectedness and continuity as the same thing, reading the any-cause clause as licensing literally any causal route whatsoever, thinking the question is really about whether such a machine is physically possible, and asserting that identity is what matters without saying what the case does to that assertion. No bibliography, no reading list, and don't have them cite anything — it's closed book and the model answer has to stand on its own reasoning.
