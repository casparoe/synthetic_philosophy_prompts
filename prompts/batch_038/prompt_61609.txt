I write the ideas column for a general-interest science magazine, and my editor has commissioned a feature on why duplication plots keep turning into probability puzzles — the working title is something like "The Arithmetic of Being Two," and I have been handed Christopher Nolan's The Prestige as my case study. I want to write the piece around the Sleeping Beauty problem and the wider theory of self-locating belief, not around personal identity, because the identity reading has been done to death and I think it misses what is actually strange about Angier's trick. The relevant fact about the film is not that the machine makes two men but that, in the moment before it fires, Angier does not know which of the two he will be: he voices exactly that question, "Would I be the man in the box? Or the man in the prestige?" and later insists that climbing into the machine every night took courage precisely because of that ignorance, while the rows of tanks under the stage accumulate the men who turned out to be the box. Meanwhile Borden's rival trick, which looks identical from the seats, has an entirely different epistemic structure: no branching at all, just two brothers alternating, each of whom always knows which one he is. So the film appears to stage two kinds of centered ignorance — one belonging to a spectator who cannot tell which of two continuing people is onstage, one belonging to an agent who cannot tell which of two successors he will be — and treats them as the same show.

What I want from you is a reading that takes both the film and the technical literature seriously, and I want it to press on the places where the analogy with Beauty threatens to collapse. Beauty is put to sleep, woken on Monday, and, on tails only, has her memory erased and is woken again on Tuesday; her predicament is that she gains no new uncentered evidence and yet seems to have to move her credence in the coin, which is why Lewis's halfer answer and Elga's thirder answer part company over whether indifference among subjectively indistinguishable awakenings is a legitimate principle. Angier's predicament looks superficially like a brancher's version of the same thing, but three structural differences seem to me to matter and I cannot tell whether they are fatal or merely interesting. First, his memory is intact across nights, so his evidence accumulates in a way Beauty's cannot; the only thing he is ignorant of is his own location in a branching, not his place in a sequence. Second, one of his two successors does not wake up to discover anything — the man in the tank has minutes at most, and is arguably not forming beliefs about his location at all, which makes me suspect that what Angier faces before the switch is thrown is uncertainty about survival dressed up as uncertainty about location. Third, and this is the point a philosopher I interviewed put to me and I have not been able to shake, there is a frequency asymmetry: over a hundred performances, half of the post-machine Angiers take a bow and half drown, but if you build your reference class out of all Angier-person-moments rather than all performances, the drowning men contribute vastly more moments, since a tank holds you for a long while and an ovation does not — so a self-locating prior over moments, of the sort thirders appeal to, would have Angier expecting the box nearly every night. Tell me whether that is a real argument or a reference-class sleight of hand, and whether the film has any resources for answering it.

I would also like the reading to do justice to the film as a film rather than treating it as a problem set with costumes. The bird cage trick in the first act — the cage flattened, the dead bird replaced by a live one, the child in the stalls asking where the brother is — seems to me to state the whole puzzle before the machine exists, and Cutter's account of the pledge, the turn and the prestige, with its insistence that the audience is not really looking because it does not want to know, suggests that the film's real subject may be the epistemics of the spectator: a willfully suspended centered belief, held by people who could work out where the brother went and choose not to. Work from scenes and staging rather than dialogue; I do not want quotations and I have the script if I need it. I do want you to be an honest critic in both directions: say where the Sleeping Beauty apparatus genuinely illuminates Angier's choice — including whether his credence should shift between stepping into the machine and the switch being thrown, and whether he is a halfer, a thirder, or something the literature has not named — and say plainly where the film's situation is not Beauty's and the comparison is being pushed past its limits, including whether the Borden half of the story is a deliberate deflation of the whole idea, a man whose trick works only because nobody ever branches. And I would like you to take a position on the diachronic question that the film raises and the papers mostly bracket: Angier commits, night after night, to an act whose outcome he cannot locate himself within, which makes him something like an agent who knowingly signs himself up for dynamic inconsistency, and the drowned men are the sure-loss bets coming due. Close by telling me what the film contributes to this debate that argument alone does not — what the hundred nights, the tank room and the applause make visible about self-locating belief that Beauty's coin cannot. Around fifteen hundred words, no bibliography and no reading list; I have the references covered, and what I need is the criticism.
