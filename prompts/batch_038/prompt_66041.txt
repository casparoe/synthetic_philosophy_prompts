**The question as set (25 marks total).**

> Consider the two-person bargaining problem (S, d) with disagreement point d = (2, 1) and feasible set
> S = { (x₁, x₂) ∈ ℝ² : x₁ ≥ 2, x₂ ≥ 1, x₁ + 2x₂ ≤ 20 }.
>
> (a) [8 marks] Define the ideal point of a bargaining problem and define the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. Compute the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution of (S, d), showing your working.
>
> (b) [7 marks] State the four axioms of the Kalai–Smorodinsky characterisation theorem. Identify which axiom of Nash's characterisation is *not* imposed on the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, explain what Kalai and Smorodinsky found objectionable about it, and explain what their replacement axiom requires.
>
> (c) [4 marks] Suppose player 1's utility function is replaced by u′₁ = 2u₁ − 4, with everything else unchanged. What does the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution recommend in the transformed problem? Justify your answer by appeal to an axiom, without redoing the geometry.
>
> (d) [6 marks] Does the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution of this problem coincide with the Nash solution? Establish your answer by computation, and say whether the coincidence is an artefact of this particular problem.

**The submitted answer.**

> (a) The ideal point (sometimes called the utopia point) of (S, d) is b = (b₁, b₂), where bᵢ is the most player i can hope for in any feasible agreement, that is, bᵢ = max{ xᵢ : x ∈ S }. Here the constraints give b₁ = 18, attained at (18, 1), and b₂ = 9, attained at (2, 9). Note that b is not itself feasible, since 18 + 2(9) = 36 > 20; it is an aspiration point rather than an available agreement.
>
> The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the Pareto-efficient point of S lying on the ray from d in the direction of b, i.e. the largest t such that d + t(b − d) ∈ S. Since b − d = (16, 8), points on the ray are (2 + 16t, 1 + 8t), and such a point lies on the frontier when (2 + 16t) + 2(1 + 8t) = 20, i.e. 4 + 32t = 20, so t = 1/2. Hence KS(S, d) = (10, 5), which is efficient and individually rational.
>
> (b) The characterisation uses four axioms.
> 1. *Pareto efficiency*: the selected point lies on the Pareto frontier of S.
> 2. *Symmetry*: if the problem is symmetric, both players receive the same payoff.
> 3. *Invariance to positive affine transformations*: if a player's utility is replaced by au + c with a > 0, the solution is transformed in the same way, so the recommendation does not depend on the arbitrary choice of zero point or unit in the utility scales.
> 4. *Monotonicity*: if the feasible set expands, the solution cannot make either player worse off, since additional options should never be a disadvantage.
>
> The axiom of Nash's that Kalai and Smorodinsky drop is independence of irrelevant alternatives. IIA requires that when alternatives are removed from S and the original solution point survives the removal, it must still be selected: alternatives that are never chosen cannot affect the outcome. Kalai and Smorodinsky object to this, arguing that the whole feasible set should bear on the settlement, because a player's bargaining position depends on the range of outcomes open to them and not only on the point finally agreed. Relatedly, under Nash's solution an enlargement of S can leave one player worse off than before, and IIA does not rule that out. Monotonicity is meant to capture the underlying intuition: what drives a settlement is each player's best achievable level, so if a player's opportunities improve, that player should not end up with less.
>
> (c) Under u′₁ = 2u₁ − 4 we have d′ = (0, 1), b′ = (32, 9), and the frontier becomes (u′₁ + 4)/2 + 2x₂ = 20. By axiom 3 the solution is simply the image of the old one: u′₁ = 2(10) − 4 = 16, so KS = (16, 5) in the new coordinates, representing exactly the same physical agreement as before. No recomputation is needed, since the axiom guarantees the correspondence.
>
> (d) Yes. The Nash solution maximises (x₁ − 2)(x₂ − 1) subject to x₁ + 2x₂ = 20. Substituting x₁ = 20 − 2x₂ gives (18 − 2x₂)(x₂ − 1), maximised at x₂ = 5, hence x₁ = 10 and a product of gains of 32. So the Nash solution is also (10, 5). The two coincide here because the Pareto frontier is a straight line: with a linear frontier, the equal-gains criterion defining KS and the product criterion defining Nash select the same point, namely the midpoint of the individually rational frontier segment between (18, 1) and (2, 9). With a strictly curved frontier the two criteria generally come apart.
