I'm coordinating a Friday-evening reading group at a teaching hospital that mixes neurology residents with a few philosophy graduate students, and this term's stretch is on higher-order theories of consciousness. The residents reliably bounce off the assigned papers, but they respond to cases, so I want to open the session with a piece of original short fiction — call it 2,200 to 2,800 words — that carries the argument the papers carry. I'd like the story to be genuinely good on its own terms: someone with no interest in the philosophy should read it as a tense clinical narrative and only notice afterward what it was doing to them.

The theory I want dramatized is David Rosenthal's higher-order thought version, and I'd rather state its machinery here than depend on the story getting it right by memory. Start from the transitivity principle: a mental state is a conscious state just in case one is, in some non-inferential and non-observational way, aware of being in it. Consciousness is then not an intrinsic property of the first-order state — not a glow the sensory state carries — but a matter of the higher-order representation that takes it as its object. That has the consequence the critics seize on: if a higher-order representation can exist without its target, the theory should still deliver a determinate experience, since on this view all the phenomenal work is being done by the higher-order content anyway. So there ought to be possible "empty" cases in which someone has a vivid experience of, say, a gray field with a red spot, while no first-order visual state of that kind exists at all — and the objection most closely associated with Ned Block is that this is either incoherent or vacuous, because nothing could make it the case that such an experience has the determinate character the theory assigns it. Running the other way, if you suppress only the higher-order component while leaving first-order processing fully intact, you should get a subject who can discriminate, guess above chance, and act on the stimulus while sincerely reporting nothing — with the further claim, which is the one my residents find hardest to swallow, that there is then nothing it is like for that subject at all. It's worth having the story brush against the dispositionalist alternative as well, on which there are no actual occurrent higher-order states but only states poised to feed a mind-reading faculty; that version is immune to the empty case, and pays for the immunity elsewhere.

For the setting, use this seed or something close to it. A university clinical research unit is running an early trial of a cortical stimulator aimed not at sensory cortex but at anterior prefrontal regions, on the rationale that restoring the higher-order component will restore report in patients with disorders of consciousness. Two patients anchor the story. In one, stimulation produces fluent, confident, detailed reports of percepts — a window, a nurse's green scrubs, a clock showing a particular time — while concurrent recordings show the relevant sensory areas are silent, and no such thing is present in the room in the way described. In the other, first-order processing is demonstrably fine (the patient's eyes track, startle responses discriminate, guessing is well above chance) and stimulation of the prefrontal target changes nothing reportable. The protagonist should be the neuropsychologist who has to write this up under pressure from a sponsor that wants a certification claim, and the story's turn should come when she realizes that her confidence about the first patient rests on exactly the kind of introspective authority the theory says cannot detect its own failures from the inside: no first-person check can distinguish the veridical case from the empty one. I want that realization to arrive through scenes — a debriefing transcript she keeps re-reading, a detail in the patient's description that could not have come from the room, her own hesitation at a moment when she is certain of what she sees — and never through a character explaining the debate; nobody may name a philosopher, cite a paper, or deliver a lecture. Keep the ending unresolved but pointed: the last thing the reader holds should be an image that makes them distrust a report they have already accepted, including possibly the narrator's own. No epigraphs, no footnotes, no section headings, and end on the image rather than on a summary of what it means.
