So — six weeks from now I'm giving a 25-minute talk with about 15 for questions at this annual interdisciplinary workshop on computation and coordination, and I want to walk in knowing exactly what's coming. The room is mostly ML people who work on large-scale allocation, online matching, RL for pricing and markets, plus two or three mechanism designers, four historians of economic thought who basically live in the interwar period, and one guy who is unapologetically Austrian and has read everything including the stuff I'm faking. I got the slot because the organizer is sympathetic to me, but my advisor thinks the paper is too philosophy-ish for the audience and honestly I think he's right, so I'd rather be over-prepped. It's a slightly weird venue: half the room's default position is that the socialist calculation debate got settled by Moore's law and is only interesting as history, and the other half thinks that attitude is the original sin.

The thesis is that the debate has been mis-framed on both sides, and that the mis-framing is why people keep saying Hayek was refuted by compute. Roughly: Mises's 1920 argument was that without genuine exchange in capital goods there's no common denominator for comparing heterogeneous uses, so the planner can't even state the choice; Lange answered with trial and error and a central board setting prices until excess demand cleared; Hayek's later answer was that the board would need data nobody has. My claim is that the interesting version of the knowledge problem isn't about how much dispersed information there is, and isn't about computational limits at all, but about which descriptions of the world are available to a decision-maker: an opportunity you can't articulate under any category you already possess isn't a big search space, it's an empty one, and no amount of optimization over a fixed representation fixes that. Which is why I say prices don't work because they aggregate — read them as an aggregator and you run straight into Grossman and Stiglitz's point that a perfectly informative price system could never pay anyone to produce information — they work because they license an agent to act on a locally generated category without having to translate it into anyone else's vocabulary. I also argue, and this is the bit I expect to bleed on, that Kantorovich's linear-programming valuations are no refutation of Mises, since the program takes the technology, the resource list and the output targets as given and therefore presupposes exactly the option set whose discovery was supposed to be the problem. And I explictly bracket the incentive question as a separate issue that my argument doesn't touch.
