I am in the second term of a graduate decision theory seminar and have been asked to run one session of our reading group on attitudes to risk. A recurring problem in the discussion is that three different people will each announce that they are risk-averse expected utility maximizers, and what they mean by it turns out to be three incompatible views. I would like to build a self-administered sorting instrument that establishes which position a person actually holds from what they choose, not from what they call themselves. I would be grateful for your help designing it, in the following order.

**1. The logical space.** Set out the axes that genuinely separate the positions in this literature, with a sentence or two of justification for each. Please consider at least these candidates: whether the risk attitude is a feature of the utility function over outcomes or of a weighting over probabilities and ranks; whether a decision is evaluated globally or event-wise, so that the verdict changes with how the state space is carved up; whether the attitude differs between a probability given by a described chance device, one inferred from a long frequency record, and one offered as a specialist's estimate; whether the utility function is bounded above; and whether the view permits an agent to change her mind partway through a decision she planned in advance. If you think one of my candidate axes is spurious — that it collapses into another, or that nothing of substance turns on it — say so and drop it rather than including it for completeness.

**2. The instrument.** Twelve items. Each is a concrete one-shot choice with explicit probabilities and explicit payoffs, and each permits exactly three responses: take the first option, take the second, or genuinely unable to choose. Six items should be scaled to a graduate student's real circumstances (a term's rent, a stipend supplement, a tuition bill) and six to stakes that are civilizational or existential, so that the boundedness axis can do work. At least three items, between them, should probe the described-chance / frequency / expert-estimate distinction without ever using the words ambiguity or uncertainty. No item may contain technical vocabulary: no rank-dependent, no convex, no sure-thing, no second-order. A first-year with no decision theory must be able to answer every item from the numbers alone.

**3. The leaves.** The taxonomy must terminate in named positions, and I want these among the leaves: expected utility with a concave utility function, i.e. risk aversion about money rather than about probabilities; mean–variance and other variance-penalizing views; rank-dependent or anticipated utility presented as a descriptive generalization; risk-weighted expected utility presented as a normative claim, evaluated globally; multiple-priors or maxmin views that are ambiguity-averse but risk-neutral at the first order; smooth ambiguity views that relocate the attitude to a higher-order belief; views on which some expectations simply fail to exist, so that the apparent risk attitude is really a repair for undefined sums; and views that treat the worst outcome as catastrophic and therefore effectively maximize the probability of avoiding it. Then identify at least four cells of the space that no one occupies, and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

**4. The scoring key.** Map answer patterns, not isolated answers, onto leaves. Tell me the minimal subset of four items that does most of the sorting, and give me an explicit protocol for inconsistent patterns — someone whose answers are intransitive across items — saying whether that counts as evidence of no view at all or of a view my tree cannot represent.

**5. A sequential appendix.** Three multi-stage problems, built by decomposing gambles already used above, whose only job is to separate the myopic, the sophisticated, the resolute, and the globally evaluating agent. State plainly which of these the twelve static items cannot distinguish.

**6. Non-separability.** Flag every pair of leaves that no static item can actually separate, and say why: representation-theoretic equivalence, dependence on introspection, or the need for outcomes that cannot be rendered numerically.

Finally, for each item, note in the key the single assumption whose denial would make the other answer rational, so that the key doubles as a teaching aid. I do not need a bibliography or a literature survey; I need the reasoning.
