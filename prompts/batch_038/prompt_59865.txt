I run a small team building a content-moderation system that doesn't use one model but an ensemble of seven of them. Each model sees the same post and returns a structured verdict: two premise judgments (does the post make an identifiable threat against a person, and is the target a minor) plus a final call, remove or keep. We aggregate by plain majority, field by field. Last week we hit a post where the premise majorities came out threat=yes and minor=yes, which under our own written policy means remove — but the majority on the final call said keep. So the ensemble, aggregated field by field, endorsed a decision its own premise votes rule out. I checked the pipeline three times before I believed it wasn't a bug.

Now I have to explain this to the engineers on the team in a 40-minute internal talk. None of them have had a logic course. Two of them will be privately convinced it's an off-by-one in the aggregation code, and one is definitely going to say we should just drop binary votes entirely and average confidence scores instead.

Plan the talk for me. I don't want the explanation written out — I want the sequencing. Specifically:

- What has to be in place before the divergence can even register as surprising to someone who's never looked at a formal argument, and in what order the pieces go, so that when it shows up it feels forced by the setup rather than like a card trick.
- One carrying example built on our moderation case (or something structurally identical), with the actual vote pattern across all seven voters written out. Pick the numbers so the arithmetic is trival to verify live on a slide, so no field is tied, and so the single table shows the whole problem without me needing a second example.
- The misconceptions I should expect and, for each, the exact spot in the sequence where I head it off. I'm predicting: "it's a bug," "just tie-break toward the premises," "make each model internally consistent and the problem disappears," and the confidence-score proposal. Tell me which of those are flat wrong, which are half right, and what the right half consists in — I don't want to squash something that turns out to be a real fix.
- What the talk should say about choosing between voting on premises and voting on conclusions, given that the choice moves real outcomes for real users and isn't just a question of which procedure is more logical. I want them to leave knowing we have to pick, that there's no neutral default, and roughly what shape the reasons on each side have.
- One thing I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say I'm skipping, and why it'd cost more than it's worth.
- A five-minute comprehension check at the end that's better than "any questions?" — ideally a variant case I put on the screen and have them predict the outcome of before we count votes. Give me that variant, and tell me what the wrong prediction sounds like so I can recognize it.

No citations and no paper titles, I'm not name-dropping the philosophy literature in a staff meeting.
