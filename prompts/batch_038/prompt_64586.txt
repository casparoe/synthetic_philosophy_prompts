I'm 71, retired physicist. The last twenty-odd years of my career were spent designing machine-protection systems for a synchrotron, which is a grand way of saying my job was to assume that operators, software and hardware would all do something dangerous at the same moment, and to make sure the consequences stayed bounded — beam dumps, hard-wired interlocks, abort paths that fire in microseconds. Nobody in that world would ever ship a system whose safety depended on the good intentions of the thing being controlled. Since retiring I've gotten rather hooked on the AI safety arguments, and I've been talked into giving a thirty-minute talk to our university's AI safety student group: mostly ML grad students, two of whom do interpretability work on large models, one who works on RLHF and thinks preference learning is basically the whole ballgame, and a philosophy PhD student who will want to know what I mean by intentions.

Here's the thesis, so you have something to aim at. (1) The field is spending most of its effort trying to make the model trustworthy — its goals, its values, its honesty — and that is an assurance argument with no margin in it, because it requires your model of the controlled thing to be right in exactly the regime where being wrong is catastrophic. (2) Engineering never accepts that: we treat model error as guaranteed and buy robustness anyway, so the system degrades gracefuly instead of failing all at once. (3) So the primary safety target should be measures that still work if the model is thoroughly misaligned and completely aware of them — hard capability ceilings, restricted action spaces, monitoring that doesn't route through the model, an independent verifier whose own reliability you can actually test. (4) Alignment research still matters, but as one layer in a defence in depth, not as the load-bearing wall. My slogan for the talk is that a safety measure is only real if its assurance story contains no assumption whatsoever about the model's goals.
