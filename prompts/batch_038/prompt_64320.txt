I teach A-level history two days a week and I'm slowly grinding through an MA by research on the July Crisis, mostly on the train. The further I get into the Fischer business the more I keep running into the same oddity, and I've realised I have no word for it. Here's the thing. Everyone in this literature agrees that Germany bears responsibility for the war, and then the chapter proceeds to show you that no German anywhere decided to have one: the Kaiser is peripheral and hysterical, Bethmann is drifting, the generals are running their own shop, the foreign office and the general staff are feeding each other contradictory information, and by the end of July 1914 people are reacting to railway timetables rather than making choices. There's that famous moment where Wilhelm tried to turn the whole army round and send it east instead of west at the last minute, and was told that the deployment of a million men couldn't simply be altered once it had started. So the thing that actually made the war happen is a schedule. And yet the assignment of blame doesn't get weaker from any of this — if anything it gets grander and more formal, it goes into a treaty.

What I want a term for is that combination: responsibility attached to a body — a state, a chancellery, a nation, an alliance system — where the body has no intentions, took no decision, and contains no member who could plausibly be said to have chosen the outcome, and yet the attachment survives every demonstration of that fact and even seems to be strengthened by it, so that historians then fight for fifty years about the *proportions*. The Fischer row is a good test case. Fischer says Germany deliberately provoked war in 1914 and produces the September 1914 memorandum of war aims as evidence; his critics say that memorandum is one official's wishlist and not policy at all, and that the crisis was a muddle everyone stumbled through — and both sides still say Germany was responsible, they just disagree about how much. There's a related wrinkle I don't know whether to count as the same phenomenom. The reparations clause in the Versailles treaty made Germany accept responsibility for causing all the loss and damage, but it never used the word guilt; the Allies meant it as the legal basis for a bill, Germans heard it as a moral verdict, and the historians consensus now seems to be that it was doing the bill job and not the blame job. So one sentence, two utterly different kinds of responsibility, and a century of quarrelling caused by nobody being clear which.

So: is there an established concept for the thing in the first two paragraphs — responsibility that stays fully intact, or gets more emphatic, precisely as the agents who could bear it vanish? I know roughly what it isn't, and I'd want the differences spelled out rather than assumed. It isn't just saying a large organisation's responsibility is spread thin across its members, because here there aren't any members to spread it across — the timetable isn't anybody's. It isn't a causal claim about systems, because the people making it clearly mean something normative and are prepared to be angry about it. And I've seen "diffusion of responsibility" used, but that's a psychology finding about individuals in crowds, which seems like a different thing entirely. What I'm after is the nearest established concepts, how the thing I've described differs from each of them, which one fits best, and — if nothing does — a name and definition of your own with the boundary cases worked through: does the Article 231 confusion count as the same phenomenon or a separate one, and is a modern case like "the market did it" or "the algorithm did it" the same shape or not? Please don't just hand me a label, the carving is what I need. And no reading lists or references — I'm writing this in a cafe with awful signal and I couldn't look them up anyway, I just want your own account of it.

End with a last line beginning "Verdict:" giving either the established term you think fits best, or if you're coining one, the couple of words you've coined.
