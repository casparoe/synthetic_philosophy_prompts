I sit on a hospital ethics committee and we have a standing policy on conscience objections that I've never been able to justify to myself. The shape of it (and its basically the same in most places, as far as I can tell): a clinician who objects on moral grounds to providing some lawful, indicated service — dispensing emergency contraception, say, or making an abortion referral — gets accommodated, but only if they hand the patient off to a colleague who will provide it. The objection gets them out of performing the act, not out of the patient's getting the act performed.

Here's what bugs me. Suppose instead the objecting clinician offered something else entirely: they'd take the overnight shifts nobody wants, or staff the rural clinic, or put a month's salary into a fund for the service they won't provide, or double their patient load for a year. On a straightforward accounting the institution comes out ahead — more total good gets done, and the patient is no worse off than under the referral arrangement. We would never except it. The swap has to be like-for-like: same duty, same patient, different hands.

So what theory of value, or of obligation, does that pattern actually presuppose? It looks to me like we're committed to the idea that duties are agent-relative and can't be paid off in some other currency — but I can't tell whether that's a real commitment or just institutional squeamishness about appearing to sell conscience exemptions. And the part I'd most like you to dig into: the policy is itself a trade in one sense, isn't it? We buy the objector's compliance with their own conscience using scheduling flexibility, which is a thing of value to them and a cost to their colleagues. If thats already a market in moral permissions, why does the currency have to be the duty itself?
