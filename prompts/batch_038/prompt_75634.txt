I want you to build me a pair of scenarios and then prove the pair actually works. Don't just tell me it works — show it.

Here's the thing I'm after. Everybody knows the US and most of continental Europe handle hate speech differently, but the usual telling is one-dimensional: Europe restricts more, America restricts less, pick your point on the line. I think thats wrong, or at least badly incomplete — I think the two systems are protecting different *objects*, so the restrictiveness ordering should cross over depending on what kind of speech you feed into them. I want a thought experiment that makes the crossover visible instead of arguing about it in the abstract.

So, design S1 and S2.

S1 = a speech act that a US-style First Amendment analysis comes out clearly lawful on, and that a typical continental European hate-speech framework comes out clearly punishable on.

S2 = the same situation with exactly one feature changed, where the verdicts flip both ways: now the American analysis doesn't protect it, and the European hate-speech provision doesn't reach it.

Hard constraints:
- No Holocaust denial, no cartoons of a prophet, no disinvited campus speaker, no march through a neighborhood of survivors. Those are the four examples everyone reaches for and I want none of them.
- Realistic and small-scale. An ordinary person, a small town, an ordinary medium — a leaflet, a Facebook group, three minutes at a council meeting. No state actors, no celebrities, no organized movements.
- Nobody gets hurt in either scenario. No violence occurs, nobody's injured, nothing gets smashed. The harm in play has to be whatever the speech itself does.
- One paragraph per scenario, and S2 has to be recognizably the *same situation* as S1, not a different story with the serial numbers filed off. Then name the single changed feature in one sentence.
- For S2 I want the clean version: the thing that costs the speaker protection in the US has to be a speech-specific doctrine (threat, incitement, that family of categories), not just "well, that conduct would be a crime anywhere."

Then the demonstration. Run each scenario through each framework, give the verdict, and say why — describe the doctrinal tests in your own words. If you name a case or a statute, only do it if you're genuinely confident it says what you're claiming; otherwise describe the test and move on, I don't need the citations. And don't treat "Europe" as one country: say what's common to the continental approach and flag roughly where it varies.

Then the checks, which is the part I actually care about:

1. Which feature is doing the work in each flip? What happens if you change it back, or if you add a second feature — does the pair stay a single-variable comparison or does it get muddy?
2. Be honest about residue. In S2, is the speaker still prosecutable in Europe under ordinary criminal law even though the hate-speech provision doesn't reach it? If yes, does that wreck the flip or just relocate it? Pick one and defend it.
3. Give me the two attacks. How would a committed American civil libertarian say this pair is rigged, and how would a European theorist who thinks group dignity is a genuine legal interest say the same thing? Neither should sound stupid — I want the strongest version of each complaint about my design.
4. Finish by saying whether "level of free speech protection" is one scale that countries sit at different points on, or two different scales that only look like one. One paragraph, and commit to an answer instead of hedging.

Aim it at smart people who've never read a law journal. And look — if you conclude a fully clean single-feature pair is impossible under my constraints, say so, and give me the closest achievable version plus exactly which constraint is causing the trouble.
