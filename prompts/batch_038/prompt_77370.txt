I'm co-running our department's logic-and-language reading group this term and we've spent the last three weeks on Lewis's triviality results — the original paper and the sequel. It's gone sideways. Half the room is philosophy people, half are folks from the probabilistic-reasoning group down the hall who wandered in because they thought "the probability of 'if A, C' should just be the probability of C given A" was an obvious design goal for anything that reasons under uncertainty (they call it the Adams thesis and think it's settled). Now the two camps are talking past each other: the philosophers say any connective satisfying that identity for *every* probability function collapses into triviality, so the identity can't be a semantic thesis at all; the ML people say fine, then just fix one distribution and be done with it, and nobody in the room can articulate why thats a cheat. Three sessions in and we still don't agree on what would count as a good answer, which makes it impossible to evaluate the answers actually on offer.

So for week four I want to hand out a one-page spec instead of another argument. Act as a philosopher of language who has genuinely worked on conditionals, not someone summarizing an encyclopedia entry, and think it through carefully — I want you to be willing to tell me that some of what we've been fighting about isn't a real requirement. Where two of them cannot be jointly satisfied, mark the pair explicitly — that's the part of the handout I most want, because it would explain why the room's been going in circles.

Here's what I've got from our arguments so far, and I've definately got the wrong end of the stick on some of these. Be blunt about which entries are confused, redundant, or question-begging, and add whatever's missing:

1. The identity P(A→C) = P(C|A) holds for every probability function a rational agent might have, not just special ones.
2. If you come to be certain of A, your new degree of belief in A→C should equal your old P(C|A) — updating should be gentle in exactly that sense.
3. The conditional embeds: you can negate it, disjoin it, and put conditionals in the antecedents of other conditionals, and all of that gets an interpretation instead of being ruled out as ill-formed.
4. Modus ponens is unrestrictedly valid for it.
5. Indicatives and subjunctives are the same connective in different epistemic settings, so a good account generalizes to counterfactuals for free.
6. It explains why asserting "if A, C" sounds weird when you already know A, or already know not-A, and it doesn't just wave that off as pragmatics.
7. It allows you to be more confident in A→C than in either A or C, and explains how that's possible.
8. It delivers truth conditions — the conditional expresses a proposition with a truth value, so ordinary compositional semantics applies.
9. Two conditionals sharing an antecedent but with contradictory consequents can't both be acceptable on the same evidence.
10. It doesn't require a similarity metric over worlds, since that's the part everyone finds mysterious.

Then, briefly: which single one of these knocks out the most of the currently popular accounts, and what would you tell the probabilistic-reasoning people to build instead, given that the unrestricted identity is off the table?
