I'm marking third-year philosophy of biology essays and haven't touched this one yet, so I don't want to steer you with my own read of it. The question was:

"'Species are individuals, not kinds.' Explain the argument for this claim and assess it, paying particular attention to what it implies for organisms that don't reproduce sexually."

Here's the core of the student's answer, condensed but I've kept their wording and their order of argument:

"Mayr's biological species concept says a species is a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. That works for a lot of animals, but it can't handle bacteria, which reproduce clonally, and it's under pressure even in eukaryotes, since hybridisation between perfectly 'good' species is common in plants.

Ghiselin and Hull's response is that species aren't classes at all but individuals: a species is one spatiotemporally located thing, with organisms as its parts, which comes into existence at speciation and goes out of existence at extinction. It follows that species can't be natural kinds, because a kind is picked out by properties shared by its members and there are no such properties here — belonging to Homo sapiens isn't a matter of having an essence, it's a matter of being hooked up to the right bit of history. That also explains why the species problem has looked so intractable: everyone kept asking for the defining criteria of a kind when the real question was what holds a lineage together.

This looks like it handles the asexual case too. If a species is an individual, nothing requires its parts to interbreed; what's required is that they be parts of the same continuing lineage. De Queiroz's unified concept makes it precise: a species is a separately evolving metapopulation lineage, and the other concepts — reproductive isolation, monophyly, ecological distinctness — are just different ways of recognising a lineage once it has acquired the property in question. So bacteria can have species in the same sense animals do.

The residual problem is that 'individual' is doing all the work, and the only account on offer of what makes a bunch of organisms one individual rather than many is reproductive isolation, which is exactly what's missing in the asexual case. So the individuality thesis works for sexual organisms, and for the rest of the tree of life species are conveniences we impose. Bacterial taxonomy bears this out: it falls back on arbitrary similarity cutoffs and makes no claim to have found anything real."
