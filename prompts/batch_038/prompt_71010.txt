Got dragged into this by a friend who thinks he's dissolved the whole "does my individual action even matter" problem, and I can't tell whether he's found a real argument or just talked himself out of recycling. Here's the chain the way he gave it to me, numbered so we can point at things:

(1) An act is wrong only if it makes things worse — only if, had you not done it, someone would have ended up better off.
(2) The harms everyone argues about — emissions, factory farming, and for that matter elections — are produced by very many people each doing one small thing.
(3) Take any one of my small things out. Either somebody else's identical act fills the gap, or the total is still over whatever line the harm needed. Either way the same harm happens, to the same people, in the same amount.
(4) So none of my small things makes anything worse.
(5) So none of my small things is wrong.
(6) Notice nothing in (1)–(5) was special to climate or meat or voting. It goes through for any jointly produced harm, and for any way of describing my act on which it isn't the one that tips the balance.
(7) So run it on Parfit's thousand torturers: each one presses a button that clicks every one of a thousand instruments up a notch, no single press makes any victim's pain perceptibly worse, and the victims are in agony whichever press you delete. Each torturer is clean by (1)–(5).
(8) But those torturers are each doing something wrong if anybody ever has.
(9) Therefore (1) is false — wrongness can't depend on making things worse.

He likes (9) because he's some kind of deontologist and thinks he's landed a free hit. I'm not: I'm a pretty vanilla consequentialist and (1) is load bearing for basically everything else I believe, so I'd rather keep (1) and give up the exoneration in (5) if I'm forced to choose. But I don't know what that costs, because if (5) fails I seem to be committed to saying my conference flight was wrong, and I don't want to say that either.

So: which step actually breaks, and why did it look fine on the way past? Then price the exits for me — keeping (1) and denying (3), keeping (1) and denying (4), giving up (1), and giving up (6)–(7) on the grounds that the torturers aren't really the same case as the flight. And don't let me off easy on the "fine, just accept that your individual acts are wrong" option; I want to know what that obliges me to actually do differently, not just what it obliges me to believe.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step, one clause, and say which way out you'd take — then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the argument back at me.
