So my department runs a tiny internal grant scheme, and last spring the five-person panel produced a result people are still arguing about. The written rule was: fund the proposal iff (P) the science is sound, (Q) it fits the budget line its competing under, and (R) nobody on the project team has an unresolved conflict of interest. All three had to come out yes. The five members each voted yes on exactly two of the three:

- A: P yes, Q yes, R no
- B: P yes, Q no, R yes
- C: P no, Q yes, R yes
- D: P yes, Q yes, R no
- E: P yes, Q no, R yes

Tally the premises and you get P 4–1, Q 3–2, R 3–2, so the conjunction says fund. Tally the conclusion directly and you get 0–5 against. Both are "what the panel decided" in some sense, and the panel had to actually write a letter.

My officemate Nico turned this into an argument, and it ends somewhere I can't follow. Here it is, roughly as he gave it:

1. A group that's supposed to have beliefs at all can't accept P, Q and R while rejecting P∧Q∧R. Its judgment set has to be consistent and closed under what it entails, same as anyone's.
2. So when the propositions are logically connected, the group has to aggregate premise-first: majority on each premise, then derive the conclusion. Anything else licenses incoherence.
3. Premise-first in the panel case outputs "fund," and every one of the five members said "don't fund." So the group's judgment contradicts the unanimous judgment of its members.
4. Nothing about this panel is special. Any conjunctive rule with enough members can do it, and the impossibility results for judgment aggregation say you can't have consistency-and-closure plus unanimity-respect plus anonymity plus systematicity without collapsing into dictatorship. The tension is structural.
5. A subject's beliefs are that subject's beliefs in virtue of something about the subject — its dispositions, its commitments, what it would assert and act on.
6. In (3) there's nothing about the group grounding "the panel believes this should be funded" except the output of a rule we picked. No member is disposed to assert it, and the group has no dispositions of its own over and above whatever its members have.
7. So the group's belief isn't a belief. It's bookkeeping — no more real than saying the roulette wheel believes in red.
8. But run the same test one level down. What I believe is fixed by my dispositions too, and those dispositions are an aggregate of states that can jointly commit me to things I'd deny: I believe each premise of an argument while denying its conclusion; I believed p on Monday's evidence and not-p on Friday's, and my "current belief" is some weighted settlement of that. My disposition-set is no more coherent than the panel's premise-majorities.
9. So if (5)–(7) rule out the panel's belief, they rule out mine, and I'm a committee with a voting rule. Since that's absurd, something above has to go — but every step still looks true to me, which is why I'm asking.

I'd rather hold onto (1)–(4) than (5)–(7), honestly, because the panel did produce that result and it did have to send a letter. But I don't know if that's a reason or just a preference.
