Okay so I got into a two hour argument with my friend Ruth last night about Tolstoy's What Is Art? and I'm still not settled on it, but I have to actually settle it because I edit a small nonprofit literary magazine and I've been asked to write a screening rubric for our volunteer first-pass readers. Ruth's position is that Tolstoy's whole test is useless in practice — that "does it infect you" is just "did you cry" with a halo on it, and that the moment you add the bit about feelings that unite people you've stopped doing criticism and started preaching. Mine is that its actually more workable than the craft-based language we use now ("pacing," "voice," "earned"), because at least Tolstoy is asking who caught what feeling from whom. I read the book maybe six years ago so I'm reconstructing from memory, and I'm pretty sure I've softened him into something more palatable than he was — my recollection is: art is when someone reawakens in themselves a feeling they once had and hands it over by external signs so other people catch it; the strength of the infection depends on three things, how particular the feeling is, how clearly it gets across, and how sincerely the artist is actually feeling it while making the thing; sincerity is the one that matters most and the one professional art most lacks; then separately there's the content test, where the best art transmits feelings available to everyone regardless of class or sect — the brotherhood stuff — and the worst is partisan, cultivated for a small in-group that shares the codes; and there's the list of counterfeit markers, which I remember as borrowing (stock imagery, moonlight and nightingales and hermits), imitation (descriptive padding in place of feeling), effectfulness (cheap wow-moments), and diversion (mere amusement). Please correct me wherever I've gotten him wrong or watered him down before you build anything on top of it.

What I need is a decision procedure my readers can actually run on a submission in fifteen minutes with no theory background, and I have about 1,200 stories a year for eight slots per issue. Our board chair is a retired literature professor who will fight me over anything that looks like it's downgrading polished work, and we just took a grant to publish writers from a community program — people who have never taken a workshop and write with terrible tense control about things that clearly happened to them. So the rubric has to be honest about which of those two it's going to hurt. Give me the questions in the order a reader should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exact points where the procedure stops with a reject, and the points where it has to hand the call up to a human because the remaining question is about values and not about facts. Also tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire — I already suspect sincerity is unfalsifiable from the text alone and that a good faker can manufacture it, and I suspect the content criterion will do something nasty to a genuinely infectious mean-spirited story — and say what the procedure cannot do that my current informal process does. Don't send me a reading list or citations, I'm not going to have a chance to chase anything down; just build the thing out of the theory itself.

Then run it on three real cases we have on the desk right now. (1) A 9,000-word story in the voice of a famous dead Russian, immaculate, our two strongest readers both called it "flawless" and then, separately, admitted they finished it and felt nothing — this is the one our board chair is championing. (2) A three-page untitled fragment from the community program, a woman writing about her brother's overdose, misspelled, no scene breaks, and our readers have not stopped talking about it since Tuesday. (3) A satirical story about a local developer that is brutally, hilariously infectious — people laughed out loud in the reading group — but the feeling it transmits is contempt for a specific named person and his neighbors, and half our subscribers are those neighbors. At least one of these should come out the way I don't want it to; if the rubric says yes to all three, it's not a rubric, it's me. For format: put the procedure itself in a table with the columns step_number, question_the_reader_asks, what_a_pass_looks_like, what_a_fail_looks_like, and fact_or_judgment (and if judgment, who makes the call). Then give the three cases as a JSON array where each object has case, verdict (accept / reject / borderline), decisive_step, tolstoyan_rationale, strongest_objection_from_the_chair, and my_reply. Finally add one JSON object called limits with the fields unfalsifiable_elements, what_the_rubric_will_systematically_kill, and what_it_cannot_replace_in_my_current_process.
