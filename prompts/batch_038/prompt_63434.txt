Six weeks ago I was elected to the coordinating council of a federation of fourteen worker-owned machine shops, and the first substantive item on our agenda is how to allocate two permanently oversubscribed resources: hours on roughly thirty five-axis machining centers, and time from a shared pool of eleven CAM programmers. I am the only member with formal economics training, so the council has asked me to bring forward a proposal in the spring. Before I design anything, I want the diagnosis right, and I want it in the terms of the socialist calculation controversy — not as history, but as a live framework for our problem.

The setting, in detail. Every job is made-to-order tooling; no two are alike, so there is no repeatable product whose price could be observed even internally. Demand arrives as customer requests for quotation with deadlines attached, and it is lumpy. The federation jointly owns an engineering library, a set of machine post-processors, and a common CAM seat license; these are non-rival, and the marginal cost of one more shop using them is effectively zero. Machine capacity and programmer time are rival, and three shops are currently queueing weeks for the machines. Three of the fourteen members do occasional outside work at arm's length, which gives us a handful of real observations on what machine time fetches, but the volume is far too thin to price anything.

I am stipulating the following vocabulary for this exercise. Treat the four failure modes as exhaustive and as roughly independent, and tell me which of them actually binds here:

- F1, aggregation failure: the relevant information is dispersed, partly tacit, or too costly to transmit to whoever must decide; the defect is a communications problem, in principle curable by better channels.
- F2, commensuration failure: even granting perfect information and perfectly cooperative agents, there is no defensible common denominator in which competing uses can be compared, so the question of whether this job is worth displacing that one has no determinate answer.
- F3, discovery failure: the mechanism allocates competently among options already on the table but generates no new options — no revision of what to make, how to make it, or whom to make it for.
- F4, compliance failure: the mechanism depends on members reporting truthfully or following directives while their own reasons point the other way.

Three tasks.

1. Distribute 1.00 across F1 through F4 as the share of our allocative difficulty attributable to each. Commit to specific numbers; do not hedge by putting 0.25 on everything. Name the single feature of the description above that does most of the work for your largest share, and the feature whose removal would most change the distribution. Then, in a sentence each, say which failure mode you take Mises, Hayek, and Lange to have been chiefly worried about, and whether our problem sits on that same axis or a different one.

2. Sort the following six candidate mechanisms into tiers S through D, judged against this federation's problem rather than in the abstract, with one or two sentences on why each lands where it does: (a) a standing allocation committee that receives job requests and assigns capacity by a published priority score; (b) an internally administered transfer price per machine hour and per programmer hour, set by this council, revised quarterly, with members treated as price-takers and required to justify overuse; (c) a quarterly sealed-bid internal auction for blocks of capacity, bids denominated in the federation's internal credit, with clearing revenue rebated to all members; (d) unmediated bargaining between members, using a common unit of account but with no central price-setter and no auction; (e) a queue with a published waitlist, plus permission for members to trade or sell their positions in it; (f) a central optimization, in which members submit routings, process times, and due dates, the council solves the capacity-constrained scheduling problem, and the shadow prices read off the solution are published as information rather than issued as directives.

3. Two closing checks. First, a counterfactual: if the engineering library and the CAM seats were instead owned by an outside licensor charging per-seat royalties, which of your placements would move, and in which direction? Second, identify the tier assignment in part 2 you are least confident about and state the fact about our federation — one you would want me to go and find out — that would settle it.

Assume I know the canonical positions and the standard verdicts on them; I am not asking for a survey, a bibliography, or citations, and I would rather you reason from the structure of the case than from authority. Roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words.
