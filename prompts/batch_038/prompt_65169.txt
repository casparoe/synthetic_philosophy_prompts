I've been reading Overcoming Bias and LessWrong since close to the beginning, and for the last few years I've been part of a small decision-theory reading group that meets in the back room of a coworking space — mostly people who came to the material through the sequences and stayed for the arguments. Two of the regulars are, functionally, the group's permanent opposition: Mira, who works as a data scientist and has an applied statistics background, and Dev, who got about two years into a mathematics PhD before leaving for industry and never quite lost the habit of asking whether a quantity has been shown to exist before anyone computes with it. Last month we spent an evening on the Pasadena game — a fair coin flipped until it lands heads, paying (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars if heads first appears on flip n, so +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, and so on. Because each outcome has probability 2^-n, the terms of the expectation series are just ±1/n, and the series is the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2, about 69 cents — but it converges only conditionally, since the absolute values sum to the harmonic series, which diverges. The two of them went at it for an hour, the rest of us took notes, and at the end they both asked me to referee. I don't have a horse in this race and I'm not going to pretend I do: I want an outside adjudication. Here is the exchange, more or less as it happened.

(1) Mira: The ticket is worth 69 cents. The expectation series sums to ln 2, that is the expectation, and there is nothing further to say. I'll sell you one for a dollar or buy one for sixty cents, and anyone who declines to put a price on it is substituting squeamishness about infinities for arithmetic.

(2) Dev: The expectation does not exist. Conditional convergence means that by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the terms can be reordered to sum to any real number you please, or to diverge outright. A quantity whose value depends on the order in which you happen to write the terms down is not a quantity. Expectation is defined as an integral, and the integral exists only when the absolute values integrate to something finite; here they sum to the harmonic series. So the game is not worth 69 cents. It is not worth anything.

(3) Mira: The ordering is not one I happen to choose; it is fixed by the rules. The payoff attached to "heads first appears on flip n" is part of the description of the game, and I am summing over the game's own outcomes in the only enumeration the game supplies. Your counterexample — the Altadena variant that redistributes the same prizes across the flips so that its series is a rearrangement of mine — is simply a different game with a different payoff function. All you have shown is that two different games can have different values, which nobody disputes.

(4) Dev: That is the question at issue, not something you get to wave off. Expected value is supposed to be a feature of the prize structure — which prizes, at which probabilities. Your canonical enumeration is a rule for selecting a number, and you have offered no independent justification for it. If your answer changes when I re-pair the same prizes with the same probabilities while altering neither, then the number you were reporting was never tracking the gamble; it was tracking your bookkeeping. And the bookkeeping rule is doing substantive work: it is what licenses you to call one arrangement ln 2 and another something else, with no account of why the first pairing is the honest one.

(5) Mira: Grant the integral is undefined. It does not follow that the decision is. Play the game many times and the average payoff per play converges in probability to ln 2: the strong law fails, but the weak law survives, because the truncated means settle down. So there is a defensible notion of value available — the number the running average converges to — and it agrees with the number the series gives, and with what Abel or Cesàro summation gives. Every summation method that does not involve deliberately reshuffling the game lands on ln 2. That is not an artifact of one notation; that is independent methods converging on the same answer.

(6) Dev: Three problems. First, the weak-law route smuggles in the notion it is meant to replace: convergence in probability is proved by truncating payoffs at a growing cutoff, so you are reading the value off a limiting procedure rather than off the gamble, and the procedure is exactly the thing in question. Second, it changes the subject. I am being offered one ticket, not a long run of them, and if the strong law fails there is no fact about where my average settles; convergence in probability is not a guide for the single case, and the single case is what decision theory was built for. Third, it does not generalize: weak expectation exists for this game by luck, and there are unbounded games — St. Petersburg is the obvious one — where it fails too, so the proposal does not cover the class of problems that generated the paradox.

(7) Mira: Two things back. First, "undefined" is a verdict about a formalism, not about the gamble. You have shown that the textbook construction fails to output a number; you have not shown that adopting ln 2 as the price is inconsistent with anything else a decision theorist wants — no Dutch book, no dominance violation, no incoherent preference ordering. Absent that, declaring the question meaningless is definition-mongering dressed as rigor. Second, incomparability is expensive. If you will not rank the Pasadena ticket against a guaranteed sixty cents, you cannot rank it against anything, and an agent with no reservation price is an agent that any counterparty who does have one can walk all over in a trade. Your honesty about underdetermination purchases a standing vulnerability.

(8) Dev: Then notice what your first point implies. If the standard axioms really do not rule out ln 2, they do not rule out ln 2 + 4 either, or −7, or any finite number you like, because none of them ever gets a grip on a gamble whose prizes are unbounded in both directions — and permissiveness that wide is not a defense of your price, it is an indictment of machinery that fails to deliver one. And your capping move, which you gestured at earlier, concedes the ground: if every gamble anyone can actually face has a well-defined expectation because real prizes are bounded, then the Pasadena game is precisely the unbounded fiction I said it was, and what remains is the question of what a theory of unbounded value should do. That is not academic for this group. Unbounded payoffs show up in Pascal's-mugging-style offers and in any attempt to aggregate value across an open-ended future, and both have exactly the structure that is causing trouble here — infinitely many states, prizes growing without bound, and a total that depends on how you group the terms.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than declaring a winner in the abstract. For each of the eight moves, assign a rating on a five-point scale — clearly mistaken, weak, roughly on target, strong, clearly right — and justify each rating in a sentence or two. Where a move contains a substantive error, say whether the error is mathematical (a false claim about the series, the integral, or the limit theorems) or decision-theoretic (a false claim about what the axioms require, permit, or rule out), since those are not the same failure and I want them separated; where a move is merely unpersuasive rather than wrong, say so explicitly. Then take up the specific contested questions one at a time and tell me which of them is right on each: does the Pasadena game have an expected value; is conditional convergence fatal, or is it defused by the claim that the enumeration is fixed by the rules; does the weak-law/long-run move yield a price usable for a single decision; does the observation that real prizes are bounded make the whole dispute moot; and is refusing to price a gamble itself a defect, as Mira claims in move 7. After that, say who is more right overall and by how much — I want a comparative judgment, not a plague-on-both-houses. Finally, tell me what neither of them reached for: some distinction, some result about what the standard axioms do and do not determine for gambles of this kind, or some reframing of the question that would settle or dissolve the dispute rather than continue it, and say whether whatever you'd add changes the verdict. One constraint: I am not asking for references, reading lists, or attributions, and I would rather you not guess at them — give me the reasoning, and if a claim needs a name attached to be believed, flag it as something I should check rather than supplying a citation I can't verify.
