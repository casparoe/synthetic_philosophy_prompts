I am a conservation biologist who now sits on a panel that reviews funding proposals touching on the welfare of wild animals, and I have been trying to write down the doctrine my own judgments actually follow. What I have instead is a list of verdicts that I suspect are not all consistent. Each concerns a proposal of the kind my panel sees or could plausibly see. My verdicts:

1. Emergency water and fodder for an elephant population in a drought that our own extraction helped cause: approve, and I think we owe it.
2. The identical provision for an elephant population in a drought that owes nothing to us: I would approve it if it were cheap, but I do not think it is owed.
3. Removing a dam that blocks fish migration: approve enthusiastically, even though the restored spawning access means many more fry hatched into a river in which most of them die.
4. Oral vaccination of a wild rabbit population against a pathogen we introduced: approve. The same vaccine against a pathogen the rabbits have always carried, at comparable cost and comparable certainty: I hesitate, and my hesitation seems to track what it would do to the population's size rather than to its suffering.
5. Extirpating a host-specific parasitic botfly where nothing else depends on it: approve.
6. Suppressing an apex predator population by two thirds on modelling I accept as sound, which lowers aggregate suffering among its prey: refuse, even though the model behind it is better than the models behind several proposals I approve.
7. Reintroducing that same apex predator to a landscape from which we exterminated it, knowing the reintroduction multiplies painful deaths: approve, and I would campaign for it.
8. A gene drive suppressing a commensal rodent on an island, expected to prevent vastly more suffering than any of the above, practically irreversible, untested at scale: refuse, whatever the expected-value arithmetic says. Funding the contained research that would eventually make it testable: approve.
9. A standing program that humanely kills every large mammal in a park that sensors identify as starving or mortally injured: this is the one I keep revising. It plainly reduces aggregate suffering; it also converts park management into a continuous killing operation whose error rate I cannot estimate, and I suspect my refusal rests on that rather than on the welfare arithmetic.
10. A habitat modification that reduces the number of insects hatched into lives that almost certainly end within days, at essentially no cost to anything else: approve. A proposal to reduce insect biomass generally on the grounds that most insect lives are net negative: refuse.
11. Reintroducing a rare raptor to part of its former range, though its recovery increases predation on other birds: approve. Establishing a population of the same raptor where it has never lived, to raise a biodiversity score: refuse.

My own tentative rule is that we should intervene where we caused the problem and never intervene to change a population's size. Cases 5, 7, and 10 break that immediately, so it is not what I am running.

The pairs I can see are in trouble: 3 against 10, since both increase the number of animals living short bad lives and I welcome one; 6 against 7, since the same animals and the same death rate get opposite verdicts; 4 against 5, since both are welfare-motivated interventions against an endemic harm-doer and I approve only one; 7 against 11; and 6 against 8, where I refuse a well-modelled local intervention and a poorly-modelled global one, apparently for different reasons.

What I want from you:

(a) one or two candidate principles, stated as decision rules precise enough to be applied by someone who does not share my intuitions;
(b) for each clause of each, the case above that forces it;
(c) a judgment on whether the principle that saves all eleven is so clause-laden that it ceases to be a principle, and if it is, the simplest coherent version together with an explicit statement of which verdicts it costs me and why those rather than others;
(d) its ruling on three cases I have not listed: a contraceptive bait and a lethal cull that produce the same population trajectory over the same number of years; relieving suffering inflicted by a species we introduced two centuries ago and no longer control; and, at equal cost, choosing between improving the lives of a long-lived, slowly reproducing species and stabilizing the population of a short-lived, explosively reproducing one;
(e) an honest account of what your principle cannot accommodate in my verdicts, as opposed to what it merely reinterprets.

Finally, say where the resulting rule sits relative to the standing positions in this debate. I do not want names, references, or a reading list, only which familiar view it most resembles, where it departs from that view, and whether the departure is a refinement or a rejection.
