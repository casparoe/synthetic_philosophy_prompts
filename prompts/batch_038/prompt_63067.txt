I am in my final year of secondary school and am writing a paper for my school's philosophy review on whether conscious experience has a single place at which it is assembled and inspected. My teacher pointed me toward Charles S. Peirce's essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," the second of his Illustrations of the Logic of Science, published in Popular Science Monthly in January 1878. I have reached a passage in the opening section that I cannot interpret with any confidence, and my whole argument turns on how it is read. Peirce is describing what he takes to be Descartes's criterion of truth:

"Self-consciousness was to furnish us with our fundamental truths, and to decide what was agreeable to reason. … The distinction between an idea seeming clear and really being so, never occurred to him. Trusting to introspection, as he did, even for a knowledge of external things, why should he question its testimony in respect to the contents of his own mind?"

Earlier in the same section, discussing the logicians' first grade of clearness, Peirce says that familiarity with an idea "only amounts to a subjective feeling of mastery which may be entirely mistaken." Later, criticizing Leibniz, he says that Leibniz "did not understand that the machinery of the mind can only transform knowledge, but never originate it, unless it be fed by facts of observation."

My questions are these.

First, in the sentence beginning "Trusting to introspection," is Peirce stating a view of his own — that introspection's testimony about the contents of one's own mind is simply not to be questioned — or is he reconstructing Descartes's reasoning from the inside so that the sentence carries an unstated criticism? The two readings pull in opposite directions for my purposes, and I cannot tell from the passage alone which is correct. What in the surrounding sentences decides it?

Second, what does it mean to say that a distinction "never occurred to" Descartes? Descartes's project begins in radical doubt, and the possibility that an idea merely seems clear seems to be exactly what that doubt is designed to expose. So what is the specific distinction Peirce believes Descartes overlooked, and how does it differ from the ordinary skeptical worry?

Third, is the remark about "a subjective feeling of mastery which may be entirely mistaken" a psychological claim (that people are in fact overconfident about ideas they know well) or a normative one (that such a feeling does not count as evidence at all)? I want to know whether the difference matters for what follows.

Fourth, and most importantly, I need my central thesis tested. I am comparing Peirce's criticism with the position associated with Daniel Dennett that denies what he calls a Cartesian theater: the picture on which sensations, memories, and inner speech converge on a stage in the brain to form one finished sequence that is then presented to an inner witness. The alternative multiple drafts model denies that there is any such finished sequence or any single moment at which experience is all there; parallel processes carry on, and what we treat as a report of our own experience is a construction produced afterward rather than a reading of something already assembled. My thesis is that the denial of the Cartesian theater is Peirce's objection to Descartes relocated from epistemology to the philosophy of mind: both refuse to allow a felt quality — for Peirce the feeling that an idea is clear, for the multiple drafts theorist the vivid presence of a unified experience — to establish that something is really there. Does that analogy survive question one? If the sentence about introspection does express Peirce's own commitment, he appears to grant authoritative access to the contents of one's own mind, which resembles the inner-witness picture the theater metaphor describes, and my comparison collapses. I would like you to tell me plainly whether it collapses, and if not, how the analogy should be stated so that it holds.

Fifth, does the remark about the machinery of the mind commit Peirce to holding that nothing about experience can be learned by attending to it, or is it a claim about where concepts originate rather than about the reliability of self-observation? I ask because my comparison needs Peirce to have something to put in place of the feeling of mastery, and I suspect it is his rule, illustrated later in the essay by the example of what we mean in calling a thing hard, that the whole of a conception's meaning lies in the effects we conceive it to have. But those conceived effects are counterfactual — how the thing would behave if tested — and I cannot work out whether such counterfactuals can do the work Peirce wants without reintroducing the very problem he began with, since the question of what we would conceive seems to be settled by, among other things, how clear things feel to us.

I am not looking for references or further reading; I have the essay. What I need is a close reading of the passage and a candid assessment of the thesis, including the places where you think it fails.
