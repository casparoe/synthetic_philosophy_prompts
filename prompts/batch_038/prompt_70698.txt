I'm on the committee of a Thai-heritage Theravāda temple up north and I'm the one who sorts out the giving side of things — the morning almsfood rota, the big festival days, the appeals. There's something about how our giving works that's been nagging me for years and I can't tell whether it's a real tension or whether I'm just thinking about it wrong. Can you help me work out what the practice actually takes for granted? No reading list or references please, I just want the reconstruction.

The practice, as concretely as I can put it. Every morning a few of us take food to the monks. On festival days the whole congregation queues up to put rice, fruit, candles, money and new robes into the monks' hands, and afterwards there's chanting and we dedicate the merit to our dead relatives. People keep a rough mental account of all this — my neighbour's late mother had an enormous merit balance becuase she fed the monks every week for forty years. When the temple needed a new roof, the appeal barely mentioned the building or the benefit to the community at all; it was all about the merit on offer, and the largest donors had their names read out and dedicated at the ceremony. And it is completely unremarkable here to be told that a small gift to a virtuous monk is worth more than a large gift to the man begging at the gate. We do give to the beggar, nobody thinks that's wrong, but nobody would call it the better use of the money. The words we chant every morning describe the community of monks as the best field in the world for planting merit.

Meanwhile the same people, in the same breath, say that compassion for all beings is the whole point, and that nibanna isn't some good state of affairs you're trying to bring about — it's the way out of the whole business of bringing states of affairs about.

So, what I want from you:

What theory of value does the first half of that presuppose, and what does it strictly presuppose as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with? I keep circling between three readings and can't settle it: that merit isn't about welfare at all; that it is about welfare, but welfare in a future life that nobody here can measure or compare; or that the whole thing is really a funding mechanism for keeping a teaching institution alive and the value talk is doing something else entirely.

Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out? In particular the version of Buddhist ethics my niece's ethics tutor teaches — right action is whatever best reduces the suffering of all sentient beings, counted impartially. If that were the real content of the tradition, our giving calendar looks upside down to me. Is it? Tell me what would have to change on the ground if we took that version seriously, and separately what would have to change if we took the funding-mechanism reading seriously instead.

Is any of this internally inconsistent, or can one and the same practice hold both halves — ranking gifts by the recipient's virtue, and the impartial-compassion talk — without contradicting itself? And where is the practice simply not deciding anything: where would two quite different theories of value license exactly the same behaviour, so that arguing about which is right changes nothing in the hall on a festival morning?

Last thing. I'm also the one who costed the roof appeal, so I do think in terms of where money does most good. Is there a defensible version of the recipient-ranking that I could hold onto without giving that up, or does one of the two have to go?

British English please.
