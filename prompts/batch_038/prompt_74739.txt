Here's the reading I'm groping toward, and I want you to build it out and pressure-test it rather than just agree. The narrator's reasoning looks to me like it straddles the SSA/SIA divide. When he argues that because the Library contains every possible combination of its 25 characters it must therefore contain the total catalogue, the biography of every librarian, and the book that explains and justifies everything — and when the searchers who go looking for those books end up ruined, mad, or dead on the flagstones below because they finally appreciate their own odds — that reads like someone discovering he's a random sample from an enormous reference class and being destroyed by it. But the idealists in the story, the ones who hold that the hexagonal rooms are the necessary shape of space itself, look to me like they're going the other way: from the bare fact that there are librarians at all, straight to a grand cosmology, which is the presumptuous philosopher move in Borges' clothing. Is that fair, or am I just stamping my hobbyhorse onto the text? The bit I find most interesting, though, and where I think the story gets ahead of the literature: the Library contains books that are complete, accurate accounts of you and your life, so there's a reference-class ambiguity that I don't think the standard discussion really confronts — do the people written inside the books count as observers? If they do, there are incomprehensibly more of them than there are flesh-and-blood librarians, and self-indication should tell you with near-certainty that you're a character rather than a reader. I've been calling this the presumptuous *librarian*, the mirror image of the original case, where the fact of your existence is evidence not for a bigger universe but for your own unreality. Does that survive scrutiny, or does it collapse because fictional people have no experiences to be had and therefore no claim on the reference class? I'd also like you to deal with the ending — the narrator dying a short way from the hexagon he was born in, his conjecture that the Library is unlimited and periodic, and that final image of the species about to be extinguished while the Library endures, illuminated and immobile. Is that doing anthropic work, undercutting it, or is it where the story stops being an argument at all?
