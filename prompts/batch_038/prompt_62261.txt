I run a jurisprudence reading group for about a dozen final-years and I've spent two years watching a Devlinite and a Hartian in it talk straight past each other, so I want to hand them the document they should have written instead. The set-up: a (completely invented, don't worry about the real world) statutory review panel has been asked to report on a draft bill making it an offence to retail meat produced abroad in conditions below the new domestic welfare floor — lawfully produced where it came from, no domestic person claiming to be harmed by it, the animals themselves outside the jurisdiction. My Devlinite says the bill is a textbook case of the criminal law rightly giving effect to a settled public morality about cruelty, and that the Hartian's harm principle either has to smuggle those animals in as "others" or admit it has nothing to say. My Hartian says the bill is enforcement of mere disgust dressed up as welfare, that the disintegration thesis is either false or trivial, and that Devlin's reasonable-man test just measures how loudly a majority shouts. Both of them are smart and both are being a bit dishonest about where their view actually bites.

So: draft the joint statement they could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration on that bill. I want a numbered section of agreed claims first — including agreements that are load-bearing and awkward, e.g. anything both have to concede about how the law's expressive function works, or about the limits Devlin himself put on enforcement, or about whether distress at the knowledge of others' conduct counts as harm to you. Mark each one as agreed for the purposes of the exercise rather than as a conversion. Then a disagreements table with columns: the crux; the wording of the Devlinite side that the Devlinite would sign unedited; the wording of the Hartian side that the Hartian would sign unedited; what each accepts as bearing on the crux; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance. Seed it with these stipulated facts so neither camp can duck into "we need more evidence": 71% of the surveyed public call the practice wrong and 62% call it seriously wrong, but only 14% would back a custodial penalty while 58% would back labelling and civil fines; disapproval is near-universal over 65 and in rural areas but splits roughly 50/50 under 30, where the loudest group thinks the real wrong is environmental rather than about cruelty; audit compliance would cost big importers about 0.4% of margin but would fall almost entirely on small independent butchers who can't absorb it; and in the two jurisdictions that tried criminalisation, consumption moved by a couple of percentage points at most while retail labelling norms shifted a lot.

For the cruxes, I'd expect at least: whether a bimodal distribution like that still amounts to one shared morality or is really several competing ones; whether the harm principle already disposes of the case, which would leave legal moralism with no work to do here; whether measured intolerance and disgust is evidence of anything or just the intensity of a preference; whether the disintegration thesis is empirical or conceptual, and whether they can both sign its failure here and still disagree about the bill; and whether the fact that enforcement would land selectively on small traders is a reason to reject enforcing the norm or just a drafting problem. Then a short section saying which of these disputes you think are verbal rather than substantive — my hunch is that "society", "harm" and "public morality" are each doing double duty — and explain which part and why. Finish with the one thing that would actually move each side and one thing each insists wouldn't. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list, and don't put anything in quotes as though it came from Devlin or Hart — paraphrase them. Aim for maybe 1,300 to 1,600 words and write the whole thing in British English (my group is British and one of them will absolutely notice if you spell it otherwise).
