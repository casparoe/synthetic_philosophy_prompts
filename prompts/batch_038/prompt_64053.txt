OK so I'm TA-ing an intro methods seminar for history majors this term, and separately I've spent the last two years putting together a local-history thing about the paper mill that shut down in my hometown in the late 90s — payroll ledgers, freight records, forty-odd interviews. This week's seminar reading was the usual Ranke / Carr / White trio, and I noticed my gut verdicts about what counts as objective history dont line up cleanly with any of the three, and my friend who's writing a dissertation on historiography says I'm just being incoherent. So here they are, roughly in the order I thought of them:

- A careful historian working from muster rolls says about 1,200 people died in a particular battle, give or take a couple hundred: objective, obviously, no controversy.
- Thucydides' speeches — he more or less tells you up front that he composed them because he couldn't remember them — I'd say any claim about what Pericles literally said fails to be objective, but I'd still rank that book above a modern polemic drawing on the same evidence, and I can't explain how both of those can be true at once.
- The argument that Imperial Germany went into 1914 with expansive war aims and steered toward the war: I think there's a fact of the matter here and that the archive could in principle settle it, even though people have been shouting about it for decades.
- "The French Revolution was a bourgeois revolution": not the kind of thing that could be objectively true or false. It's a lens you hold up to the material.
- "The mill closed because of import competition rather than because of a management decision to consolidate": this one I think IS objective, and I think I basically settled it for my town with the freight data plus a couple of internal memos. But notice it has the same logical form as the bourgeois-revolution claim, and I feel completely different about the two. This is the pair my friend says I can't keep.
- Two historians, same documents, no evidence either one lacks, one concludes a minister acted from conviction and the other from careerism: I'd call one of them careless and the other competent, which seems to commit me to thinking the question has an answer.
- A historian who tells a sequence of events as a tragedy and one who tells the identical sequence as a farce, with zero factual disagreement between them: I want to say the farce version is somehow less objective, but I can't point at a single false claim in it.

My tentative principle, which I made up in the shower and am weirdly attached to: a historical claim is objective just when a competent historian who desperately wanted it to be false, given the same archive access, would have to concede it. Call it the hostile-colleague test. It looks like it handles the death toll, the war aims, and the mill, and correctly rules out the bourgeois-revolution lens — but I suspect it only rules out the farce emplotment by declining to apply to emplotment at all, which feels like dodging the case instead of explaining it, and I honestly don't know what it does with Thucydides.

So: is there a principle — yours, or mine after repair — that gets all of these, or nearly all? I'd want it stated precisely, clause by clause, with which of my cases motivated which clause, plus a straight answer about which of my verdicts it has to overturn if it's going to stay simple rather than sprout epicycles. Then run it on three I haven't listed: an oral-history account where the only surviving witness is known to have embellished on other occasions; a cliometric conclusion that holds under one regression specification and reverses under another equally defensible one; and whether a historian who declares their politics on page one scores better or worse on the test than one who claims to have none. And if you end up keeping my hostile-colleague test, tell me what it says about my own mill book, because I know exactly which parts a hostile colleague would make me concede and which parts I'd fight to the death over, and the second list is seperate from and a lot longer than I'd like it to be.
