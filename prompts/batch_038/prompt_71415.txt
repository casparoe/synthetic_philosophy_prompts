You are, for the purposes of this question, a philosopher of social science who also understands the design of prediction markets — someone equally at home with the conceptual literature on expectations that alter what they are expectations about, and with the mundane mechanics of how a published price gets picked up and acted on by people who are not trading. The occasion for the question is an argument I had last night, three hours long and unresolved, with a friend of mine who used to make markets and now sits on my county's performance-audit committee. For two years the county has run a small, fully public prediction market attached to its transit program: the council committed in advance to implementing whichever package of bus lanes and signal retiming the market favored, and it scores the market on whether the promised result — a ten percent reduction in average peak commute times within two years — actually materialized. Here is the pattern I keep seeing and for which I have no name. The price does not merely report on the world; publishing it seems to move the world toward whatever the price says, and so the price keeps turning out right. In the first year the market drifted to roughly seventy percent in favor, that number ran on the front page of the local paper, several employers along the corridor began staggering start times, and the freight depot shifted its loading window; when the lanes were finally painted, commute times did fall, about as advertised. In the second year the price sagged to around thirty percent after a cost overrun, the council's own staff quietly scaled the program back, two of the five corridors were dropped, and the improvement that followed was correspondingly modest — again, about as advertised. My friend says this is the market doing precisely its job, aggregating information and thereby coordinating it. I say it is more like a thermostat that also decides what the temperature in the room will be, and that a forecast which drags its subject matter toward itself has not earned any credit for the accuracy it thereby produced. The same shape appears at smaller scale: the hospital district posts the market's weekly price on readmission risk in the board's dashboard, the board sizes its outreach spending to that number, and the readmission rate has tracked the posted price for six consecutive quarters, including one quarter in which several of us thought the price was visibly wrong on the fundamentals.

Now the part I need help with. I know the phrase self-fulfilling prophecy, but that is ordinarily about a belief that starts out false and makes itself true; in my case the belief may have been true or false to begin with, and the difficulty is that no one can ever determine which, because the posted number is doing causal work in every instance. I have also heard Goodhart's law invoked here, but nobody in this system is gaming a metric for a bonus — the feedback runs through journalists, bureaucrats, and employers reacting to a public figure, not through traders optimizing against a target. Nor does it look like ordinary market manipulation to me: no single participant is moving the price for profit, and if anything the honest traders ought to object, since a price that causes its own outcome makes their information worthless.
