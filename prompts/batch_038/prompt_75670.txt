I chair the board of the Calderwood Foundation, a $190 million private family foundation in a mid-sized manufacturing city, and I came to this role as an outsider: I was a high-school principal here for twenty-six years before retiring, and four of our current scholarship recipients were students of mine. The endowment was set up in 1961 by a man who made his money in metal plating. Three provisions of the original instrument still bind us. First, two of our nine seats are reserved in perpetuity to his lineal descendants, removable by nobody, and those seats are currently held by a brother and sister in their sixties who have no other connection to the city. Second, at least half of every year's grantmaking must go to organizations operating in the two school districts that covered the founder's boyhood neighborhood. Third, the principal may never be invaded. The awkward fact that governs everything we do is that the neighborhood in question sits directly downwind of the old plant, whose solvent plume contaminated the groundwater; it is now the city's poorest area and majority recent-immigrant, and there is active litigation over the contamination in which the family has never admitted liability and the foundation has never paid a dollar. Our largest program is a scholarship that covers full tuition at any in-state public university for graduates of those two districts — about ninety students a year, which our spending policy can sustain but not expand.

Six weeks ago a woman who made a fortune in logistics software offered us $14 million to endow a statewide version of the scholarship, which our finance committee estimates would support roughly 120 additional students a year in perpetuity, entirely new money layered on top of our own program. Her conditions are governance conditions: within five years we must eliminate the descendant-reserved seats, drop the geographic restriction, adopt a publicly posted application and selection process, and publish an annual accounting of returns and grants; if we fail, the gift reverts to her. Our counsel thinks we could get a court to modify the deed on the seats and the geography, that the state attorney general would have to be given notice, and that it would take perhaps eighteen months. Her counsel says the conditions are negotiable in form but not in substance. The board is split three ways. The two descendants argue that the geographic restriction is the whole point of the instrument — that tying the money to the neighborhood the plant ruined is the closest thing this family has ever had to an acknowledgment, and that converting a bounded obligation into a statewide program with better branding is a betrayal dressed as an expansion; they have told me privately they will resign and talk to the press if we accept. A third trustee, a former city council member, objects to both sides: she thinks a tax-advantaged private endowment deciding which children in which zip codes attend college is precisely the power that belongs to a legislature, that the second donor's gift carries a large deduction and would reduce her exposure if the wealth-tax measure on next year's ballot passes, and that the honest course is to spend ourselves down and hand the remainder to the public schools. Everyone else is some shade of reluctant yes.

The donor needs an answer before the end of the quarter, eleven weeks out, and our next board meeting is in three weeks. Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the trustees beforehand. It should recommend one course of action — accept, decline, or accept with specific counter-terms — and it should say concretely what we do about the five-year clock, the court petition, and the ninety students now in the program if we decline. State the reasoning in terms the descendant trustees and the council member could each actually answer, rather than in abstractions, and tell me what we should say to the downwind neighborhood in either scenario, including whether any part of a deal should be pointed at the contamination and the health consequences rather than at tuition. Finally, give the strongest case against your own recommendation its full weight and explain why it nonetheless loses — or, if you conclude that it does not lose, say that instead and recommend accordingly. I am not looking for a survey of how foundations handle donor conditions; I need a document the board can vote on.
