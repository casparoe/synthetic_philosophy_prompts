I'm about 60% through my second novel and I need a document that appears in it more or less verbatim — one character emails the other a PDF of it, and a chunk gets reproduced in the text. So it has to hold up. If a labour sociologist picks up the book they shouldn't be able to tell I faked it.

The setup: two of my characters have been fighting for eleven years. Marlene is a post-work economist — she thinks the entire point of automation is to get rid of work, that work's supposed goods are contingent historical arrangements that could be delivered other ways, and that "the dignity of work" is mostly a consolation prize handed to people in bad jobs. Theo is a labour sociologist and ex-organiser — he thinks socially necessary paid work is, in a market society, the main available route to standing, recognition, structure and self-respect, and that abolishing it doesn't liberate people so much as manufacture a class nobody needs. They like each other. They've finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration and to sign one joint statment.

Write that joint statement. I want the actual document, not notes toward it.

What it needs:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should be substantive rather than the anodyne stuff real committees retreat to — not "work matters to some people," but claims that are load-bearing even though they sound mild: about which of work's goods are seperable from the wage relation and which aren't, about what automation has actually done so far to hours versus task content versus job quality, about what a society loses if it loses the category of "necessary."

2. A disagreements table. Each row states the disagreement twice — once in wording Marlene would accept as a fair rendering of her own view, once in Theo's. No strawmen, and no hedging so thick that they accidentally agree. If a row turns out to be a disagreement about words, say so and move it out of the table.

3. Speaking of which: "meaningful work" is doing at least three distinct jobs in their argument — the felt meaningfulness of the activity itself, the recognition and standing that comes from being needed by other people, and the structuring of time and identity. I want the statement to pull those apart explicitly and say which ones the two of them genuinely disagree about and which ones they've spent a decade talking past each other on. If you think there's a fourth sense they're both smuggling in, name it.

4. The cruxes: three to five sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. For each, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, in their own voice, dated — before the result is known. Then say what outcome would count as a loss, and for which specific claim.

For two of the cruxes, use cases from the novel, because they have to line up with scenes I've already written:

- The Kessler plant. Order-picking automated in 2031; management kept all 900 on full pay rather than take the political hit, and invented "monitor" roles watching dashboards for machines that escalate to a human maybe twice a month. Nobody made redundant, nobody's pay cut, the work is safe and undemanding. By year four the internal survey shows pride in the job down sharply and absenteeism up, and — this is the bit I need handled carefully — the workers split almost exactly on whether they have a child under ten at home.

- The care credit scheme in Ashford, same city as the plant. Unpaid caregiving and neighbourhood maintenance logged and paid at a solid rate out of general taxation, with everyone's hours visible in a public ledger.

Both camps have to say, in advance, what they expected each of those to show, and then what they make of the actual pattern.

Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no philosopher name-dropping — these two argue in their own words and I don't want to fact-check a single citation. Around 1,200–1,500 words. Keep the register of a real joint statement: dry, precise, faintly defensive in places, the way these things sound when two people have agreed to stop scoring points off each other. And don't resolve it. I need the residual disagreement to be exact and still live on the last page, because the final chapter turns on neither of them budging.
