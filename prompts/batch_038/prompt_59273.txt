For thirty-four years I worked in atomic and molecular spectroscopy, the last fifteen of them directing a small group at a national laboratory, and I retired two years ago with the intention of finally reading the philosophy of science properly. I attend a monthly reading group here — six of us, mostly retired academics, one doctoral student — and we have been working through the question of whether the special sciences carve the world up at joints that are genuinely there. I should like your help auditing an argument I have been making in that group, because I suspect it is carrying more weight than it can bear, and I would rather learn that from you than from my colleagues.

The position I am resisting belongs to one of our members, a retired anthropologist, who takes his cue from Dupré's promiscuous realism in The Disorder of Things: there are indefinitely many ways of classifying the world that answer to our various interests and none of them uniquely correct, so realism about kinds can be preserved only by conceding that the joints lie wherever we find it useful to cut. My reply has been an analogy drawn from my own working life, and it is that analogy I want examined.

Consider an emission spectrum. The wavelengths present are fixed by the emitter — by the Hamiltonian, if you like, or by the potential-energy surface of the molecule. A spectrometer of poor resolving power records a broad blend; a better instrument resolves that same blend into three or four distinct lines. Nothing has been invented. The structure was there all along, and the quality of the instrument determines how much of it is visible, not whether it exists. So, I say, with kinds: classificatory schemes differ in resolution rather than in subject matter, and the interest-relativity of taxonomy establishes only that different questions call for different resolving powers, not that the answers are ours to choose. The conclusion I have been using the analogy to licence is a pluralism about granularity coupled with realism about structure — many classifications are legitimate, but they are not all equally apt, and their aptness is settled by something outside our purposes.

My colleague's response has three parts, and I find I cannot dismiss any of them. First, that the analogy presupposes what is in question, since spectroscopy possesses a fixed generator which settles both what features exist and which resolutions are faithful to them, whereas in biology and the human sciences the entities are historically produced, mutually entangled, and changing while they are measured, so nothing plays the part of the Hamiltonian. Second, that resolving power in my field is a quantity I can state independently of anyone's interests, in reciprocal centimetres, whereas in taxonomy coarser and finer do not lie on a single ordered scale: lumpings and splittings cross-cut one another rather than nest. Third, that even in spectroscopy the identification of a line is partly theoretical, since the grouping of lines into series and multiplets and the assignment of quantum numbers come from the theory and not from the plate.

To these I must add a difficulty of my own making, which I have thus far kept quiet about. Lines are not infinitely sharp. There is natural lifetime broadening, and Doppler and pressure broadening; and as one approaches the series limit the lines crowd together until they merge into what presents itself as a continuum. The joints in my own paradigm case are therefore blurred, and blurred for reasons that are intrinsic rather than instrumental. I do not know whether that observation destroys the analogy or rescues it, and I should welcome a clear view of the matter.

What I should like from you is this. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what plays the part of the emitter, the lines, the spectrometer, the resolving power, the detector, the calibration standard, and the question the observer brings to the instrument. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the use I have made of the comparison from those that are merely decorative, and say why each falls on one side or the other. I should especially like to know which features of the source domain are quietly imported into the target, above all the assumption of a fixed generator and of a single ordered scale of resolution. On that basis, tell me whether my conclusion survives in some weakened form, and if it does, state that form precisely enough that I could defend it next month; and tell me plainly if it does not survive at all. Please also give your judgement on the broadening problem: does the intrinsic fuzziness of spectral lines undermine the claim that nature supplies the joints, or does it merely relocate the fuzziness, and if so, to where? Finally, if you think a better analogy is available — whether from spectroscopy itself or from elsewhere in the natural sciences — describe it and say what it can and cannot carry; and if you think that arguing from any analogy about measurement to a claim about the furniture of the world already concedes my colleague's point, say so and explain why.

I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of further reading, and I would much prefer the argument worked through to a survey of the positions. Please write in American English, notwithstanding my own habits of spelling.
