I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about insects by going case by case, and I have arrived at a set of verdicts that I cannot unify. I would like your help identifying the principle, if any, that lies behind them, and telling me which of them I should abandon if none does.

Here are the verdicts.

One. Pouring boiling water into an ant nest in my garden because I dislike the ants is wrong. Not distasteful, not imprudent: wrong, and I would be culpable for doing it.

Two. Mowing the lawn that contains the same nest, killing tens of thousands of grasshoppers, spiders, and beetles over the course of a season, is permissible. The faint unease I feel about it does not seem to me to track anything morally real.

Three. Given two pesticides registered for the same crop, equal in cost and efficacy, one of which is documented to kill far more non-target arthropods, I ought to choose the less lethal one. This is a genuine moral improvement and not a matter of lifestyle or aesthetics.

Four. A single bee trapped in my kitchen: I catch it under a glass and release it outside, and I think that is what I should do. A colony of the same species inside my wall cavity, which a contractor offers to remove lethally: I authorize the removal and feel nothing afterward. Number is evidently not doing the work here, since the colony vastly outnumbers the bee.

Five. I eat oysters and mussels without compunction, on the grounds that bivalves are very unlikely to be sentient. But I would stop eating crabs if I came to think the probability for them was around thirty percent. So my verdicts do respond to probability, and not merely to taxonomy or to size.

Six. After a storm, tens of thousands of ants are stranded on floating debris in my yard. Assisting some of them would cost me an afternoon. I think doing it would be good, and I do not think refraining is blameworthy. A single dog stranded in the same position: walking past it would be blameworthy.

Seven. If there were a cheap and targeted way to reduce the number of insects that die of starvation, desiccation, or parasitism in the wild, I think I should take it. But I firmly reject the inference that we should pursue insect extinction, or mass sterilization, in order to bring that suffering to zero, even granting the arithmetic.

Eight. Killing one insect that I am certain is sentient strikes me as worse than killing a million insects each of which has a one-in-a-million chance of being sentient. Yet killing a million insects each of which I am certain is sentient strikes me as vastly worse than killing one. My intuitions are not linear in probability: below some point the product seems to go dark rather than merely to grow small.

Running underneath all of this is the scale consideration. The figure I keep encountering is on the order of ten quintillion individual arthropods alive at any moment. Even a probability of sentience as low as one in a thousand, applied to a population that size, yields an expected number of sentient beings millions of times larger than the human population. I find that calculation arresting, and I also find that it does not move me in the least. I want to know whether my failure to be moved is a rational defect in me or evidence against the calculation.

My own tentative principle is this: the moral weight of an option is the number of individuals affected, multiplied by the probability that each is sentient, multiplied by the severity and duration of the harm, compared across the options available; together with a floor clause stating that if the probability of sentience falls below some threshold, the entire term is set to zero. Cases one, two, three, and five come out correctly. The first half of case eight comes out correctly if the floor sits above one-in-a-million. But the floor clause is precisely what makes the second half of case eight and the scale argument come apart, and I cannot see any non-arbitrary place to set it.

With that in view, please do the following.

First, say whether any single principle, or a short list of rival candidates, delivers all eight verdicts. If none does, say which verdicts each candidate rescues and at what cost.

Second, for each candidate, state it precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases motivates each clause. I am particularly interested in whether cases one and two can be separated by anything other than intent, and in whether the real difference between cases two and three is avoidability rather than number, since in both a large number of insects dies incidentally and in only one of them is there an alternative at hand.

Third, test each candidate on two cases I have not raised. Would I be required, at equal cost, to prefer a control method that kills quickly and certainly over one that kills slowly but allows some individuals to escape? And does the moral assessment of keeping insects in conditions that would be objectionable for a mammal, such as severe crowding or the frustration of species-typical behavior, change anything when the probability of sentience is held fixed?

Fourth, tell me plainly which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to surrender, and then say which one you think is the weakest link and should be the one to go.

Fifth, take up the floor clause directly and assess the alternatives to it: a concave function of probability, so that very low probabilities count for less than their linear share but not for nothing; a requirement that I choose options permissible under every view about insect minds I take seriously, rather than maximizing across them; a split between what I may do and what I must do, so that probability operates differently on prohibitions than on positive duties; and the treatment of sentience as graded rather than binary, so that my uncertainty concerns degrees of moral weight instead of the presence or absence of it. Say whether any of these yields a floor that is not arbitrary.

Finally, consider whether any of this changes once I acknowledge that I am uncertain about moral theory and not only about insect minds. Case seven looks as though it requires something deontological or at least anti-totalizing, while cases three and six look consequentialist. Tell me whether a single framework can hold both, or whether I should recognize that I am switching frameworks between cases and say which switches are defensible.
