I'm a retired physical chemist — crystallography mostly, plus about twenty years teaching thermodynamics — and I've been reading philosophy seriously on my own for eighteen months now, badly. The social science stuff has grabbed me because the arguments look suspiciously like ones I've had for decades about whether thermodynamics "reduces" to mechanics. Here's something I wrote up for a reading group I go to. It was supposed to establish that methodological individualism about explanation is false. Instead it seems to prove way more than I wanted: it seems to show my own field has no explanations worth the name. I can't live with that, and I can't find the bad step either.

(1) Every social fact is constituted by facts about individuals — what they believe, want, do, and are disposed to do. Call this constitution individualism. I want to grant it for the sake of arguement.

(2) If a fact X is wholly constituted by facts about Y, then any true explanation citing X can be replaced, with no explanatory loss, by an explanation citing only facts about Y.

(3) So, granting (1), every explanation citing social structures, institutions, or markets can be replaced by one citing only individual-level facts.

(4) But take a real case: why a commodity price jumped last spring. The market-level explanation works because of what would have happened across a wide range of situations — who else would have bought, what the marginal trader would have done, how the price responds to a supply shock. The individual-level replacement has to list the actual beliefs and actions of the actual traders, and it can't carry that counterfactual weight. It's too specific: it tells you why this price moved here, not why prices behave that way.

(5) So some social explanations can't be replaced by individual-level ones without loss, and (2) is false.

(6) Therefore constitution individualism doesn't buy you individualism about explanation. Structure is explanatorily real on its own account.

Now the trouble. Run (2) and (4) on temperature. What makes the gas hot is constituted by facts about molecules. But the good explanation of why the piston rose cites the temperature, and it works across an enormous range of different molecular configurations — that's precisely why it's a good explanation. A complete list of the positions and momenta of the actual molecules would be "too specific" in exactly the sense of (4). So by my own argument thermodynamics is irreducible and physical chemistry has been smuggling in something non-mechanical for 150 years. I know thats wrong. But every step looks just as good for molecules as it does for traders, and I can't see which one is leaning on something social-science-specific.

Two things I want. First: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? I'd especially like to know whether (4) is doing something illegitimate — whether I'm sliding between "the obvious translation doesn't work" and "no translation could work," or between two different senses of "explanatory loss" — or whether the real damage is further up, in (2). Second: price the alternatives. Suppose I keep (1) and (2) and just deny (4): does individualism then come out true but empty, because the individual-level base I have to appeal to keeps getting inflated with the dispositions of traders who never actually traded? Or suppose I keep (4) and drop (2): does that commit me to structures doing causal work that isn't the individuals' work — i.e. to the holism I was trying not to buy? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) if I can, but tell me what I'm paying for them.

And please don't be polite with me. If the argument is confused from the ground up, or if the question as I've posed it can't be answered cleanly — if (1) and (2) aren't even the right way to carve the issue, so that "which premise should I reject" is the wrong question to be asking — say so straight out and tell me what the right question would have been. I spent forty years being wrong in public; I'd rather find out fast. No need to give me a reading list, I have a library card and nothing but time.
