I co-produce a student-run podcast on practical ethics, and next month's guest is a moral philosopher who has made her name with what she calls the scale argument for insect moral significance. As she states it, the argument has exactly two premises: first, that whatever our present evidence about insects really licenses, the credence that a given insect is sentient is small but not negligible — she works with something in the neighbourhood of one in a hundred — and second, that moral weight adds up across individuals with no threshold, so that a tiny per-individual probability multiplied by the standing population of insects, for which she uses something on the order of ten to the eighteenth individuals alive at any moment, yields an expected quantity of morally relevant experience that swamps every other candidate in practical ethics. Her signature way of putting it is that each insect is a lottery ticket that may or may not contain a mind, and that with that many tickets you are not gambling on whether there are winners, only on how many there are. When it is objected that sentience is vague and therefore not the sort of property that admits of a precise small probability, her standing answer is that the vagueness lives in our concepts rather than in the animals, so the probability is perfectly well defined even though we cannot state it to better than an order of magnitude.

I spent the better part of two hours last night defending all of this to a friend who does statistical work for an insurer, and I came away considerably less sure of my ground than I went in. His charge is that the argument is expected-value theatre: the first premise, he says, is not a probability but a refusal to have an opinion, dressed up so that it can be multiplied; and the second premise is doing all the work, since if weight really aggregates without threshold then the same arithmetic hands the crown to nematodes, or copepods, or anything else numerous enough, and the argument proves far more than she wants. The part I genuinely could not answer was this: if she accepts the vagueness reply, she owes us an account of why indeterminacy in our concepts should resolve into a number rather than into a range that includes zero, and a range including zero makes the multiplication collapse altogether. He also insists that a fair test is whether she can still reach her conclusion with the probability premise deleted, leaving only the aggregation premise to carry it.

So I would like eight to ten questions I can actually put to her in the room, in an order I can follow from a single page, arranged in three movements — what the small probability is and whether it is the kind of thing a credence can attach to at all; what the aggregation premise demands of her, including where the arithmetic is supposed to stop and whether it can be turned on cases she would rather not sign up for; and what is meant to follow for action, given that almost none of the disvalue in her numbers is caused by human beings and nearly all of it sits in wild populations nobody can plausibly intervene in at scale. For every question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and make at least one question force her to say whether the conclusion survives once the probability premise is removed. Two constraints on tone: she is quick and generous and will happily talk for six minutes about a question she likes, so each question must be short enough to hold in one breath and pointed enough that a long answer cannot slip past it; and none of them may be leading, since if she simply agrees with the premise under attack the question still has to do its work. End with one question about what evidence would push her credence down rather than up, and please leave out anything generic about her intellectual formation — I want the content of the view, not the biography, and no citations or reading lists. Finally, write the whole thing as continuous prose of the kind one would send in an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbering, just each question introduced in a sentence or two of your own and its follow-up immediately after, so that I can read straight down the page in order.
