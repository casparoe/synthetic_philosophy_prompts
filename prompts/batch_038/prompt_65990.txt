My metaphysics seminar put together this argument and our TA called it the best bad argument of the term, then refused to say anything else. Its supposed to show that on Russell's theory of descriptions, hardly anything anybody says is true.

(1) On the Russellian analysis, "the F is G" is true only if exactly one thing is F and that thing is G — uniqueness is part of the truth conditions, not a pragmatic extra.
(2) Count nouns like "table" are vague: for any table-shaped lump of matter in my kitchen there are indefinitely many overlapping candidates (one short a molecule at the top, one with a molecule added, one that came into being a second later), and no principle way to single one out as *the* table.
(3) So either lots of things are tables or nothing is — the problem of the many.
(4) If lots are, uniqueness fails and "the table in my kitchen is brown" is false. If none is, the existence clause fails and it's false anyway.
(5) The same goes for any ordinary claim resting on a vague noun: the cloud over the hill, the book on my desk, the man at the door.
(6) So nearly everything we say is false.
(7) But a lot of what we say is true.
(8) Therefore the Russellian analysis is wrong.

I'm genuinely torn between two reactions and don't know how to choose: either some step here is doing a quiet switch on me, or the argument is valid and I'm supposed to swallow (6). Which is it? Tell me exactly where it breaks if it breaks, why that step looked fine going in, and what the main ways out cost — including whether a contextual-restriction reply just pushes (2) up a level. Id rather give up (1) than (7), but show me what I'd be paying.
