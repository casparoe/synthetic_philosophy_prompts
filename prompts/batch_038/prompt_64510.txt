**Exam question.** *Lucas and Penrose both argue from Gödel's incompleteness theorems to the conclusion that the idealized reasoning of a human mathematician cannot be reproduced by a Turing machine.* (a) *Set out the argument they have in common as precisely as you can, stating which theorem does the work and what must be assumed about the machine.* (b) *Lucas assumes the machine is consistent; Penrose assumes it is sound. Explain what turns on that difference and say which version of the argument is stronger.* (c) *Does either argument, if successful, establish that mechanism is false?*

**Student's answer.**

(a) Suppose for reductio that the idealized reasoning of a human mathematician is captured by a Turing machine F: the sentences F proves are exactly the mathematical truths the mathematician can establish. Assume F is consistent, that its axioms are effectively specifiable, and that it is strong enough to represent arithmetic. By the first incompleteness theorem there is a sentence G(F), obtained by diagonalization, such that F proves G(F) only if F is inconsistent; since F is consistent, F does not prove G(F). But G(F) says, in effect, that F does not prove G(F), which is just what we have established. So the mathematician can see that G(F) is true, though F cannot prove it, and the mathematician's powers exceed F's.

Two things beyond the theorem are needed. F must be a determinate system whose axioms we can actually inspect, otherwise there is no fixed target. And the mathematician must be idealized — indefatigable, immune to slips, with unbounded working memory — so that "what the mathematician can establish" picks out a definite set of sentences at all. Without that idealization there is nothing to compare with F's theorems.

(b) Consistency and soundness are not the same assumption: a consistent theory may still prove false arithmetical sentences, so soundness is strictly stronger. That is why Penrose's argument is the weaker of the two, and the weakening shows up in his conclusion, which is carefully restricted to the claim that our reasoning is not captured by any formal system we could *know* to be sound. This leaves the mechanist a perfectly comfortable position: we are a sound system F, but we cannot determine F's soundness, and so no contradiction arises.

Lucas's argument has no such escape. The reply that we cannot establish the consistency of our own system trades on the second incompleteness theorem, and the second theorem is a result about formal systems; it applies to us only if we are a formal system, which is precisely what is in dispute. Penrose, having given away the escape, then has to import his positive hypothesis — gravitationally induced state reduction in neuronal microtubules, a process that is neither deterministic nor computable — to close a gap the Gödelian argument leaves open. The fact that he needs physics to do this work is itself evidence that the argument alone will not bear the weight he puts on it. Lucas, who appeals to no such hypothesis, is on firmer ground.

(c) Neither argument, if successful, shows that the mind is not physical. Penrose accepts that the brain is a physical system; his conclusion is that physics as we now have it is incomplete, because a complete physics would have to accommodate non-computable processes. Lucas likewise is attacking a thesis about mechanism, not defending Cartesian dualism. So what the arguments threaten, if anything, is a specific claim: that mathematical insight is algorithmic. A mechanist could concede that and retain a computational account of perception, memory, and ordinary cognition, since the argument is run on idealized mathematical reasoning and nothing else. It also leaves open that the mind realizes something stronger than a Turing machine rather than something non-physical.

One further point about what the conclusion amounts to. The argument does not merely show that for each machine there is a truth it misses; it shows that we can prove strictly more arithmetical sentences than any Turing machine can, since given any F we can prove G(F). Our deductive power is therefore of a different order from any computational power. This also disposes of the familiar objection that the mechanist is not committed to any particular machine being the mind, and that we could never identify which machine it would be. That objection would bite if the conclusion concerned a specific F. It does not: the conclusion is a general comparison of powers, and it stands whichever machine the mechanist nominates, or indeed if she nominates none.
