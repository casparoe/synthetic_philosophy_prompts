I am drafting a novel in which two field biologists who have worked the same island chain for a decade come to disagree about whether one of its populations is a species in its own right, while a third and older colleague insists that they are not really disagreeing at all. I want their quarrel to be substantive rather than a symptom of unfinished fieldwork: please construct a case in which, every relevant fact being known to all three, the biological species concept and the phylogenetic species concept still deliver opposite verdicts about that population. Since the phylogenetic concept is normally the splitter in such disputes, I want the reversal to run the other way — the biological concept must divide what the phylogenetic concept is obliged to join. The scenario should occupy a single paragraph, be set in a plausible temperate archipelago, involve no speculative technology and no data the characters could not in principle obtain; the isolation between the populations must be demonstrable rather than merely untested, and their mutual indistinguishability must not be an artefact of crude characters.

Then tell me which single feature of the case produces the divergence, what the two verdicts become if that feature is removed, and how far the colleague's view that species are individuals rather than classes really dissolves the quarrel — whether it leaves the two merely talking past one another, or whether it quietly favours one side of it.

Please write in British English and keep the whole answer under seven hundred words. Above all, mark clearly for me which parts of this are settled ground and which remain live disputes among those who work on the species problem; I do not want my characters asserting as agreed what specialists are still arguing about, and I would rather know where the design of my case leans on a contested reading of the concepts involved.
