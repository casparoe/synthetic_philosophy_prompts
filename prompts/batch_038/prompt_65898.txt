I am the seminar tutor for a module on self-knowledge and its limits in a joint philosophy/cognitive-science MA program: fourteen students, one 110-minute session, whiteboard only, no slides. Their background is uneven but usable — Gettier cases, closure, and a safety condition on knowledge from an intro course; the S4 axiom mentioned once in a logic module and dismissed as obviously false; and, just last week, a metacognition lab in which they rated their own confidence on perceptual discrimination tasks and were shown that their second-order sensitivity is imperfect. The session has to carry a single arc: from the definition of a luminous condition, through Williamson's argument that no non-trivial condition is luminous, to what that does to the principle that knowing implies being in a position to know that one knows.

I am not asking for the arc to be explained to me. I am asking for it to be planned, on the assumption that I cannot consult anything while preparing.

1. The order of introduction, with the dependency claims made explicit — what each component requires to be in place before it can be stated. In particular: where the gloss on "in a position to know" must go, and why it cannot be deferred until the argument is already on the board; and where the step from one non-luminous condition to the general claim, together with its triviality caveat, belongs relative to the application to knowledge itself.

2. The carrying example. The case of someone who feels freezing at dawn and slowly warms until noon is in their assigned reading, and the takeaway I have to dismantle is that the whole argument is the sorites paradox dressed up in feelings. I need a replacement, and I need to know what it must have and what it must lack: must the state be phenomenal? May I run it on an external stimulus — a tone rising in pitch, a light dimming — or does that hand the session over to my students' discrimination-threshold intuitions? Must the endpoints admit of clear verdicts? Must the change be continuous in the subject rather than in the world?

3. The misconception map, each entry tagged with the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off. I expect at least these: that the argument is a vagueness argument in disguise; that it is an empirical finding about poor introspective sensitivity, revisable by better calibration data; that it licenses scepticism about one's own mind generally; that the iteration principle is trivially false because infants and animals know things without knowing that they know; and that if feeling cold is nothing over and above the belief that one feels cold, the crucial premise collapses. On that last one, tell me whether to engage it in the session or to name it and defer it, and what either choice costs me.

4. One question for the final five minutes whose answers would separate a student who has the argument from one who has only acquired a mood about it.

5. What I should bracket, and announce as bracketed rather than silently omit. I have in mind the degrees-of-belief formulation of the crucial premise, the connection to the surprise examination, and whether the conclusion depends on the actual limits of human discrimination rather than on the nature of knowledge. Tell me whether that is the right list, and what the students lose by not seeing each item.
