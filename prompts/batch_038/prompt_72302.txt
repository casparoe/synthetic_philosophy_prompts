im putting together a small zine of fiction about philosophy of mind and i need one on computationalism, roughly 1500-2000 words. but not the upload/emulation story everyone writes — the one i want turns on the implementation problem, the part that usually gets skipped.

here's the setup. a bureau certifies "continuations." when someone dies, relatives submit whatever physical process they think is still running the deceased. sometimes it's a scanned connectome in a rack, fine, but mostly it's weirder: a warehouse of relays, a tide-prediction model, a server farm clearing trades, a slab of quartz being warmed and cooled on a schedule. the bureau doesn't check whether the thing *behaves* like grandma. it checks whether the thing *implements* her — whether there's a mapping from its states onto the states of the process she was running that isn't gerrymanderd, that holds up under counterfactuals (had this input arrived at that moment, the right internal transition would have fired), and that wasn't cooked up after the fact by re-labelling whatever states happened to be there. because if any old physical system can be redescribed as running any computation, the whole certification racket is worthless, and the clerk who signs the certificates knows this and has to keep telling grieving people that their submission is cheating.

the story should turn on him finding a submission that passes every one of his tests. genuinely robust, genuinely the right causal organization, no re-labelling tricks, survives probing — and him sitting there realizing his criteria were only ever about structure, so passing them settles nothing about whether anyone is home in it. i want him to have to sign or not sign, and i want the reader to feel that two questions have been getting filed under one form number: is this running the computation, and is it like anything to be this.

things i care about:

- make the counterfactual test concrete. show it applied — a submitted system actually probed with a fabricated input, someone waiting to see which way it turns. and show one submission failing it in an interesting way, not just because it was a hoax or a fraud.
- the clerk can't be a strawman for either side. give him a real argument, then dent it.
- keep the philosophy inside the furniture of the world: forms, precedents, office jargon, dust in the relay warehouse, the queue at the window. nobody lectures. if someone does lecture it's under oath and gets cut off by an objection.
- end on the signature, or the absence of one, doing the work. no closing line that explains what it meant.

tone: somewhere between a clerk story by kafka and a very tired civil servant's diary. please no puns in the title, i beg you.
