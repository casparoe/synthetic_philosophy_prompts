There's an anti-naturalism argument I keep seeing in intro ethics discussions and I want you to actually build the case against it, not just list objections. It runs: (1) you cant validly get an ought-conclusion out of purely is-premises, Hume's point; (2) if moral naturalism were true, some purely descriptive premises would have to entail moral conclusions, since the moral facts just are natural facts; (3) therefore moral naturalism is false. And usually someone chimes in that this is really just Moore's naturalistic fallacy, or the open question argument, in different clothes — so you get two supposedly independent lines converging on the same target.

Argue that stacking them is self-undermining rather than mutually reinforcing. The shape I have in mind: if (1) is an a priori truth about entailment then it's silent on whether moral properties are natural ones, so it cant license (3) on its own; but if (1) is instead a substantive thesis about what morality is like, then whatever work it does depends on the very Moorean metaphysics it was supposed to be propping up. Make that precise enough that I can see exactly where each horn bites, and don't fudge it into "they're just different arguments" — I want the positive claim that the combination is worse than either part alone.

Also handle the a posteriori identity reply: the water/H2O, Hesperus/Phosphorus move where an identity can be necessary without being entailed by the descriptive vocabulary. Say whether it rescues the naturalist or concedes too much, and whether it changes which horn of the dilemma you're on.

Then work in the fact that half the time "naturalistic fallacy" is used to mean reasoning from unnatural to bad, which is a third error entirely. Explain why that slide does real damage to the argument's credibility rather than being a pedantic terminology complaint — i.e., what work the equivocation is doing for the person making it.

Finish with what you think is left standing for the anti-naturalist once you've taken the stack apart. Is there a version of either claim that still does work, and if so, is it interesting? No references or reading suggestions needed, just argue it from what you know.
