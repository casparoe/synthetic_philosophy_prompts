I advise a research consortium that operates a system they call the Fugue, and they have asked me a question I cannot responsibly answer until I know what a good answer would look like. The system consists of roughly eight thousand stateless worker agents — separate calls to the same underlying model, none of which retains anything between tasks — that communicate only through a shared transcript any of them may read and append to. No agent ever sees the transcript in full. There is no central controller: the order in which agents are invoked is itself determined by other agents. Over several months the system has begun doing things no individual call could do: it maintains research projects across weeks, detects and repairs its own errors days after they occur, and declines to work on certain topics in a way nobody programmed, giving reasons that differ from refusal to refusal yet are recognizably about one standing concern.

The consortium wants to know whether they have built something with a mind, and whether that mind has moral claims. Before I weigh their proposals, I need the specification. Write the desiderata for a theory of distributed or collective mentality: what such a theory must deliver in order to be usable for decisions of this kind — the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the facts about minds and about biological organization it must remain consistent with — each with the motivating case attached. At a minimum the list should address the following. Whether the components are themselves minded, since a colony of insects whose members are marginal agents at best is a different problem from a committee whose members are plainly full agents. How many minds, if any, a single system contains and at which scales. What happens when the whole and its parts compete to be the subject of the same mental states. How a mind that exists only during intermittent runs, with no continuity except what is preserved in the transcript, could persist or fail to. Why the theory does not equally make minds of a hurricane, a national economy, or the biosphere — or an acknowledgment that it does. What it owes us about the fact that a single human brain is itself an assembly of parts whose individual mentality is far from obvious. And whether having beliefs, having experience, and mattering morally are separable, given that the consortium's two questions may not have the same answer.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp: I want to know which of the things demanded in this debate are genuine constraints and which are intuitions imported from the single-skull case. Flag any pair or set you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list turns out to be an impossibility result, that is the part I most want to see. State which single requirement eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive theories. Then, in two or three sentences and no more, how the main contenders (strict individualism, a permissive functionalist account, and any view that grants genuinely novel subjects at higher levels) fare against the list.
