So I got volunteered to run the opening session of our department's new philosophy-of-AI reading group. Fifty minutes, one whiteboard, fourteen people, and the mix is cursed: nine ML PhD students who are quietly convinced embedded agency is already handled by "just condition the model on its own past actions," three philosophy students whose entire background is Searle and Nagel, and two from the law school who came because they saw the word agency on the flyer. Don't explain embedded agency to me, I've read the material — I want a plan for how to explain it to them.

Start with the dependency structure. What has to be in place before what? Can anybody feel the force of "the agent is smaller than its environment, so its map can't contain a full copy of the territory" while they're still thinking a model is just a probability distribution over possible worlds, or does that assumption have to be broken first and seperately? Where does the realizability problem sit relative to the self-model problem relative to the "your own reasoning is part of the world you're predicting" problem — and is there any ordering that doesn't make one of those three feel like a footnote?

Then the carrier. I'm torn between two and I want you to actually argue, not hedge. One is the self-referential map: a map detailed enough that it has to include a detailed map of itself. The other is the Lucas critique — a macro model whose parameters break exactly when the model gets used for policy, because the people inside the model respond to being modeled. My worry is that the second one teaches them "the world pushes back" and then stops, never getting them to the part that's actually about the agent living inside its own model. Tell me which one carries the whole thing, what that example has to have, and what it has to conspicuously lack.

Hard constraint I've imposed on myself: no AI examples for the first twenty minutes, because the ML people pattern-match instantly and the session turns into a world-models discussion. Design around that.

Then the misconception map, in the order they'll actually surface rather than the order they occur to me now, with the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. I'm counting on "this is just the frame problem," "this is just reflexivity, the map affects the territory," "so there's no objective fact about what the agent believes," and "this is the Chinese Room again." I'm sure I'm missing at least two — name them.

Finally: one question I can put to the room at the end that seperates the people who got it from the people who nodded politely, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I'm skipping. I'm assuming logical uncertainty and the whole counterfactual-reasoning-about-your-own-code mess goes in the skip pile, but tell me if that's the wrong call.
