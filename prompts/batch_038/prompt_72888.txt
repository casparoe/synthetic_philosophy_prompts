Our reading group runs a little interview series — two armchairs, an hour, no slides, mostly philosophy grad students plus a handful of people from the CS building down the hall. Next week's guest works on logical induction and defends it hard. For context: the idea is that a Bayesian is supposed to assign probability 1 to every tautology and have credences closed under implication, which no bounded reasoner can actually manage, so instead of demanding coherence you characterize good reasoning as a sequence of prices over sentences that no efficiently computable trading strategy can exploit for unbounded profit.

The guest also has a signature thesis I want to make them sweat over: that the real content of the framework sits in the traders rather than in the prices, so that restricting traders to polynomial-time computation is a substantive normative commitment we could rationally revise upward or downward, not just a convenience for getting the proofs through. They like to say that different complexity classes give you different legitimate ideals of reasoning.

Write me 8 to 10 questions I could say out loud, in the order I'd ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer they're most likely to give — so the follow-up should bite on the obvious dodge rather than restate the question. Atleast one question on each of these:

- the only known construction is computable but so slow its a curiosity rather than an algorithm: is there any reason to hope a reasoner with a realistic budget could satisfy the criterion, and if not, what is the criterion for?
- the guarantee is purely asymptotic, so nothing seems to rule out an inductor that prices blatant contradictions near 1 for a googol steps and only shapes up in the limit — what normative work is the criterion doing on any given day?
- the Dutch-book ancestry: getting money-pumped shows you'll lose money, which people have argued isn't the same as being irrational. Does non-exploitability inherit that worry, especially when the market here is hypothetical and the reasoner never actually offers to trade? And does the criterion happily license an agent that is confidently wrong in ways only an uncomputable trader could catch?
- self-reference: sentences about the reasoner's own prices, and what's genuinely left of the problem once you grant that inductors are supposed to form accurate beliefs about their own beliefs without running into paradox — including what happens if those prices get fed into a decision procedure.
- the signature thesis about complexity classes: what happens at the extremes, does a big enough trader class collapse back into logical omniscience, does a small enough one make the criterion vacuous, and is there a principled stopping point.
- and one on their own motivating case, the feeling that a proved bound on gaps between primes is evidence for a conjecture it doesn't imply: what in the framework licenses that update, given that only patterns writable in polynomial time are supposed to be learned in a timely way.

Nothing generic, please — no "what drew you to this," no softballs — and no reading lists, references, or citations, just questions I could actually ask in a room. American English.
