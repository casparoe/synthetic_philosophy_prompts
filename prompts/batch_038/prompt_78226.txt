You're a professional philosopher who works on the paradoxes of self-deception — the dynamic ones and the static ones, plus all the deflationary accounts that try to make the whole problem go away. Please think carefully and don't rush to a verdict. Before you commit to anything, spend a bit of brainstorming on what dimensions a principle of self-deception could even trade on (how the evidence gets gathered vs how it gets read; whether the state or the process is what matters; what the person can be brought to admit; how much blame or comfort is riding on the belief), and only then settle.

Here's the thing: I don't want a theory of self-deception yet. I want to know what my own gut is already committed to. So here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases. Some I'm sure about, some not.

1. Dave recieves a text on his partner's phone while she's in the shower. He's suspected for months. He doesn't look, and afterwards he tells himself he didn't look because it would be a betrayal of trust — but he also arranges things so he never has the chance again. Self-deception, obviously.

2. Marguerite finds a woman's earring under the passenger seat and thinks: I must have dropped it, it must have rolled in from somewhere, it isn't even the kind she'd wear. Self-deception.

3. Same Marguerite, same seat, but now she thinks: that's hers, it wasn't there last week, and his sister definitely doesn't wear silver hoops — even though she does. Not self-deception. Just an anxious person doing anxious reading of ambiguous data. I'm quite firm on this one.

4. A terminal patient, told plainly by two doctors that she has weeks, keeps telling everyone she's getting better and starts planning a trip for the autumn. Self-deception… but I don't want to say it about her. I want to say it's hope and it's doing her good.

5. A professor writes a thirty-page argument he's proud of. Two years later he can't reproduce it and doesn't really accept the conclusion any more, but he still says "as I've argued" and bristles if you press him on premise 4. Self-deception, though the mildest kind?

6. A philosophy student knows there's a hole in step three of his proof. He writes "therefore" and moves on to the corollaries. Not self-deception. He'd admit the hole instantly if you pointed at it. He's just cutting a corner, and he knows it.

7. Someone who has suspected the affair for weeks and has carefully never formulated it blurts out "you're leaving me" at dinner and is stunned to hear himself say it. I want to call this self-deception too, but notice what it does to the standard picture — there don't seem to be two beliefs in there at once, just one that's been kept away from the places where he'd have to say it out loud.

8. The drinker who tells himself, sincerely, that tonight's the last one. Not self-deception — that's weakness of will, which is a different thing and I don't want the two collapsed.

9. A woman with anorexia, visibly skeletal, saying in total sincerity that she isn't too thin, and getting angry at the suggestion. Self-deception.

10. A man with a clenched jaw saying, also in total sincerity, that he is not angry. Here I genuinely don't know. Something feels different from 9, and my guess is that the difference is between deceiving yourself and just not having access. If so, does the theory need to say what that difference is, or can it get away without it?

One worry before you start: 2 and 3 look structurally identical to me — same person, same evidence, same motivated reading, opposite valence — and I've given opposite verdicts. And I suspect the reason I called 6 self-deception but hedged on 4 is that I like her and don't want to say she's fooling herself, not anything about the cases. If that's right, tell me, and tell me what a verdict can legitimately trade on when praise and comfort are off the table.
