I've been volunteering at our county historical society for about three years now and I've gotten kind of obssesed with the gaps in what we hold — more interested in them than in the stuff we actually have. Nobody here has training, it's mostly retirees and me, so I've been making up my own way of thinking about it and I'd like to know if it holds water or if it's just a thing in my head.

My idea is you can score any gap on two different numbers. One is whose doing it is: 0 means history just ground it up and nobody in that building had anything to do with it, 10 means the archive itself — the people whose whole job was keeping things — made the hole. The other number is what it costs someone a hundred years out who's trying to work out what life was actually like here: 0 for nothing much lost, 10 for a permanent canyon. I think those two come apart a lot, and I honestly can't tell if the "whose doing" one means anything at all.

Here's what I've got. Give me both scores for each item, then one ranked list of the seven from worst to least bad on whatever combined judgement you think is actually defensible, and tell me what you're weighting when you combine them.

1. Board of supervisors minutes, every single meeting from 1902 on, beautifully bound. The only trace of the people who worked in the county infirmary is a payroll ledger where most of them show up as initials and a wage.
2. About nine years of the Black weekly paper from the 1920s and 30s — except we don't have the paper. We have clippings someone from a white family cut out and pasted into scrapbooks, with notes in the margins. So what survived is whatever they thought was worth cutting.
3. A photographer's entire business archive, something like 40,000 glass negatives, all still in his client order. If your family never paid him for a sitting, you're not in there and you never will be.
4. The poorhouse and county welfare records, gone in the 1957 flood. But the loss got written down at the time — there's a page in the old accession book saying exactly what was lost.
5. Eleven boxes of diaries and letters from one prominent family's men, all digitized, everybody cites them. From the wives we have a recipe book with a few lines about a funeral in 1888.
6. Around sixty oral history tapes from the mid-70s about the plant closure. The interviewer had a list of questions about the union and stuck to it, and you can hear her steering people back every time they wander off into thier own memories.
7. Since last year we've been adding best-guess identifications to the unlabeled photo albums — "possibly a Hendricks daughter" and so on — because otherwise nobody can use them for anything. Roughly half of those are me guessing from jawlines and hem lengths.

Two more things I keep going around on. First, whether number 4 is better or worse for having been written down — a hole with a sign on it versus a hole nobody knows is there. Which of those does more damage to the future, and does it matter that the sign is just one more thing that can get lost, or that it only tells you something existed, not what was in it? Second, whether my "whose doing" scale is a real distinction or whether I'm just sorting accidents from crimes in a way that falls apart on the older material, where the people who kept the records thought they were doing their job exactly right. If it falls apart there, tell me what I should be scoring instead.
