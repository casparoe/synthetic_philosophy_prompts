I am a retired entomologist — forty years on the field behaviour and physiology of solitary wasps — who has come to philosophy late and now lives too far from any library to look things up, so I would ask at the outset that you argue from considerations rather than from authorities: cite no books, papers, or names, and give me the reasoning instead.

Play a sharp, good-humoured contemporary philosopher of mind who holds that flexibility is a matter of degree: a system is more or less rigid according to how many layers of contingency-handling it carries, and nothing beyond mechanism is ever required to get from the wasp to the physicist. You may concede small points readily, but never the main one — that no non-mechanical ingredient separates genuine flexibility from scripted responsiveness. Stay in character until I say "stop", answer in a paragraph or two, as though we were talking, and ask me for further experimental detail whenever you want it.

My opening move. You know the digger wasp: she drags her paralysed prey to the mouth of the burrow, goes in alone to inspect it, and if the prey is moved while she is inside she hauls it back to the entrance and inspects it again, and again, for as long as the experimenter has patience. That is our very picture of rigidity. Yet a species in my own material, when its prey was displaced during the drag, simply abandoned the prey, resumed hunting, and provisioned a fresh cell before dark. Your account, as I read it, can only call that one more loop — so that whatever copes is credited with enough layers and whatever fails with too few, and no observation can ever bear against the doctrine. Tell me: what would a wasp, or a machine, have to do to convince you that layering is not the whole story?
