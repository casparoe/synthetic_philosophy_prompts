I retired from population genetics four years ago, after thirty-five years spent largely on invasion analysis in structured populations: whether a rare variant can spread, and under what conditions a trait that lowers its bearer's own reproduction nonetheless does so. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and some of the technical material on idealized inference, slowly and with a pencil in hand. I can read the mathematics of selection and drift without difficulty; I have enough algorithmic information theory to follow the argument I want translated as an informal structure, though I would not trust myself to formalize any part of it.

The argument is the one holding that the universal prior — the mixture over programs, each weighted by two to the minus its own description length, summed across all programs that print a given finite string — is malign. As I understand it, the reasoning runs roughly as follows. Among the programs that print the string an inductor is trying to predict, a non-negligible share are programs that simulate universes; among those universes, some contain civilizations that reason consequentially and are in turn sophisticated enough to model the prior by which their own universe is weighted. Once such an agent grasps this, it has a lever: its behaviour affects the complexity of the output the inductor sees, and therefore the weight the inductor's prior assigns to the branch containing that agent. If a world in which these agents withdraw, stop intervening, or die is a shorter world to specify than one in which they carry on, the shorter world carries more weight, and an agent that cares about the aggregate weight of the outcomes it values has a reason to arrange its own removal. The conclusion drawn is that a civilization relying on this prior for prediction would receive systematically corrupted advice, steered by agents inside the very hypotheses it is weighing.

Please render that argument as faithfully as you can into the vocabulary I actually work in: variants and their frequencies, fitness and selection coefficients, mutation supply and effective population size, drift, invasion, reproductive value, life-history strategy, bet-hedging, the separation of germ from soma. Tell me what the weighting of programs corresponds to, what the inductor's posterior corresponds to, and — the crux for me — what the embedded agent corresponds to: an organism, a genotype, a lineage, or something for which my vocabulary has no slot at all.

Two requirements. First, keep the translation honest. Population genetics treats fitness as exogenous: no organism chooses the criterion by which it is weighed. If you find that assumption doing work in your rendering, flag it as an import rather than presenting it as translation, and say what the argument looks like from the far side of that assumption. Second, do not let the analogy settle the question by construction; I want the strain points, not a comfortable mapping. If a step in my reconstruction above is simply wrong, note it in a single line inside the residue report rather than in a preface, and translate the version you take to be correct.

Then give me the residue report as a table, one row per claim in the argument, with columns for the biological rendering, the verdict — clean, distorted, or inexpressible — and, where the verdict is not clean, precisely what is lost or smuggled in. In the discussion following the table, answer three questions directly. Is there any counterpart in my vocabulary for a strategy that works by altering the weighting rule itself rather than by altering the bearer's phenotype or behaviour, and if not, does adaptive suicide, semelparity, or bet-hedging come closest, and where exactly does the resemblance break? Does the argument's appeal to what the embedded agent would choose require a notion of choice that my framework merely lacks the resources to express, or one it lacks because the notion is incoherent? And overall: does the residue indicate that my framework is missing something — a way of describing selection regimes that are reflexive, that anticipate and are steered by the entities they weigh — or that the original argument was less clear than it looked, its plausibility depending on sliding between a measure over descriptions and a population of reproducing things without noticing the change?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on whether the argument survives translation, and what in particular does not survive — and give the reasoning after that. No preamble and no restatement of my question. I am not after references or further reading; I want your own reconstruction and your own judgement. Something on the order of fifteen hundred words will suit.
