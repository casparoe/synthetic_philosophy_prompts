i'm teaching a jurisprudence seminar and the week on legal moralism always goes the same way: someone says "but the law enforces morality everywhere already — seatbelts, insider trading", someone else says "harm principle, done", and nobody ever reads Devlin's thing as an actual argument with premises. so instead of a summary handout i want to give them a poem.

form: a Petrarchan sonnet, iambic pentameter (loosely — don't wreck the sense to save the meter), octave ABBAABBA, sestet CDECDE, so the final two lines deliberately don't rhyme with each other. i want that non-resolution to be felt rather than merely present: the sestet shouldn't close on an epigram or a tidy moral, it should close on something still open.

the content constraint is the part that matters. it's one speaker throughout, not a dialogue, and not two talking heads trading quatrains. the octave is a juror — call him Devlin's man on the Clapham omnibus — in or just after 1957, when the Wolfenden report says that what consenting adults do in private isn't the law's business. he is not a bigot and not a cartoon; he should sound like someone who genuinely thinks the disgust in the jury box is the only instrument a community has for knowing what it can't survive, and who can lay out the disintegration argument as steps rather than as a mood: a society is held together by its shared morality at least as much as by its government; treason is punished because it dissolves the state, immorality because it dissolves the bond; the man who feels intolerance, indignation and disgust is reporting a fact about that bond, not confessing a preference. let him have the word "fabric" in the octave — it's his metaphor and he's entitled to it.

the volta is time. the sestet is the same man a decade or so on, and the things he was certain would come apart haven't — or not visibly. here's where i need an argument and not a confession scene: don't let him convert, and don't let him just shrug that people are sturdier than he supposed. the point he has to arrive at himself, in his own voice, is that his thesis can't lose: every counterexample gets re-described as slow rot, hairline cracks, a generation's lag, and he notices that a claim no evidence could shake was never a claim about what holds societies together — it was his own revulsion in a lab coat. the last two lines should land precisely there, not on "morals change" but on something like *i've been reading my taste as a fact about survival*. ban "fabric" from the sestet — he gives the metaphor up — and no "slippery slope", no "yuck", no "who am i to judge", and no naming Hart, Devlin, or Mill anywhere in the poem.

please don't hedge this into a both-sides piece. i want it to have a verdict and to earn it; i'd rather it be unfair to Devlin in a specific, interesting way then fair to him in a vague one.

after the poem, give me about five sentences: which side you think it comes down on and how the rhyme scheme's break between octave and sestet supports that; where you took liberties — in particular which reading of Devlin you assumed (the strong disintegration one, or the softer claim that law has an expressive function of its own), and whether the juror's realization is the objection Hart would actually press or a cheap psychologising of a respectable argument; and anything in there you know is historically loose. be definite in that note too — don't list the readings you could have taken, say which one you took and why.
