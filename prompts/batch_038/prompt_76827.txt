I'm speccing a pictorial notation for showing non-experts what a risk model actually outputs — probabilities, ranges, what-if scenarios — and the only real precedent is Neurath's picture language, so before I borrow anything from him I want to know what I should be demanding of an account of picture languages. Give me the spec: what any theory of what a picture language is, and of when one is adequate, has to deliver. Which phenomena does it have to explain (why does rendering a bigger quantity as more identical little figures work for some comparisons and fall apart for others), which cases does it have to get right, which distinctions can't it collapse — self-explanitory vs. merely convention-bound, a chart that is silent about something vs. one that marks it as unknown, faithful to the source data vs. memorable — and what does it have to stay consistent with about how pictures get read. For each requirement, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement and not just a taste. Then tell me which ones aren't negotiable and which are leftovers from the job the Vienna museum happened to have in the 1930s, which was adult visual education for a municipal socialist public. Neurath conceded the thing was never a full language, only something language-like: what precisely is it missing — negation, generality, conditionals, relations, uncertainty — and is that a gap you could close or a ceiling on the medium itself? If some subset of these can't all be had at once, say which and why; that's worth more to me than the list being exhaustive. Same for whichever single requirement does the most to rule candidates out. Don't run existing notations through it, I've seen them.
