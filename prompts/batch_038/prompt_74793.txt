I sit on the exhibits-and-grants commitee of a regional natural history museum. Our curator wants funding for a traveling show, working title 'Collapse: How Civilizations Destroy Themselves,' built around three cases: the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE, the Classic Maya cities, and Rapa Nui. The throughline is Diamond-shaped — each society outran its environment and in the end chose to fail. Two of us object. One says the Rapa Nui gallery is a couple decades stale: plenty of recent work puts the forest loss substantially on the rats the Polynesian settlers brought, and puts the actual demographic catastrophe in the slave raids and epidemics of the 1860s onward, which is a story about outsiders rather than self-inflicted ecocide. The other says the frame is dishonest right across the board: the Maya didn't vanish, southern cities emptied out while things kept going north and there are still millions of Maya speakers; and in the Bronze Age what "collapsed" was a palace bureaucracy, while the farmers who had been feeding it may well have eaten better afterward. The curator's reply is that an exhibit needs a story, that 'complexity stopped paying for itself' won't hold a school group for ten minutes, and that any frame you pick is somebody's politics anyway.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote: fund as proposed, fund with conditions, or decline. If conditions, make them concrete — what the throughline becomes instead, and what a panel label in each of the three galleries would have to say and would have to avoid saying. And be fair to the curator: give me the strongest version of the case for keeping a dramatic arc, not a memo that just ratifies the objectors.
