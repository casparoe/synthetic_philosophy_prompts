So me and my roommate spent what was supposed to be two hours of assembling a bookshelf on the question of whether an ant colony has a mind. She's read the Hofstadter piece with Aunt Hillary, the colony he has a conversation with, and she kept saying the ants are just neurons and the mind is up at the colony level. I'm the skeptic, or I thought I was. But somewhere around the third hour I made a move she couldn't answer, and then she made a counter-move I can't answer, and now I've got the shelf half-built and I'm still thinking about it, so I'm outsourcing.

My move was the standard ones: there's nobody home, no single place where the colony's experience could be, the ants are too dumb for it to add up to anything, and a colony's "decisions" are just what you get when you describe a big pile of simple stimulus-response loops from far away. She brushed all of that off. What I couldn't brush off was her version, which was not about intelligence or about experience at all. She said: an ant colony isn't one thing that wants stuff, it's a crowd of things that each want their own stuff, and the crowd only looks like a single want-er if you squint. Her examples: workers in some species sneaking eggs into the brood and other workers policing them out; slime molds where certain strains reliably dodge being the part that dies to hold up the stalk; ants from the same species but different nests tearing each other apart. Then she said the killer line, which was basically that neurons don't have little careers, they don't have a side, they don't negotiate — so the colony-is-like-a-brain analogy breaks exactly at the joint that matters, and it's not a joint anyone in that dialogue notices, because Hofstadter seems to just build in that the ants all work for the common good even against their own interest, which is precisely the thing entomologists spend whole careers showing is false.

Here's what's bugging me. I think she's tracking something real, and I also think she's being inconsistent, because five minutes later she said "part of me wants to take that job in Denver" without blinking — same structure, sub-parts pulling in different directions, except there it's one person with mixed feelings and in the colony case it's supposedly a mere crowd. And I don't think the difference she cares about is consciousness in the parts, since she also refuses to say a corporation has a mind, and corporations are made of obviously conscious people with obviously their own ends, and yet we say things like "the company thinks it'll miss its targets" all day long. So there seem to be two separate axes getting tangled up: whether the parts have experiences, and whether the parts have their own goals that can conflict with the whole's. And there's a middle case nobody talks about, like a bee swarm picking a nest site, where the scouts are individually invested in different cavities and effectively argue, and then the whole thing commits to one answer.

So — does what she was doing have a name? I want the concept, not just a word. Specifically: what are the nearest established ideas and how does each one differ from hers (I'm guessing "the parts are conscious so the whole can't be a mind" isn't it, and neither is plain free-riding or selfish-gene talk, since she wasn't talking about incentives)? Is the thing she's pointing at best read as a criterion for being one *agent*, one *individual*, or one *subject* — I suspect those are three different questions and she's sliding between them. Is the Denver case actually the same structure, or is there a difference that would let her be consistent after all? And does her criterion come in degrees, or is it a switch? If nothing in the literature actually names this axis, coin something, with a definition and a couple of boundary cases — I'd want it to say something about the clonal aspen grove, where the parts are genetically identical and completely on the same page and yet nobody wants to hand it a mind, which suggests being conflict-free isn't sufficient either. No need for references, I just want the carving.
