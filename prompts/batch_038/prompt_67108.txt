I am head of animal welfare at a mid-sized aquaculture group in northern Europe. We farm Atlantic salmon in marine cages across four sites — around 1.6 million fish at any one time — and whiteleg shrimp in lined ponds at two sites, on the order of thirty to forty million animals per crop cycle, harvested twice a year. I sit on a monthly welfare and operations committee with the managing director, the production manager and the head of sales; none of them has any training in ethics, we have a fixed annual welfare capital budget, and we have about two hours per meeting in which to reach a decision that the board will accept. Our salmon are stunned and bled at harvest. Our shrimp are harvested by draining the ponds and immersing the animals in a chilled ice-water slurry, a practice one of our retail auditors has queried. Our principal retailer has now asked us to demonstrate how we prioritise welfare improvements, noting that decapod crustaceans fall within the scope of the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022.

The difficulty is that I have no defensible way of ranking the proposals that come to me, because the two halves of the business differ on every dimension that matters ethically: the strength of the evidence that these animals are capable of suffering, the number of individuals affected, how much we can actually measure on farm, and what is technically feasible at harvest. Please write me a prioritisation procedure that this committee can genuinely run inside two hours.

The procedure should consist of ordered steps, and for each step it should say what counts as a sufficient answer, what makes an answer fail, and where the step hands the decision over to a person because the question is one of values rather than of fact. In particular, it needs to address the following, explicitly and in sequence:

1. How much weight to give evidence about capacity for suffering when that evidence is strong and consolidated for one taxon and thin and actively disputed for the other — without either letting uncertainty license inaction or letting precaution consume the entire budget.
2. How to compare a change affecting many millions of individuals whose moral status is contested against a change affecting a smaller number of individuals whose status is not, and whether this requires a trade-off ratio; if it does, say where such a ratio could come from and flag it as a value premise rather than a finding.
3. A class of "no-regret" measures that are justified whichever way the sentience question resolves.
4. The measurement problem: the indicators we can cheaply collect (mortality, growth, feed conversion, deformity rates, fin condition, eye and rostrum damage, behavioural agitation at harvest) are correlated with productivity, so a procedure that ranks by what is measurable will drift towards whatever also improves yield. It should distinguish interventions that improve welfare and output together from those that improve welfare at a real cost to output, since only the latter test whether the commitment is genuine, and it should note which indicators can be gamed.
5. Where the procedure's value premises should be written down as premises, so that a critic or an auditor can locate the disagreement instead of quarrelling with arithmetic.

Then say which of its own steps would give different answers under a view that treats killing as a distinct wrong, and under a view that asks whether the animal's life is worth living rather than how much suffering occurs in it. Be candid about which steps are judgement calls wearing the clothes of procedure, and close with a plain statement of what the procedure cannot do.

Finally, run the procedure on three live proposals: (a) roughly £600,000 to replace the shrimp harvest slurry with electrical stunning in the pond water followed by immediate chilling, where efficacy in high-salinity water is uncertain, where immobilisation is easily mistaken for insensibility, and where we have no validated indicator of unconsciousness in a shrimp; (b) a twenty per cent reduction in stocking density at one salmon site to lower lice burden and fin damage, costing perhaps £400,000 of revenue a year with no retailer premium available; and (c) a plan to double shrimp production by bringing the second site to full capacity next year. At least one of these should come out badly for us, and if so I would rather you said so plainly than softened it.

Please give me the procedure in a form that could be handed to the committee as a single page, with the three applications shown as worked examples against it. I do not want a reference list or a reading list; where you are relying on a contested empirical claim, mark it as contested in the body of the answer. End with a short paragraph stating how confident you are in the procedure's central claims — especially in the weight you assign to the crustacean evidence and in whatever ranking rule you propose — and identifying the one step or premise you are least sure of, together with what would change your mind about it.
