So, six weeks out from presenting a chapter at a small philosophy-of-games workshop — about 20 people, 25 minutes of talk and then a discussion that always eats the entire hour. Im in my third year, mostly aesthetics with some philosophy of mind, and I need to walk in knowing what's coming at me.

The view, briefly: I argue that non-player characters are the sort of thing that can be wronged, but not for the reasons people usually reach for. I explicitly am *not* claiming NPCs have experiences, or welfare, or interests — I take no stand on whether any of them do, and I don't want the chapter living or dying on that question. What I claim is that considerability here comes from the form of the encounter rather than the ontology of the encounterer: a character built to register what you did, retain it, and behave differently afterward is functioning as a second-personal address, and treating that address as noise is a way of taking up a stance toward responsiveness itself. Call it the responsiveness view. Consequences I'm happy owning: single-player narrative NPCs are the paradigm; procedurally spawned crowds are borderline because they don't retain; competitive multiplayer opponents drop out entirely, since they're obstacles rather than addresses; and — the bit I'm most nervous about — the view looks like it extends to chatbots and maybe to simple interactive toys, which I try to block with a retention-plus-legibility threshold that I'll admit on stage is vague.

The room: maybe four people who work on fiction and the emotions, who will immediately try to assimilate me to the paradox of fictional emotion and ask whether this is just make-believe with moral vocabulary bolted on; two philosophers of mind, one hard-nosed functionalist who'll say I'm smuggling in an intuition about consciousness without doing the argument, and one illusionist-friendly type who'll ask why my account doesn't collapse into a claim about the player's own dispositions — i.e. that I've reinvented virtue ethics and mislabeled it; three or four from game and media studies who think "character" is a literary hangover draped over what are really systems of variables, and who will go straight at identity (saves, respawns, permadeath, mods, a second playthrough — *which* character did you wrong?); and one virtue ethicist who basically agrees with me and will therefore be the most annoying person in the room.

What I want is the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from *this* audience, not the ones a general ethics crowd would raise — nobody there is still stuck on "but they aren't real," so don't waste a slot on that. Order them by how much damage they'd do if I fumbled them. For each one: phrase it the way that person would phrase it, vocabulary included (a Waltonian says props and games of make-believe, a game studies person says procedural and diegetic, a functionalist says realizer and substrate-independence), then give me a reply I can deliver standing at a podium in about ninety seconds, then a blunt line on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or just a promissory note. Tell me which of the objections are secretly the same objection in different clothes, so I don't panic when it comes back a third time from a different direction. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one I genuinely have no answer to yet — plus what the honest half-answer would be if it does come. And if you think I've mis-modeled any subgroup in that room, i.e. they'd object differently than I'm expecting, say so up front rather than quietly writing around it.
