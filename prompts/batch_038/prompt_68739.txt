I work on agentic systems — models that call tools, read and write to a vector store, and keep a persistent scratchpad across a task. Over the past year my group has settled into a way of talking about these systems that we took almost verbatim from the extended mind literature: a deployed model plus its retrieval index and tools is a single cognitive system, in the same way that a person with a notebook is a single cognitive system once the notebook is reliably carried, trusted, and consulted. We invoke Otto and his notebook, and the parity principle behind it, to argue two things. The modest one: benchmarking the bare weights is a category error, since we would never assess a person's memory by isolating their hippocampus from the rest of their life. The ambitious one: the boundary of the system that has cognitive states — maybe the boundary of whatever mind is present at all — runs around the whole loop, so ascriptions of understanding, remembering, and believing should target model plus scaffolding rather than model alone. One of my colleagues thinks this is just the coupling-constitution objection with extra steps: retrieval is causally coupled to the weights the way a pencil is coupled to long multiplication, and no amount of reliability converts coupling into constitution.

I would like a careful audit of the analogy rather than a defense of either side. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly: what in our stack plays Otto, what plays the notebook, what plays Inga's biological memory, and what plays each of the features Clark and Chalmers insist on — constant availability, ease of retrieval, endorsement of the contents at the time of writing, and the disposition to trust what comes back. Where our stack has no counterpart for one of those features, say so plainly rather than stretching something to fit. Then work through the disanalogies, separating those that are merely cosmetic from those that bear on the argument. I expect at least the following to be assessed: that the retrieved corpus consists overwhelmingly of other people's utterances rather than the system's own prior commitments, which may make retrieval closer to testimony or perception than to memory; that the same weights serve many concurrent sessions against a shared, vendor-controlled index that can be re-embedded or revoked at will; that nothing written to the scratchpad is ever re-encountered by a continuing subject who could endorse or reject it; and that the parity principle is a claim about external processes in a creature already granted a mind, whereas here the presence of a mind is itself what is disputed.

For each disanalogy, tell me whether it merely weakens the comparison, whether it inverts the analogy into evidence for the rival conclusion, or whether it targets only the ambitious claim while leaving the modest one standing. Also tell me what the analogy quietly imports from the notebook case that our stack lacks — I suspect the notebook's contents are supposed to have been earned by the person who wrote them, and that this is doing more work than anyone in my group admits. Then state whether either of our two conclusions survives the audit, and if the ambitious one fails but the modest one holds, explain what principled line separates them, since both were supposed to follow from the same parity reasoning. Finally, if the notebook analogy is the wrong vehicle, propose a better one drawn from something I actually work with — a scaffold someone else owns, a prosthesis, a cached computation, a shared read-only dependency — and say which of the analogy's load-bearing work the replacement can and cannot carry.
