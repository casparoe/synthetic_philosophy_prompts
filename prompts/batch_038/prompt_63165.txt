ok so I'm running a small workshop and I've got two camps who have spent a year sniping at each other about whether we should be pushing to build permanent off-world settlements. One side — call them the expansionists — holds there's a standing moral reason to get self-sustaining communities established beyond Earth: spread the risk of a single-planet bottleneck, keep options open, and don't foreclose on the enormous number of future people who could exist. The other side — call them the closure camp — holds the program is either impermissible or wildly premature: it's moral hazard dressed up as insurance, it exports an extractive logic to a place where nobody can audit it, and a sealed habitat with no rescue option is a rotten place to put people who never agreed to be there.

They should have done an adversarial collaboration a long time ago and produced a single document both could sign. Write that document.

Here's what I need in it:

- A numbered section of claims both sides can actually sign. That should include the physical and engineering floor they don't dispute — delta-v, radiation shielding, the point past which resupply is impossible, the fact that nobody has ever run a materially closed life-support system for years (the two-year Biosphere 2 closure in the early '90s with its crew of eight is the closest thing to a data point and the oxygen and food numbers were bad), the consent problem for anyone born inside the system, and the legal baseline that outer space isn't open to national appropriation. Then the shared normative claims: what both sides agree the question is even about. Don't pad this section with vacuous stuff like "both sides care about human flourishing" — if a claim wouldn't be contested by anyone it doesn't belong here.

- A disagreements table. Columns: the issue, the expansionist formulation, the closure formulation, and what species of disagreement it is — factual, normative, or verbal. Every position has to be worded so its own side would accept it as their view, not a strawman they'd spend the first paragraph correcting.

- The cruxes, maybe four of them: the sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move somebody. I suspect one is the exit-option question — does having a viable off-world backup make us more careful about Earth or less, and which direction does the evidence run? Another is whether the absence of anyone to displace removes the colonial wrong, or whether the wrong actually lives in the structure (who does the one-way labor, who pays, who inherits) rather than in displacement. Another is whether far-future off-world lives belong in the calculus at all or whether that's just a Pascalian move with better PR. Name the ones you think are real; don't force mine in if they aren't.

- For each crux, the case, dataset, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, plus each side's prediction written down in advance so we can go back and check who called it. Historical analogies are probably the best available evidence here — Antarctic winter-over stations, submarine crews, long-duration ISS expeditions, the actual record of terrestrial colonization — and I want the predictions stated concretely enough to be wrong.

- A closing section on what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and an honest note about whether any chunk of this fight was really about words. If some of it was, say which part and what the shared meaning should have been all along.

Aim for somewhere around 1400–1800 words. Don't adjudicate — I don't want a verdict on who's right, and watch that the document doesn't quietly drift into one camp's vocabulary while pretending to be neutral. Also don't seperate the cruxes from the disagreements table by just restating them; a crux should be something narrower and more settleable than the row it came out of.
