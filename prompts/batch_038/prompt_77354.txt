I am drafting a position paper for a game-theory reading group on two-person bargaining solution concepts, and I want the package below audited for internal coherence before I circulate it. The setting is fixed throughout: two parties; utilities cardinal and unique up to independent positive affine transformations of each party's own scale; a feasible set of utility pairs that is compact, convex, and comprehensive; a disagreement point strictly inside it, and strictly worse for both than some feasible agreements. A solution is a function assigning one feasible pair to each such problem.

Here is what I hold.

1. Pareto efficiency and symmetry are non-negotiable. A solution that recommends a pair both parties would rather leave, or that treats parties alike in every relevant respect unequally, is not a candidate for me at all.

2. So is invariance to affine transformations of utility: changing the zero point or the units in which one party's payoffs are recorded must not change the real allocation the solution recommends.

3. I reject the Nash solution, and my reason is its failure of monotonicity. There are problems in which the feasible set expands in one party's favor — the best payoff she can attain rises while the best the other can attain does not — and yet the maximizer of the Nash product awards her less than before. A rule that penalizes a party because the pie grew in her direction is disqualified. I therefore endorse the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution.

4. Losing independence of irrelevant alternatives is, as I understand it, precisely the price of the behavior in (3), and I pay that price without reservation.

5. But I also hold a requirement I would call non-bossiness of the menu: no party should be able to improve its recommended share merely by enlarging the set of available agreements with a proposal that is jointly feasible and not dominated by anything else on the menu, yet so lopsided against the other side that the two would never in practice settle on it. If an addition changes nothing about what the parties could realistically agree to, it should not change the recommendation.

6. Methodologically I am implementation-first. I distrust solution concepts that exist only as axiom lists, and I count a concept as earned when it arises as the limit of a concrete non-cooperative protocol. The protocol I find most compelling is alternating offers with a small probability at each stage that negotiations collapse; as I understand it, that protocol is the standard vehicle for underwriting one of these two solutions without appealing to axioms at all.

7. Two objections from a colleague that I do not find troubling, but that I want checked rather than assumed away. First, he says (2) and (3) cannot both be held, because monotonicity is phrased in terms of rises and falls in payoff levels, which is cardinal talk, whereas (2) says those levels have no fixed units. Second, he says the Kalai–Smorodinsky construction — running from the disagreement point toward the vector of each party's best attainable payoff and taking where that segment meets the frontier — can land outside the feasible set, or on a point that is not efficient.

Please audit the package. Identify the genuine tensions and rank them by severity; for each, say exactly which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere, and say in particular whether any of the tensions can be repaired jointly rather than one at a time. Then say which of the apparent tensions dissolve, and why. Finish with the single revision that would leave me with the most coherent position overall. Begin your response with the bottom line in the first sentence — the most serious tension and the revision it forces — and only then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of the question.
