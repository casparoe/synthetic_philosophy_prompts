So I've gotten into the habit of checking the annual country-by-country happiness rankings every spring, and the more I look at how they're actually built the weirder they seem to me. The headline table that everybody screenshots comes from one question — the ladder one, where you picture the best possible life for you as a 10 and the worst possible life for you as a 0 and say which rung you're standing on — asked of about a thousand people per country, averaged over the last three years of surveys, then averaged again into a single national number and ranked. But the same outfit also publishes a seperate set of numbers built from questions about how much people smiled, laughed and felt enjoyment yesterday, and those lists look nothing like the ladder lists: a pile of Latin American countries sit near the top on the daily-emotion measures and well down the ladder, while the Nordics are basically the reverse. National statistics offices do the same double thing — the British one asks how satisfied you are with your life, how worthwhile your activities feel, how happy you were yesterday and how anxious you were yesterday, all on 0-to-10, and prints the four series side by side without ever saying which one is the real thing.

What account of well-being does the ranking practice actually presuppose? I want to know which parts of it are load-bearing and which are just convenient — like, does ranking countries by a three-year national mean commit you to some particular view about how individual welfare aggregates, or about whether the target is a stable standing property rather than a mood? And the ladder is deliberately framed so each person uses their own idea of a good life, yet the numbers get pooled across cultures and treated as comparable: what has to be true about self-report and interpersonal comparison for that to be legitimate? Tell me which rival theories the practice quietly rules out, which ones it's actually consistent with even though it doesn't need them, whether publishing both tracks at once is a contradiction or a deliberate hedge, and what specifically would have to change — the question, the aggregation, the whole ranking format — if we decided the yesterday-emotions track was the one measuring happiness all along.
