1. causation is the ancestral of counterfactual dependence — c causes e if there's a chain of steps each counterfactually dependent on the one before. that's how I handle late preemption, and I keep transitivity.
2. the counterfactuals are non-backtracking in the strict sense: to evaluate "if c hadn't happened" I hold the actual past fixed right up to just before c, allow at most one tiny local departure from the laws there, then run the actual laws forward. that's what kills the bogus reverse dependence (shorter shadow → shorter pole). I take this to be a fact about similarity between worlds, not about how people happen to talk.
3. what I like about this whole family of views — the counterfactual one and the interventionist one — is that neither helps itself to causation. both analyse it in non-causal terms: similarity across worlds in the first case, what would happen under manipulation in the second.
4. as a working tool I lean interventionist: a causal claim is a claim about what would change under an intervention, and the reason the barometer doesn't cause the storm is that surgically setting it to a different reading leaves the storm exactly where it was.
5. but "intervention" has to be spelled out with no reference to agents or anybody's practical abilities, and no leaning on changes that couldn't actually have happened. I think the macro world is deterministic, and I'm a realist about causation anyway — the causal facts don't care whether there are manipulators around.
6. causes don't have to raise the probability of their effects. the pill → pregnancy → thrombosis case is genuine causation even though the pill lowers the overall chance of the clot.
