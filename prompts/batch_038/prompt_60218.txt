I convene a small joint seminar between our law faculty and our philosophy department on automated decision-making, and next week's session is on the responsibility gap. Two of my regulars have dug in, and I mean to break the deadlock with a designed case rather than another round of traded intuitions. The first, a tort lawyer, holds that the gap is an artefact of philosophers conflating moral responsibility with liability: vicarious liability, strict product liability and organisational negligence will always find a solvent defendant, so there is never a practical vacuum, only a disagreement about who pays. The second holds the mirror-image view: that there will always be someone answerable in law and almost never anyone properly blameworthy, so the gap is real but purely moral. I want a scenario that forces both of them to say precisely which of their claims they are defending, and for that I need a case built to a fairly demanding specification.

The constraints are as follows. First, the setting must be a realistic civilian deployment of a trained, partly autonomous system — an insulin-infusion fleet across a group of care homes, a coastal vessel-traffic management system, a municipal flood-defence controller, or something of that kind. No battlefield, no autonomous weapons, no trolley problems: I want to avoid the just-war literature doing the work for us, and I want Sparrow's punishment-based argument to have to survive being transplanted into a context where nobody is ordering anyone killed. Second, the outcome must be a death or a grave injury for which an equivalently placed human decision-maker would plainly have been blameworthy. Third, and this is the constraint I care about most, the gap must not be generated by opacity alone: stipulate that a complete post hoc mechanistic explanation of the failure is available, and then show that the gap survives the explanation. Interpretive difficulty may feature in the case, but it must not be load-bearing. Fourth, the failure mode must have been identified in advance in an internal risk assessment and knowingly accepted as within tolerance, and yet the acceptance decision must itself have been defensible by the standards of the profession — I want foresight without negligence, which is where I suspect the interesting pressure sits. Fifth, every human link in the chain — the researchers who specified the objective, the engineer who curated the training data, the certifier, the procurer, the operator on duty, the firm's board — must be individually exculpable, and the excuses must be of *different* kinds rather than one excuse replicated six times: at least one epistemic, at least one normative (the person was required by their role or by regulation to act as they did), and at least one counterfactual (their alternative course of action would not have prevented the outcome in any event). Sixth, the system must not be a moral agent: no stipulated personhood, no inner states, no talk of its having chosen anything.

Deliver the case first, as a single paragraph of roughly 250 words, and then demonstrate that it meets each specification rather than asserting that it does. The demonstration should walk through the candidate bearers one at a time, giving the excuse and saying whether it actually holds; then audit which of the three gaps the case opens, treating the now-standard tripartite distinction separately rather than running them together — a gap in culpability, a gap in moral and public accountability, and a gap in active, forward-looking responsibility — and say plainly if the case opens only one or two of the three, since a case that opens all of them automatically has probably stipulated its conclusions. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the most work and tell me what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed: if the failure mode had never appeared in the risk register; if the operator had possessed a functioning and timely override; if the mechanistic explanation had genuinely been unavailable. Next, put the case to my two seminar regulars: how far do vicarious liability, strict product liability, enterprise or organisational liability, the distributed "many hands" move, and the demand that someone be capable of bearing punishment actually close the gap, and against which of the three gaps does each reply succeed or fail? Finish with an honest note on whether any part of the case is smuggling in its verdict by stipulation, and on whether the whole construction would be weakened if the system had been independently certified and audited before deployment. I am not after references or a bibliography — I want the design and the reasoning.
