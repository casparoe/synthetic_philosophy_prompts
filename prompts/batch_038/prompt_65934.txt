I am the training director at a sixty-person structural engineering firm, and I am rewriting the three-year development program for our junior engineers. The problem I need help with is not the one people usually raise: our AI tools are checked. A licensed engineer reviews anything the software produces before it leaves the building, so the familiar risks — confident error, fabricated code citations, undetected nonsense in a calculation — are largely handled. What is not handled is something else. The routine work we now hand to the software (load take-downs, connection checks, first-pass design notes, code lookups) is precisely the work that used to occupy a junior's first two years, and that used to be how a junior acquired the capacity to catch a senior's mistake. We appear to be consuming the pipeline that produced our checkers.

Give me the most important implications of this for how I should design those three years. Work through the epistemology of testimony and epistemic dependence — Hardwig's picture of modern expertise, on which rational deference rests on a division of labor plus the social machinery of incentives, mutual monitoring, and accountability that makes experts worthy of it — and treat my situation as a test of whether that machinery survives when the "expert" has no stakes, no reputation to lose, and no one who can hold it to account. Every implication should be one that follows from that framework rather than a restatement of the general worry about deskilling, and each should tell me something about a choice I actually face: what work to keep deliberately inefficient, what to stop requiring, what a junior's competence should consist in if not independent recalculation, and how I would know the program is failing.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Where you think the analogy to human expert communities breaks down in a way that changes what I should do, say so inside the relevant item.
