Three months ago I was appointed as the external lay member of the animal welfare and ethical review panel at a biomedical research institute. My background is in law rather than biology, and I have spent the first part of my term trying to reverse-engineer what principle my colleagues are actually applying, because the one they state out loud — that the only thing they look at is the complexity of an animal's nervous system, never its charisma or its resemblance to us — does not visibly generate the pattern of decisions I have watched them reach. When I pressed two members on this, one said that what mattered was the raw number of neurons an animal has, the other said that what mattered was how many neurons it has relative to its body, and a third said both were beside the point since what actually decides cases is whether the animal can be shown to do something flexible. I have gone back through the minutes, and I cannot tell which of them is right, because the record seems to fit all three.

Here is what the panel has done in the meetings I have attended:

1. A routine pain study in mice: approved with a standard harm-benefit analysis, no unusual conditions.
2. Work on zebrafish up to five days after fertilization: treated as outside the panel's remit entirely, with no welfare case required, on the grounds that the nervous system is not yet functionally mature.
3. A sensitization assay in fruit flies: outside remit, same reasoning.
4. A learning study in common octopus: returned twice because the panel would not accept the anesthesia and killing plan, and one member argued at length that a nervous system two-thirds of whose neurons sit in the arms makes it harder, not easier, to say where the animal's subject of experience is located — yet nobody proposed lowering the animal's status on that account.
5. A causal-reasoning study in New Caledonian crows: subjected to the full analysis the panel normally reserves for non-human primates. The chair explicitly said brain volume was irrelevant here, since a crow's brain is a small fraction of a macaque's; afterwards, several members said privately that neuron density was exactly what they had been reacting to.
6. An operant study in domestic pigs: approved with mandatory social housing and enrichment.
7. A noxious-stimulation assay in edible crabs: approved narrowly, with a strict single-exposure limit and no repeat testing; three members dissented, saying a decapod's nervous system is nothing like an insect's and nothing like a cephalopod's either.
8. Human blood-forming stem cells engrafted into immunodeficient mice: declared out of scope, because no neural tissue was involved.
9. Human glial progenitor cells engrafted into mouse brains, with the sponsor claiming enhanced learning: deferred, pending a general policy on animals containing human neural tissue. That policy was never written, and the proposal has simply sat there.

The case coming before us next month is this. A sponsor proposes to grow human cortical tissue from donor-derived stem cells in the lab and transplant it into the somatosensory cortex of young rats, where it is expected to be vascularized by the host and driven by input from the rat's own whiskers. The sponsor's estimate is that the graft will contribute roughly one percent of the neurons the rat already has, but that those neurons will be far more mature than any comparable amount of tissue kept in a dish, and more mature than one would expect from their number. The animals will then be trained on a whisker-dependent discrimination task, and the sponsor wants to record from the human neurons during training to test whether the graft changes how well the animals learn. The sponsor has also asked us to note that at the time of testing the graft will still be at a stage resembling fetal human cortex, while the rat itself is a healthy adult.

I would like the following, in no more than 900 words total, beginning with a single sentence stating your predicted verdict before any argument:

- Set out at least three decision rules that are jointly consistent with items 1 through 9, each phrased as a test the panel could actually apply to a new protocol, and say what each rule has to be supplemented with in order to survive the record — in particular, whether the crab decision and the octopus decision force a rule based on lineage or on neuron numbers to acquire an epicycle.
- Say what each rule delivers on the new case, and pinpoint where they diverge. My own guess is that they will separate on whether the graft counts as part of the animal's nervous system or as foreign tissue inside it, on whether maturity or integration is the trigger, and on whether the panel's jurisdiction attaches to the animal or to the tissue.
- Give me your best guess at what the panel will in fact decide, and separately the decision most consistent with the principle they claim to hold, and say plainly whether those two come apart and why.
- Identify the closest analog in the record, and defend that choice against the second-closest.
- Name the single further proposal — one the panel has not seen — that would discriminate best between the two strongest candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict about it.
- Finally, tell me what the panel is tacitly committing to either way: if it treats the graft as crossing a threshold, what principle has it adopted; if it treats the animal as a mouse-with-human-cells and defers again, what principle has it adopted by not deciding.

I will add citations and internal references myself; what I need from you is the analysis of the rule the panel is running and the recommendation I can take into the room.
