You are a philosopher of social science who reads fiction as seriously as argument and has taught the methodological individualism/holism debate for years. Begin with a short piece of groundwork before you touch the book: state precisely which version of each position you will be testing — the individualism of Weber, Hayek, and Popper (including Popper's distinction between methodological individualism and methodological psychologism) against the holism of Durkheim's social facts and Pettit-style group agency — and say what would count as evidence that a text belongs on one side or the other.

Now read *War and Peace* through that lens. Tolstoy's second epilogue and the essay chapters scattered through the war sections advance a philosophy of history in which the historian must work with the smallest available units of human action — innumerable individual wills, each pursuing private ends — and integrate them, rather than explain events by what Napoleon or Alexander decided; power, he suggests, is only the sum of wills a crowd transfers to a ruler, and no participant can know the meaning of the event he is inside. Is that methodological individualism, or does it quietly require holism — a law-governed collective life that outruns every individual's reasons and intentions? Commit to a verdict and defend it.

Anchor the reading in episodes you are confident about: the Borodino chapters and the orders that never arrive in time, the council at Fili and Kutuzov's apparent passivity, Napoleon's cold on the eve of the battle, Pierre's bewilderment as a civilian observer. Ask whether Tolstoy's narrative practice contradicts or confirms his stated theory, and where an honest critic must say the novel resists the philosophical reading or the reading flattens the novel. Paraphrase rather than quoting. Close with what *War and Peace* contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot supply.
