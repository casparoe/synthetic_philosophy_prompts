I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and last week somebody passed around two paragraphs from a popular book on animal intelligence that tried to explain Dennett's four kinds of creatures — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian ones — and now half the group talks about that summary as if it were the theory. Here's the passage, more or less verbatim:

"Dennett sorts minds into four grades arranged on a ladder of increasing flexibility. Darwinian creatures are hardwired: everything they can do was installed by natural selection, so they can't learn anything at all — sea slugs, or the digger wasp that will let its paralyzed bee get stolen rather than interrupt its routine. Skinnerian creatures added operant conditioning, so their behavior can be reshaped by rewards and punishments; that covers basically every vertebrate from fish up. Popperian creatures are the big step: instead of trying things out and being punished for the failures, they learn from other animals' mistakes, so their hypotheses die instead of they do — that's where the Popper name comes from. And Gregorian creatures are the tool-users: chimps stripping leaves off twigs to fish for termites, crows bending wire into hooks. Because a ladder is a ladder, there's no real gap between a tool-using crow and us, just more tools, which is why the moral wall between human and animal minds comes down once you take the scheme seriously. Dennett has essentially reinstated Aristotle's scale of souls with the theology deleted."

A few things bug me but I can't tell whether the bug is in the book or in me. The Popperian paragraph feels wrong — learning from someone else's failure looks like a *social* achievement, not the thing that would separate a pigeon from a chimp, and I doubt Dennett thinks chimps are just better at gossip than rats — but the bit about hypotheses dying in your place is obviously supposed to be the slogan, so what is Popperian-ness actually marking if not that? Second, I'm not convinced the ladder does the work the book wants: are the four grades genuinely nested so that every Gregorian creature is also Popperian, Skinnerian and Darwinian, or can a creature be, say, Popperian about food-caching and nothing else? And does tool use really define the top tier, or is Gregorian-ness about something those chimps and crows lack? Third, and this is where the group got properly stuck — if you apply the criteria honestly instead of rhetorically, where does a large language model land? It's built out of the accumulated products of Gregorian culture but doesn't seem to use tools in an environment, and whether it "tries candidate actions out in advance" is exactly what's in dispute.

So give me the audit. Which sentences would someone who actually works on this sign off on, which are simplifications that quietly drop something load-bearing, and which are just false — including whether the Aristotle comparison is the harmless compliment the book thinks it is. Then tell me whether the moral-wall conclusion survives once the errors are fixed, or whether it needed them to get off the ground. I'd rather keep the idea that the grades come in degrees than the idea that crows and chimps are Gregorian, but I want to know what I'm paying either way, and I'd like the LLM question answered rather than waved at.
