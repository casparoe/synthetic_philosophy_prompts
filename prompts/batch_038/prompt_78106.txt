ok so I've fallen down a rabbit hole on gambles that supposedly don't have expected values, and I want to find out what my own gut actually commits me to. Here's my verdicts on a bunch of coin games. In all of them we flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and n is the number of the flip that heads landed on.

**P**: heads on flip n pays (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars. So +$2 with probability 1/2, -$2 with probability 1/4, +$2.67 with probability 1/8, -$4 with probability 1/16, and so on. The probability-weighted payoffs are 1, -1/2, +1/3, -1/4, ... which adds up to ln 2, about 69 cents — except it only converges conditionally. My verdict: P is worth something, definitely more than nothing, and I'd gladly pay 50 cents to play it. But I don't think "its expected value is 69 cents" is literally true. I'd rather say it has no expected value at all.

**M**: exactly like P but every payoff has the opposite sign. Verdict: worse than P, obviously, and I'd want to be paid about 70 cents to play it.

**The package**: you play P and M off the same coin, so the same n comes up in both and they cancel. Verdict: worth exactly zero, every branch pays zero, and I'm more confident about that than about anything else on this list. But if the package is worth 0 and M is worth minus 70 cents, then arithmetic says P is worth plus 70 cents, which I just refused to say.

**Rearrangement**: someone told me that since P's weighted terms are the alternating harmonic series, and you can add those same terms in a different order to get any number you like including a million, P's value is up for grabs however you write it down. Verdict: no — the flip number isn't a notational convenience, each payoff is bolted to a specific probability, so re-summing in a new order doesn't produce a new value or a new game. But that was literally my only reason for saying P has no expected value, so now I don't know what my reason is.

**R**: a game with the same list of possible payoffs and the same list of probabilities as P, but paired up differently — say +$2 now goes with probability 1/64 instead of 1/2. Verdict: plainly a different gamble from P, and I wouldn't assume it's worth the same, though I can't say what it is worth.

**S**: plain St. Petersburg, 2^n dollars on flip n, infinite expected value. Verdict: I'd pay twenty bucks, no agonizing over it, money just has diminishing value to me. It strikes me as backwards that the infinite one gets a confident price and the 69-cent one gets none.

**D**: P except every payoff is exactly one dollar lower. Verdict: worse than P by exactly one dollar, and I'm more sure of that than I am of what P is worth in the first place.

**T**: P, except if heads hasn't come up in 40 flips the game ends and pays zero. Verdict: T has a real expected value, about 69 cents, and I'd pay roughly that. But chopping off an event with probability around one in a trillion shouldn't be the thing that converts a valueless gamble into a valuable one.

**L**: heads on flip n pays (-1)^(n+1) · n dollars. Verdict: converges no matter how you slice it, has a value, I'd pay close to it, no worries at all. The only difference from P is how fast the payoffs grow, and I find it weird that "does this gamble have a value" hinges on a growth rate.

So: is there one account of what a gamble is worth that gets all of these, or most of them? State it precisely and tell me which clause is doing the work for which case. Then tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — I assume at least one goes, probably M or the package. And say what it predicts about a thing I've been assuming without thinking: that if I played P a thousand times, the average payout would settle down around 69 cents. No reading list, I just want the principle.
