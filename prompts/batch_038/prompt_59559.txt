The question we set:

State Lewis's Principal Principle as exactly as you can, and explain what work the notion of admissible evidence does in it. Then explain the trouble that "undermining futures" cause for the Principle given Lewis's Humean account of chance, using this running case: a coin that will be tossed a million times after t; H, the chance hypothesis in force at t, says the chance of heads on each toss is 1/2; W is a possible continuation of the actual history such that, were W to obtain, the best systematization of the complete history would entail some different chance hypothesis H\*; and, by Humeanism, H itself assigns W a chance c, where c is small but not zero. Finally, say whether Lewis's later New Principle actually fixes the trouble, and why.

The student's answer:

The Principal Principle says that if the objective chance of A is x, then a rational agent's degree of belief in A should be x. Lewis's own formulation is conditional: your credence in A, given your total evidence together with the information that the chance of A is x, should be x. That is why admissibility matters, since not just any evidence can occupy the conditioning slot. Evidence is admissible as long as it doesn't itself tell you how A turns out. So learning that the coin is weighted toward heads is admissible, while being told by a time traveller that this particular toss will come up heads is not, because the second would let you set the chance aside altogether. The Principle is really an instance of van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, which says you should now defer to what you expect your future self to believe: chance is just the future's opinion about the present, so PP inherits whatever problems Reflection has with self-distrust.

Now take the case. Humeanism says the chance of heads isn't a separate feature of the world but whatever the best system over the whole mosaic says it is. W is a continuation on which the coin behaves in a way that makes H the wrong systematisation. Nothing about the present rules W out, since the chances are fixed by the complete history, future included, and any finite stretch of the mosaic leaves plenty of ways for the rest of it to go. So H gives W a chance c, and PP therefore requires credence c in W conditional on H. But were W to obtain, the best system over the whole history would entail H\*, not H. So W entails that the hypothesis we conditionalized on is false. PP then requires a positive credence in something that contradicts its own condition, which is a flat contradiction rather than a puzzle about how to apply the principle, and it shows that PP as Lewis stated it is simply false.

Lewis's response was to abandon Humean supervenience and treat chance as a primitive, non-supervenient feature of the world. If chances are primitive, no pattern of outcomes can undermine them, and the contradiction goes away. But the price is the entire point of the Humean project, which was to show that chances aren't occult additions to the mosaic. And the move isn't even necessary. A cheaper fix is to have the agent conditionalize not on the fact that the chance of A is x but on the complete chance hypothesis, taken as a package to be accepted or rejected as a whole. Supervenience survives, and W stops being an alternative the hypothesis has to describe, since the hypothesis is now the very thing whose truth W would overthrow. I think that works, though it leaves open why an agent should adopt a package she has some reason to think might be false.
