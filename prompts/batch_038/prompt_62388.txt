I serve as recording secretary of the Hartsell Mutual Benefit Association, a fraternal benefit society chartered in 1903 with about three hundred and eighty members, a hall we own outright, and two general meetings a year. Our bylaws contain an amendment clause, Article XII, which in its original 1903 text permitted amendments by a two-thirds vote of the members present, provided the proposed amendment had been noticed at the previous meeting; it said nothing whatever about whether Article XII could itself be amended. At last spring's meeting the membership used Article XII to adopt what we now call XII-a, which raises the threshold for any further change to Article XII alone to four-fifths of the entire membership roll — a deliberate entrenchment, aimed at a proposal to sell the hall. This spring, a faction that had lost that fight argued that the entrenchment could not bind them, that Article XII as originally written still authorized an ordinary two-thirds repeal, and the board accepted the argument, gave the required notice, and processed the repeal at a special meeting in June with 68 percent in favor. A challenge is now pending, and as recording secretary I have been asked to prepare a written opinion for the fall meeting on whether XII-a survives. I have gone around in circles on this with our parliamentarian, a retired insurance adjuster who is extremely pleased with himself, and who keeps saying that I cannot have it both ways but will not say which two ways I am having. So I want to put down what I actually believe and have the package audited.

Here is the package. (1) The bylaws are the association's supreme instrument: no officer, board, committee, or meeting can act legitimately except as the bylaws permit, and anything done outside them is simply not an act of the association. (2) Article XII is an ordinary rule, with no special status; it is valid on exactly the same footing as the article governing dues or the one governing the eligibility of associate members, and it authorized XII-a in the ordinary way. (3) XII-a was validly adopted and does what it says: from the moment of adoption, further amendment of Article XII required four-fifths of the whole roll. (4) No meeting can disable a later meeting. The members assembled are the association, and a body that made an entrenchment by ordinary vote can unmake it by ordinary vote; the June repeal was therefore valid and should stand. (5) Validity never comes from a rule's own say-so. A rule that certified itself would be proving itself by itself, and I have never accepted that move anywhere else in my life. (6) The 1903 founders adopted the first bylaws under no prior procedure at all — there was literally nothing in existence to authorize them — and I regard that founding as wholly legitimate and as the source of everything the association has done since. (7) Where the bylaws are silent, silence is not permission: if the text does not confer a power, the members do not have it, and I have applied that principle in past opinions about the board's borrowing authority and about who may propose amendments from the floor.

What I need is an audit of this set, not a lecture. Tell me which pairs of commitments genuinely conflict and which merely look as though they do — I would rather you find one apparent conflict that dissolves on inspection than pad the list — and rank the real conflicts by severity, worst first, rather than presenting them all as equally bad. For each real conflict, identify which commitment has to go or be narrowed, state the narrowed version in a sentence I could actually use, and tell me what it costs me: what I would lose the ability to say about our governance if I adopted it. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage across the whole set. Finally, answer the practical question directly: on the package exactly as I have stated it, does XII-a survive, and was the June repeal valid — and if the answer differs depending on which revision I make, say so explicitly. One housekeeping request: I have come across the phrases continuing and self-embracing sovereignty, and I know Hart wrote an essay on self-referring laws, but I have read neither properly and I am not asking you to catch me up. Do not give me references, a bibliography, a reading list, or a history of who argued what; assume neither of us has the books to hand and work only from the commitments above. Please write in American English.
