ok so I keep circling back to self-deception and I want you to actually take a side rather than survey the field. Here's the case I've been chewing on — call it the journal case. Someone writes brutally honest entries every night about their marriage: names the specific resentments, admits the fear, all of it in plain declarative sentences. Every morning they reread yesterday's entry and their reaction is genuine, unprompted, and total: that was a massive overreaction, I was in a bad mood, obviously nothing is actually wrong. Crucially they still accept every factual claim in the entry — they don't dispute that they wrote it, or that the events described happened, or that the feelings were real at the time — they just can't feel any of its force, and they'd happily bet money the marriage is fine. There's no intention to deceive themselves, no moment of deciding to suppress anything, and no false belief ever gets formed, not even briefly and not even implicitly.

Argue that this person really is self-deceived, and that the case is a problem for both of the standard families of accounts: the ones that insist the self-deceiver has to somehow simultaneously hold p and not-p, and the ones that build in an intention or plan to bring about the false belief. I want the strongest positive story of what's actualy going wrong in the journal case — what state or process is carrying the deception, if it isn't a belief and isn't an intention — and I want it to explain why the pattern here is self-referential in a way that mere optimism isn't. Then you have to handle the obvious deflationary pushback: that the morning reader is just making an ordinary interpretive mistake about how far the evidence goes, exactly the kind of error anyone makes when they're tired or attached to an outcome, and that calling it deception just inflates the term. Say why that response doesn't work here, or if you think it partly does, be precise about which part of the case it covers and which part it leaves hanging.

Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'm not after sources, just the argument. And be willing to bite bullets where the position needs them — I'd rather have a committed view with acknowledged costs than a hedged one.
