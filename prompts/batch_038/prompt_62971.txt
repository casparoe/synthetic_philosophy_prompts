ok so context: third year in philosophy, nominally phil lang though I've drifted a fair way into formal semantics, and last week our reading group (six of us, one guy's kitchen, wine of questionable quality) got completely derailed by an analogy. We were on deflationism and Dan, who is extremely confident, argued that the prosentential line basically settles things against the correspondence people. His pitch went like this: English already has bits of grammar that do for sentences what pronouns do for nouns — the ones you use when you just answer "yes", or when you say you think so; Ramsey complained that if you strip the truth-predicate out of a generalization about what John says, English leaves you hanging mid-sentence with nothing available to put at the end; and the prosententialists said fine, that gap is exactly what "is true" fills, so it's a pro-form rather than a predicate (Brandom is the recent one pushing that framing, calling it a pro-sentence-forming operator). On this picture "that's true" doesn't ascribe anything to anything, it just picks up the content of an earlier sentence the way "she" picks up its antecedent. Then Dan went further: the upshot, he said, is that asking what truth consists in is malformed in the same way it'd be malformed to ask what "she" stands for apart from any particular antecedent — the inflationists are reifying a piece of grammar, like the old chestnut about people who think "nothing" names something because of how the word behaves in a sentence.

I wasn't convinced but I also couldn't say why on the spot, which is annoying because I'm supposed to be the semantics person. Can you audit the analogy for me properly? What I want is first the correspondence table, spelled out: what plays the pronoun, what plays the antecedent, what plays the anaphoric link itself, what corresponds to the agreement features (gender, number, case) and to the context that fixes reference — and then whatever in the target domain has no counterpart whatsoever. After that, the disanalogies, and please seperate the ones that actually threaten Dan's conclusion from the ones that are just technical curiosities. A few pressure points I've got already, in case they're useful: English apparently has sentence-level anaphora in "so" and "yes" already, so what job is left over for a truth-predicate; the generalization case ("everything she said is true") looks like it needs something like a variable ranging over sentences, which I don't think English straightforwardly has; "is true" inflects for tense, and there's the two-place "true of" that doesn't look disquotational at all; and the paradigm example the whole debate turns on — it is true that snow is white — arguably has a dummy subject rather than an anaphoric one, so does the analogy fail to even get started at the one case deflationists always lead with?

I'd also want the smuggling question taken seriously: does treating "is true" as a pro-form already presuppose the deflationist conclusion instead of supporting it, i.e. is Dan's analogy just a picture he likes because it matches what he believes? And is there a scope shift going on — a claim about what an expression contributes to a sentence being used to answer a question about what makes the contents expressed true, which the pronoun picture simply never addresses? Tell me whether his stronger conclusion (that the metaphysical question is malformed, not merely hard) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. Finally, if pronouns are the wrong source domain, is there a better analogy for "is true" that doesn't quietly hand the game to either side — and what would make an analogy non-question-begging here in the first place? No need for citations or a reading list, I've got those; I just want the reasoning laid out.
