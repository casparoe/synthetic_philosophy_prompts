In three weeks I present a forty-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on ancient and modern institutions. The room will be roughly five Greek historians and classicists — two of whom work on Athenian epigraphy and one who writes on Thucydides' compositional method — two political theorists who treat the polis as a serious case for deliberative democracy and are broadly sympathetic to it, and two graduate students who apply formal models to institutional design. Nobody in the room works in finance, economics, or decision theory, so the apparatus I borrow will arrive stripped of its native defenses.

My thesis is that the usual trio of Athenian failures — the decree against Mytilene in 427, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates in 399 — are not three separate failures of judgment that happen to be famous, but three instances of a single institutional defect: Athens had no procedure for holding a decision open. I frame this in the vocabulary of irreversible commitment and option value — the claim being that what a collective decision-maker must manage is not only whether a choice is right under uncertainty, but what it costs, and how late, to change one's mind. On my account Athens had exactly three routes back from a decree, and none was designed for reconsideration. First, the ad hoc revote: the second assembly at Mytilene happened only because someone demanded it, and succeeded only because the second ship overtook the first, so what saved the Mytileneans was initiative and rowing, not procedure. Second, the adversarial route: the public suit against a proposer for an illegal decree, which suspended the measure once sworn notice was given, but which attached liability to a person rather than reopening the question, and which became a routine weapon between rival politicians. Third, no route at all — which is what the penalty phase of a capital trial looks like from inside, and what Athens looked like in 415, when a decree committing most of the city's ships and tens of thousands of men was passed and the only subsequent revisions on offer were the recall of a general mid-voyage and, after the disaster, fury aimed at the very orators who had promoted the expedition, as though the demos had never voted for it. I then argue that the post-restoration reforms, which separated permanent laws from assembly decrees and gave panels of lawgivers the power to repeal, are best read as Athens belatedly building the missing instrument — and that the trial of Socrates, falling just after them, shows how partial that discovery was, since it yielded a second hearing on the penalty and none on the verdict.

Prepare my question period. I want:

- The eight to ten objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to the paper if left unanswered, and each phrased the way that person would phrase it aloud — in their own vocabulary, naming the source, inscription, or institution they would invoke.
- For each: what I should concede within one sentence, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without notes and in my own register, and a separate line identifying where that reply is softest.
- A mark on the one or two objections that are global rather than local — those which, if they land, take down the thesis rather than a section of it.
- Which two I should neutralize pre-emptively in the opening ten minutes, and which I should be content to let the room raise.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks, together with an honest account of why I cannot answer it.

Assume the obvious charges come properly and are not waved at: the anachronism of importing a contingent-claims concept into a polity with no market for contingent claims and no habit of pricing futures; that my lead case refutes me, since Mytilene is above all a story about reversal working; that I am constructing institutional theory out of speeches Thucydides composed, and that the closest the sources come to my position is Diodotus arguing that holding proposers liable distorts deliberation — which is a defense of existing practice, not a demand for a new mechanism; that the council's preliminary resolution already supplied a two-stage filter, and that the assembly's periodic votes on its own officials, the audits of outgoing magistrates, and ostracism while it was still in use were all institutionalized second looks; that reversibility is not free, since an empire and a navy run on credible commitments and a polity that can always reopen its decrees forfeits the deterrent value of its threats; that the slack which let a second ship be dispatched and overtake the first was a by-product of Athenian speed rather than a design feature, so my prescription may collapse into the trivial advice to build in delay; and that at Arginusae an objection of illegality was lodged against the collective trial of the generals and the presiding officers initially refused to put the question — which will be turned on me as evidence that the brake existed and was applied by individuals, leaving me to explain why it worked there, nearly worked at Mytilene, and was nowhere in 415. The two political theorists will separately press whether a demos can be treated as an agent with options at all, and whether I have simply relabeled Thucydides' own diagnosis — that the city at home failed to support what it had sent — in the jargon of a discipline he would have despised.
