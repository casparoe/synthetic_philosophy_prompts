Okay so I just spent about two hours arguing with my friend Dana at a bar and I want a third opinion, because we're both convinced and neither of us is budging. We're both on the board of a mid-sized regional animal shelter network — three sites, roughly 9,000 intakes a year, funded partly by a county contract and partly by a private foundation. Two years ago Dana built us a dashboard with one headline number: save rate, meaning live outcomes (adoptions, returns to owner, transfers out) divided by total intake. The pitch was that one clean number would end the endless arguments about which site was pulling its weight. Instead, in about eighteen months the number went from 71% to 92% and nothing about the actual situation in the county improved. The mechanism is completely legible in hindsight: intake staff started "counseling" owner-surrender appointments out to next month, past the reporting period; strays sat in the car park in the carrier of whoever found them until someone decided whether to accept them; hard cases got transferred to a rescue partner three hours away so the eventual death lands on another org's books; and a chunk of euthanasia got reclassified as died-in-care on the theory that the animal declined after admission. The arithmetic is real, the metric moved, the animals didn't.

Dana's conclusion is that this is just Goodhart's law with the serial numbers filed off — the moment a measure becomes the thing people are judged on it stops measuring anything — and so the honest response is to scrap the dashboard and go back to narrative quarterly reports from the site managers plus a rotating in-person visit. I think that throws out the only thing that ever let a bunch of part-time volunteers see across three sites, and that the failure was in the design, not in the idea of designing at all. We each wrote down fixes and I want them ranked, because I suspect some of ours are worse than useless.

Here's the list, with Dana's pet ideas flagged:
1. Paired opposing metrics — report save rate only alongside intake volume and median days-in-care, and evaluate sites on the pair, so any move that lifts one has to survive the other.
2. Denominator audits — a random 10% sample of declined or never-booked surrender requests and of died-in-care reclassifications, re-adjudicated blind by an outside vet, results published.
3. Shadow metric (Dana's favorite) — compute a second, harder-to-game version of save rate, e.g. counting any animal that came within 24 hours of intake and counting transfers that die within 30 days as our deaths; never publish it, never tie anything to it, and treat the gap between the published number and the shadow number as a tamper alarm.
4. Percentile targeting — instead of an absolute 90%, target the 75th percentile of a peer group of comparable shelters, re-estimated each year.
5. Rotate the headline metric quarterly from a pool of six, and don't tell staff which one is live this quarter.
6. Subjective evaluation — no formula at all, year-end rating by a supervisor who reads the raw intake logs and talks to the rescue partners.
7. Radical transparency — publish every animal-level record, per site, monthly, and let volunteers and the local paper do the oversight.
8. Dana's actual position: measure nothing, narrative reports and site visits only.

Three things I want out of you. First, sort all eight into tiers by how much they'd actually reduce gaming over a three-year horizon in an org like ours — part-time staff, high turnover, real budget pressure, managers who have known each other for years — with one sentence each on why it lands where it does, and please be willing to put something in a bottom tier if you think it makes things worse rather than better. Second, there's a wrinkle neither of us priced in: both funders ratchet. Every year we beat the target, next year's target goes up and the funding formula doesn't, so the site managers have learned that visible headroom is a trap and they sandbag deliberately. Score each option a second time, 0 to 3, for how much it exposes headroom to the funders and feeds that ratchet, and tell me which items change tiers once you subtract it. Third, give me one number from 0 to 1 for the probabilty that, if we adopt your entire top tier and nothing else, someone at one of the three sites is gaming the numbers again within three years — and then say plainly who won the argument at the bar, me or Dana. I'm fine losing. I mostly want to know whether item 3 is the clever fix Dana thinks it is or a surveillance toy that will make the managers hate us.
