I'm trying to stress-test the claim that acting together requires some kind of we-mode attitude, and I need a case built to order rather than a survey of the literature.

Construct me a scenario in which a bunch of people plainly act together — the joint thing actually gets accomplished, and it's not just a pile of individual actions that happen to line up — but where nobody in the group ever forms a we-intention or any attitude with a collective subject in its content. Constraints:

- realistic and present-day, no sci-fi machinery;
- no company, committee, board, or any other body with standing rules or a decision procedure — I don't want the case getting its collectivity for free from a group agent;
- no agreement, promise, contract, or advance arrangement of any kind between the participants;
- the whole thing unfolds in under an hour;
- if you could freeze any one participant mid-action and ask what they're doing, their honest answer would come out in I-terms, not we-terms;
- but the participants genuinely adjust to one another, so that if they didn't, the joint thing fails.

One paragraph for the scenario. Then run the check: Searle's we-intentions, Gilbert's joint commitment (obligations, rebuke, the whole apparatus), and Bratman's shared cooperative activity with its meshing subplans — for each, say whether your case satisfies it and which specific clause is doing the work. Then the honest part: which of the three is your case hardest on, what is the minimal tweak that would let them back in, and how would a defender of that account redescribe your case so it stops being a counterexample at all? Finally, flag anything about the scenario that only satisfies the constraints because you stipulated the participants' psychology instead of letting it fall out of the setting — I'd rather know where the seams are.
