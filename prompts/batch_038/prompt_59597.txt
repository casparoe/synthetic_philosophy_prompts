I'm a junior, and this spring I'm presenting a paper at my school's humanities symposium on Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life," the 1998 novella that the film *Arrival* was adapted from. The connection I want to make is not the obvious one. Nearly every write-up I've found treats the story as a free will parable: Louise learns the aliens' written language, comes to experience time the way they do, and goes on to have a daughter whose short life and death she already knows. But the mechanism Chiang actually supplies for all of this is a piece of physics, and my AP Physics C teacher set me on this track without meaning to. When we got to Fermat's principle of least time, he told us it isn't a rival to the ordinary ray-by-ray account of refraction — same content, different bookkeeping, and the two are interconvertible. That is precisely the relationship Chiang's story treats as decisive. In the story the heptapods' physics is variational through and through: a process is explained by what it has to achieve at both ends rather than by what pushed it at the start. Louise's realization that this is why the heptapods lay down a sentence as a single two-dimensional design rather than one sign at a time is what unlocks their language, and eventually her own future.

So I want to read the story through the problem of the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I want the reading tested rather than admired. Set it against the deflationary reply I came across in an encyclopedia article — the one associated with Glymour, roughly, that when you actually write out two supposedly empirically equivalent rivals they turn out to be intertranslatable notational variants, so there is nothing to choose between and nothing lost by not choosing; or else the equivalence is local and fragile and further evidence breaks it. On that view Chiang's case is the harmless one: a variational formulation versus a causal one is the textbook example of an equivalence class, not a live underdetermination. Yet the story insists the choice is anything but harmless — that adopting one member of the class gives Louise something the other cannot, namely knowledge of her daughter's life and death and a willingness to go through with it anyway. My proposal, and the coinage I've been leaning on in my draft, is that this is a distinct phenomenon I've been calling transformative underdetermination: two formulations equivalent in evidence and in prediction but non-equivalent in the capacities they confer on whoever adopts one, so the choice between them is not settled by the evidence, is not arbitrary either, and cannot be dissolved by pointing out that they are the same theory in disguise. I'd like you to say whether that category survives scrutiny — whether it collapses into the familiar discussion of pragmatic or theoretical virtues like simplicity and fruitfulness, whether it rests on a plain confusion between what a theory says and what a person who has grasped it can do, or whether it names something real that the intertranslatability reply has no slot for, on the grounds that reply concerns relations among theories while Louise's situation concerns a relation between a theory and a knower.

I'd also like you to test a second claim of mine, about the story's form rather than its physics, because I suspect it cuts both ways. The narration is in the second person, addressed to a daughter who has not been conceived at the moment of telling, and it moves back and forth between the past of the heptapod contact and the future of that daughter's life. If Louise ends up composing her own account the way the heptapods compose one of their designs — the whole thing apprehended at once rather than built up in sequence — then the frame isn't a device for reporting foreknowledge but an instance of it, and what looks like Louise's decision to have the daughter isn't a decision under uncertainty at all. Does that sharpen the underdetermination reading or hollow it out? Please also tell me where the story resists being read this way. The film keeps the linguistics and largely drops the variational physics, and I have a suspicion it could afford to because the physics was never doing the philosophical work I'm assigning to it; if you think that suspicion is right, I would rather hear it now than in front of an auditorium. Two requests about how you answer. First, don't give me a survey of positions closing with a note that reasonable people differ — I need a verdict, either that transformative underdetermination is a genuine category the story earns or that it's a mistake I should strike from the paper, argued for directly, with the cost of whichever verdict you reach stated plainly. Second, I can't consult anything from here, so please make the case in your own words rather than sending me a reading list, and if you find yourself reconstructing a detail of the story or of the physics that you aren't sure of, say so at the point where you use it.
