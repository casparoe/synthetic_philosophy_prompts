I teach an applied ethics course in which the doctrine of double effect comes up every year, and in the course of teaching it I have become preoccupied with a pattern for which I cannot locate a name. I will describe it and then say what I want from you.

Consider a bystander who can redirect a runaway trolley onto a side track where one worker stands rather than the main track where five stand. I can describe what he does as redirecting the trolley away from the five, foreseeably killing the one; or I can describe it as using the one's body, positioned on the side track, to stop the trolley and thereby save the five. Both descriptions are literally true of the very same events — nothing about the physics, the sequence, or the mental states of the agent distinguishes them — yet one yields a verdict of permissible side effect and the other yields a verdict of impermissible use of a person as a means. The parallel case in my medical ethics unit runs the same way: a physician titrating morphine for a dying patient can be described as relieving pain while foreseeing respiratory depression, or as bringing about respiratory depression in order to relieve pain, since the relief arrives only by way of the depression, and again both descriptions fit the identical course of conduct.

The pattern I want to isolate is not simply that the boundary between what is intended and what is foreseen is fuzzy or contestable at the margins. It is this: for a single course of conduct there are several equally accurate descriptions, they sort the conduct into opposite verdicts, and when two competent judges disagree about a case they very often turn out to have been selecting different descriptions all along, before any argument was exchanged. Worse, when I press students to justify the description they chose rather than another, the only justification on offer is that it "best captures what really happened," and any more determinate criterion I can articulate — the most proximate description, the one tracking the agent's own conception of the act, the one under which the effect is causally explanatory of the good — has a counterexample, and tends to select for each case whichever description supports the verdict the critic already favors.

So: does this have an established name, in the literature on double effect or anywhere adjacent to it? I want to know which existing concepts come closest to it, and for each one, precisely where the phenomenon I have described exceeds it or falls short of it — I am not satisfied with a bare label, because the point is the carving, not the name. If nothing in the literature picks out exactly this, say so plainly and then give it a name, with a definition sharp enough to generate boundary cases: tell me which of my two illustrations it clearly covers, and describe one case that sits at its edge and one that lies just outside it.
