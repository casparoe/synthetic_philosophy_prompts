I serve as the quantitative analyst on a national biosafety review panel, and at the close of a four-hour session last week our chair disposed of a release application with a sentence he repeated three times: "we should err on the side of caution." The sentence ended the argument. Nobody challenged it, and I did not challenge it either, because I could not have said what it would have to mean in order to be wrong.

The application, in brief. A consortium seeks authorization to release a self-limiting strain of *Aedes aegypti* on an island jurisdiction of roughly 200,000 people in order to suppress dengue transmission. The construct depends on an antibiotic that is absent from the environment: female offspring of released males die before adulthood, so the introduced trait cannot sustain itself and should decay from the population within a bounded number of generations after releases cease. The staff numbers, which no member of the panel disputed, are these. Dengue currently kills about 14 people a year on the island and hospitalizes about 900. The program is expected to reduce transmission by 60 to 80 percent. The release would be phased over four seasons against pre-specified stopping criteria, with a monitoring network able to detect suppression failure or non-target effects within one season. The principal identified risks are niche replacement by *Ae. albopictus*, already present on the island and a competent secondary vector, and the possibility that environmental tetracycline residues permit some female survival; the staff's pessimistic parameterization puts the worst credible harm at ten to twenty deaths across a ten-year window at a probability below two percent. Every parameterization they ran, the pessimistic ones included, has authorization saving substantially more lives than refusal. The panel voted 7 to 4 to deny.

I am not asking you to evaluate the decision, or to tell me whether the panel was right. I want the sentence taken apart.

1. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "err on the side of caution" could be making in a deliberative setting like ours. Not folk glosses: principles that could be stated, defended, and shown to be satisfied or violated by a particular case. For each, fix the parameters the bare sentence leaves open. Whose caution is at issue. Caution against which error, a false negative on harm or a false positive on benefit. What decision rule it recommends, whether a tilted evidential or decision threshold, a maximin rule, a rule that is averse to ambiguity rather than to risk, a demand for information before an irreversible step, a reallocation of the burden of proof, or a refusal to aggregate certain harms at all. Under what conditions on the evidence the rule is supposed to bind, and over what time frame.

2. Give each reading a verdict: analytic or platitudinous, substantive and defensible in general, defensible only under conditions you should state explicitly, or false. Where a reading is false, say what has to be true of a case for it to become false. I expect several readings to fail precisely because the status quo is not one of the safe options, and I would like that failure exhibited rather than asserted.

3. Run each reading against our case. Does it, given the numbers above, license the denial? Show the check, not just the outcome, so that I can see where each reading gets its traction and where it runs out.

4. Tell me which readings collapse into one another, which entail others, and which pairs are mutually exclusive. I suspect that several of the seven votes in the majority were cast for propositions that cannot all be true at once, and I want to know which.

5. Three members spoke in support of denial, and as far as I can reconstruct it they meant three different things by the chair's sentence. One said that the arithmetic cannot capture what is at stake when the people who will bear the risk were never asked to bear it. One said that our models are calibrated on systems we do not understand and that the confidence intervals are decorative. One said that authorizing this release makes authorizing the next one easier, and that the panel should weigh the effect of its own decision on its own future decisions. Identify which reading, if any, each of these corresponds to; whether any of the three is a reading of the slogan at all rather than a separate objection the slogan merely gestured at; and whether any of the three would have survived being stated plainly and voted on by itself.

6. If no defensible reading licenses the denial, say what the chair's objection would have to have been, stated precisely enough that we could argue about it in the room, and whether that objection, so stated, is any good.

Two further notes. Our enabling statute permits approval only where the panel finds that a release will not pose an unreasonable risk to human health or the environment, a phrase that fixes no threshold of its own; I mention it because some readings of the slogan may be claims about what standard of proof that phrase imports rather than claims about what to do, and I want the two kept strictly apart. And my own guess, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the sentence was doing work no expected-value reading could do, so that the panel was voting on something about legitimacy while arguing as though it were arguing about probability.

Format: a numbered list I can bring to the next meeting, each entry giving the principle, its parameters, its verdict, and the result of the check against our case in a few sentences; then a short comparative section on collapse, entailment, and incompatibility; then the closing statement of the objection the chair should have made instead. Please do not survey the literature on the precautionary principle or summarize what international declarations say about it. I have read that material, and none of it was on the table last week.
