I have no academic training in philosophy. Over the past two winters I have been working my way through Quine's *Word and Object* on my own — I got through the first two chapters before the going became too rough — and I have been stuck ever since on the rabbit example: the linguist in the field, the rabbit scurrying past, the native's utterance, and the discovery that everything the linguist could ever observe would fit just as well with a manual that rendered the word as "rabbit," as "undetached rabbit part," or as "a temporal stage of a rabbit."

I volunteer at a branch library, where I run a Thursday-evening discussion group. It is not a class and there is no syllabus. Eleven to sixteen people usually come, aged anywhere from about twenty-five to seventy-five, and almost none of them have studied philosophy formally. They are bright, they enjoy a puzzle, and they disengage quickly if they suspect they are being lectured at. I have one ninety-minute session, and I would like you to design an activity for it.

What I want is a game in which the group does the field linguistics themselves and runs into the wall on their own. The words "Quine" and "indeterminacy" should not be spoken until the debrief is nearly finished. Please give me:

1. Roles, rules, a materials list I can assemble from a stationer's shop and a home printer — nothing requiring an app, a screen, or the internet — and a timeline broken into blocks of roughly ten minutes.
2. A scoring scheme that uses no money but that people genuinely care about, and a final phase in which the group must put the manual it has settled on to actual use in dealing with the natives. Both rival manuals should turn out to work equally well there; that is part of the point.
3. A solution to a problem I could not solve myself. If a person plays the native and improvises, the group will simply read that person's mind — a hesitation, a glance toward a card, a shrug — and the puzzle evaporates. Design the native's responses so that they come out of something mechanical rather than out of anyone's head, and tell me precisely what to write on the cards or table and how to generate it in under an hour.
4. Anticipation of the tests the group will invent to break the tie — pointing at a part instead of the whole, presenting the same rabbit twice, a rabbit half hidden behind a screen, negation, "is this the same one as before?" — and materials built so that each of those tests comes out even. If some test would in fact settle the matter, I would much rather you told me so than rigged the game to hide it.
5. A debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto steps of the argument, in the order those moments occurred, plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what I should do in the room when it does. I am especially worried about two reactions: someone declaring the whole exercise trivial because obviously the word means "rabbit," and someone becoming genuinely indignant that the game has no answer. Also tell me what to cut if only seven people turn up.

Finally, I would like you to be straight with me about the limits of this. As far as I can see, a game of this kind can at best show the group that they were unable to work out the correct answer, which is much weaker than what Quine is claiming — that there is no correct answer to work out. In my version there will be a deck or a table that fixes what the native's sounds are hooked up to, so someone in the building will know the answer, and that someone will be me. Tell me whether the activity can be constructed so that this gap does not wreck it, whether some structural feature of the game can be made to carry the stronger claim on its own, and which part of the claim I am simply going to have to assert without the group ever experiencing it.
