Working on Camus's *Reflections on the Guillotine* for a seminar paper, and I've reconstructed his asymmetry argument in a way that now looks like it refutes his own fallback position. As I have it:

(1) A killing is worse the more premeditated it is — cold calculation and a long wait add something a sudden act lacks.
(2) Execution is the most premeditated killing there is: the state fixes a date, and the condemned man is held at its mercy for months knowing what's coming.
(3) Most murders are nothing like that, and even a calculated one doesn't warn its victim in advance and then keep him waiting.
(4) So the state's act is worse than the crime it punishes, and the eye-for-an-eye equivalance can't actually be had.
(5) But the same reasoning runs against prison, which is what Camus wants instead. Lifelong hard labor is also imposed coldly and after full deliberation, and it stretches the condemned man's suffering over decades rather than months.
(6) So no punishment anyone deliberately imposes can ever be equivalent to the crime it answers.
(7) And a punishment that isn't equivalent to the crime is unjust — so all punishment is unjust, including the life of labor he endorses as the maximum penalty.

He obviously doesnt say (7), and I don't want to just call him inconsistent, but I can't find where the chain stops. Something slips around (5), or maybe between (6) and (7), and I can't tell whether it's an equivocation on "worse," on "equivalent," or on "unjust." Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of his case? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1)–(4), since the premeditation point seems to be the thing doing the real work in the essay — but tell me what I'm paying for it.
