In about six weeks I'm giving a forty minute talk at a small workshop on the epistemology of expertise, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defence of the view. The room will be a dozen or so people: seven or eight social epistemologists who think in terms of testimony, trust, epistemic dependence and the division of cognitive labour; two formal types who'll want to know how anything I say could be modelled at all; a historian of science who is allergic to anything that smells like taking the current consensus for granted; and one Wittgenstein scholar who has already told me twice that people read far more into the late notebooks than there is in them.

My thesis is that hinge commitments — the things that in On Certainty aren't up for doubt because everything else turns on them — can be held by proxy. You don't have to have soaked one up through your own upbringing and practice; you can borrow it, by standing in a relation of trust to an institution that embodies it. That's how I can be certain the earth is vastly older than my great-grandmother without being able to check a single step of the reasoning, and it's how a nurse is certain that the readings on the ward thermometer mean what she was taught they mean. The bit I think is genuinely new, and the bit I want to lean on, is that a borrowed hinge is not a believed report: it doesn't sit in my evidence waiting to be weighed, it gets used, and it isn't the kind of thing the lender can revise for me by offering more evidence. Lenders can pass hinges on but can't easily take them back — which is why loss of trust in an institution feels less like changing your mind and more like the world shifting under you.

So: give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, each one phrased the way that person would phrase it (the formal guy's will sound nothing like the historian's), with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a blunt note on where each reply is soft. Mark how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, and say which of them comes from the Wittgenstein scholar and whether I should meet that one on exegetical ground or refuse to fight there. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, what I have to hold onto for the talk to be worth giving, and the single question I'm praying nobody asks.

Answer in American English please, even though I've written this in the other variety. And no reading list or references — I want the arguements and the objections, not the bibliography.
