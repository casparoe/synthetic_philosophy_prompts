My friend keeps bringing this argument up at dinner and I cant answer him. I don't have any philosophy training, so maybe I'm mangling it, but here's roughly how he puts it: evolution built our moral feelings, and feelings that made our ancestors cooperate and protect their kids got passed on whether or not they were true, so there's no reason to think our moral beliefs line up with any moral facts sitting out there in the world. Rewind the tape and we'd have totally different convictions and find them just as self-evident — we might think leaving old people on the ice is obviously fine. And you can't test your intuitions against anything neutral, because the thing you'd test them with is the same evolved machinery. So, he says, there are no moral facts, morality is just biology wearing a disguise, and the only honest thing to do is pick whichever instincts are useful and admit you're picking.

Two things bother me. First, I don't believe him that nothing is really right or wrong — I think my grandmother was plainly right about some things and plainly wrong about others, and that doesn't feel like a matter of taste. But second, and I think this is the sharper one: if evolution is that bad at producing true moral beliefs, what makes his beliefs about evolution, or about logic, any better? He waved it off and I couldn't say why it seemed like a real problem.

So: which parts of his version would someone who actually works on this accept, which parts are distortion or overreach, and does his conclusion survive once you fix them? And please separate what's genuinely settled here from what's still a live fight — I don't want to go back and announce that everyone agrees on something they don't.
