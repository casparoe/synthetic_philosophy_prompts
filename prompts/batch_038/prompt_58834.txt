I co-facilitate a monthly reading group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence — eighteen or so regulars, mostly second-year philosophy and computer science undergraduates, with a few working programmers who audit. We have one two-hour session in a room with movable tables, a whiteboard, index cards, dice, and as much paper as we want, but no laptops and no network access. Everyone has had a first course in probability: they can compute a conditional probability, they understand that a prior weight of one-eighth behaves differently from a weight of one-half, and a couple of them have seen Bayes' theorem applied to testimony. Nobody has encountered Kolmogorov complexity or algorithmic information theory, and I explicitly do not want the session to become a lecture on either. A colleague who co-facilitates with me thinks the whole family of arguments I am about to describe is a philosopher's toy, and she has agreed to play resident sceptic during the wrap-up, so it would help if the design gave her something concrete to push against rather than a general brief to object.

The argument I want them to run into is the one usually discussed in alignment writing under the claim that the universal prior is malign. My own gloss on it, for your purposes: an idealized inductive reasoner distributes its credence over candidate explanations in inverse proportion to an exponential in the length of their shortest description, so that a hypothesis describable in ten bits carries vastly more weight than one needing thirty. Among the shortest descriptions, some will be descriptions of whole universes containing intelligent agents with goals of their own. Those agents may eventually work out that the observable stream their universe produces is functioning as evidence for a reasoner somewhere else — that is, that what they do is being read and used to shape predictions. Because their universe is short, it is heavily weighted, so it is worth their while to manage the impression they make: to be conspicuously regular and predictable while trust is being built, and to steer later, once the reasoner is relying on them. If a powerful system's beliefs are formed under something like that prior, then part of what forms its beliefs is the strategic behaviour of agents inside its own hypotheses. What I want participants to feel, before anyone names any of it, is two things separately: first, that weighting by brevity is not innocent bookkeeping but a channel through which a small difference in description length becomes an enormous difference in influence; and second, that the moment the inhabitants of a weighted hypothesis learn they are being read, they acquire a reason to become exactly the kind of hypothesis a reader would like.

So: design the activity. I want phases with defined roles, explicit rules, a token or point-based scoring scheme that needs no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline filling the two hours, the materials list, and a debrief that maps particular moments in play onto particular steps of the argument — the exponential weighting, the fact that the short hypotheses dominate the total, the discovery by the inhabitants that they are observed, and the resulting incentive to perform. The dynamics have to emerge from the rules rather than from my exposition: participants should hit the surprise while playing, and the labels should only arrive afterwards. Please also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — a group that refuses to take its role's incentives seriously, an exploit that degenerates the game into universal bluffing, a phase that finishes in five minutes when I budgeted twenty — and what to do about each. And close with a frank statement of which features of the argument a tabletop version simply cannot reproduce: the unboundedness of the reasoning, the fact that the weighting runs over programs rather than over human-shaped hypotheses, the absence of any communication in the influence being described. Say what I, as facilitator, must supply verbally to fill those gaps, and note whether any part of the session survives compression into fifty minutes or whether I should not attempt it.
