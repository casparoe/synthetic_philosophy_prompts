I am the standards editor at a mid-sized digital science outlet — a general-interest desk with roughly a million readers a month, four staff editors, and one part-time consulting statistician who turns questions around in two days but never sooner. Between six and ten items a week reach my desk citing a heritability estimate, a polygenic score, or the phrase "partly genetic," and my team has to decide, usually in under twenty minutes and always without a specialist, whether the inference the piece draws is licensed by the number it quotes. Our style guide currently requires that any variance-component figure carry a context line of no more than thirty words naming the population and the range of environments it was estimated in, and it bans the formulation "X percent genetic" in headlines; those are our only existing tools, and they are not enough. I argued about this for most of Saturday with a friend who does quantitative social science. Her position is the deflationary one: a heritability estimate is a ratio of variances in one population at one time under one distribution of environments, it does not travel to individuals, it does not bound what an intervention can do to a mean, it says nothing about differences between groups, and therefore it licenses essentially no claim a newspaper would want to make. My reply was that if the figure licenses nothing, we should print none of them, which is absurd, because our reporters use these numbers to knock down claims that are genuinely knocked down by them — the assertion that the variation in a trait must be entirely a matter of upbringing, for instance, or the assumption that whatever nobody can change must have a high heritability. What I need is an instrument that says positively what such a figure does entitle a writer to assert, and under what conditions, not a list of disclaimers.

So: write me the decision procedure my four editors can run on a submission. I want the questions in order — sequence matters, since some are cheap and some require reading the methods section — and for each one, what a sufficient answer looks like in finished copy, what phrasings fail it, and where the procedure stops with a no rather than a request for revision. It needs an explicit off-ramp for the most common case, in which the article cites no heritability estimate at all but something adjacent that our writers routinely call one: a polygenic score's share of variance explained, a genetic correlation, a twin concordance figure, a within-family coefficient. It should say what to do when the estimate comes from a population, a birth cohort, or an environment range other than the one the piece is about, and what to do when the measure itself changed between the study and the story. It should include the section my friend will attack hardest, on how far a writer may go in describing a single person's trait as partly genetic, given her contention that the flat denial of any individual-level reading is a slogan rather than a theorem. And it should tell me loudly which questions are really about our mission or about foreseeable harm rather than about what the statistic means, and must therefore be handed up to me instead of settled by the checklist — I would rather the procedure flag its own limits than smuggle values in as method. Mark, as you go, the steps where a judgment call is wearing a lab coat: the places where "sufficient answer" is doing covert normative work. No references and no further reading, please; I need something usable at 9:15 on a Tuesday morning.

Then run it on three items sitting on my desk now, showing what your questions extract from each. First, a piece by our most senior reporter built on a study that re-estimated a behavioural trait's genetic associations using sibling pairs, arguing that the larger figures reported by earlier studies of unrelated people were inflated by family environment and population structure; the piece concludes that those earlier numbers were largely an artefact, and I have already told the reporter I like the framing. Second, a piece reporting that the heritability of IQ-test scores is much higher among affluent families than among poor ones in the country where the study was done, which goes on to conclude that cognitive outcomes among poor children are essentially environmental and that only social spending can move them; the replication record for that interaction is mixed and the piece does not say so. Third, a contribution from an outside writer with a reputation for hereditarian argument whose draft is, so far as my editors can tell, technically scrupulous — it reports a within-population estimate, declines to draw any inference about group differences, and even supplies its own context line — but which my deputy wants spiked on the grounds that it will be excerpted by readers who do draw that inference. If your procedure clears the third item, say so plainly and tell me what it leaves me free to do about it; I would rather have an instrument that occasionally tells me something I do not want to hear than one that ratifies my instincts. Close with what the procedure cannot do: the classes of claim it will pass or fail wrongly, and where you expect my friend to break it, since she has agreed to read the thing.
