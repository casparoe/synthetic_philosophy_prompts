I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend about Kierkegaard's critique of the press, and I came away unable to articulate what I think is wrong with his position, which bothers me, because I am fairly confident that something is.

His claim was that the Kierkegaardian public is simply a crowd equipped with a distribution network. On his telling, the press is a megaphone: it takes the familiar pathologies of an assembled mob -- anonymity, emotional contagion, diffusion of responsibility, the individual dissolving into the many -- and extends them from the town square to the whole kingdom. He pointed out that Kierkegaard himself insists the crowd is untruth and addresses everything to the single individual, so for him the two terms are doing one and the same work, and the public is just the crowd under its modern name. From this he drew a conclusion he believes is continuous with Kierkegaard: if the disease is anonymous aggregation, the cure is to reattach names to opinions. Make everyone speak in their own person, forbid pseudonymous and aggregated address, and the phantom breaks back up into identifiable individuals the way a crowd disperses when the lights come on. He wants to apply this directly to platforms, where he favors real-name policies, and he took himself to have Kierkegaard's authority for it.

I want an audit of that analogy rather than a verdict on who won the argument. Specifically:

1. Lay out the mapping explicitly. What in the crowd corresponds to what in the public -- the assembled bodies, the shared mood, the loss of personal responsibility, the press as organizer, the individual in each case? A table of correspondences is welcome.

2. Then say where the mapping breaks, and rank those breaks by whether they matter for the use he is making of it. I am thinking of questions like: can a public ever be assembled, witnessed, or counted, in the way a crowd can? A crowd can act -- it can cheer, storm a building, stone someone -- and I have the impression that for Kierkegaard the public is precisely what cannot act at all, what never exists anywhere as a body. If that is right, does the analogy survive the fact that its source is an agent and its target is not? And is the press amplifying an aggregate that already exists, or producing a kind of entity that could not exist without it?

3. Identify what the crowd picture quietly imports from the domain of mob psychology that the public lacks: that the harm is an excess of passion rather than its evaporation into chatter and reflection; that the public is at bottom a sum of real people who could in principle be enumerated and named; that a defect in social ontology is the sort of thing an administrative rule could repair.

4. Tell me what becomes of leveling in all this. My recollection is that Kierkegaard treats it as a negative process that nevertheless has an educative, almost providential function, which would make it a strange target for a naming requirement. And I suspect the single individual is not a citizen speaking under his legal name but something closer to a religious category -- if so, say plainly whether my friend has simply exchanged two different senses of individual.

5. Finally, state whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether de-anonymization is a Kierkegaardian remedy or an anti-Kierkegaardian one, and whether there is a better analogy for the public than the crowd -- something like an index, an average, a readership that is addressed but never present, a corporation's share price -- and what that better analogy would license instead.

Please also mark clearly the places where I think he has a genuine point, since the crowd and the public are not wholly unrelated, and I would rather not be handed a one-sided refutation.
