I held a chair in polymer science for the better part of four decades, latterly working on cross-linked networks and the sol–gel transition, and I retired four years ago with the intention of reading metaphysics properly rather than in airport bookshops. The special composition question has taken hold of me, in part because it looks to me like a question my own discipline spent a century answering in practice without ever noticing that it was doing so. I am to give a talk to a local philosophy society — retired professionals, two schoolteachers, and a handful of graduate students — in which I mean to set out the position I have arrived at, and I should like that position audited before I commit to it in public. A former doctoral student of mine, now a philosopher of science, glanced at my list of commitments over lunch and said only that it was a mess and that she would enjoy taking it apart in writing; she declined to say where the trouble lay, which is why I am asking you. Please do not infer my priorities from the order in which I state them, and do not spare a commitment merely because it seems to me obviously right or because a working scientist would be expected to defend it. I have deliberately not told you which of them I would be most reluctant to give up.

Here is what I hold. (1) Composition is a worldly, mind-independent matter: which pluralities compose something is settled by how things are, not by our concepts, our interests, or our classificatory practices; two investigators examining the same physical situation cannot both be right about whether it contains one object or many. (2) Composition is restricted: I deny that there is any object made up of the left half of a beaker on my shelf together with a dust mote out near the Kuiper belt; unrestricted composition strikes me as an artifact of a notational convenience that philosophers have mistaken for a discovery. (3) What makes composition obtain, where it does, is a physical condition of exactly the kind I studied: the parts must be bonded or cohering — chemically joined, or in sustained contact and cohesion — such that they behave as a single unit rather than as a mere collection. (4) There is no vagueness in reality: composition is all-or-nothing and never a matter of degree, and where I cannot say whether a whole is present, that is a limit on my knowledge or on my terms, not an indeterminacy in the world. (5) A whole is not identical to its parts and is something over and above them; this is precisely why I take the restriction in (2) seriously, since if wholes came for free I should not care how many I admitted, whereas if each is a further item of being, admission has a price and parsimony is a reason to be restrictive. (6) I am nonetheless a thoroughgoing physicalist about causation and reject downward causation: a whole has no causal powers beyond those of its parts arranged as they are, and macroscopic regularities are consequences of what the parts do. (7) I am no eliminativist: molecules, crystals, cells, organisms, and also ordinary artifacts — beakers, analytical balances, chairs — exist. (8) Finally, I take the answer to the special composition question to be in principle an empirical discovery; chemistry and biology have already told us a good deal about when parts make a whole, and I expect further investigation to tell us more. I have read enough to know the general shape of the alternatives — universalism, nihilism, the organisms-only answer, composition as identity, and the view that any restriction is brute — and I do not want a survey of them. What I cannot do for myself is trace exactly what my own set commits me to.

So: treat this as an audit rather than an exposition. Identify which of these commitments pull against one another, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say specifically what would have to be withdrawn or restated and what the restatement costs me. Equally important, tell me which pairs only appear to be in tension and why the appearance misleads — I would be annoyed to give up something on the strength of a bad argument. Then say which single revision buys the most consistency, and name the position I should expect to be driven toward if I make it, since I would rather know in advance whether I am heading for universalism, for a brute restriction with nothing to recommend where the line falls, or for giving up the artifacts I have just insisted upon. Finally, run your diagnosis on three situations from my own bench, and for each say which of my commitments decides it and at what price: a reaction mixture taken just past the gel point, at which a single covalently connected network first spans the vessel while a sol fraction of finite chains remains entangled with it but not bonded to it; a crystal withdrawn from its mother liquor carrying a monolayer of solvent on its faces, part of it hydrogen-bonded to the surface and part merely condensed and about to evaporate; and an oil droplet dispersed in water and held together by a shell of surfactant, considered alongside a dry heap of sand as a control. I ask only for your own reasoning — no reading list, please, as I have retired and intend to stay that way.
