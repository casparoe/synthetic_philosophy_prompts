I cover science and ideas for a weekly general-interest magazine, and for the past few years a university philosophy department has asked outside readers of that kind to mark the submitted essays for its undergraduate prize in a second-year interdisciplinary seminar that pairs philosophy of social science with science and technology studies. I have three scripts this round, a shared rubric, and a deadline at the end of the week. I would like an independent read on one of them before I write my own report. I have not seen the other readers' marks and would rather not form a view in advance, so I am giving you the script cold and telling you nothing about my own reaction to it.

The question the students were set, verbatim from the assignment sheet:

> Is the world becoming less violent? State what would have to be true for that question to have a determinate answer, and then assess the strongest available case that it does not.

Word limit 1,500. The rubric allocates 60 percent to quality of argument, 25 percent to accuracy in the student's use of the authors and findings they invoke, and 15 percent to clarity and structure.

Here is the script, with paragraph numbers I have added for reference:

(1) The question is ambiguous between an absolute reading and a rate reading, and the two come apart. The twentieth century produced more battle deaths than any other, but it also contained far more people, and ranked by the share of the population killed, its two worst wars fall well down the list of the deadliest conflicts on record. A serious answer must therefore fix a denominator and defend the choice. For the long run the standard measure is annual homicides per 100,000 population, because homicide is an event that essentially every legal system already classifies, and because population estimates exist to normalize it.

(2) On that measure there is a large and sustained decline in Europe. Reconstructions from medieval and early modern court and coroner records put annual homicide rates in parts of Western Europe an order of magnitude or more above today's figures, and the fall appears in independent series from several countries over several centuries, which makes it hard to explain as an artifact of one jurisdiction's record-keeping. Skeletal material provides a further check: the frequency of cranial and post-cranial trauma in remains from small-scale and early state societies is far higher than in modern samples, and this evidence is immune to disputes about definitions, since a depressed skull fracture is a depressed skull fracture.

(3) The mechanism usually given first place is the state's monopoly on legitimate force. Where a central authority reliably punifies killing, private retaliation stops paying, and with it the premium on maintaining a reputation for immediate violent response. Elias supplies the sociological version of the same argument: as courts, markets and administrative careers expanded, people came to see that self-control paid, weighed the long-run costs of impulsive aggression against the benefits of holding a stable place in a lengthening chain of economic dependence, and internalized restraint as a matter of prudence. On this account the decline in violence is the sediment of a great many individual calculations about what it is rational to do.

(4) Two complications cut in opposite directions. First, the expansion of state capacity that reduced killing also produced the records through which we observe the reduction, and it enlarged the class of acts that get counted at all. The beating of wives, children and apprentices, duelling, cruelty to animals, and enslavement were for much of the period either lawful or beneath the notice of the courts; most current definitions of violence count them. So a comparison across the two periods is not the same quantity measured twice. Second, and in the other direction, the earlier period is under-recorded rather than over-recorded, and under-inclusive rather than over-inclusive: much of what we would now call violence left no trace. Both biases flatter the past, which means the recorded decline in bodily-injury offences understates the real one. But the inference holds only for that narrow conception. Widen the category to include harms delivered at a distance or through institutions, and the comparison reverses, because the modern period has capacities for large-scale harm that the past simply lacked.

(5) That brings me to the second half of the question, and to what I take to be the strongest case against a determinate answer. It begins with measurement and ends somewhere more ambitious. Premodern rates are reconstructions with very wide error bars. The category of violence is not fixed but has grown, and it grows in contested ways: is economic coercion violence, is a sanction that kills indirectly violence, is risk imposed by pollution violence? And different defensible choices about category, denominator and time window yield different verdicts, so that the twentieth century looks catastrophic on one specification and unremarkable on another. It follows that the world is not becoming less violent and is not becoming more so; the proposition has no truth value. What presents itself as a factual disagreement between defenders of the decline and their critics is really a disagreement about which concept to use, and once the concept is stipulated both sides accept the same numbers. The dispute is verbal rather than substantive, and the live question is which stipulation best serves our purposes.

(6) Conclusion: the decline in homicide is real on the narrow definition and is probably understated by the evidence, but the general claim that the world is becoming less violent is neither true nor false, and should be treated as a choice of vocabulary with practical consequences rather than as an empirical report.
