My town is 150 this summer and the Redwater copper mine — which has been closing and not closing since 1994 — is finally done, and the headframe is being opened as a museum the same afternoon. I've been asked to give the address, about seven minutes. The room will be roughly: fourty or so retired miners and their widows, the company's regional VP who paid for most of the museum, two history professors from the state university, a documentary crew, and the whole town council. Nobody but the professors knows any philosophy, and the miners will smell a lecture a mile off.

Here's my problem. I've been working through Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and I think it's actually the right instrument for this occasion, but I need the speech built out of it rather than decorated with it. Concretely, the town has all three of his sicknesses at once and every one of them is on display that day:

- There's a new bronze of Ezra Redwater, the founder, that the VP commissioned and that I signed a petition against in 2022. That's the monumental one.
- There's the lamp room, which is now the museum entrance, so every visitor walks in under the brass tally board exactly as it hung on the last shift — 41 tallies, including two belonging to men who went down in the cage in 1979 and never came back up. The shift boss couldn't take them down and nobody has since. That's the antiquarian one. The committee wants a plaque under the board with the town motto, WE KEEP WHAT WE DUG.
- There's the 1934 strike, where a company deputy shot a striker named Voss. Voss's grandson and the deputy's granddaughter are both on the museum committee and are second cousins. That's the critical one, and it's the part I care most about, because Nietzsche's point is that the tribunal in critical history is staffed by the living, who are themselves the issue of the thing they're condemning — which in that room is not a metaphor.

The health test he keeps coming back to is plastic power: whether a person or a people can grow out of themselves and incorporate and reshape what they've inherited, instead of being embalmed by it or crushed by it. I want the speech to say something about whether this town has any, and to say it entirely in the language of tallies, shift bells, tailings and lamp oil. Nobody should hear a German word.

Things I need, and things I won't allow:

1. No "we must learn from the past so we don't repeat it," or any cousin of that sentence. I think its exactly backwards here and I don't want to be complicit in it.
2. It has to license forgetting somewhere — the point that action needs a horizon, that a person who can't forget can't do anything — but it cannot tell the widow sitting in the second row to forget a single thing.
3. It has to end on the tally board, and it has to say what should be done with those 41 tallies. "Leave them exactly as they are" and "take them down" are both out. If your answer is that the families decide, tell me what happens when they don't, because two of them already haven't.
4. By the last paragraph the motto on the plaque has to mean something different than it meant in the first one, without my ever saying the motto is wrong.

Write the speech — call it 950 words, spoken rhythm, sentences I can say out loud once and not stumble. And please make it possible for the VP to sit through it without being humiliated; I have to keep working with this man for another decade.
