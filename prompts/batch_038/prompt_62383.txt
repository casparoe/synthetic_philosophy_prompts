Two episodes, one professional and one personal, have left me unable to articulate a distinction I now need.

I am the cataloguing officer at a regional archive. Over roughly two decades we dated a very large body of correspondence by handwriting analysis, watermarks, and internal cross-reference. The method was validated many times over against independently dated material, and the resulting finding aids have been published and cited by other institutions. A commercial laboratory now offers physical dating of paper and ink, at a cost we could comfortably afford for a sample of, say, thirty items. If it agreed with us throughout, we would have learned nothing we were not already prepared to assert, and we would have paid for the privilege. If it disagreed on several items, we would be obliged to reopen thousands of descriptions, retract published aids, and cast doubt on every other collection dated by the same method. We have therefore never commissioned the test. When I put the question to my predecessor and to two colleagues in the field, all three said the refusal was not merely convenient but correct, and I find I agree with them without being able to say why.

The personal case is structurally similar, as far as I can tell. A friend is confident of his wife's affection on the basis of thirty-odd years of shared life. He once had an entirely feasible opportunity to settle the matter conclusively — a bundle of letters, a candid conversation with someone who would not lie to him — and declined it. He also told me, quite calmly, that if the answer had come back negative he would have concluded that the letters were misread or the informant mistaken, rather than that his marriage had been something other than what he took it to be. I did not think less of him for this, and I am not sure that I should have.

What I keep circling is this. A check is available, affordable, and close to decisive. Its two outcomes are wildly asymmetric: agreement adds nothing, while disagreement detonates a large body of beliefs that rest on the same source or method. And the agent already knows, in advance, how a negative result would be treated — as a defect in the check or in its application, not in the original belief, because that belief rests on far more than the single item under test. The check therefore cannot, as a practical matter, move the agent, and the agent declines to perform it. The refusal nonetheless strikes onlookers as legitimate rather than as a rationalization.

I have looked for the concept and keep landing next door to it. Confirmation bias does not fit: nothing here is sought, weighted, or spun, and the question is not how evidence is handled but why none is ever generated. Begging the question does not fit either, since there is no argument being offered to anyone. The nearest thing I have found is a puzzle I read about concerning whether you can establish that an instrument is reliable simply by trusting its outputs and comparing them with one another; my cases look like a mirror image of that, but I cannot say how, or whether the resemblance is real. I have also seen phrases used in other contexts — culpable avoidance of facts one has a duty to know, the claimed prerogative not to be told unwelcome personal information, beliefs that come with a built-in reply to any objection — and none of these seem to be it either.

So: is there an established name for this configuration, and if so, what precisely does the literature take it to be? I would want to know how it is distinguished from each of those neighbors — from blameworthy avoidance, from ordinary calculations about the cost of information, from self-insulating belief, and from the reliability puzzle mentioned above. If the two episodes are not in fact the same phenomenon, I would want that pointed out, and the difference identified. And if nothing established covers it, I would like to know whether the configuration is even coherent: can a feasible check be epistemically worthless by construction, or does a confirming result always add something, however small, so that the real explanation of the refusal is not epistemic at all but about liability, or grief, or reputation? In that case, please supply a term with a definition and its boundary conditions — what would convert this into plain self-deception, and where along the asymmetry does the refusal cease to be defensible? The label is the least of what I am after; a bare name would leave me exactly where I started.
