I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on the philosophy of mind and the emotions — about forty candidates, nearly all of whom have completed one prior philosophy course but none of whom specialises in the area. The paper is three hours long and candidates answer two of four questions. I should like you to draft one of the four, together with everything needed to mark it reliably across two markers.

The topic is what kind of thing an emotion is: an evaluative judgment, a perception of value, or a feeling, where "feeling" means a pattern of felt bodily change together with the experience that goes with it. The question must not be answerable by rehearsing that tripartite taxonomy and attaching a champion to each term. What I want is a question that makes candidates apply the three accounts to a pair of stipulated cases constructed so that the accounts come apart as sharply as possible, and then judge what the cases do and do not decide.

Please build the stem around the following two cases. You may tighten the prose and adjust incidental detail, but keep their structure intact, since the structure is what does the work.

Case 1 — "cold fear." R. was badly bitten as a child and has spent thirty years avoiding dogs. When a large off-leash dog walks toward her on a path, her heart rate climbs, her palms sweat, she startles at a sudden noise behind her, her gait changes, and she steps off the path. Asked immediately afterwards, she says plainly that she was not afraid: nothing seemed dangerous to her, she was not thinking about being bitten, and she has no experience she would describe as dread or panic. She is not confused about the words and has no motive to conceal anything; on further questioning she is as confident of this as she is of what she ate for breakfast.

Case 2 — "hot calm." S. sits waiting for the result of a biopsy. He is certain, and says he is certain, that the news will be devastating and that this is the worst thing that could happen to him. He reports a vivid, insistent sense of impending catastrophe that occupies his thoughts and that he cannot set aside. His heart rate, skin conductance, facial configuration, posture, and voice all remain within his normal resting range for the whole hour. He avoids nothing, seeks nothing out, and behaves exactly as he would have behaved had he been told the result was benign.

The question should then ask candidates to do two things. Part (a), carrying the bulk of the marks: state what each of the three accounts says about whether R. fears the dog and about whether S. is afraid; for each account, identify the specific addition or concession it would need in order to avoid whichever verdict looks wrong in its case; and say which of the three can absorb that concession least damagingly, arguing for the choice rather than merely announcing it. Part (b), shorter: assess the objection that the stipulations have already settled the question, on the ground that to describe R. as avoiding, startling, and sweating, and S. as physiologically and behaviourally unmoved, is to describe them in vocabulary that one of the accounts privileges.

Two constraints on the stem. It must be self-contained: no candidate should be required to supply a fact about the world, or about any particular experiment, that is not stated on the paper. And it must not ask candidates to cite, name, or date any author or text. Where you think a strong answer would naturally mention a philosopher, keep the marking scheme neutral — reward the move, not the name. The stem should run to roughly 350 words, with the marks for each part shown and a recommended length of answer.

Please supply, in order:

1. The question stem as it will appear on the paper.
2. A model answer of about 900 words that takes a definite position on part (a). I do not want a survey that ends by saying the cases pull in both directions; the model answer should commit to one account's concession being least damaging and defend that commitment, while acknowledging honestly what it costs.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 that does not simply paraphrase the model answer. It should list the individual moves a strong answer makes, the marks each carries, what the move looks like when executed well, and the characteristic error that forfeits it. For instance, an answer that treats the judgment account as refuted by Case 1 without noticing that the account may simply deny that R.'s avoidance expresses an emotion at all should lose marks in a specified place; so should an answer that reads Case 2 as a description of a belief rather than of an emotion and thereby assumes what is at issue. Please identify which single move is worth most, and make the scheme show clearly what separates a first-class answer from a competent but unambitious one — my expectation is that many candidates will map the three accounts onto the two cases correctly and then stop, and the scheme must make that ceiling visible.
4. A note of five or six lines on the two or three responses you expect most often from candidates who have memorised the taxonomy without grasping the dispute, indicating where in the scheme each is penalised.

Finally, please tell me whether you think the case pair is fair to the feeling account or weighted against it. If you judge it weighted, propose the smallest change to the stipulations that would even things out, and say how that change would alter the marking scheme.
