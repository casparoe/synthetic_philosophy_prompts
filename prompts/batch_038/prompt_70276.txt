I am in the final weeks of an undergraduate seminar on self-locating belief, and I run the student-organized review session that precedes our examination. Two of the students who attend are statistics majors who have not done any of the philosophy reading, so the session's materials have to be self-contained. I want to hand out a diagnostic instrument — a quiz titled something along the lines of "what do you actually believe about the Sleeping Beauty case?" — that sorts participants by their answers to concrete situations rather than by which label they claim. Please write it, together with a scoring key.

**The quiz.** Ten numbered items. Each item must state its own situation completely and in ordinary language, so that a reader who has never encountered the canonical coin-and-amnesia experiment can still answer it; do not rely on the reader supplying the standard apparatus from memory. Each item should offer two to four options, and every option must be a substantive response — a number, a choice about a bet, a yes or no about what the subject learns — never a name of a view, never a description of a philosophical camp, and never anything of the form "I am inclined to say…". No item may ask the respondent to characterize themselves, and no item may be answerable by arithmetic on which all the rival positions already agree. Simple fractions only; nothing requiring calculation.

**Three items are mandatory, and I want them built exactly as specified.**

*Item 1 — the deferred toss.* On Sunday the subject is put to sleep and told the whole procedure. She is woken once, interviewed, given the amnesia drug, and put back to sleep. Only after that first interview is a fair coin tossed; she knows on Sunday that the toss will happen after her first interview, and hence that at the moment of that interview it has not yet happened. If the coin lands heads she is not woken again; if it lands tails she is woken a second time, interviewed, and again given the drug. This item has two parts: (a) at the first interview, what should her credence be that the coin will land heads, and (b) is that answer the same as, higher than, or lower than the answer she should give in the ordinary version of the experiment in which the coin is tossed on Sunday before she is first put to sleep?

*Item 2 — the three-waking experiment.* On Sunday she is put to sleep and told the following. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads she is woken and interviewed once, on Monday. If it lands tails she is woken and interviewed on each of Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday. After every interview she receives the amnesia drug, so that during an interview she cannot tell what day it is or whether she has been interviewed before. At an interview she is asked two things: her credence that the coin landed heads, and her credence that the interview she is having right now is her first. Offer numeric options for each of the two sub-questions and treat the pair of answers jointly in the key.

*Item 3 — the Monday disclosure.* She is woken, interviewed, and then truthfully told that today is Monday. What is her credence now that the coin landed heads? The options must include the possibility that her answer is unchanged from the one she would have given on Sunday before the experiment began.

**The remaining seven items** are yours to design, but between them they must cover: whether waking up at all counts as gaining evidence; a control situation in which the number of interviews does not depend on the outcome, so that the tempting answer and the correct answer coincide; a pricing or betting situation in which tickets bought at successive interviews are all retained and paid out rather than cancelled; whether being vulnerable to a sequence of bets that jointly guarantee a loss settles the question of what her credence should be; a case that turns on how many subjects like her will exist rather than on how many experiences one subject will have; whether the original question as posed has a determinate answer at all; and whether the disagreement is really about the probability calculus or about how to represent and update on content that picks out one moment in one life.

**The key.** Sort by combination of answers, not by accumulating points — no numerical scoring anywhere. For each item, one line saying which fork it probes. Then give the positions that the pattern of answers can produce, and these must appear among them: the view that says one half on Sunday, one half on waking, but two thirds once she is told it is Monday; the view that says one half on Sunday, one half on waking, and one half even after being told it is Monday; the view that says one third on waking; a view on which the right answer is a range rather than a number; at least two views distinguished by the general principle they apply to one's own place among observers or observer-moments; the view that conditions on everything one knows including the indexical part; the deflationary view that the question is ambiguous and asks for more than one thing; and the view that the dispute concerns the representation of self-locating content rather than the rules of probability.

Also include: at least three combinations of answers that no recognized position endorses, each with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unstable under further reflection, or merely unexplored, and a remark on whether any of them is worth occupying; a procedure for respondents whose answers conflict with one another, which identifies the two offending items rather than assigning them to the nearest position; and at least two pairs of positions that these ten items cannot separate, each with a description of the eleventh item that would separate them.

**Constraints on nomenclature.** Where a view has a settled name in the literature and you are confident of it, use the name; where you are not, coin a descriptive label and mark it in a footnote as yours rather than standard. Name positions, not people: no attributions to individual philosophers, no titles, no dates, no bibliography or further reading, and no quoted passages from any text. Finish with a short paragraph on how I should run this in a forty-minute session — in what order to read the items aloud, where to expect disagreement to break out, and which item to drop if we run short.
