**What I want.** I want a case study that runs in both directions. The theory: the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction between two ways a subject's experience of a temporal sequence can fail to match the sequence itself, as it was developed for the color phi phenomenon. The case: Benjamin Libet's experiments on voluntary action and the readiness potential, plus his earlier cortical-stimulation work, which I think is the part the distinction actually fits. I already know the standard color phi story — two differently colored lights flashed in rapid succession at nearby locations, the reported experience of a single moving spot that changes color somewhere along a path that was never physically present, and the resulting dilemma about whether the subject first had one experience that was subsequently overwritten in memory or never had the earlier experience at all because the revision happened before publication. I do not need that rehearsed. What I need is the transplant into a different modality of case, and an honest verdict on whether the distinction survives it.

**The facts I am supplying so that you do not have to recall them.** In the readiness-potential experiments, awake subjects made a small spontaneous movement such as flexing a wrist while watching a spot sweeping around a circle like a very fast clock hand; they reported the position of the spot at the moment they were first aware of the urge to move. Averaged scalp recordings, time-locked to muscle onset, show a slow negative shift building up over motor and premotor areas beginning on the order of half a second before the movement — call it 500–550 ms — while the reported time of the urge falls much later, around 200 ms before the movement. The gap between the electrical onset and the reported urge, a few hundred milliseconds, is the entire controversy. In a separate line of work on awake patients undergoing neurosurgery, Libet found that direct electrical stimulation of exposed somatosensory cortex had to be sustained for a surprisingly long train — on the order of half a second at near-threshold intensities — before any sensation was reported at all, and that subjects then located the resulting sensation back near the onset of the train rather than at its end, a backward referral in time. He also ran comparative trials pairing a cortical train with a peripheral stimulus on the skin, where the ordering subjects reported did not track physical onset order in the naive way. Later, other labs reported decoding a forthcoming left-versus-right choice from frontoparietal activity several seconds before the subject's own report of deciding. And separately, a later reanalysis argued that the readiness potential is better modeled as the slow drift of ongoing noisy neural activity toward a threshold, so that its onset in any single trial is not a well-defined event at all but an artifact of averaging trials aligned to movement.

**The new ingredient.** Here is a third model I want you to take seriously as a live option, and it is my coinage, not something you will find in the literature. Call it the postmark model. On it, the content of the experience is delivered accurately and is not tampered with, and there is no delayed publication either; what can go wrong is only the timestamp attached to the experience afterwards by a separate process. The letter is written honestly and filed unaltered; the postal service stamps it with the wrong date. On the postmark model the subject genuinely has the experience of urging at whatever time it in fact occurs, and the reported time is a postdictive attribution computed from available evidence — the movement itself, proprioception, the clock reading, expectations about how long deciding takes — rather than a reading off the experience. This is not the Orwellian option, because nothing that was there is overwritten or misremembered; it is not the Stalinesque option, because nothing waits to be published. It claims that the debate has been arguing about the wrong component of the system.
