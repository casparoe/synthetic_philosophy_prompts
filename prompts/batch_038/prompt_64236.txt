I am convening a two-hour session on how regularities in behaviour persist, for a mixed group: economists, anthropologists, two practising lawyers, and philosophers. Roughly half have read Lewis's analysis of convention; the other half have met none of this literature and have no patience for jargon. I want to hand them a classification instrument — a diagnostic questionnaire, plus a decision tree and a scoring key — that sorts each of them by what they actually believe holds a regularity in place, rather than by which theorist they have read or which label they reach for when asked afterwards.

Please build the following.

1. First, the conceptual space, before any names. Lay out three axes along which regularities in behaviour differ. (a) The swap test: whether some different regularity would serve the participants equally well, so that all of them would be willing to move to it together while none of them would want to move to it alone. (b) What keeps it in place: a disposition to do as others do, conditioned on believing that others will do it; a belief that third parties will react badly to departures; an uneasiness that survives the total absence of onlookers and of any belief about what others will do; or nothing beyond the momentum of habit. (c) How it arose: by drift and convergence with nobody choosing it; by negotiation, enactment, or explicit agreement; or by the imposition of one party on others who were in no position to refuse. State how many cells the three axes yield, and for every cell that no well-known position occupies, say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored — and name one real regularity that would or would not sit there.

2. Then the tree itself, in indented text. Every branch node must be a question a reader can answer from the items in section 3 without knowing which way the branch leads, and no node may use any of the vocabulary listed in section 1. The leaves must include, at minimum: a Lewisian convention in the strict sense; a norm of proven cooperation of the kind Ullmann-Margalit describes; a Bicchieri-style social norm that binds an agent only when they believe enough others will conform and enough others think they ought to; a norm so thoroughly internalized that it holds with no audience, no belief about anyone's conduct, and nothing to gain; a bare descriptive regularity carrying no ought whatsoever; and a regularity sustained by an asymmetry of power, in which the appearance of mutual benefit is an artefact of stakes that were never symmetrical. If a seventh leaf is genuinely required to stop the tree from collapsing, add it and defend it in one sentence.

3. Twelve to fourteen diagnostic items, numbered, each a vignette of no more than ninety words followed by two to four lettered options. These constraints are load-bearing:

 - The words convention, norm, normative, coordination, cooperation, equilibrium, salience, salient, common knowledge, expectation, sanction, internalize, preference, payoff, game, and rational may not appear in any vignette or option. Ordinary-language paraphrase is precisely what I want — say what a person would say at a dinner table. What I do not want is the technical term with a euphemism bolted on the front, so informal norm, implicit expectation, and the like are banned too.
 - Omit the four stock illustrations: which side of the road people drive on, which sounds mean what, money as a generally accepted medium of exchange, and the handshake. Reach instead for underworked cases — buying rounds of drinks at a pub, the order that re-forms when a service window closes and reopens, whose surname comes first in a hyphenated name, who carves or serves at a shared meal, the rota for a village irrigation channel, how long a standing ovation goes on, whether to answer everyone or only the sender, the last portion left untouched on a shared plate, the timing and wording of an apology, two people meeting in a narrow corridor.
 - At least two items must come as matched pairs: the same behaviour described once in the reader's own community and once in a community they are merely passing through, will never return to, and whose members have no way of treating them badly afterwards. The pair exists to expose whether the reader's verdict depends on who is in a position to notice.
 - No item may name the payoff structure of any game. If stakes matter, put them in plain words: that each would rather the other did the thing while they did not; that all do better if all do it; that a person acting on their own gains nothing by departing.
 - No item may ask the reader to identify a position, a school, or a person. Every item asks what the reader takes to be going on in the case described.

4. A scoring key in table form: which patterns of answers across which clusters of items place a reader at which leaf; a tie-break rule for readers who split evenly between two leaves; and a named flag for the reader whose answers show them applying different criteria on different items, together with the one sentence of feedback that flag should carry on the handout.

5. A short section listing every pair of leaves your items cannot reliably separate. For each, either give the item that would separate them or explain why no such item is available to this audience and the two leaves ought to be merged on the handout.

6. Finally, for each leaf, the single sentence the reader is handed at the end of the session, stating which of the vignette regularities their answers have committed them to treating as conventional in Lewis's technical sense and which they have committed them to treating as something else. The purpose is to make readers notice that the word they use casually is doing far less work than the analysis does.

The finished handout must be printable in eight pages. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or page citations; where a view is attributed, a name in the running text is sufficient.
