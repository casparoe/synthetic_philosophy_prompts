I'm a house tutor at a boarding school — 28 kids aged 16-18, and I've got one 90-minute slot with all of them on the Sunday evening before half term. Backstory: until June the house ran on a genuinely tight rulebook (room inspections twice a week, phone curfew, sign-out sheets, prefects with real sanctioning power), there was a near-revolt in March, and the new head has scrapped the whole thing and told them to write their own house constitution, due in about three weeks. The house has since split into a "we'll rot without structure" faction and a "we're not children, leave us alone" faction, and the same argument has now gone round the same circle four times in house meeting with nobody converting anybody. Over the summer I read one of those pop-science books about tight and loose cultures, and what struck me is that both sides are right about different things and the trade-off is the actual content of the dispute: tightness buys coordination, safety and follow-through at the cost of initiative and resentment, looseness buys creativity and genuine buy-in at the cost of drift and free-riding, and which one is correct depends largely on how much threat a group is actually under and who gets to say whether the threat is real. I want them to run into that trade-off physically instead of re-litigating it verbally.

So: design me a 90-minute activity for those 28 teenagers. Hard constraints — no lecture up front, and nobody hears the words "tight" or "loose" until the debrief at the end. I want them split into two sub-groups operating under different institutional regimes, assigned by lottery rather than chosen, each with a real task that produces something they can see, and a scoring scheme that converts into actual voting weight in the constitutional drafting (no money involved, no budget at all; I have index cards, four whiteboards, markers, a deck of cards and a kitchen timer). Somewhere past the halfway point there needs to be a shock that changes which regime is better adapted, and I want at least a few students to have a genuine option to migrate to the other group — partly because I suspect the fight over whether exit is allowed is where the interesting normative stuff lives, and partly because I want the tight group to be tempted to close the door. Please don't hand me the standard public-goods-with-punishment game dressed up in new language; punishment is a single dial, and this isn't about how hard you punish, its about how much of life is specified in advance, what counts as a deviation, how much room is left for improvisation, and how the group treats the person who goes off-script.

Give me the full package: the two rule sheets pretty much verbatim as I'd read them out, roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, the shock and how I trigger it without looking like a puppeteer, the migration mechanism, and then a debrief that maps specific things that will have just happened in the room onto the concepts and onto the questions I actually care about — who decides whether the threat is real, whether a tight group can legitimately block exit, whether a group can be too loose to function and what that looks like from inside. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with real teenagers who already dislike each other's factions and one very clever boy who will probably work out the design in minute five and announce it. And be frank about which feature of the idea the activity just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in talk — my guess is that my rules are handed down by an adult, arbitrary, and known to be a game, whereas real tight norms are internalised and experienced as legitimate by the people living under them, which is exactly the thing the "leave us alone" kids don't currently believe about last year's rulebook. I don't need a reading list or any references, I just need something I can actually run in four days.
