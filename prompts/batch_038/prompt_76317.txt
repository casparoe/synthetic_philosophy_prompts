My sister-in-law has written the advice column for a small weekly paper for nineteen years. She is recovering from surgery, and the column has been handed to me to ghostwrite. Her husband — my brother-in-law — is a philosopher of mind and a sincere eliminative materialist about propositional attitudes, and he has spent the past fortnight explaining to me, with real enthusiasm, that his wife's entire professional toolkit is a discredited theory. Nobody has beliefs, nobody has desires; the column's job, properly understood, is to describe behavioral dispositions and patterns of neural activation, not to reason with readers about what they think and want. He has asked me to produce one installment written the way he says it ought to be written. I intend to oblige him. I also intend for him to feel what is lost somewhere below the level of argument.

Please write a single installment of that column, in the voice of an advice columnist who is a fully committed eliminativist and who is, in all sincerity and without any irony of her own, trying to do her job properly. Use the newspaper's standard apparatus: a masthead line and name for the column, two reader letters with invented small-town sign-offs in the manner of "— Perplexed in Piqua," and her reply to each. Roughly 700 to 800 words in total.

The constraints are the whole point, so please treat them as binding:

- The letters are written by ordinary readers and may be as thoroughly folk-psychological as real letters are. Her replies may not ascribe a single propositional attitude. No "you believe," "he wants," "she hopes," "they suspect that," "you're afraid he's lying." Her only permitted resources are the ones she thinks she actually has: overt behavior, behavioral dispositions, physiological and neural states, learning history, and reinforcement.
- She must nevertheless give each writer something they could act on, and she must be warm, competent, and clearly trying to help. She is not a fool. Where she fails, the failure should be visible entirely in what these particular people get back from her.
- Choose the two hardest letters you can: one from a wife who is convinced her husband is lying to her about something, and one from a young woman asking whether the man she is seeing really loves her. In neither case may the columnist say what anyone believes, suspects, wants, or means.
- At least once she should deploy the standard complaint that the folk theory has made no progress in twenty-five centuries and cannot explain sleep, dreaming, memory, learning, or mental illness — and the deployment should land very badly against the letter it is answering.
- Include one moment where she uses an intentional verb, catches herself, and repairs the sentence. The repair must be funnier than the slip and philosophically better than the original.
- She should gesture at the eliminativist's favorite analogies — witches, phlogiston, vital spirit, the crystalline spheres — in a way that turns out to undercut her.
- Handle the self-undermining objection the way a sophisticated eliminativist would: the thesis is a claim about how the world is rather than about the speaker's own states, and entertaining or grasping a proposition is not the same as standing in an attitude toward it. She must not name the objection, and she must not sound defensive.
- End one of the replies with a practical recommendation that, on inspection, smuggles an intentional-state attribution in through a back door. The back door should be legible to a philosopher of mind and invisible to a general reader.

Target the position and its claim to be adequate to the business of advising people — never a named individual, living or dead. Stay deadpan throughout; do not wink, do not signal the joke, and do not let the columnist realize she is being satirized.

Begin your response with the column itself, masthead and all. No preface, no framing note, no explanation afterward of what the piece is doing.
