Background. My team builds a system that infers what users want from logged choices — clicks, purchases, accepts, declines — and converts that into a scalar score used to rank items and, in some flows, to act on the user's behalf. The word "preference" is load-bearing in every design document we write, and three people on the team mean three different things by it. Our economist consultant says a preference just is what choice reveals, so there is nothing further to measure and the logs already contain everything there is to contain. Our designer says users often have no settled preference until we show them a slate, so what we log records our interface at least as much as it records them. Our legal reviewer wants to know when we may decline to honor a choice the user demonstrably made. Nobody has said what would settle the dispute, and I would rather have the specification in hand before I start reading theories of preference.

Deliverable: the desiderata — the requirements any theory of preference-as-inferred-from-observable-choice must meet to serve as the foundation for a system like ours. The list must cover, at minimum:

- Cases the theory has to get right. A user whose choices are detectably intransitive — our economist says an agent like that can be "money-pumped," and I want to know whether that is a reason to demand transitivity of the preferences we attribute, or a reason to demand something else. Menu-dependence: what a user takes changes because of what else was on the slate (the last of two items gets taken; the same item on a six-item list does not). The strategic user who is gaming the recommender rather than expressing anything. - Distinctions the theory may not collapse: preference versus choice; ordinal versus cardinal; descriptive attribution versus normative endorsement; having a preference versus how strong it is.
