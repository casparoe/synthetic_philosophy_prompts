I am drafting an internal memo for a municipal government that is weighing whether to extend the local franchise to long-term residents who are not citizens. At the hearing that effectively settled the council's position, one member disposed of every objection with the same sentence: 'those who are subject to the law must have a hand in making it.' I want the memo to establish what that sentence can and cannot be made to mean, since I believe several incompatible claims were running through it at once, and I suspect the reading the council needs is not the one that sounds most plausible.

Please set the readings out as a menu rather than settling on a single gloss. For each, state it precisely enough to be assessable as true or false: which population it picks out (legal residents, persons coerced by the rules, persons whose interests are causally affected, persons with a stake in the outcome), what a 'hand in making' requires (an equal vote, a veto, consultation, a procedure that could not reasonably be rejected), and what normative work it is doing (legitimacy of the law, an obligation to obey it, justice in the composition of the electorate, fairness of the decision procedure). Then say of each whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false, and whether it in fact entails enfranchising our long-term resident non-citizens.

Two complications should be addressed explicitly. First, the same council administers a business improvement district whose rules bind commuters and out-of-town property owners who are considerably more numerous than the residents they would outvote, and the member invoked the same sentence on their behalf as well. Say which readings survive that application, which collapse under it, and what a defender would have to add to keep them alive. Second, the sentence is being used to determine the boundaries of the electorate itself. Explain what happens when the criterion is turned on the question of who gets to apply the criterion, and whether that reflexive use refutes it, is harmless, or merely relocates the difficulty.

Close by identifying which reading the hearing actually required, and what the council would have to argue for if that reading turns out to be the weakest item on the menu.
