**The question set (compulsory for students on the Leibniz stream, 1,000 words maximum):**

> (a) Leibniz's formula for justice is the charity of the wise. Explain what work the appeal to wisdom does in that definition, and how the account of love on which it rests is supposed to be compatible with Leibniz's own claim that every agent acts for the sake of its own pleasure. (b) Does Leibniz's moral philosophy stand or fall with his thesis that God has created the best of all possible worlds? Argue for your answer.

**The student's answer (978 words):**

Leibniz's claim is that justice is charity, or benevolence, regulated by wisdom. The addition of wisdom is what does the work. Benevolence on its own is simply a disposition to want good for others, and unregulated it can do harm: a benefactor who gives without measure, or who favours one person at the expense of a greater good, is benevolent but not just. Wisdom is the knowledge of the order of things, of which goods are greater and how they fit together, so the wise benefactor distributes good will in proportion to what each recipient can make of it. Justice is therefore not a feeling but a feeling governed by a science, and this is why Leibniz thinks it can be treated demonstratively, in the manner of the jurisconsults.

He develops this through three ascending degrees. The lowest is strict right, which enjoins us to harm no one; it concerns claims that can be enforced between parties who share no common superior, and in the absence of such a superior it leaves the parties in something like a condition of war. The second is equity, which enjoins us to give each their due and which corrects the letter of strict right by attention to proportion and to the preservation of peace. The third is piety, the universal justice that enjoins us to live honourably, and whose object is no longer this or that claimant but the whole society of minds under God, where the good of each is bound up with the good of the whole. The degrees are not alternatives but stations on one ascent: the higher contains the lower and supplies its rationale.

The moral psychology behind all of this is hedonist in a fairly strict sense. For Leibniz pleasure is the perception of an increase in one's perfection, and happiness is not a momentary gratification but a durable state of pleasure sustained through a series of such perceptions. He glosses love as a disposition to take pleasure in the perfection or happiness of another. Since every agent acts for the sake of its own pleasure, and since the just man is the one who loves universally, the just man is the man whose pleasure is taken in the greatest possible perfection of the greatest possible number.

It is worth noting what this is directed against. Leibniz rejects the Hobbesian reduction of justice to the will of the stronger, and in his remarks on Pufendorf's principles he objects to the attempt to ground obligation in the command of a superior backed by sanctions. His complaint is that such an account makes justice extrinsic: it locates obligation in fear and in the power of another rather than in an inward disposition of the just person. Against this Leibniz maintains that the truths of justice are eternal and independent of any will, including God's, and that God is subject to them rather than their author.

This is where I think Leibniz is in difficulty. He protests against Hobbes while keeping Hobbes's psychology. On his own account every agent acts for its own pleasure; and love is defined as taking pleasure in another's perfection. But then the other's perfection enters the account only as the occasion of the lover's own pleasure, which is to say that the desire terminates in the lover. Disinterested love, as Leibniz describes it, is self-love with a longer description attached: the just man loves universally because universal love is what feels best to him. Leibniz can only escape the Hobbesian conclusion by abandoning the hedonism, which he plainly will not do, since it is the hedonism that allows him to connect justice to happiness and hence to make virtue attractive. So the definition of justice as the charity of the wise does not deliver a genuinely other-directed motive; it delivers a refined prudentialism and calls it benevolence.

Turning to part (b), I think the answer is yes, and for reasons Leibniz would resist. God's intellect necessarily represents the best as best, and God's will necessarily follows the representation of the intellect. If the antecedent cannot fail and the consequence cannot fail, then the actuality of this world cannot fail. Leibniz calls the necessity here moral rather than metaphysical, but the label seems to me to soften a conclusion he cannot avoid: an alternative that is excluded by the nature of the chooser is not a live alternative merely because we have declined to describe its exclusion as a contradiction. So God does not choose the best of a set of genuinely open possibilities; the best unfolds. And if the value of the world is necessary in this way, obligation is necessary in the same way, since obligation for Leibniz is just the requirement to conform to the order of perfection that God's choice has fixed. Nothing is in the end up to anyone.

This is why, I think, Leibniz's ethics has a quietist shape. If whatever happens contributes to the best of all possible worlds, then there is nothing to be gained by striving to improve it, and the wise man's task is contemplative assent rather than reform. The practical upshot of the optimism is patience. Leibniz does not want to say this in so many words, but it follows from making the goodness of the whole independent of what any particular agent does.

One further point about the third degree. Piety has no earthly forum. There is no court before which universal justice can be pleaded and no sanction that can enforce it in this life, so the highest degree of right is unintelligible apart from the government of God over the republic of minds. Leibniz is explicit that theology and jurisprudence are the same science taken at different scopes, and piety is the point at which the identity becomes visible. Strict right and equity can be described in a purely human register; piety cannot.
