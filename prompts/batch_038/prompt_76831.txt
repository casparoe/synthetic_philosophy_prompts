Okay so — I write literary fiction, and I've got a third novel going about a character whose language is coming apart (early-onset aphasia, roughly), and I keep drifting back to Flowers for Algernon, which I read when I was about fourteen and havent touched since, and which I now suspect is a much cleverer book than anyone gives it credit for. I want to think about it properly before I let it into my own draft.

So: please put yourself in the seat of a philosopher of mind who also reads a lot of novels. Not a lit-crit person who's heard of philosophy — someone who could referee a paper on the intentional stance and also has an opinion about Keyes's pacing and where he cheats.

Before you write any of the actual reading, brainstorm out loud for a bit. Take Dennett's four kinds of creatures — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian ladder — and work out, honestly and with an eye for where the ladder strains, which rung Charlie Gordon is on at each phase of the book: before the operation, at the peak, during the stretch where he figures out why the gains arent going to hold (the Algernon-Gordon effect, as he names it), and in the last few reports. Then do the same for Algernon. Then, if you can be bothered, for Alice Kinnian and for Nemur — Nemur might be the interesting one, because he's fully Gregorian and yet never seems to have an original thought.

Then give me the reading. Things I want grappled with rather than gestured at:

Does the book actually dramatize a ladder, or does it treat intelligence as a single dial that gets turned up and then down again? Those are very different claims and I think Keyes slides between them, and that the sliding is where the book's real interest lives.

Make the obvious objection against me, don't let me discover it later: the Gregorian rung isnt about raw horsepower, it's about having the shared symbolic kit — language, writing, arithmetic, the borrowed tools that live outside the skull. On that account Charlie was already Gregorian at an IQ of 68, and the surgery changes something the taxonomy has no slot for. If that's right, what is the operation doing in Dennett's terms, and does the novel know what it's doing?

The progress reports as a Gregorian artifact documenting its own decay: spelling and syntax going while the self-model that notices it stays intact. Is there something about the form here that philosophy of mind simply can't say? And does Charlie's continued ability to tell us that he's losing the ability to tell us count as evidence about which rung he's on?

Algernon. He gets the identical treatment and never gets a report. What does the book commit itself to about mouse minds by making him Charlie's twin instead of his control?

Then tell me where the novel resists all of this — the two or three places where the ladder is a Procrustean bed and the honest critic has to say so, because I dont want a book flattened into an illustration. And close with what Keyes gets right about ascending and descending minds that the four-creatures literature doesn't capture, i.e. the thing I'd be stealing, knowingly, if I go ahead.

Think carefully and don't hurry toward a tidy ending — I'd rather have a couple of real tensions left standing than a bow on it. No bibliography, no suggestions of further reading, and don't quote the novel at me, I'll have it open on the desk. If you're reconstructing a plot detail from memory rather than reasoning from it, flag that you're doing it.
