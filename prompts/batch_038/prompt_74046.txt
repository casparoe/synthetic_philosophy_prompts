Recording an episode for our dept podcast next month and the guest is a philosopher who thinks the whole wrong-kind/right-kind reasons debate rests on a mistake. Her line: the demon who'll punish you unless you admire him isn't offering a competing reason to admire him, he's answering a different question — whether admiring him would be good, not whether he's admirable. Same with belief, which answers "is it true" rather than "would it be good to believe it", and same with the million dollars on offer if you intend, by midnight, to drink something that'll make you sick tomorrow. She pushes it further: this makes evidentialism come out true by analysis instead of being a contested thesis, and what look like wrong-kind reasons for grief or fear are really reasons to cultivate or display the feeling, not to have it.

Draft me seven questions, each with a follow-up — and for at least three of them the follow-up needs to be a fresh case she has to handle, not the same question reworded. I want one on the self-fulfilling beliefs (talking yourself into thinking your partner will pull through, a coach's confidence in a kid) where the truth question and the good question look fused; one on whether her analysis leaves a pragmatist about belief unable to even state his view; one on whether she explains our inability to believe or intend for a reward or just renames it; and one on why the demon doesn't give you an all-things-considered ought even if it isn't a reason. Listeners are grad students plus curious outsiders, so no philosophers' names or paper titles inside the questions, and each case gets described in one plain sentence.
