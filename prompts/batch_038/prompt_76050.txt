I'm teaching a graduate epistemology seminar this term and we're deep in the Williamson material — the anti-luminosity argument and what it's supposed to do to the KK principle — and I've realized I can't explain one transition in a way I'm happy with, so I want to think it through with you before class. Here's the setup as I have it. A condition C is luminous iff whenever C obtains you're in a position to know that C obtains. The argument takes a fine-grained series of cases running from one where C obtains to one where it doesn't, invokes a margin-for-error premise (roughly: if at case i you know that C obtains at case i, then C obtains at case i+1), and walks that premise down the series by induction until it lands on a case where C fails to obtain — contradiction, so no non-trivial condition is luminous. And then KK is meant to fall with it, because KK would make the condition "knows that p" luminous, indeed more than luminous, since it delivers actual second-order knowledge rather than mere position-to-know. Fine so far. But there are three things I dont want to just wave at in front of the students:

(1) The margin-for-error premise is doing all the work, and it looks like it's bought from safety. But safety is normally stated as a constraint on the actual basis of belief across nearby cases where you form the same belief, whereas the premise as used here has a knowledge claim at case i licensing the *obtaining* of the condition at a different case, i+1. Walk me through why safety is supposed to deliver that, and whether someone who accepts safety but denies the margin-for-error premise has a stable position or just gets the argument re-run on them with safety plugged in directly.

(2) In the cold-morning style series the condition varies smoothly through the world, so the series is easy to construct. For "S knows that p" with p fixed — say, that the letter is in the second drawer — what plays the role of the series? I assume it has to be a series of epistemic positions rather than of the world, with the subject's evidence degrading a hair at a time until knowledge is gone and then some. But then I'm not confident the non-triviality requirement is doing the same work it does in the first-order case, and I'm especially unsure whether the evidence at stage i+1 counts as "just barely worse" in the sense the premise needs, since its evidence about evidence — the second-order series is one step removed from the thing that's gradually changing.

(3) One of my students wants to rescue a restricted KK. Her proposal: call it monitored knowledge — S monitors-ly knows that p iff S knows that p via some method M and S also has a reliable, separately exercised capacity to detect whether M was actually deployed in the case at hand. Then, she says, KK holds for monitored knowledge, because the margin-for-error premise isn't available for the second-order claim: the self-monitoring channel doesn't degrade along the same series as the first-order evidence, since what it tracks is the deployment of a method, not the strength of the evidence for p. I think there's something to that, but I also suspect the argument either re-runs a level up (you'd want monitored knowledge of monitored knowledge, and then of that) or else the restriction smuggles in exactly the sort of sharp cut-off that the higher-order vagueness considerations are supposed to rule out. Tell me whether her principle actually escapes, and if it does, sketch what an epistemic logic containing it but not plain KK would look like — is it still normal, does it still support anything like introspection for the interesting cases, what gets lost?

What I'm after is a careful but intuitive explanation I can crib from for the seminar, not a survey of the literature, and no reading list. If you think one of these three worries rests on a misreading of how the argument works, say so plainly and point at exactly where I've gone wrong.
