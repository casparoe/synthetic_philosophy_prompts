Two people I've been reading on a rationalist-ish Discord for the better part of a decade got into it about Nuremberg last week — Dan, who's mostly AI safety, and Miri, who does legal history — and it ran to something like two hundred messages and neither of them backed down. I don't want to pick a side before I actually understand the argument, so: can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me whos right at each point?

(1) Dan opened with the aggressive-war charge being invented. Nothing before August 1945, he said, made starting a war a crime for which an individual could be prosecuted. The Kellogg–Briand Pact renounced war as an instrument of national policy and Germany signed it, but it created no tribunal, no penalty and no individual liability, so when the London Charter defined crimes against peace that summer, the tribunal was convicting men under a rule that didn't exist when they acted. The defence's nullum crimen objection was just correct, and since the bench was made up entirely of judges from the four winning powers there wasn't a neutral decision-maker either.

(2) Miri: retroactivity is unfair only where it defeats a legitimate expectation, and legality is a device for giving fair warning — it isn't a value in itself. There was warning. Germany had renounced aggressive war by treaty along with dozens of other states, and nobody at the top of the Reich supposed a war of conquest would be treated as lawful by anybody, victor or vanquished. So what was new was the machinery, not the wrong, and a procedural irregularity isn't the same thing as an injustice.

(3) Dan said that's a concession, because if the point of legality is fair warning rather than legality, you have to run the same test against the alternatives the Allies actually had on the table. Churchill wanted the leading Nazis shot with no trial at all and it was the Americans who pushed for a court; on a pure fair-warning metric you can at least ask whether a retroactive charge is worse than no charge. And separately, if the test is pre-existing wrongfulness, the same test reaches the Allies — unrestricted submarine warfare, the area bombing of cities — and the reason none of that appeared in an indictment isn't a principle, it's the score at full time. He agreed tu quoque isn't a defence a defendant can run, but said it's fatal to the claim that Nuremberg was law being applied rather than power being exercised.

(4) Miri split it in two. On procedure: the trial wasn't a nicer-looking execution, it made the victors prove things and produced a record, the defendants spoke and called evidence — Dönitz's side got written answers out of Nimitz about American submarine practice in the Pacific, and the tribunal said it would not weigh the submarine breaches in his sentence. Whatever else that is, it isn't a firing squad with a script. On the Allies' own conduct: Dan was implicitly demanding that a legal system be impartial between the parties to it in order to count as law, and that standard is far too strong, since in every domestic system the state is both rule-maker and prosecutor.

(5) Dan said that's exactly the disanalogy. Domestically, rules come from a legislature the defendant has some standing to influence, and the state binds itself prospectively; at Nuremberg the people who wrote the Charter were the people running the prosecution. And here's what I thought was his best move: the tribunal was happy to treat its jurisdiction as a hard limit when it cut one way — it declined to convict for the pre-war persecution of German Jews on the reasoning that those acts weren't shown to be connected to the aggressive war — so it clearly did think itself bound by the Charter's edges. But those edges were drawn by the winners after the event, so being bound by law collapses into being bound by the winners' preferences. He added that Tokyo is worse: eleven judges, a full dissent from the Indian judge on ex post facto grounds, the Dutch judge dissenting in part, the French judge writing separately to doubt whether a tribunal appointed only by victors could be fair. If the people on the bench can't agree what the law was, it wasn't sitting there waiting to be applied.

(6) Miri: the dissents prove the opposite. A show trial doesn't generate reasoned minority opinions that the majority then has to answer at length; hundreds of pages engaging the Indian judge's legal argument is what a legal process looks like from the inside. Then she made what seemed to me her actual point — that the cost of Nuremberg's retroactivity isn't owed to Göring, it's a forward-looking cost paid by future defendants and future states, and it bought an enormous amount, namely the idea that individuals can bear responsibility under international law at all and eventually the ICC. So she accused Dan of a category error: he keeps sliding between "the trial was unjust to these defendants" and "the trial was just power", and those are different claims needing different evidence.

(7) Dan's last move: its a dodge. If the retroactive charges really were unjust to the men in the dock, and the answer is that the injustice bought a good precedent, then Miri has abandoned her step-2 argument in favour of a consequentialist one, and now owes a showing that the precedent paid off — which he said it hasn't, since the ICC couldn't touch aggression at all for years after Rome and still doesn't reach the states most likely to start a war, and the clearest lesson everyone else drew was: don't lose.
