For three years running, the closing session of our workshop on the foundations of computation has ended the same way: in an argument about the epistemic status of Church's thesis in which nobody moves and in which, so far as I can tell, nobody has ever disagreed with anybody else about a single concrete computability question. What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of that argument — not a verdict on the thesis, and not a survey of the considerations that have been marshalled for it.

The three positions in the room, as their holders state them:

The mathematician holds that the thesis is a theorem, or is on its way to being one. Begin from explicit principles that any discrete deterministic machine must satisfy — a configuration admitting a fixed finite description, the effect of a single step depending only on a bounded neighborhood of that configuration, and the like — and you can derive that any device meeting those principles is simulated by a Turing machine. On this view, to call the thesis "empirical" or "stipulative" is to mistake a result for one of the informal arguments that historically motivated it.

The philosopher holds that the derivation shows only that a stipulated class of devices is captured by a formalism, which is unsurprising given that the stipulation was designed for exactly that purpose. Since "effective procedure" is an informal notion rather than a defined one, the thesis cannot be a theorem at all; it is at best a well-confirmed empirical generalization about what we and our machines can do, and one that remains falsifiable in principle.

The computer scientist holds that both are arguing about nothing. "Computable" is a technical term fixed by a formalism, the thesis is a definitional convention, and the substantive questions lie elsewhere: in claims about physical devices, about human cognition, about which resources are to be counted as finite.

My own hunch, which I offer as a hypothesis for you to test rather than as a conclusion, is that the mathematician and the philosopher are answering different questions — one about a class of devices, the other about the reach of human rule-following — and that the appearance of a single dispute survives only because both parties express their answers in the same sentence.

Please address the following in order. First: whether the dispute between the first two is verbal. Identify the term doing the work, say whether it is "effective," "mechanical," or "proof," and apply the ordinary test — would they still disagree if the contested term were replaced on both sides by a precise surrogate? Second: whether the empiricist reading has any content, given that the standing candidate counterexamples (machines performing infinitely many steps within a finite interval, spacetimes in which an observer could receive the output of an endless computation, analog models with unlimited real-valued precision) each seem to fail at being simultaneously effective and physically realizable. Is the absence of counterexamples evidence for the thesis, or is it precisely what a stipulation would predict? Third: whether the deflationist reading changes the subject, and what explanatory work is left for the thesis once it is a definition — in particular, whether it can still account for the extensional coincidence of all the formalisms on offer. Fourth: whether the three are simply talking past one another, or whether a genuine disagreement is buried in the exchange and has gone unarticulated; if the latter, state it as a single question on which they give incompatible answers. Fifth: what would count as progress, given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a thought experiment, a change of question. If your answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that explicitly.

Write for an audience that already knows the material. Give me reasons and structure, not a bibliography, and no citations or reading lists.
