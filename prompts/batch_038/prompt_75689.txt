I am assembling a set of considered judgments about a single question — when a rule-making body can bind its own future self — and I would like help determining whether they add up to a principle. The judgments below were reached separately, over several years, in response to concrete cases rather than to any theory. They are mostly stable, but two or three pairs of them look to me irreconcilable, and I would rather be told which to abandon than be given a survey of the literature on entrenchment.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. A society's constitution provides that it may be amended by a two-thirds vote. The society amends it, by two-thirds, to require unanimity for future amendments. A decade later it amends it, unanimously, to restore the two-thirds rule. Both amendments are valid. I am confident of this.

2. A constitutional amendment article closes with a proviso that "no state, without its consent, shall be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate." A subsequent amendment, adopted by the full prescribed supermajority and with the full prescribed ratifications, abolishes equal state suffrage in that chamber without any state's consent. I think the amendment is invalid: the proviso does what it says. I am fairly confident.

3. A constitution states that Article 12 may never be amended or repealed, and Article 12 is the article containing that sentence. The legislature first deletes, by the prescribed supermajority, the entrenching sentence; then, at the next session and by the same procedure, it deletes the remainder of Article 12. I think both steps are invalid — including the first, even though the first considered in isolation looks like an entirely ordinary exercise of the amending power. I am much less confident here, and this is the case that bothers me most.

4. A sovereign parliament, subject to no judicial review of the validity of its enactments, passes a statute providing that no later parliament may legislate inconsistently with its third section. A later parliament legislates inconsistently with that section, by ordinary majority. I think the later statute prevails and the attempt to bind simply failed. I am confident. But I cannot say what distinguishes this from case 2: in both, the same kind of body made the rule, and the same kind of body broke it.

5. The same sovereign parliament provides that no bill touching a certain subject may receive assent unless it has first been approved by referendum. A later parliament passes such a bill without holding one. Here I think the requirement bites — the later act is defective. My instinct is that case 4 concerns substance and case 5 concerns procedure, and that procedure is bindable while substance is not. I am not sure that is a principle rather than a slogan.

6. A court adopts a standing rule that only the full court sitting together may overrule one of its published precedents. A three-judge panel overrules one. I think the overruling is void. But the same court, in an earlier era, declared that it would never depart from its own decisions, and decades later departed from one. I think that departure was perfectly proper and that the declaration bound nobody. Two self-binding rules of one court, one of which holds and one of which does not.

7. A constitution confers emergency decree power on the executive "for the duration of the emergency," and empowers the executive to determine when the emergency exists and ends. The executive decrees that the emergency shall continue until it decrees otherwise. I think this is invalid. I cannot explain why without also condemning case 5.

8. An amendment, procedurally flawless in every respect, extends the sitting legislature's term by ten years and cancels the scheduled election. I think it is invalid, although there is no entrenchment clause anywhere to invoke. By contrast, an amendment lowering the voting age is plainly valid. My reason for the difference seems to be that the first destroys the very body whose authorization any later amendment would require. But if that reasoning is right, it threatens cases 2 and 3, which I want to keep.

9. A residential association's recorded covenants state that they run in perpetuity and may not be amended. Thirty years later, eighty percent of the owners vote to amend the restriction on animals. I think the amendment is valid. This surprises me, because it is structurally identical to case 2. Perhaps the difference is that case 2 protects a party that is not part of the amending majority, whereas here every owner is inside the coalition that amends. I do not know whether that is a distinction or a rationalization.

10. A body adopts a rule providing that the rule itself may be suspended by majority vote for the duration of one session. The majority suspends it and, while it is suspended, repeals it outright. I think the repeal is valid. Separately: where a constitution is silent about how it may be amended, I think a bare majority may amend it. Silence plus majority seems to carry all the weight, which makes me suspect that every one of my verdicts is simply tracking who holds the power at the moment of decision — and if so, then cases 2, 3, 5, and 6 are the outliers that need explaining, and perhaps some of them are just wrong.

What I want from you:

(a) State the principle, or the short list of competing candidates, as precisely as you can — precise enough that a third party could apply it to an unfamiliar case without making further judgment calls of their own. For each clause of the principle, identify the case or cases above that motivate it. If a clause is needed only to handle a single case, say so; I want to know where the principle is being fitted to the data rather than derived from it.

(b) Say whether the principle you offer is a criterion of validity — about what makes an amendment or self-binding rule legally effective — or a criterion of what officials ought to treat as effective, and whether that distinction is doing real work across my ten cases or merely restating my uncertainty.

(c) Apply the principle to at least three cases I have not listed. At a minimum: whether an amendment procedure may itself be amended so as to entrench a new unamendable provision; whether a body may validly entrench a sunset on its own power to entrench; and a case in which the entrenched provision protects a minority that happens to be inside the coalition doing the amending.

(d) Identify honestly which of my verdicts no reasonably simple principle can accommodate. Then tell me which single verdict I should give up, and what I lose by giving it up — which of the remaining verdicts become harder to defend once that one falls.

(e) If your considered view is that there is no principle here, only a standing political fact about who is presently obeyed, say so plainly — but explain why that view is more than a redescription of the pattern of verdicts, and what it predicts that a principled account would predict differently.
