so, third year in phil of mind here and my chapter is on whether dreams are experiences at all — Malcolm's line that dreaming isn't something that happens to you during sleep, so the waking impression does all the work, versus Dennett's more mechanistic version where nothing phenomenal occurs overnight and the content just gets written into memory at the moment of waking. My supervisor's complaint is that anything I put between those two collapses into one of them, and honestly he might be right. I want you to argue for a third position I've been calling "the sketch view", and argue it hard enough that I could put it in front of a room that already knows both of the others.

The view, roughly: what sleep leaves behind is underdetermined — a bundle of constraints and an unfinished narrating task, not a determinate content and not an experience of a determinate content. The dream only becomes a determinate dream in the course of being told. So the telling is neither a report of something that was already there nor the playing back of a ready-made tape; it's the completion of something deliberately left incomplete. That carries a weird consequence I want you to own rather than dodge: identity conditions for the dream are partly fixed by the telling, so two retellings of one night can differ in detail and neither be false, because there was no fact of the matter about those details until somebody told it.

I need the argument to do five specific jobs. First, show why this isn't just Dennett with the tape swapped for an outline — on his picture the content is fully determinate before waking and merely gets inserted, on mine nothing determinate is inserted at all, and you need to explain why that difference is substantive and not cosmetic. Second, show how it escapes Malcolm's point that the waking impression is the only criterion available, without ending up committed to the view that whatever you say you dreamt, you dreamt — the sketch has to still constrain the telling somehow, and I want that mechanism stated. Third, deal with the lucid dreaming signalling results, where people execute pre-agreed eye movement signals in the middle of the episode they later report on, which looks like direct evidence for determinate content running online during sleep. Fourth, deal with the duration studies where subjects' estimates of how long a dream went on track the actual elapsed time pretty closely, since that seems to need something with a genuine temporal shape happening overnight. Fifth, answer the objection that I've only relocated the experience: if the telling is what completes the dream, isn't the telling itself an experience, in which case I've conceded the whole point to the experientialist and gained nothing by the detour.

Format: exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap up at the end — I'm pasting them straight into my notes. And don't soften them into a list of considerations on both sides, I want them as premises doing actual work toward the conclusion that a dream is not an experience.
