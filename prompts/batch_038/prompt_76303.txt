I spent thirty-eight years in a physiology laboratory, most of them studying ion channels in smooth muscle, and I have been retired for five. Since retiring I have read my way, unguided, through a good deal of philosophy of mind and philosophy of biology, and this term I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar devoted to predictive processing and the free-energy principle. The convenor, knowing that I have read the material and that I have no career to protect, has asked me to act as second reader on the final essays. I have the mark scheme and a set of exemplars, so I am not entirely unarmed; but before I commit my own judgement to paper I should like a considered second opinion, because there is one script on which my instinct and the mark scheme seem to be pulling in different directions.

The essay question set was this: "Is the free-energy principle, together with the predictive-processing account of cognition built on it, a genuinely testable empirical theory? If it is capable of being made compatible with any possible observation, explain how it escapes refutation, and say whether that capacity disqualifies it from counting as science."

Here is the answer I have to grade, reproduced in full.

"It is often said that the free-energy principle cannot be tested, because it can be adjusted to fit whatever happens. This is too quick. The framework generates predictions that have been confirmed in the laboratory: a deviant stimulus in a predictable sequence evokes a larger neural response than a standard one, and repeated presentation of the same stimulus produces a reduced response. These are exactly what a system that constantly anticipates its own sensory input should show, and if the pattern were reversed the hypothesis would have been refuted. The framework is therefore empirical in the ordinary sense.

It is also more robust than its critics allow, and for an instructive mathematical reason. What an organism minimises is variational free energy, which is an upper bound on surprisal rather than surprisal itself, so a system can be doing well by the principle while its surprisal remains high. When an experiment appears to contradict the principle, what has usually been refuted is the particular generative model the experimenter assumed, not the principle. The same point applies to action. Under active inference, prediction error can be reduced either by revising one's predictions or by acting so as to change the sensations that arrive; a creature whose forecast fails can repair the mismatch by moving. The theory therefore never predicts failure, and this is a consequence of its mathematics rather than a dodge.

There is a further difficulty, which is that the inference has to be performed by something. To minimise free energy over a large space of hypotheses, the brain must represent probability distributions and compute over them, and nothing in the account says who pays for that computation. Taken literally, the framework describes real probability distributions inside the skull being operated on by something else inside the skull. Taken instrumentally, as a way of describing a system from the outside, it loses its claim to explain anything: on that reading the brain does not do Bayesian inference at all, and the free-energy principle is a convenient fiction.

One consequence of the literal reading is worth stating plainly. Since the brain is forever minimising how surprised it is by its own sensory input, the framework implies that perception is a construction generated from the top down and only loosely disciplined by what is actually there, and that action selection is driven by the same quantity as homeostasis and reward. On this view there is no principled difference between a delusion and a perception, only a difference in how strongly the prior is held, which is why the approach has been attractive in psychiatry.

My conclusion is that the free-energy principle is either an empirical claim that can be falsified or a description that cannot, and that its defenders are not entitled to both. The first horn is false, because the principle is compatible with any observation whatsoever. The second makes it a mathematical device rather than a theory, and a device is not science. I would add, in fairness, that a framework can earn its place in science by organising experiments and unifying phenomena even when it is not directly testable; but that is a defence of its usefulness, not of its truth, and the question asked was about science."

So that you can calibrate the commentary rather than merely decorate it, here are the distinctions I regard as load-bearing for this question, and a good script should either exploit them or explain why they do not matter. There is a difference between a mathematical formalism and the empirical hypothesis that a given system conforms to it, and the answer moves between the two without marking the move. Free energy is a bound, and minimising a bound is not the same as minimising the quantity it bounds, which cuts against the direction the answer wants to push it. Surprisal is a technical quantity, defined over sensory states by the model, and is not the ordinary feeling of being surprised, though the answer's rhetoric depends on sliding between the two. A system can be described as performing inference without anyone inside it representing probabilities, so the homunculus worry the answer raises is an assumption about implementation and not a consequence of the framework. And testability operates at more than one level: a general principle may be difficult to falsify on its own while the specific models derived from it are falsifiable, which is a difference in degree from astrology rather than a difference in kind. This list is offered as calibration and not as a checklist, and I do not want the mark to turn on whether the student happens to use my vocabulary; what matters is whether the reasoning is sound and whether the second part of the question is actually answered.

One last request about form. I should like the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one writes a letter or a report to a colleague, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicised emphasis, and no tables. Begin with the mark and the one-sentence verdict, then proceed in paragraphs through the answer in the order of its own exposition, and close with your view of my hesitation.
