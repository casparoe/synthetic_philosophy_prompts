Three weeks ago I was appointed chair of the review panel for a foundation program on "cultural drivers of economic development," and I inherited a panel that has stopped functioning. My predecessor left in October rather than chair another meeting. For four months the members have traded memos, and the substance of the dispute is an old one wearing new clothes.

The program's call for proposals requires each submission to identify a cultural variable meeting three conditions: (i) it must be measurable independently of the economic outcome it is invoked to explain, so that it cannot be a relabeling of that outcome; (ii) it must be manipulable — at minimum, there must be a plausible intervention that could shift it, since the foundation funds programs rather than descriptions; and (iii) it must be meaning-bearing, in that the variable must figure among the reasons the people studied give for acting as they do, so that the proposed explanation renders their conduct intelligible from their own point of view and not merely predictable from outside it.

The panel has divided over condition (iii), and the division opens directly onto the classic case. On one side, whom I will call the mechanism camp and who are mostly economists, the view is that culture is a cause like any other: an explanans is a variable one could in principle intervene on, and condition (iii) is at best a heuristic for locating good proxies and at worst an unfalsifiability device, a demand that the explanation be insulated from the possibility of being wrong. One of them has circulated a memo citing the quantitative literature on regional religious composition in Germany — the county-level work that attributes the Protestant economic advantage to literacy and Bible-reading rather than to any work ethic, and the city-level work that finds no measurable economic effect of adopting Protestantism at all — as proof that questions of this shape are decidable by data, and that this one has been decided against Weber. On the other side, the interpretation camp, mostly historians and Weberian sociologists, holds that the memo proves nothing because Weber never advanced a claim of that shape. On their reading he offered an account of the intelligibility of a form of conduct: an elective affinity between an ascetic religious orientation, the notion of a calling, and an ethos in which methodical profit-seeking becomes an end in itself rather than a means to consumption. To test that as a causal hypothesis about religion producing capitalism is a category mistake, and one committed jointly by the thesis's defenders and by its would-be refuters for a century. They add the sharper claim, which I take to be the real crux: that conditions (ii) and (iii) cannot be satisfied by one and the same variable, because a variable whose content is the meaning agents attach to their own conduct cannot be set from outside by an intervener without ceasing to be that variable. The intervention destroys the explanans.

Two things I have already said to both camps, and which the draft must accommodate rather than avoid. First, Weber himself warned against substituting an equally one-sided spiritualistic causal interpretation of these matters for a materialistic one, which the mechanism camp reads as his own concession that he was making a causal claim. Second, his methodological writings require that an explanation of social action be adequate both on the level of meaning and causally, which the interpretation camp reads as evidence that he took the two to be distinct requirements. I have asked both camps to arrive at our meeting with the document they should have written four months ago, and I want a first draft of it in hand before I do. Please write that document.

It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about what Weber's argument was and was not, about what the thesis entails and what it does not, about what the century of empirical work has and has not addressed, and about what the phrase cultural variable is doing in our call for proposals. Nothing so hedged that it commits no one.

2. A disagreements table, with one row per live disagreement and four columns: the disagreement in neutral terms; the mechanism camp's statement of its own position in wording its members would sign; the interpretation camp's statement of its own position in wording its members would sign; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or at least partly verbal — and if partly verbal, which word is doing the work and what the two camps would have to say to be disagreeing for real.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one camp. I expect one to be whether the reasons for which people act can be causes in the sense the interventionist uses, and another to be whether the claimed impossibility of satisfying (ii) and (iii) together is a genuine impossibility or a difficulty attaching to particular operationalizations. If you find further cruxes, add them; if you think either of mine is spurious, say so and replace it.

4. For each crux, the case, test, or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance, in the past tense of a future result. At least one of these must not be drawn from Protestant Europe at all, so that no crux is settled by the fate of a single historical case.

5. A short closing section, because our deadline will not wait on the philosophy: the rule the panel should apply to proposals that satisfy (i) and (ii) but not (iii), and to proposals that satisfy (iii) but not (ii), stated so that a member of either camp can apply it and recognize the outcome as one they had reason to expect.

Please do not append references or a reading list. Where you rely on an empirical study, describe its design and its finding in enough detail that I can see what it would take to replicate it, rather than naming it. Keep the two camps' vocabularies distinct throughout: I want to be able to hand each side its own paragraphs and have them recognize themselves, not a compromise written in nobody's language.
