I'm about halfway through one of those trade-press books on trust and cooperation — the sort with a one-word title and a subtitle about the hidden science of why we keep our word — and there's a passage in it I keep going back to because it feels like its doing something slippery. The author is a science journalist and the chapter is mostly about hostage negotiators and kidney-donation chains, so I was surprised by how much philosophy it wanted to do on its own. Here's the bit, more or less in the order it appears:

'Promising is the strangest thing human beings do. Nothing in the world changes when you make the sounds, and yet afterwards you are bound. Hume's explanation is that the bindingness is entirely a matter of convention: there is no more of a natural fact about your uttering "I promise" that creates a duty than there is a natural fact that makes a banknote worth anything. What binds you is the general usefulness of the practice — without a reliable custom of giving one's word, strangers could never plan together and society would come apart. A promise obligates you for the same reason a rule of the road obligates you: because the rule is worth having. Notice what this commits him to. Whether you're bound in a given case depends on whether the practice is served, so a promise broken in secret, where nobody ever finds out and nobody's plans are disturbed, isn't broken in any real sense at all — Hume isn't squeamish about this, it's the whole point. Later philosophers tried to patch the hole by saying that when you promise you transfer a right to the other person, a right to your performance, which is also why they and only they can release you. But that just relocates the mystery instead of solving it: it assumes you have the power to conjure rights out of thin air by making noises, which was exactly the thing that needed an account.'

My own inclination going in was something like the trust explanation — a promise binds because you've gotten somebody to lean on you and pulling out afterwards is a kind of betrayal — so I was primed to dislike this passage. But I've read Hume in bits and pieces and he usually comes out subtler than he's made to look here, and my suspicion is that the chapter is doing two things at once: giving a broadly decent popular gloss, then smuggling in a conclusion the gloss doesn't actually support. Can you go through it claim by claim and sort the pieces into what a Hume scholar would sign off on, what's a forgivable simplification, and what's just wrong or attributed to Hume when he didn't hold it? I'm most interested in the "broken in secret" line — is that a fair consequence of the conventionalist picture, or is it running together two different levels of the argument, and if Hume himself took up the free-rider worry somewhere, what's the shape of his reply (no need to cite anything, I just want to know whether he had one)? Same question for the charge against the rights-transfer view: is that a real objection, or does it only look strong because it measures that view against a job it isn't trying to do — and does the summary get right why anyone would propose it in the first place? Finally, does the chapter's overall moral, which is that every account of promissory obligation collapses and we should just live with the mystery, survive once the passage is corrected? Full disclosure, I have skin in this: two years ago I committed to doing something for a friend who has since gone off-grid in Patagonia, unreachable and never going to learn either way what I did, and I've caught myself using this exact paragraph to talk myself out of it. Tell me whether that reasoning is any good once it's fixed up.
