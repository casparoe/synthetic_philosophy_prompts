I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been circling a feature on the necessity of origin — the claim, familiar from Kripke, that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones you did come from. My editor's objection, which I think is fair, is that the claim is nearly impossible to make vivid for readers who have never met a possible-worlds framework: every example I've drafted so far sounds either trivially true or like science fiction. I want to build the feature around one well-designed case instead, and I'd like your help engineering it.

Here is the specification. Construct a single thought experiment set in a fertility clinic that offers mitochondrial donation — the procedure in which the nuclear genetic material of an intended mother and father is placed in an egg from a second woman whose own nucleus has been removed, so that the child's mitochondrial DNA comes from the donor rather than from the intended mother. The scenario should present a graded series of at least five rungs, beginning with a control case in which no donor material is involved and ending with a case in which a substantial share of the child's cellular constituents come from the second woman. The rungs should exploit the fact that these procedures are imperfect: a small amount of the intended mother's mitochondrial DNA is routinely carried over into the reconstructed egg, so the series can vary how much of the egg's cytoplasm and mitochondrial complement comes from which woman. Add one further rung in which the manipulation is made on the paternal side instead — a different sperm from the same man, drawn from the same ejaculate and used with the same egg — so that the series lets us compare the two gametes.

The constraints matter, because they determine what counts as cheating. Everything in the case must be a procedure a clinic performs today: no cloning, no gamete duplication, no artificial wombs, no time travel, no fission or fusion of persons. There may be no laboratory mix-up, mislabeled specimen, or clerical error anywhere in the story, because I want a metaphysical puzzle about where a person comes from, not an epistemic puzzle about which gametes were used. Every rung must be a determinate, discrete specification of what came from whom — the case may not get its bite from vagueness in the phrase "how much egg." Keep the scenario itself under about 350 words and written so that a reader who has never encountered the necessity of origin can follow it without any technical vocabulary.

After the scenario, do the analysis. State two candidate origin principles explicitly — one that identifies a person's origin with the gamete pair as two particular individuals, and one that identifies it with the material and genetic contribution from which the person was built — and then walk the series rung by rung, recording the verdict each principle delivers about whether the child at that rung is the same child as the one at the control rung. Tell me the first rung at which the two principles actually diverge, if there is one, and identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work. Then give me the honest caveats: what happens to the case if that feature is removed — for instance, if the second woman's contribution is made entirely non-genetic, such as a donated uterus or a gestational carrier — and does it collapse back into an uninteresting case? How would a defender of the necessity of origin attack the series, including by arguing that the donor egg is not an origin at all because it is not a gamete in the relevant sense, or that the sperm-side rung shows the thesis was never about material quantity? Finally, close with two or three sentences I could put to my editor: does this case genuinely threaten the necessity of origin, or does it merely reveal that the thesis was never a claim about amounts of stuff?
