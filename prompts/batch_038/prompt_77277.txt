I teach an upper-level environmental humanities seminar — sixteen students, one eighty-minute session next week — and they will already have read Crutzen and Stoermer's 2000 coining, Lewis and Maslin's case for a 1610 boundary tied to the post-Columbian dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide, one of the Capitalocene rejoinders objecting that no unified Anthropos ever did the changing, and a short piece on how the golden-spike procedure actually works.

Last weekend I argued about all of this for two hours with a friend who is a marine sedimentologist. Her position: the start date is an empirical question, full stop. A boundary stratotype exists to be correlated worldwide, so whichever marker is detectable and synchronous in ice cores, lake varves and marine sediment simply is the answer, and anyone who prefers 1610 or 1780 is doing politics in a geologist's vocabulary. My position: the record underdetermines the date the moment you ask what a boundary is supposed to do, and settling that — signal or cause — is not something the cores can do for you. I pointed out that the working group endorsed Crawford Lake and a 1952 base by twenty-nine votes to four in 2019, and that its own parent subcommission rejected the epoch outright in March 2024. She said that is the procedure working as designed, not evidence for my side. Neither of us moved.

Design an in-class activity that puts the students inside that fork instead of letting them spectate it. Specifically:

- Roles and written briefs for a mock stratigraphic commission. The engine should be that different sub-committees are handed different, individually respectable criteria for what makes a boundary — global synchronicity and detectability, explanatory salience, continuity of Earth-system state, representativeness of the marker — and discover the incompatibility only when the plenary convenes. They must not be told that is what is happening.
- Five candidate dates in play: a Ruddiman-style early onset several thousand years back, 1610, roughly 1780–1800, 1950/52, and the 1964 peak of bomb fallout. Give me an evidence packet for each that I can assemble from index cards and a whiteboard: no lab data, no equipment, nothing that requires me to source real measurements.
- A decision procedure that reproduces the nested institutional structure — working group recommends, subcommission votes, commission ratifies — with a supermajority at one level and a simple majority at another, so that who decides is something they experience rather than discuss. Include a stake that is real for them but is not a grade.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for eighty minutes, including how to chair the plenary so the conflict between criteria surfaces in their own argument rather than being announced from the front.
- A debrief that maps particular moments of the exercise onto the distinction my friend and I were fighting over, and onto the 2024 rejection. Tell me whether to reveal that outcome before or after their vote, since revealing it early risks turning the exercise into a history lesson.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: where the class deadlocks, where a confident student short-circuits everything by declaring the whole business merely political, and how to handle that without conceding my friend's point for her.
- An honest note on which feature of the dispute the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect it is the sheer technical force of correlation — she is not wrong that an uncorrelatable boundary is not a stratigraphic boundary at all — together with a real community's fifteen-year investment in a craft norm. Say plainly what I will have to supply by hand.
