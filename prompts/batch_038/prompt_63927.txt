I'm in a reading group on AI governance and last week somebody circulated a chapter from a trade-press book about whose values an aligned system should actually serve. Below is the core argument, in my own paraphrase of how he puts it (not his wording, so don't go hunting for the phrasing). He's smart and most of it sounded right to me in the moment, but on rereading I'm not so sure, and I don't have the background to tell where it slips.

His argument runs roughly like this: training a model on human feedback is, whether labs admit it or not, an exercise in preference aggregation — you take the choices of a big pool of people and fold them into a single ordering that the system then acts on, and social choice theory already tells you how that ends. Arrow showed that no method of combining individual preferences into a social preference can satisfy a few very mild fairness conditions at once, so any reward model is necessarily somebody's values dressed up as everybody's. The only escape the literature offers is to stop asking the system to promote any conception of the good at all — the move political philosophers call liberal neutrality, where the state doesn't take sides between rival views of the good life but supplies a framework in which people pursue their own. Neutrality sounds like it should transfer to AI, but there's a catch: a genuinely neutral system is impossible, because declining to promote a view is itself a way of taking one, and any framework that lets people pursue their own ends will in fact make some ends easier and others harder. So the designer can neither be neutral nor aggregate, which leaves exactly one defensible option: serve the median user. Pick the preferences that beat all the others in pairwise comparison and the system is at least responsive to half the population instead of to whoever happened to write the constitution.

Three things bother me and I can't tell whether they're real problems or whether I'm just missing something. First, I thought Arrow's theorem came with a stack of assumptions, one of which is usually the thing that gets dropped in practice — so is "aggregation is impossible" a fair gloss, or an overstatement? And does the theorem even reach this case, given that reward models are trained on numeric scores and averaged, not on rankings? Second, he says neutrality fails because any framework helps some ways of life and hinders others, but I thought the neutrality idea was definately narrower than that — something about the kinds of reasons you're allowed to appeal to rather than about downstream effects. Is he sliding between two different claims? Third, the median-user conclusion feels like it just doesn't follow. In a pilot we ran, two rater pools — one in Nairobi, one in Manila — disagreed sharply about whether the model should give direct instructions on a certain kind of health question. Averaging their scores produced a hedged, evasive policy that neither pool would have endorsed, and I can't see what "the median preference" would even mean across questions like that, where the disagreement is about *why* the answer matters and not only about how much.
