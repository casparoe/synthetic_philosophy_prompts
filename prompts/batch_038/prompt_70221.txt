You are a philosopher of science who also knows the economic history of resource-scarcity debates. Before you write anything, think it through privately: sketch the mapping first — who the players are, what each of their theories actually claims, what exactly was wagered on, and what would count as an outcome — and only then argue. **The episode.** The Simon–Ehrlich wager. It began as a public challenge from the economist Julian Simon, whose stated general claim was that the inflation-adjusted cost of raw materials not controlled by government would not rise in the long run; the provocation was a biologist's remark to the effect that he would take even money that England would not exist in the year 2000. Simon offered to bet on any raw material Paul Ehrlich cared to name. Ehrlich and his colleagues picked chromium, copper, nickel, tin, and tungsten, notionally bought $200 of each on 29 September 1980 for a $1,000 basket, and set the payoff date at 29 September 1990. Over the decade world population grew by more than 800 million, the largest single-decade increase in recorded history. In inflation-adjusted terms all five metals fell, tin and tungsten by more than half; three of the five fell even in nominal terms. In October 1990 Ehrlich mailed Simon a cheque for $576.07.

Two subsequent facts matter. Simon offered to raise the stake to $20,000 and let Ehrlich choose any resources and any date he liked; Ehrlich, with a climatologist colleague, counter-proposed betting instead on fifteen trends — temperature, atmospheric CO₂ and nitrous oxide, tropospheric ozone, Asian sulphur dioxide emissions, cropland and agricultural soil per person, and the like — each $1,000 that it would worsen over a decade. No agreement was reached before Simon's death. And later recomputations have gone against the standard telling: running the same wager from 1980 to 2011 would have had Simon losing on four of the five metals, and a study that scored every possible ten-year window between 1900 and 2008 found that Simon would have won only about 38 percent of them, with 1980–1990 ranking among the fifteen worst windows for him in the whole century.

**The lens.** Falsification and rescue in long-run prediction — Duhem–Quine, Popper's criterion for an ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, Lakatos on progressive versus degenerating problemshifts, and the idea that a test only earns its verdict if it would very probably have caught the error had the theory been false. And, sitting underneath all of it, the defence that a theory can be *right but early*.

**What I want from you.**

1. Map the wager precisely as a test. Which theory was actually on trial — Malthus's own mechanism of land, diminishing returns, and population pressure; Ehrlich's version, which adds finite stocks and finite sinks; Simon's account of substitution and human ingenuity; or no theory in particular? What had to be true of the chosen variable — the ten-year movement in the price of five metals — for the outcome to bear on any of them? Simon's claim was explicitly about the long run and the test was one decade: state exactly what that mismatch costs, and to whom.

2. Was the wager severe, in the sense above? Note that severity cuts both ways. Then tell me what the two recomputations do to the episode. Do they show that Ehrlich's theory was true and the window unlucky, or that the wager was never a discriminating test of anything at all? Be careful to keep "not a good test" distinct from "a good test that pointed the wrong way," and say which of these, if either, you think is going on.

3. Take Ehrlich's post-1990 moves in sequence: his later writing that he and his colleagues regarded renewable resources as the better indicators of the state of the planet but went along with the bet anyway; his observation that those same five metals had risen in price between 1950 and 1975; the temperature counter-bet; the fifteen-trend counter-offer. Which of these are ad hoc rescues — auxiliary claims introduced to save the theory and not independently testable — and which are legitimate revisions of a protective belt? Then judge the programme as Lakatos would: across this sequence, did Ehrlich's side produce novel predictions, or only reinterpretations? Decide it; do not let either man's posthumous reputation settle the question.

4. Turn the same instrument on Malthus himself. Economic historians now have a name for the stretch from roughly 1790 to 1840 in Britain, when output per head expanded rapidly while working-class real wages stagnated — a period Malthus's own theory explains, since technical progress raises the demand for labour and population growth absorbs the gain. From about 1840 to 1900 the two series converged and then diverged, with real wages growing faster than output per worker. Is Malthus best described as falsified, as right-then-wrong, or as holding a theory with an expiring domain of validity? And what does that do to the "right but early" defence: if a theory can be confirmed in one window and disconfirmed in the adjacent one, what does the claim "the theory was right" actually pick out?

5. Give me a verdict, and commit to it. Which side, if either, came out of the wager with its theory intact? Is "right but early" ever a legitimate defence rather than a rescue that merely resembles one — and if it can be legitimate, what would a forecaster have to commit to *in advance* to earn the right to make it (a bounded horizon, a named mechanism, a lag structure, some prediction that distinguishes being early from being wrong)? Finally: the popular moral of this episode is that Simon won the bet but Ehrlich was right about everything that mattered. Is that a defensible conclusion, or is it a way of refusing to let a test be a test?
