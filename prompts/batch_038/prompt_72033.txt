ok so I've been asked to write my actual position down for a reading group I'm in — weird mix, maybe five philosophy grad students, three people who do clinical work, and a couple of folks from a nonprofit leadership program I consult for — and the format is that everyone puts their commitments on the table so the rest can attack them. My topic is self-deception and I'm basically a Sartrean about it, but I've also built a practical exercise that I run in the nonprofit workshops, and I have a nagging feeling the exercise doesnt fit the theory. A friend who sat in on a session told me afterwards it was "just existentialism with a spreadsheet," which annoyed me enough that I want to actually check instead of stewing on it. Here's where I've landed:

1. Radical freedom, no excuses. For anything I do I can't point at my character, my upbringing or my circumstances as something that took the choice out of my hands. And freedom doesn't come in degrees — the person in an awful situation and the person picking lunch are equally free.
2. Bad faith is real and it doesn't need a censor. I'm not a Freudian, I don't think there's a second agency in the head. What makes self-deception possible without one is that awareness of my own state is built into being conscious at all, just not as something I'm thematically attending to.
3. Bad faith is nonetheless a long-run thing, not a string of little flinches. People can live inside it for decades, it can amount to a whole way of being, and I want to be able to say that in print.
4. Situation is real and I don't want to sound like a motivational speaker: the mountain I can't climb, the body I was born with, the class I was born into. But I also hold these only show up as obstacles against the background of what I'm trying to do — the boulder resists me because I want to move it, not the other way round.
5. Anguish is the honest register. When I actually apprehend my freedom I don't feel empowered, I feel vertigo, and bad faith is the move of getting out of that feeling. So the point of any honest self-examination is to become able to stay in it rather than to arrive at comfortable conclusions.
6. The exercise: people write down every situation in some domain where they say they had no choice, and we go line by line through what they were in fact choosing. I hold that once somebody has done this honestly for a domain, they can no longer be in bad faith about that domain. Bad faith depends on not looking, so looking should close the door.
7. One hedge I do make in practice: when I'm talking to somebody who is severely depressed or under coercive control, I say they had less room to move, and I dont think that costs me (1).

Go through this as an audit, not a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they conflict? Rank the real ones by severity — don't flatten everything into a uniform "well, there's some tension here" — and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision costs me. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I honestly don't know where the problem is, so please don't assume I'm protecting (6) or any of the others; if it's the load-bearing one, say so.
