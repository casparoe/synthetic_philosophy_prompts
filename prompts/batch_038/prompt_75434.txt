so my study group for a seminar on bargaining and conflict got volunteered to make the one-pager for the week we do commitment, and I'm drafting it. We've all read the Schelling stuff about how tying your own hands can be a source of strength, but half the group still thinks it sounds like a magic trick, and nobody can say when it actually pays off versus when it just guarantees a wreck. What I want is a decision tree that walks a student from a starting point — "I'm in a situation where me and one other player can each bind ourselves to a move before play starts, and the binding is public" — all the way to a named commitment strategy. Same spirit as those "which position do you actually hold" flowcharts, except the endpoints are moves you'd make rather than labels you'd subscribe to.

Audience is second-years with one term of intro game theory. They know what a payoff matrix is and can follow "if you both do X you each lose more than if one of you had yielded." They have not read Schelling and don't have a technical grip on words like credible or subgame perfect, so every node has to be a question they can answer just by thinking about the case — not a question whose answer already smuggles in the name of the leaf it leads to.

Hang it on this case, and phrase the nodes in terms of it. Two student societies, the debate union and the film club, both want the same Friday night in the main hall for their flagship events. The student union lets you lock a date with a non-refundable £300 deposit and posts every booking on a public board outside the hall office. If both book that Friday, the hall office voids both bookings and keeps both deposits (insurance reasons, apparently). If one books and the other doesn't, the booker gets the hall and the other ends up running a much smaller thing in a seminar room. If neither books by the deadline, the office has said it'll offer them the night jointly, which both sides rank second-best. So: win, split, lose, wreck, in that order, for each of them.

What I need from the tree:

- somewhere between six and nine nodes, and eight to twelve leaves. Each leaf gets a plain-English name of your choosing (not a citation of anybody's term), one line on what it buys you, and one line on the specific way it backfires — I mean specific, like "when the other side has already hard-committed" not "sometimes it doesn't work."
- These leaves have to show up somewhere, even if you'd name them differently: binding yourself so yielding costs more than holding firm does; committing to a conditional rule rather than to an act; handing the decision to an agent you can't overrule; making yourself unreachable so you can't be offered a deal; announcing a commitment you could quietly undo; escalating in small reversible steps; committing by randomising in public; and conceding early and loudly. Add any others you think belong in the space.
- One node has to seperate commitment-as-self-control from commitment-as-strategy — the Ulysses-lashes-himself-to-the-mast use versus the get-the-other-guy-to-swerve use — and I'd like you to say explicitly where in the tree that split belongs and why there rather than earlier.
- One node has to capture the race part: whether you still have time to bind yourself before they do, versus whether they've already bound themselves and you're now just choosing how to lose. If a hard commitment made after the other side has hard-committed isn't really a strategy, say so on the tree rather than giving it a leaf.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually distinguish from the inside — my guess is a genuine binding and a sufficiently convincing performance of one look identical to the other player, but tell me if there are others and whether it's a fixable defect or a real one.
- Mark the combinations of answers that lead nowhere, and for each say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's thought to occupy it. I'd rather have three of these diagnosed properly than a tree with no gaps.

Then a stress test: re-run your own tree on one other case that you pick, as long as it isn't two vehicles heading at each other or a nuclear standoff, and report whether the same leaves come out or whether it misfires at some node. If it misfires, tell me which node is doing the damage.

An indented outline is fine, no graphics, and no references or suggested readings — we're not writing a paper, I just want the structure and the reasoning behind it.

Last thing: at the end, tell me how confident you are that this is carving the space at the joints rather than dressing up my food-hall example in general language, and single out the one node or leaf you're least sure about. I'd also like your best guess at what a real game theorist would say is wrong with it, if anything is.
