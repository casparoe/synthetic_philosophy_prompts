I run a lunchtime reading group at a public library — about ten people, some retirees, two nurses, a guy who listens to stoicism podcasts. Nobody's got any philosophy training but they read the actual text and they argue. Next week we're doing the part of Franklin's Autobiography where he sets out the project of arriving at moral perfection: the thirteen virtues each with a one-line precept, the little book ruled with seven columns for the days and thirteen red lines, a black spot for every fault found on examination, one virtue per week in a fixed order, Temperance first because it gives coolness and clearness of head, Order third because it buys him time for the project itself.

My problem is that last month we read a self-help book and the whole hour dissolved into people comparing apps, and I can feel this one sliding the same direction — someone says 'so Franklin invented the habit tracker' and we're done. I want to plan the session so they leave holding a philosophical question rather than a productivity tip.

Sixty minutes, whiteboard, no slides. I don't want the content from you, I want the plan:

- The order the pieces go in, and what each one depends on. I suspect you can't explain why Order sits where it sits until they've got the spot-marking scheme in hand, since its job is partly instrumental — but tell me if that's the right dependency or whether something has to come first that I'm putting third.
- The one thing the whole hour hangs off. I'm leaning toward the physical book and the act of making the marks, as against the *sequence* of the virtues, which feels like a seperate and better anchor. Pick one, and say what it has to have and what it has to leave out in order to carry the rest.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. My list so far, cut or add as you like: (a) it's a technique, so whether he could ever have succeeded never comes up as a question; (b) he thought virtue was just knowing what's right — when he says pretty much the opposite, that speculative conviction wasn't sufficient and the contrary habits had to be broken and new ones established; (c) the thirteen are an arbitrary puritan shopping list so there's nothing to discuss; (d) the failure was a willpower failure, therefore try harder; and (e) the one I find most interesting — that a precept about letting your things have their places and each part of your business have its time counts as a *moral* virtue at all, which I think is the best door into the whole thing but I don't know when to open it without losing them.
- One question I can put to the room out loud at the end that tells me whether the idea landed or whether I just told a charming story about an 18th century printer. Ideally one where a particular wrong answer points at a specific misconception from above.
- What I should flatly refuse to get into at this level — for instance the passage where he compares himself to the man who takes his new axe to the grindstone, gets sick of polishing, and decides he'd rather keep it speckled — and whether refusing it costs me something I'll regret in week two.

Assume they've read the passage, so nothing in the plan should just recount it back at them.
