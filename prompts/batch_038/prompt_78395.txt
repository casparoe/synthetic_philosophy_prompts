I have been arguing about the absent-minded driver problem on and off for the better part of two decades, and I would like to convert the argument into a number. Please give me your credence in the proposition below and, more importantly, the decomposition that produces it.

Call the proposition R: the absent-minded driver problem poses a genuine question about what a rational agent should do, one with a determinate answer that survives complete specification of the case; it is not a confusion manufactured by pressing a question that has no well-defined answer for an agent who cannot locate himself inside his own plan.

To keep us off the familiar version, here is the case I care about, with the payoffs fixed. A trucker is driving a stretch of highway with exactly two exits, indistinguishable from the wheel and without landmarks; a sedative prevents him from remembering whether he has already passed one. Exiting at the first pays 2, exiting at the second pays 9, passing both pays 0. Before setting out he may settle on a policy. On the road he cannot bind himself, he carries no randomizing device, and his deliberation is deterministic: whatever he decides at one exit he will decide at the other, and he cannot tell which he is at.

Two features of this setup should show up in your answer. First, the best symmetric mixed policy — exit with probability q at each junction — is q = 11/18, worth 121/36 ≈ 3.36, against 2 for always exiting and 0 for always continuing; the familiar point that mixed and behavioral strategies stop being interchangeable without perfect recall is doing real work here. Second, and I think less often noticed: if he could implement q = 11/18, he would find himself exactly indifferent at the junction. Conditional on being at a junction his credences would be 18/25 on the first and 7/25 on the second, so exiting is worth (18/25)(2) + (7/25)(9) = 99/25, and continuing is worth (18/25)(11/18)(9) = 99/25. Equal to the digit. The planning-optimal policy, were it available, would leave the junction-level decision with no purchase at all.

Give me a number for R and a rough probability for each of the sub-questions it rests on, with a line of justification apiece, then show me how they combine into the headline figure. Candidate terms — reject, regroup, or add as you see fit: whether that exact indifference shows the junction-level question is empty once a plan is fixed; whether stipulating "no randomizer" really forecloses the mixed policy, or whether a deterministic-but-self-opaque key (exit if the next plate is even) does the same work, and if so whether that relocates the puzzle from chance to self-opacity; whether the causal, evidential, and updateless readings genuinely diverge in this case or merely describe the same ex ante calculation in different vocabularies; whether the real engine here is self-locating belief rather than decision theory, and what the state of that adjacent dispute implies for R; and how much weight the outside view deserves, given that the nearest analogue, Newcomb's problem, has produced decades of argument and no consensus.

Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to; what would actually move it — an argument, a new variant, a result about how people deliberate under these conditions; and what, if anything, would count as R being settled rather than merely neglected. My own credence is about 0.35; a friend I have argued with for years is at about 0.8; say which sub-question you think the gap between us most likely lives in. Finally, be blunt about whether R is precise enough to deserve a number at all, or whether you are mainly reporting confidence in your own framing of it.
