I am drafting a seminar paper of about five thousand words for a graduate course on naturalist philosophy of mind, and my assigned topic is whether Dennett's defence of beliefs as real patterns amounts to realism about the intentional, or to an instrumentalism dressed in realist language. I know the standard moves already: the three stances, the insistence that folk-psychological attribution is not a mere calculating convenience because the pattern it tracks is robust and projectible, and the stock reply that predictive indispensability is a poor guide to what there is. What I need is an essay that gets underneath those moves instead of reciting them.

Two complications should organise the argument. The first concerns the comparative, attributer-relative form of the criterion: a high-level description is said to reveal a real pattern when it yields predictions that are better, or more compact, than anything obtainable from the design or physical stance. Now suppose a predictor with effectively unlimited computational resources, a Laplace's-demon civilisation that forecasts behaviour straight from the physics and has no occasion for intentional talk whatsoever. Does the criterion then entail that beliefs are not real patterns for that predictor, so that their reality is indexed to the computational poverty of the attributer? If so, is this a reductio, a bullet worth biting, or evidence that the criterion was never about predictive success but about something else entirely, such as description length relative to a fixed coding scheme? A friend of mine who works on model compression says the relevant question at any level of description is simply how much it shortens the encoding of the data without sacrificing too much accuracy, and I suspect that reframing quietly changes the metaphysical stakes, though I cannot say exactly how. The second complication is underdetermination: one can describe systems whose behaviour is equally well captured by two incompatible intentional interpretations, differing in the contents of the beliefs posited, or in the balance struck between belief and desire, with the physical facts settling neither. If the criterion goes silent in such cases, does it fail to deliver determinate attributions, and does that failure undercut the claim that beliefs are the entities it picks out?

Please write the essay, and write it to a conclusion. State the realist and the instrumentalist readings of the real-patterns criterion precisely enough that a reader could see which of them the criterion's own wording favours, and keep metaphysical realism about beliefs carefully distinguished from claims about the instrumental adequacy of the intentional idiom, since conflating the two is how this debate usually goes in circles. Then say explicitly which of the two complications, if either, is fatal, and whether an analogy with the indispensability argument for mathematical entities helps here or actively misleads, given that in the mathematical case the inference runs from indispensability within our best physical theories rather than from indispensability to finite predictors. Finally, deliver a verdict on whether Dennett should be counted a realist about belief and justify it; a qualified answer of the sort that makes him a realist in one respect and an instrumentalist in another is acceptable only if you specify the respects and show that the division is principled rather than a way of evading the question. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a survey of the secondary literature, as I will supply references myself, and assume throughout that your reader already knows what the three stances are.
