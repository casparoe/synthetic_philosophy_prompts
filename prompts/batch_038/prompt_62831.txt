**Question:** *Nurses on a cardiac step-down unit began reporting that a newly installed infusion-pump interface shifted extra checking work onto them and had been involved in two near-misses. Their reports were logged, forwarded to the vendor's engineers, and answered with documentation showing the device was operating within specification. For roughly eighteen months no shared vocabulary existed on the unit for what the nurses were describing; individually they called it "stress," "being overworked," or "not being technical enough." After a visiting human-factors researcher introduced the phrase "automation capture," the same reports were taken up by the safety committee within three weeks.*

*Identify which form(s) of epistemic injustice, on Fricker's account, the case exhibits, and explain why. Then say who, if anyone, is culpable for the injustice, and what follows for how it should be remedied. (400 words max.)*

**Student's answer:** *This is a clear case of hermeneutical injustice. The nurses had an experience — that the pump interface pushed cognitive work onto them and contributed to two near-misses — that they couldn't render in terms anyone with power to act on it could recognize. For eighteen months the only vocabulary available to them was "stress" and "being overworked," which locates the problem inside the nurses rather than in the device, and that is exactly what Fricker means by hermeneutical marginalization: a group being shut out of the practices through which shared meanings get made. The fact that the reports became actionable as soon as an outsider supplied a term confirms that the barrier was interpretive rather than motivational — nobody was refusing to listen, there just wasn't anything to listen to yet.*

*I don't think testimonial injustice is present as well. The engineers weren't downgrading the nurses because of a prejudice about who nurses are; they were downgrading reports they took to be technically inaccurate, and answering them with spec documentation. Even if you want to say "not technical enough" functions as a stereotype about nurses, Fricker's requirement is a credibility deficit generated by an identity prejudice, and judgements about technical competence aren't identity prejudices. So the two forms can't both apply here, and its important not to stretch testimonial injustice until it covers any case of somebody not being believed.*

*On culpability: Fricker holds that the hearer is not blameworthy for hermeneutical injustice, because the gap in the interpretive resource is a matter of bad luck rather than of anyone's doing. It follows that the hospital administration isn't culpable either, and the "willful hermeneutical ignorance" idea from the week's secondary reading — where dominant knowers decline to put in the effort to understand experiences they aren't positioned to grasp — is really just the flip side of the same structural fact, so it doesn't change the conclusion. The lesson is that the remedy can't be blame; the unit had to wait until someone with the right training happened to supply the missing concept.*
