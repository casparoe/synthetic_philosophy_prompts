I write novels, and every so often I also write the sort of essay that ends up as an afterword in the back of one of them. I've been chewing on an argument for an afterword for about two years now and its started to feel like a splinter. The argument is supposed to be about whether my readers' taste can ever be right or wrong, and it seems to finish by proving that nothing anyone says about anything can be right or wrong, including the argument. Which makes it useless to me as an essay and faintly alarming as a thing to believe.

Hume's setup first, since I'm building on it. In his essay on the standard of taste he says beauty isn't in objects but in the mind contemplating them, and yet he also wants some judgements of taste to be better than others, so he proposes that the standard is the joint verdict of the true judges — people of strong sense and delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, cleared of prejudice. He illustrates delicacy with that Cervantes story he retells, Sancho's two kinsmen who taste iron and leather in a hogshead of wine everyone else thinks is first-rate, and get laughed at until the cask is drained and a key on a leather thong turns up in the dregs. That anecdote is doing work for me, because it looks like a case where the kinsmen were right and were right because there was something there to be right about.

My argument:

(1) A judgement of taste is correct if and only if it matches the verdict the true judges would give.

(2) There's no independent way to establish who the true judges are. Nothing on Hume's list of qualities can be checked except through the verdicts: we call someone delicate because they spotted the key, we call someone prejudiced because their verdicts keep diverging from ours.

(3) So "true judge" gets fixed by the verdicts and "correct verdict" gets fixed by the true judges. Call it a two-sided fixpoint: the right assignment of judges-and-verdicts is whatever assignment makes both clauses come out true at once.

(4) Constructions like that are how people handle the liar sentence and the truth-teller — you build up from a base, you take the least assignment satisfying the clauses, and you're told this is safe because such an assignment always exists.

(5) Existence is the easy half. Nothing guarantees the fixpoint is unique.

(6) Here it plainly isn't. An assignment on which every reader who has ever lived is a true judge and every verdict is correct satisfies both clauses, since everyone's verdicts match everyone's. So does an assignment on which I am the only true judge and my verdicts are the correct ones. So does one built around anyone else you care to nominate.

(7) If many assignments satisfy the definition, the definition picks out no standard. A standard equally satisfied by every possible state of affairs isn't a standard, it's wallpaper.

(8) So Hume's account, patched in the most charitable way I can find, delivers no standard of taste.

(9) And the same reasoning runs for any account where the credentials of judges are read off their verdicts — which is every account on offer, since the only alternative is to point at credentials that aren't verdicts, and I can't think what those would be that weren't just verdicts in disguise.

(10) Therefore there is no standard of taste. And this conclusion is itself a claim about how things stand, with no credentials behind it and nothing that could correct it, so the argument takes the floor out from under itself on the way down.

I want a diagnosis, not sympathy. Which step do you reject? If it's a bad step, say what's wrong with it and why it looked fine to me for two years. If nothing in it is wrong, then tell me which premise I should be prepared to lose and price the loss for me. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (2) than (1) — I've spent twenty years watching people's actual responses teach me things that no amount of theorising about responses could, and I don't want to hand that over — and I'd like to keep (7), because "wallpaper" is doing real work there and I don't want to be told a standard can be satisfied by everything. I'd give up (10) without a fight. But I know those are preferences, not arguments.
