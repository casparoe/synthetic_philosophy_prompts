For several years I have been cataloguing the records of a mid-sized manufacturing city whose public self-image rests on two stories. The first holds that the founding families sheltered a column of refugees during a border war; the parish registers I transcribed show that those same families billed the crown for every night's lodging and turned away roughly a third of the people who arrived. The second holds that a great strike in the early twentieth century ended when the mill owner magnanimously conceded; the company ledgers show that the settlement was imposed by a state arbitration the owner had lobbied for, and that wages were cut again the following spring.

What interests me is not that the stories are false. Flattering founder myths are unremarkable. It is what happened after the evidence became public. In both cases the correction was accepted without argument: it was printed in the local paper, written into the school curriculum, and mounted on the museum panels. And in both cases the commemoration grew more elaborate rather than less. The city now celebrates itself as the place that took in the refugees and had the honesty to admit how badly it treated them; the strike is commemorated as the moment when ordinary workers forced the state to intervene, with the owner's lobbying cited as proof of how entrenched his power was. Nobody denies the record. The record has been folded into the story, and the story has emerged stronger, more textured, and much harder to argue with, because any further objection can be met with the observation that the city has already confessed to more than the objector knows.

I have since watched the same sequence twice more — in a university's handling of documents about the source of its founding endowment, and in a religious community's treatment of a prediction that failed on a specified date — and in each case the pattern held: unwelcome evidence surfaces, is conceded promptly and in full, and is then absorbed into a richer version of the founding narrative, which the concession itself now seems to warrant.

I want to know whether this has a name. I am fairly confident it is not denial, since denial rejects the evidence and here the evidence is embraced; and it is not quite the much-discussed tendency of a correction to strengthen the mistaken belief it was aimed at, since in my cases nothing is disbelieved and nothing is corrected away — the group updates on the facts and the narrative survives by changing what the facts are taken to mean. Nor is it invention, in the ordinary sense of myth-making, because no new falsehoods are introduced; if anything the community's story becomes more factually accurate while growing less answerable.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — either the name of the established concept or a plain statement that there isn't one — and then give your reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question. A bare label will not satisfy me; I want the carving. So: take the nearest established concepts from the study of social and cultural memory and from the study of belief revision under disconfirmation, and say precisely how the pattern I have described differs from each of them, and which, if any, actually covers it. If none does, coin a term and define it tightly enough that someone else could apply it to a case I have not described without consulting you. Mark its edges — in particular, tell me how to distinguish it from a community's genuinely correcting itself historiographically, where the unwelcome evidence does reduce the narrative's grip rather than deepening it. And say what turns on drawing that line correctly, insofar as it bears on which of the two should give way when a community's memory and the archival record collide.
