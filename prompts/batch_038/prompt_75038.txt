i'm teaching a session on population ethics next week and i want to open it with a poem instead of the usual diagram of Parfit's worlds. the conceit is that the poem should do the mere addition paradox in its own shape. start with three or four lines that are dense and gorgeous and clearly about a small world of very good lives, then keep adding: each stanza gets more lines than the one before, and the lines get shorter and flatter as the count climbs, maybe with one near-identical line recuring so you can feel the thinning. every addition has to read as a gain on its own terms — more people, lives worth living, nobody made worse off — and the speaker should only ever be allowed to comment on the next increment, never on the whole, so that the verdict about the whole can only form in the reader. aim for around 35 lines total, and the last stanza should be a lot of very small lines that is still worth reading and still somehow worse than the opening, with no single step the reader can point at where it went wrong. no fixed rhyme needed, don't name the paradox inside the poem, don't preach, and don't resolve it — i basically want them walking into the discussion already annoyed at the shape of the thing.
