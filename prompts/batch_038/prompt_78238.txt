I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I serve as one of two non-specialist members on the grants advisory board of a private foundation whose charter commits it, in a single undifferentiated phrase, to "the relief of needless suffering and the support of lives that go well". Nobody has ever unpacked that phrase, and this year it has stopped being academic.

Two proposals have reached the final round of our adolescent wellbeing programme, and the budget permits only one at full scale. Both would operate in the same city, in schools drawn from its lowest income decile, with the same age band (fourteen to sixteen), and their catchments overlap almost entirely, so this is a genuine either/or.

Proposal A is a three-year school-based programme built from contemplative practice and attention-training methods. Its investigators describe their theoretical orientation as tranquilist: what matters, on their account, is the absence of craving and of suffering, while pleasure and satisfaction count only insofar as they quiet craving. The programme's aim is therefore not to help participants get more of what they want but to reduce the intensity and the number of their wants. Their headline outcome measure is a "craving-burden index", combining self-reported intensity of current unsatisfied wants, the number of distinct standing goals a participant reports, and a measure of the gap between what participants say they want and what they say they enjoy when they get it.

In a one-year pilot across two schools (roughly four hundred participants), the index fell by about a third, and standard measures of anxiety and low mood fell with it. But a secondary inventory — a short list of things participants said they were looking forward to, or working towards, over the following five years — also fell, by a similar margin, as did self-reported ambition about further education. The investigators present this as corroborating evidence: fewer forward-looking wants means fewer occasions for frustration and disappointment. One of our trustees reads the very same table as a picture of resignation being produced on demand, and points out that the pilot schools were the two with the highest recorded staff turnover in the district.

Proposal B comes from a different research group and takes no philosophical position at all in its covering letter. It offers subsidised, near-universal access to music-making, organised sport, and paid summer work placements, evaluated on capability-style measures — what participants say they are actually able to do and be — alongside straightforward enjoyment measures and the same anxiety scales used in A. It costs about forty per cent more per participant, and its own pilot shows a smaller effect on anxiety and low mood than A's.

The board is split three–three, and the chair has asked the two outside members for written opinions before we reconvene. The trustee favouring A argues that B buys episodic satisfaction at a price, leaves the underlying economy of wanting untouched, and that a foundation which cannot fund the view that less wanting is better has simply made its own view invisible rather than neutral. The trustee favouring B argues that A, if it works as advertised, teaches adolescents in a poor city to want less of what they are owed, and that a foundation which funds that has mistaken a theory of the good for a coping strategy for injustice.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate, in British English, of about 900 to 1,200 words. It should open with a bottom line — fund A, fund B, split the award with a stated rationale, or return one or both to the applicants with conditions they could realistically meet before the autumn round — and a timetable attached to it. Then give the reasoning in a form the applicants could actually answer, and set out two or three criteria the board could apply to whatever arrives next year without re-litigating the whole dispute.

Two things matter most to me. First, name the interpretive judgement on which your recommendation turns — what the fall in forward-looking wanting in A's pilot really tells us — and say how confident you are in it, together with what further evidence from data the investigators already hold, and could supply before we vote, would move you. Second, take the strongest version of the losing side seriously enough that its advocate would recognise it as their own argument rather than a courtesy paragraph.

Don't send me a reading list or citations; I want your judgement, not a syllabus. And if you think I have framed this wrongly — that the two proposals are not really competitors, that the pilot table cannot bear the weight I am putting on it, or that this is not a question a grants board is competent to settle on evidence of this kind — say so plainly at the top instead of working politely inside my framing.
