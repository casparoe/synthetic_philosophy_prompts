I'm running a session for our grad reading group on Williams next month and I want to hand people a case that isn't one of the famous ones. No George and the chemistry department, no Jim, no husband standing around wondering whether he's permitted to rescue his own wife — half the room has those memorised and stops thinking the moment they hear them, which rather defeats the object. What I want is a thought experiment built to a pretty awkward set of specs, and I want you to show me it actually meets them rather than just gesturing at the general territory.

Here's the spec. First, the target shouldn't be utilitarianism or any other consequentialism: make the demanding view a rigorously impartial but non-aggregative one — a Ross-style pluralism where beneficence has genuine weight, or a contractualist view on which what you do has to be justifiable to each person it affects, something in that neighbourhood — and it has to issue a real requirement in the case, not just a permission that gets outvoted by the agent's tastes. Second, the ground project the agent is being asked to give up has to be one most people would call trivial or faintly silly. Nothing noble, no vocation, no sick relative, no great work of art. This is the crux for me: if the project is admirable the objection just smuggles in the value of the project and isn't about integrity at all, and I want a case that can't be accused of that. Third, the motive the agent actually acts from can't be love or loyalty or anything derived from a special relationship — it has to be something more like personal fastidiousness, professional pride, taste, or plain stubbornness. Fourth, ordinary and realistic: a workplace, a street, a hobby, a family. No sci-fi, no exotic beliefs, the agent is fully informed and reasoning normally, and the whole thing fits in one paragraph because I want to put it on a slide.

Then do the check for me. Name the demanding theory precisely enough that we could argue with it, say what it requires here and by what route, and say what the integrity objection says about that — and keep it separate from the point Williams makes about the husband having one thought too many, because my own suspicion is that those two are doing different work and I'd like to see whether that's right once love has been taken out of the picture. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting, and run the swap: what happens to the verdicts if you replace the trivial project with an admirable one? If that makes the objection stronger rather than weaker, does that show it was never really about integrity? Finish with the best attack a defender of the impartial view has on the case — I assume it's something about an agent dressing up a preference as a commitment — and say whether the case can be tightened against that or whether it just collapses. And if you think one of my four constraints can't be met alongside the others, say so and tell me why; I'd rather know then be handed a case that quietly cheats.
