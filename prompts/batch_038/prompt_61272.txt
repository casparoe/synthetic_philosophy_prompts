I am a novelist by trade, and in six weeks I must file a two-page application for a year-long residential fellowship; the theme this cycle is the future of work. The selection panel will include two labour economists and a historian of economic thought, so I cannot hope to compete on data. The application must argue instead that a conceptual question at the centre of the debate has been missed, and that a writer is a defensible person to pursue it. Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into the proposal.

Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for his grandchildren is usually faulted in one of two ways. Either the prediction of a working week of some fifteen hours failed to materialise, or it could not have materialised, because the wants that govern us are relative to what others have and therefore insatiable. Both criticisms, and both of the familiar defences against them, treat the good life Keynes sketches as a quantity — sufficient income, sufficient unobligated hours. The difficulty I want to work on is structural rather than quantitative. Keynes describes the good life as one no longer organised around deferral, in which activities are valued for themselves and not for what they make possible later; yet the temperament capable of inhabiting such a life is, on his own account, one shaped by centuries of accumulation, thrift, and the postponement of present satisfaction for a distant end. The disposition the good life requires is therefore produced by precisely the arrangements the good life is meant to supersede. If that is so, abundance does not merely fall short of the good life; it removes the kind of person who could occupy it. Keynes may already half-see this, insofar as he tells his contemporaries to keep the old money-morality in service for another hundred years on the grounds that it alone can lead us out of our present economic necessity — which concedes that the scaffolding is load-bearing.

Please write the proposal itself. It should state a question precise enough to admit of a determinate answer, and distinguish that question from the two standard objections, explaining why neither they nor the usual replies settle it. It should then set out the method for the twelve months, month by month. My own working method would be close reading of the essay and of Keynes's remarks elsewhere on character and the purposive temperament, set against constructed case-portraits — composite lives in which work has stopped, through retirement, windfall, inheritance, or buyout — used as structured thought-experiments rather than as evidence. The proposal should say plainly what epistemic work such portraits can do, what they cannot do, and what would make them decorative rather than probative. It should describe what a successful result would look like, and state in advance what would count as failure, including the possibility that the whole difficulty dissolves into a confusion between a psychological claim about how dispositions form and a normative claim about how a life should be lived. Give an honest assessment of whether the project fits a single year and what I would cut if it does not. Finally, include the paragraph I expect the panel to demand: why a novelist rather than an economist should run this.

Do not supply a bibliography or citations; I will add those myself. Wherever the argument would need empirical support, specify the kind of finding it requires and how I would go about testing for it, rather than naming studies.
