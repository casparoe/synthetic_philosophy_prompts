Take the Atlanta school cheating scandal and run it through Foucault's account of the examination. The facts, in case they're fuzzy: in 2009 the Atlanta Journal-Constitution published a statistical analysis of state test scores that turned up implausible wrong-to-right erasure patterns, in 2011 state investigators reported organized cheating at 44 of the 56 schools they looked at and named roughly 178 educators, and in 2015 twelve of them went to trial under Georgia's RICO statute and eleven were convicted — teachers and principals erasing kids' answers becuase the district had made test-score targets the basis for bonuses, transfers, and getting fired.

I don't want the easy "it was a panopticon" take. I want the mapping. In Discipline and Punish the examination is supposed to fuse hierarchical observation with normalizing judgement: it ranks people against a norm, makes them individually knowable, and yields a truth about them. So work through who was observing whom in that district, where the norm actually came from, and how a score spreadsheet did disciplinary work no principal could have done by yelling. Then say where the machinery breaks. Two spots bother me. First, the teachers and principals weren't just the exam's operators, they were also its objects, and they falsified the record — so the instrument that's meant to generate truth about those it examines got pointed at itself and gamed. Does Foucault have resources for that, or does he quietly assume the exam works? Second, the response to the gaming was a big, public, theatrical criminal prosecution, which is exactly the sovereign spectacle that discipline supposedly retired. Is that spectacular power coming back, or is a RICO show trial itself just another disciplinary technique? Finally, tell me which Foucault actually fits this case better — the Discipline and Punish one or the governmentality and pastoral-power one — and whether the episode confirms his account, is an anomaly for it, or is something he never anticipated at all.
