For two years I've been the engineer on my team arguing that we are trading away something we cannot buy back, and this fall our org is finally giving me a forty-minute slot plus open Q&A at an internal engineering summit to make the case to everyone at once. My thesis is narrow and I want to keep it that way: that assistant-generated code has not merely moved understanding around inside our organization but has removed the mechanism that used to produce it — the long, unrewarded stretch of a junior engineer's first two years spent reading and debugging code they did not write — and that the resulting deficit is a debt no individual can pay down, because unlike technical debt it does not live in the codebase where a smart person can go read it, it lives in the absence of people who could. I want to argue that our normal safeguards were calibrated against a world in which the reviewer had, at some point, read everything, and that this makes the deficit non-renewable on a roughly three-year horizon. I close with a proposal for what I've been calling a comprehension budget: a named human on every change who can walk through any line of it unaided, an assistant-free debugging rotation, and a tracked indicator with an actual target rather than a vibe. My supporting story is a six-hour outage last spring in a module that had been generated, reviewed, and merged across nine months by four different people, none of whom could tell me what a particular retry loop was for; the fix ultimately came from a vendor support engineer who had never seen our repository.

The room is about two hundred people: mostly staff and principal engineers from product orgs who use assistants heavily and have the throughput numbers to prove it is working for them; three engineering directors who own delivery metrics and will hear any proposal as a velocity tax; one human-factors researcher embedded in our safety group who works on automation complacency and will be listening for whether I have rebranded something from her field; and an ML researcher from the team that builds our internal assistant, who is friendly but will want to know why a better explainer isn't a better answer than a policy. Nobody there is a philosopher and neither am I — I'm self-taught, I've been reading about testimony and epistemic dependence on my own time, and I'm aware I may have gotten parts of it wrong. The piece I most want pressure-tested is a disanalogy I'm leaning on: the standard story about large collaborative science is that each contributor genuinely understands their own narrow slice and nobody understands the whole, which is uncomfortable but survivable; my claim is that ours is worse in kind, because the slices aren't understood by anyone either, so there is no distributed understanding to fall back on, only distributed confidence. I've also borrowed the finding that people systematically overestimate how well they understand a mechanism until asked to explain it, and I'm using that to argue that self-reported comprehension from our engineers is not a usable signal.

What I need is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise — not the generic AI-skeptic talking points, and not the ones a philosophy seminar would produce — ranked by how much damage each would do and how likely it is, and phrased in the register of the person most likely to raise it: the directors' objections will be about measurement and cost, the human-factors researcher's about novelty and evidence, the assistant team's about whether my policy solves the problem or merely makes it expensive to encounter. For each one, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes while standing at a podium, an honest note on where that reply is soft, and what I should concede before the concession is extracted from me. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the comprehension-budget proposal to survive the room, and give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one where the honest answer costs me something. Two smaller things while you're in there: flag anywhere I have mangled or oversimplified a philosophical concept, since I have no training and would far rather fix it now than be corrected in front of two hundred colleagues; and tell me which of my coinages will be heard as a rename of something that already has a name in the human-factors or education literature, so I can credit it up front instead of looking like I've dressed up a known effect. I don't want a reading list or citations — I couldn't chase them before the talk anyway — just the objection map and the warnings.
