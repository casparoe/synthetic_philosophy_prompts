I've been working on my own through the problem of comparing two futures that both go on forever, and I've arrived at a rule I'd like to see stress-tested. My rule is this: set the totals aside, since both are infinite and the totals therefore can't discriminate; instead ask what each future's welfare level settles down to. Take the average over an initial stretch, let that stretch grow without bound, and rank the two futures by the value each average converges to. The higher settled level wins, no matter how much was gained or lost along the way.

Please construct a case that puts genuine pressure on this. I want a single decision by an ordinary mortal person — a foundation director, a treaty negotiator, a chief engineer — whose choice fixes which of two endless futures obtains, arranged so that: the totals come out indifferent; the comparison by cumulative sums (whichever future's running total pulls permanently ahead and stays ahead) favours one; a discounted total favours that same one; and my settled-level rule favours the other. Constraints: the endlessness must come from something self-sustaining, not from immortals, infinitely many rooms, or an unending sequence of coin flips; no exotic physics; quantities a layperson could actually reason about; and the scenario itself in a single paragraph.

Then demonstrate that the design works. Give each rule's verdict with the arithmetic behind it. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what the four verdicts become if I delete that feature. Finally, give the strongest objection a defender of the cumulative-sums rule would press against mine — specifically, whether my rule is really just a discount rate in disguise or a genuinely different standard, and how I could tell the difference from inside the case.
