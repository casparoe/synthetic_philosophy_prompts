I am presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary judgment-and-decision-making workshop, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of my view. Please treat the following as the thesis and the room, and work out the questions I will actually be asked.

My thesis. The label "sunk cost fallacy" is a misdescription in a substantial class of cases. I argue that weighting one's own past expenditure is rational whenever three conditions hold together: the agent cannot directly observe the quality of the ongoing project; her earlier investment decisions were themselves made on the basis of private evidence and judgment that she no longer fully recalls or can reconstruct; and permitting herself to abandon the project whenever present prospects look marginally worse than an alternative would destroy her capacity to commit at all, where commitment is precisely what made the long-horizon project worth starting. On this account, persistence after heavy investment is the visible trace of a precommitment technology, not a reasoning error. The normative evaluation should therefore be of dispositions rather than of individual choices, and I claim the standard experimental paradigm is blind to this because it scores single, isolated decisions.

The room. About twenty-five people. The regulars are experimental economists who run incentivized choice tasks and care about preregistration, demand characteristics, and whether an effect survives a framing in which subjects have real money on the line. Two are decision theorists who work on dynamic choice, dominance reasoning, and exploitation arguments against non-Bayesian agents. One is a behavioral ecologist who studies persistence in non-human animals — whether an animal keeps investing in a patch, a nest, or a mate after costs have already accrued — and who will be interested in whether my account is a claim about human rationality specifically or about any agent facing uncertain, long-horizon projects. One is a philosopher who writes critically about heuristics-and-biases research and about what follows normatively from showing that a bias is widespread. Nobody in the room works on precommitment in the context of personal projects, and nobody is sympathetic to me on that front.

The objection I most expect, and cannot answer yet, is that the classic vignettes are constructed precisely to defeat my informational story: the tickets are stipulated non-transferable, the ranking of future enjoyment is given outright, and there is no reputation to maintain, no audience, and no future project whose start-up depends on being a person who finishes things. If that is right, my account has no purchase exactly where the effect is measured, and it can only be rescued by insisting that the vignettes are unrepresentative — which will sound like special pleading.

What I want from you:

1. The eight or nine objections I should expect, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, not by how interesting it is.
2. Each objection phrased in the first person as that person in the room would phrase it, in their own idiom. I want the decision theorist's version to sound like a decision theorist and the experimentalist's version to sound like an experimentalist; do not write them all in the same neutral voice.
3. For each, a reply of two to four sentences that I could plausibly say aloud in the time available, and a separate one-sentence note on where that reply is softest — the point at which a good follow-up would leave me with nothing better than a shrug.
4. A flag on the one question I am hoping nobody raises: the objection that, if it comes, ends the session.
5. The single concession I should volunteer in the first five minutes of the talk in order to make the rest of the questioning land more softly, and what I gain and lose by making it.

Finally, a note on your own confidence. Tell me which of these rankings you are least sure of and why; what you would need to know about this particular room — or about my argument, which I have only sketched — that would make you reorder them; and where your reasoning is most likely to fail, whether that is in predicting the audience or in assessing whether my thesis is defensible at all. If your honest view is that no preparation will save this position in front of this crowd, say so plainly, and say what would have to be true for you to withdraw that judgment.
