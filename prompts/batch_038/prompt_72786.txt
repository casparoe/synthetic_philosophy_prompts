I'm 71 and retired five years ago from a national lab, where I spent most of my career doing neutron scattering on alloys and a fair amount of variational modelling on the side. Since I stopped working I've been chipping away at philosophy, mostly philosophy of maths, which I always meant to read when I was young and never did. Last month I took out a popular book on infinity from the library — nothing academic, the kind with chapter titles like "Choosing" — and it gave this summary of Zorn's lemma, which I've copied out here because it's the bit I can't make up my mind about:

> Zorn's lemma is the workhorse of modern mathematics. It says that if you have a collection of objects with a notion of "bigger than," and if every increasing sequence in the collection has something above it, then the collection contains a biggest object. With that one sentence in hand you can prove that every vector space has a basis, that every nonzero ring has a maximal ideal, that products of compact spaces are compact, and even that the real numbers can be arranged in a well-ordering. It looks like a new assumption, but it isn't really one: in the 1930s logicians showed that Zorn's lemma and the axiom of choice are equivalent, each implying the other, so the choice you face isn't between Zorn's lemma and choice but whether to accept that whole package — and most mathematicians accept it because it's so useful. The proof is nothing mysterious either. Start with any object in the collection and keep choosing a bigger one. Sooner or later you can't go any further, and there you are: your biggest object. If the choosing never stops, you simply continue out through the ordinals; there are enough ordinals that the proccess can't run forever, so it must terminate. In other words Zorn's lemma is just ordinary induction with a longer runway, and if you trust induction on the natural numbers, as everyone does, there is no extra cost in trusting it here. The price shows up elsewhere. Some of the objects Zorn's lemma hands you can never be written down: nobody has exhibited a basis for the real numbers regarded as a vector space over the rationals, and nobody ever will — we know one exists only because the lemma says so. That is the real oddity of the axiom of choice, not that it proves strange things but that it proves things that are true and forever out of reach.
