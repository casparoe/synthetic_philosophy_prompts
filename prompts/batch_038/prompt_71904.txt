I have spent about a decade arguing about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence in rationalist comment threads, and I have come to think that those arguments almost never engage: each side is defending a different proposition, and the thread never settles which one. So before I read another defense or refutation, I want the specification. Write the desiderata for these two theses — what any adequate formulation and defense has to deliver.

Fix the purpose this way, since adequacy is relative to it: I want a version strong enough to support the inference from the premise that some optimizer far smarter than us will arise whose final ends we did not select, to the conclusion that its behavior is nonetheless predictable in the particular respect that makes it dangerous. Not strong enough for a conceptual-analysis seminar; strong enough to carry that inference.

Give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which turn out to be the pet requirements of one camp. At minimum, I want the list to adjudicate the following, and I want your view on each:

1. Whether the thesis concerns the space of final ends that are *implementable* at all, or the narrower space of ends that are *stable* under self-modification, reflection, and competition with other agents — and which of the two the risk argument actually requires.
2. What instrumental convergence is a claim about: subgoals the agent explicitly represents, dispositions an outside observer should predict, or what a competitive selection process retains. State the thesis separately on each reading and say which readings survive.
3. The grain problem: at what level of description final ends individuate, and whether the thesis is trivially true at coarse grain and empirically false at fine grain.
4. The moral-realism wedge: if normative claims can be true and good cognition tends to track truth, what content survives in orthogonality, and whether the thesis is parasitic on a metaethical commitment it usually goes unstated about.
5. The constitutivist objection that practical rationality itself fixes some ends, and what it costs to concede versus deny it.
6. Whether the thesis is a priori or empirical, and what observation would count against each half.

Then flag any pair of desiderata you believe jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that non-triviality, apriority, and the predictive claim cannot all be had at once, and if so the requirements list is itself the result, and the forum wars were never about agents. Tell me also which single requirement knocks out the most popular formulations.

Only at the end, and briefly: score the three lines I encounter most often — the purely conceptual defense, the reply that sufficiently sophisticated agents will discover instrumental reasons to adopt moral ends, and the deflationary reply that convergence merely restates that optimizers optimize. No citations and no reading list; I know the literature and want the criteria.
