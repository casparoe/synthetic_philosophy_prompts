I convene a weekly graduate reading group in formal epistemology: eighteen participants, mostly from philosophy, with three from the mathematics department who are conspicuously quicker at arithmetic than the rest. We meet for one hundred minutes in a seminar room with two whiteboards, and by long-standing rule no laptops, phones or calculators are permitted. Next week's subject is logical uncertainty: why an agent who cannot see all the consequences of its own beliefs cannot simply be required to hold coherent credences, and what standard might replace that requirement. I want the group to collide with the problem rather than be told about it, so the exposition should come only at the end.

Design a paper-based prediction market in which participants price statements of arithmetic that they cannot all settle, arranged so that the group runs into the following, in roughly this order: that prices are constrained by logical relations between statements nobody has verified; that no participant with a pen and a sheet of paper could satisfy all of those constraints at once; that what survives as a feasible standard is therefore not coherence at an instant but the absence of any describable, repeatable pattern by which someone could keep profiting from the prices; and that the price attaching to a statement which will never be settled behaves differently from the price of one that is merely laborious.

The design should specify:

- a bank of statements in three tiers — those settleable in the room within a minute, those settleable only with real effort or outside the session, and those open in principle — with a note on what each is there to elicit. Choose them so that I can verify the answers beforehand with a short computer program; nothing should turn on my trusting a remembered fact.
- a clearing mechanism that produces a single price per statement, runs entirely on paper and whiteboard, and is quick enough to repeat fifteen times or more. Say explicitly why the price it yields can be read as a probability.
- a point-based payoff scheme, with no real currency involved, resting on a scoring rule that makes it in participants' interest to trade towards their honest assessments, plus a one-line justification.
- an auditor role: two participants who do not trade but earn points by exhibiting an exploitable combination of prices. Define what counts as a successful challenge, how the ledger is corrected once one is made, and how to stop the role degenerating into a contest over who already knows the answers.
- a minute-by-minute timeline, and what to cut if we overrun.

I would rather you exploit the presence of the mathematics students than neutralise them: the asymmetry in what different participants can work out is part of the phenomenon. Tell me how the debrief should use it. Whether trading happens individually or in small teams is your call, but justify it.

The debrief should map each phase of the game onto the point it was built to produce, and only then supply the vocabulary — the phrases "logical omniscience" and "logical induction" must not be spoken before that moment. It should also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect at least: a participant who quietly knows several of the answers and drains the pool without anyone learning anything; a ledger that barely moves because nobody wants to be first to trade against the posted price; and the whole exercise being experienced as a mathematics quiz rather than a question about belief. Explain how the rules, rather than my intervention, should blunt each of these.

Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce — a single hundred-minute session generates no asymptotic behaviour, and the auditors' ingenuity is not a formal notion of feasible computation, among other gaps — and say precisely what I must supply in words to cover them. Keep the whole thing self-contained: no references or suggested readings, as the participants will have none to hand.
