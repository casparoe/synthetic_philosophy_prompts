I write the ideas-and-science column for a regional paper, and I've been reading our fortnightly liberty columnist — call him Hal — who is oddly consistent but has never once spelled out his rule. I have to file Thursday predicting where he'll land on a story that's breaking right now, so I need his implicit principle reverse-engineered. You're a political philosopher who works on theories of freedom: think carefully and show your checking.

His verdicts over the past year:

1. A state law requiring 3,000 hours of supervised training before you can braid hair for money — reduces freedom.
2. A no-strings $1,000-a-month cash grant to long-term unemployed adults — increases freedom.
3. A city ordinance making landlords show cause and get a hearing before evicting — increases freedom. He wrote that the landlord's loss of options is beside the point; that's his phrase, not mine.
4. A one-employer company town where the firm owns the houses, the store and the clinic, has never once cut anybody off, and has no legal obligation to keep any of it going — reduces freedom. He says the standard of living there is fine and that it's something else he's objecting to.
5. A benefits office that turns down an applicant, but works from published criteria, issues written reasons and has a functioning appeal — does not reduce freedom.
6. A prison credit system where inmates earn back early release under published, non-discretionary rules instead of at a parole board's mood — increases freedom.

Now the live story. A company has opened a "charter community" forty miles from here: signers get a furnished apartment, a clinic, a school for their kids and a $85k stipend, all materially better than anything else within a hundred miles. Everything's in the handbook — published grounds, written reasons, a three-person appeal panel with one resident on it. Two catches: the published grounds for expulsion include conduct inconsistent with the community's values as determined by the resident director; the panel can only reccomend, the director decides; and the company owns all the housing and jobs in the area, so being out means starting from nothing. Nobody's been expelled in eight years. My editor keeps asking whether Hal is a non-interference person, a real-options person or a domination person, and I honestly can't tell from the columns.

So: (a) brainstorm at least three candidate rules that could generate all six verdicts, and actually test each rule against each verdict one by one — say where a rule strains; (b) give the prediction each surviving rule makes about the charter community; (c) tell me your best guess at Hal's verdict and why you'd bet on that rule over the others; (d) name one further case, as concrete as the six above, that the two leading rules would call differently — that's the one I can put to him in an interview. And if any of the six verdicts is genuinely inconsistent with the rest under every rule you come up with, flag that rather than forcing a fit. No "it depends," please — I need a call.
