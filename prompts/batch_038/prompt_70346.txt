I'm working a column on why smart people can't agree about what counts as evidence, and my fact-checker budget is zero, so you're it. A glossy ideas-magazine feature I read on a flight set out a thought experiment like this:

'A soldier, Judy Benjamin, gets dropped behind enemy lines with no idea where she is. She figures she's equally likely to be in the northern or the southern half of the region. A radio operator at headquarters, generally reliable but not infallible, sends her a message: if you're in the north, there's a 3 in 4 chance you're in Company Two's territory. The philosopher Bas van Fraassen's answer is that this leaves her odds of being in the north exactly where they were -- still 50/50 -- because the message is only about a conditional, and a conditional can't touch an unconditional belief. It's the Monty Hall problem in camouflage: once you see the report as a hint rather than a fact, Bayes' theorem forces the same counterintuitive result. The lesson for the rest of us is that being told "if A, then probably B" should never shift how confident you are in A.'

That sounds mostly right and I've got 900 words to fill, but my editor will defintely poke at it. Can you take it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher of probability would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? In particular: is the setup as stated actually the example, is the 'changes nothing' answer the settled one or just one move in a live dispute that other people answer differently, and is the Monty Hall comparison doing any real work or is it decoration? And does the source's reliability matter at all, the way the passage implies?

Then the practical bit: does the closing lesson -- that hearing 'if A, then probably B' never moves your confidence in A -- survive as general advice about how to update, or is it overreading one contested example into a rule?

End with a line starting 'Verdict:' followed by a three-to-five word label for how much of the passage holds up.
