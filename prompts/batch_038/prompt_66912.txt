I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind. In week six of this term I give a fifty-minute seminar on whether dreaming is a form of experiencing, to twenty-four first-year graduate students in a cognitive science programme: mostly computational neuroscience and experimental psychology, two from linguistics, nobody with sustained reading in philosophy and nobody at all in Wittgenstein. They arrive having just had a fortnight on sleep architecture — staging, arousal thresholds, the asymmetry in report frequency between REM and NREM awakenings — and the phrase they will reach for in discussion is that dreaming is basically a hallucination that happens to be self-generated. The room has a seminar table, two whiteboards and no projector: the module is screen-free this year, so anything I want in front of them has to be drawable by hand in under a minute.

My aim is for Norman Malcolm's thesis in *Dreaming* (1959) and the paper Dennett devoted to the same question, "Are Dreams Experiences?", to strike them as two live and genuinely incompatible positions, rather than as a piece of mid-century linguistic legislation with a scientistic gloss on it. I do not want the explanation. I want a plan for the explanation.

1. Give me the order in which the components must reach the board, with the dependency rationale for each placement — what has to be in hand before the next thing can be intelligible. My own instinct is that the first thing they must acquire is not a theory of dreams but an account of what a memory report commits one to, since both authors attack the sentence about having dreamt as a report of a past experience; I do not know whether that grammar-first route survives contact with an audience freshly trained to treat reports as data points. Tell me whether to open with the grammar or with the sleep lab, and defend the choice.

2. Name the single case that should carry the whole session, and specify what it has to have and what it has to lack. My candidates are: the guillotine dream retold since the nineteenth century, in which the blade falls at the instant the bed's headboard drops on the sleeper's neck; Dennett's waterfall case and the temporal-compression worry it is built to raise; and a composite I assembled from an informal pilot of my own — sixty written reports collected from undergraduates, of which roughly a third were confident and event-rich while the writers declined to say whether the scene was in colour, whether it was seen from inside or from outside, or whether the speech in it was heard. Tell me which one to use and why each of the others fails, or tell me to build a fourth and describe it.

3. Map the misconceptions to the minutes. I expect at least: that Malcolm is denying that people dream; that Dennett is saying dreams are not real; that the polysomnographic record settles the matter by itself; and that the disagreement is merely verbal. For each, state the point in the sequence at which it must be headed off rather than corrected afterwards, and what I should write or ask at that point. Then tell me which misconception I am most likely to manufacture myself — I suspect the slide from no experience to nothing happens — and how to build the guard rail into the wording.

4. Give me one question, askable in the final five minutes and answerable on paper in two, that separates a student who has the distinction from one who has learned to recite it. Note the downstream use: three weeks later this same cohort must critique a dream-lab methods paper for the module assessment, and the critique turns on keeping apart what a subject said at four in the morning from what, if anything, was occurring at three. The check should rehearse that transfer.

5. Tell me what to cut, and to cut visibly, announcing the omission rather than leaving a hole. I have shortlisted the private language argument, the multiple drafts model, statistics on dream bizarreness, the lucid-dream eye-signal experiments, and any appeal to what it is like. On the last of these I may be wrong: I have been assuming the lucid-dreaming data must be excluded because pre-agreed eye signals produced from inside a dream look like a decisive refutation of both authors and would end the session twenty minutes early. Argue with me if that is a mistake, and if it is, say where it has to go and what it costs.

Finally, specify the board layout: what is written where at the midpoint, what is never erased, and the single sentence I must not say aloud at any point in the fifty minutes.
