Weird gig, and I could use help thinking it through. I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-size outlet, and last spring I did a piece on the metaphysics of ordinary objects — basically, when does a bunch of stuff add up to one thing. The people who run a regional professional-development day for museum registrars, conservators and collections staff read it and have asked me to come run a seventy-five minute session on it in October. Sounds flattering until you realize the room: about thirty people whose entire working life is deciding whether the armature, the paint layer and the 1970s replacement panel in front of them are one object or three, and who will absolutely want to walk out with something they can put on an accession record. I want to teach them the real thing — the Special Composition Question as van Inwagen poses it, plus the handful of actual answers: anything-goes universalism, never-any nihilism, contact-or-fastening style restrictions, and his own view that the only composite material objects there are, are living things. I'm not going to pretend any of this has professional applications, and part of the plan has to be how I say that out loud in the first five minutes without losing them.

What I need is the teaching plan, not a summary of the view. Give me the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — my guess is you can't land the vagueness argument for universalism until they've stopped thinking composition is about things touching, but you tell me, and tell me why the dependencies run that way. Pick the one example the whole session should hang off and say what it has to have and what it has to lack: it needs to be something they already unreflectively believe in, it needs to survive being taken apart and its bits scattered around the room and then reassembled in a different order, and I suspect it shouldn't be an artifact with an obvious designer, or they'll just say "the artist's intention makes the difference" and we'll spend an hour on certificates of authenticity. Then map the misconceptions, including the ones this particular audience brings, and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each off. The big one I'm braced for: they will all think this is the ship of Theseus, and they'll be annoyed when I say it isn't, so I need a crisp way to show that persistence-through-time and what-composes-what-right-now are different questions — ideally with the same example doing double duty. Also expect "it's just a verbal dispute about the word chair," "nihilism means people don't exist," and somebody raising composition-as-identity, which I do not want to open but need one honest holding line for.

Near the end I want a single check — a case they haven't seen, a question to the room, whatever you think is sharpest — that would tell me whether the idea actually landed rather than just entertained them, and say what a good answer to it sounds like versus the answer that only sounds good. And tell me what to leave out entirely at this level: seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, no slides, and I'd rather cut something honestly then blur it. If there's a chunk of the debate that genuinely can't be conveyed to smart non-philosophers in that time, name it and say why, so I can flag the gap to the room instead of papering over it.
