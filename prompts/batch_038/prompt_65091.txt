I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the structure of justification by writing down my verdicts on cases, and they refuse to add up. I am not after a tour of the three standard camps — I know what they are called and roughly what they say. What I want is the principle my own verdicts are tracking, if there is one.

1. I believe I locked the front door because I remember locking it. Press me further and I have nothing. Justified.
2. I believe there is an even number of cars in the car park because it strikes me that there is. Press me further and I have nothing. Not justified — and the structure of my situation looks identical to case 1.
3. I believe there is a red patch in front of me because it looks red, and I have no present reason to distrust my eyes. Justified.
4. I believe a colleague is honest because everything else in my picture of her coheres with it; the picture has never been checked against anything outside itself. Not justified.
5. I believe the suspect is guilty because four separate strands — the timeline, the motive, the gap in the alibi, the witness — hang together, each making the others likelier, though none would carry it alone. Justified, and better justified than any single strand would leave me. I suspect this one may turn on the strands being independent of each other rather than on coherence as such, and I do not know how to say which.
6. A chain of reasons that runs on for ever without repeating, where I have only articulated the first few but am confident I could always produce another: better justified, I think, than a belief whose chain simply stops.
7. Yet a belief whose justification consists in an actually infinite chain of reasons, such that no one could ever have completed any finite fraction of it, is not justified at all.

Cases 6 and 7 are the pair I cannot reconcile. My own tentative principle is this: a belief is justified when every reason in its chain is itself better supported than the belief it is offered for, and the chain either terminates in a non-doxastic input of a type appropriate to the subject matter or runs on without end or repetition. That appears to handle 1 through 5, and then 6 and 7 both look like counterexamples to it.

So please commit rather than survey. State the single principle you would actually defend, clause by clause, naming the case above that motivated each clause; give me its verdicts on two or three cases I have not listed, including a proof I worked through years ago and can no longer reproduce but distinctly remember having checked; and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I ought to abandon them.
