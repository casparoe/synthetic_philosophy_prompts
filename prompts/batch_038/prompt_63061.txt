But then I tried extending it on my own and got somewhere I really don't want to be, and I can't tell whether the extension is broken or whether it's showing that his argument was broken all along.

Here's his argument, as near to how he runs it as I can manage:

(1) A commodity's value is fixed by the socially necessary labor time it takes to make it.
(2) Labor-power is a commodity like any other, so under competition it sells at its value, which is the labor time needed to keep the worker alive and trained and to replace them.
(3) Machinery and raw materials add no new value; they only carry forward the value they already contain. So every bit of new value in the product comes from living labor.
(4) What the capitalist buys is the use of labor-power for a stretch of time, and the use-value of labor-power happens to be the creation of value — a day's labor-power can add more value in use than it cost to reproduce.
(5) Therefore, in the cleanest capitalism you can describe — every exchange at exactly its value, no fraud, no monopoly, no state violence behind the contract, workers free to quit and free to shop their labor around to any of a hundred employers — the capitalist still ends the day holding new value that the worker produced and he didn't.
(6) Taking hold of the product of someone else's labor without returning an equivalent is exploitation, regardless of whether the taker is pleasant, legally entitled to it, or the giver a willing adult who prefers the deal to his alternatives.
(7) So capitalism is exploitative essentially, not contingently. Better wages, unions, or kinder managers change the size of the gap, not the structure that produces it; only ending the wage relation does.

And here's my extension, which is where I got stuck:

(8) Run (6) on Marx's own lower phase of communist society — the bit where he goes after the Gotha program for promising producers the undiminished proceeds of their labor. There, before anything is divided among individuals, the total social product has to cover replacement of used-up means of production, a fund for expanding production, reserves against disasters and dislocations, general administration, common needs like schools and clinics, and support for people who can't work. So even in that society the individual producer does not receive the full product of his own labor, and what gets deducted is decided collectively rather than by him.
(9) By (6), the collective takes the product of the producers' labor and returns no equivalent to them as individuals. So the workers' own society exploits the workers.
(10) Same move on saving. A worker who lives below his means, buys a machine, and thereafter lives partly off what the machine turns out while he sleeps has arranged for his past labor to command his present labor. On (6), he exploits himself.
(11) But (10) is not a thing anyone can mean, and a notion of exploitation that fires on any society that has ever saved, invested, or planned for next year isn't a criticism of capitalism — it's just a description of time. Which contradicts (7): if everything is exploitative, nothing is exploitative *specifically*, and the ideal-case result stops being a discovery about capitalism.

So. Which step do I reject, and is this a case where one premise is just false, or where there's a slide between steps that I'm not seeing?
