Twice a month about twelve of us meet in my dining room to argue about large questions — neighbours, two former colleagues, my sister — and I prepare a handout each time, because otherwise the same three people do all the talking. I am a retired bookkeeper with no formal training in any of this, but for eleven years I have kept a two-column ledger of what I read in the news: the left column for improvements (a disease eliminated, a prison closed, a war ended, a law repealed), and the right column for what each improvement cost or left behind (the languages that died out under the schools that taught reading, the valley flooded for the power line, the ten years a family lost to a conflict that produced no settlement anyone now honours). Next month's topic is whether history bends toward justice — the slogan you see on posters about the long arc of the moral universe — and my group is splitting in an unhelpful way. One half says look at the left column, the direction is obvious and proved; the other says look at the right column and at the last hundred years, and the whole idea is a comforting story the winners tell about themselves. Each side thinks the other is being dishonest with the evidence, and I have come to suspect they are not actually answering the same question. So please build me a decision tree that sorts a person into a named position on this, the way a good optometrist sorts you into a prescription: question by question, never once asking what they already think the label is.

Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of views. The distinctions I want it to turn on, roughly in this order, are: whether the pattern in my left column is a trend at all or a lucky streak; whether "better" is one thing that can be added up or several things that cannot be traded against one another; whether any of the improvement was bound to happen, or whether all of it could have failed to occur and can still be reversed; who or what is doing the bending — nobody, human beings, something internal to the structure of history, or something standing outside it; and what the right column means, which is the distinction my group has entirely failed to articulate, namely whether the suffering that preceded an improvement is somehow taken up and made to count in the result, or whether it is a permanent loss that nothing afterwards can cancel. Please make the last of these a real question with real answers, not a footnote. Use concrete situations rather than abstractions: for instance, a person in the year 1900 who can see falling infant mortality and rising literacy and can also see empires being carved up, and what they should have concluded by 1913; a country that built a welfare state and dismantled it inside a decade; a public health campaign that worked and was carried out by force; and a catastrophe that erased a generation's gains and left nothing standing behind it. Six to eight questions, each with two or three options, and no question may require knowing a philosopher's name or a technical term.

For the leaves I want at least these: a providential position, on which history is guided by a purposeful God; a position on which the direction is built into history's own structure and works through conflict and contradiction regardless of anyone's intentions; a position on which it depends on which class holds power; a cumulative optimism grounded in knowledge, where each generation inherits what the last one worked out; the middle answer I keep reaching for — improvement real, but neither automatic nor guaranteed, made and unmade by particular people (I have been calling it meliorism, though I may have the word wrong); a declinist answer on which the left column is a temporary borrowing; a sceptical answer on which the improvement is partly a gentler and more efficient form of control, so that the left column is not neutral evidence; and an answer on which the arc is the shape we impose when we look backwards at a sequence that had no shape while it was happening. Add any leaves you think I have missed. If a leaf is one nobody seriously defends, say so and mark it unnamed. Where two leaves cannot really be separated by my questions, flag them and tell me what further question would be needed. And please do not put anything in quotation marks and attribute it to a famous historian or preacher: I have no way of checking such things and my group has caught me out on that before.

Format the handout in these sections, in this order: "How to use this" (about 100 words, addressed to people who will be filling it in with a pencil at a kitchen table); "The questions" (the bulk of it, roughly 700 words, each question numbered with its options and one line after each option saying what that answer registers); "The leaves" (about 450 words, each position named, with two or three sentences covering what it claims, what it does with the right-hand column of my ledger, and its most awkward commitment); "Empty rooms" (about 200 words, the combinations of answers that no position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether the gap exists because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored); "Blurry borders" (about 150 words, the pairs the tree cannot separate); and finally "Scoring key" (about 150 words, mapping patterns of answers onto leaves, including what to make of someone whose answers come out mixed — which, I expect, will be most of my group).
