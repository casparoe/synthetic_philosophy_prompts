I'm a junior, and since my school doesn't offer philosophy I've been working through Dennett's Consciousness Explained on my own and running a Sunday reading group with three friends; last year I built a color-phi demo out of two LEDs and a microcontroller for our regional science fair, which is how I got into this. Here is the setup I want to reason about, stated in my own terms so we aren't at the mercy of how different textbooks describe it. Two small lights sit a few degrees apart on a dark screen, a red one on the left and a green one on the right. The red one flashes for about twenty milliseconds; roughly one hundred to one hundred fifty milliseconds later the green one flashes for the same duration. Subjects reliably report that they did not see two discrete flashes but one dot that traveled from the left position to the right position and abruptly changed from red to green somewhere near the middle of its path. The difficulty is that at the moment the dot would have had to change color, the second flash has not yet occurred, so nothing out there in the world carries the information that the color is about to be green. As I understand it, Dennett and Kinsbourne say there are two ways to fill this gap, labeled Orwellian and Stalinesque. On the Stalinesque version the brain holds back and does not let the first flash into experience until the second one arrives, then stages a single already-resolved sequence — motion plus mid-path color change — which is literally what the subject undergoes at the time. On the Orwellian version the subject genuinely undergoes a red flash, or two discrete flashes, as it happens, and only afterward is the record rewritten in memory, so the report of a mid-path color change is a confabulated memory of something never experienced. Dennett's claim, as I read him, is that no experiment can separate the two, because they agree about everything going on in the brain and about everything the subject could ever say or do, and differ only about whether one location in the processing stream counts as pre-experiential or post-experiential. That is the claim our group will spend the next month on, and I want to test it.

So: design me a thought experiment — an experimental scenario, worked out concretely enough that I could put it in a science-fair abstract — that does separate the two models, or else demonstrate to me that no such scenario exists. Constraints: nothing beyond what an undergraduate psychology lab has (two lights, a screen, buttons, millisecond-accurate timing, ordinary volunteers); no brain imaging and no implants, since those would be doing the work for you rather than the design doing it; the trial sequence spelled out with actual timings; at least one control condition that rules out the boring explanation, which is that the subject is simply inferring or guessing what they must have seen; and — this is the one I care about most — the discriminating data may not be a free verbal report collected after the trial ends, because a later report is precisely what Orwellian rewriting could contaminate. I have sketched two ideas of my own and I suspect at least one is broken for a reason I can't articulate: first, having the subject press a button the instant they see the color change so that the motor response is time-locked to the experience; second, inserting a demanding counting task immediately after the green flash to interfere with any memory rewriting. Please diagnose both of these before you give me yours, and tell me whether your design depends on either. I know what cheating looks like here: a case whose predictions differ only because experience has been quietly defined in a way that forces the difference, or a control condition that doesn't actually control anything.

After the scenario, walk through each model's prediction step by step, saying where in the trial the mistaken content enters on each version and what the data would look like on each. Then identify the single feature of the design that is doing the discriminating work, say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and give the strongest version of the attack that a defender of the model coming out worse would launch against your design — not a strawman. Finally, please be committed rather than even-handed: take a definite position on whether the Stalinesque-Orwellian distinction is empirically testable in principle, give me your reasons, and resist the temptation to lay out the options and let me choose. If your verdict is that Dennett is right, say so plainly and explain exactly why my two ideas fail.
