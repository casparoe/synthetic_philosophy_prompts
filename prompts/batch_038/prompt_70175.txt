I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether physics has refuted Leibniz's principle that no two things can be exactly alike. I'd like you to write the argumentative core of it. Please write as a working metaphysician who has thought hard about haecceitism, bundle theories of substance, and structuralist reactions to perfectly symmetric universes.

Here is the case I want the essay to turn on, described so that a reader who never heard of Max Black can follow it. There is a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of the same diameter, temperature, and composition, in otherwise empty space. Distance relations are the only ones available; there is no absolute space assigning either sphere a place of its own; and no light or other signal has ever passed between them, so neither could in principle be observed or affected by the other. Now a twist of my own: a century after the universe begins, one sphere spontaneously acquires a hairline crack, and nothing in the prior state determines which of them it is.

Adjudicate this: before the crack, was there one sphere described twice, or genuinely two? Give the strongest case for each answer — including the view that the question is defective, since a difference no possible observation could reveal is no difference at all, and the view that the spheres are numerically distinct in virtue of something no description captures — and then defend a verdict. Address explicitly whether the crack settles the earlier question retroactively, what the unaccounted-for choice of *which* sphere cracks costs each side, and whether your verdict changes if the crack was there from the start. A qualified conclusion is acceptable if you say which qualification is doing the work; fence-sitting is not.

Begin with a few sentences of open brainstorming about the possible verdicts before you commit, and throughout, separate what is actually settled among philosophers who work on this from what remains live dispute — I don't want a contested position dressed up as consensus. No bibliography; I only want the reasoning.
