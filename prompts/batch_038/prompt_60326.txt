I have been covering science and research policy for a national magazine for the better part of two decades, and I am writing to you because this is not a question that our newsroom handbook can settle, so I would like it treated as a matter of principle rather than house style. In 2016 I spent several months reporting a story about a small research group whose signature result had been cited hundreds of times. The central account came from a retired laboratory manager — I will call him R — who had kept his own notebooks across four decades of other people's experiments and who believed the headline figure had been assembled from runs chosen after the fact. R was not an author on any of the papers and could name no professional stake, only a conviction that the record was wrong. He agreed to talk to me on one condition, which he stated twice in plain words and which I accepted in plain words: his name would never appear, and nothing I published would let former colleagues work out who he had been. We attached no time limit and identified no circumstances under which the arrangement would lapse, and I do not think either of us imagined any. The story died for reasons of my own — I could not corroborate the notebooks independently, and my editor would not run it on a single anonymous account — after which R and I exchanged perhaps four emails a year, mostly about his garden.

He died fourteen months ago at eighty-four, and his local paper ran an obituary giving his full name, his job title, and his years of service. Since then the ground has shifted. The journal retracted the original paper last month, though on an entirely different basis, and the episode is being re-reported everywhere; a professional society is midway through establishing a named lecture in honor of the group's principal investigator. My editor has asked, reasonably enough, whether my old material is now usable, given that the man it protected is dead and named in print regardless. Then last week R's daughter — whom I met exactly once, in her father's kitchen, some ten years ago — emailed to say that her father would want the account told now, that the family regards the matter as closed, and that I should consider myself released from whatever I promised. In the same message she notes that her brother disputes this, and that in his final months R told at least one visitor something close to the opposite: that he regretted ever speaking to a journalist. So the people with the best claim to speak for him contradict one another, and I have no way to establish what he actually wanted. There is a third complication I have been slow to face. The notebooks identify a technician who still works at that institute, who was twenty-nine at the time, who never agreed to anything, and who would be recognizable from the details of the work even with R's name in the obituary pages. He has a family and, so far as I know, no interest whatsoever in being folded into a decade-old scandal.

I want to know what I am obliged to do, and I want the reasoning rather than a verdict. A colleague who covers the courts told me over lunch that I am making a category mistake: on her account the promise was never really a private transaction between R and me, but my participation in a practice that reporters and sources run together, so the live question is not what R would now want but what sources in general can expect from people like me. I find that half-persuasive and half-evasive and cannot tell which half. The instinctive answers both fail on inspection — that a dead man's confidence lapses with him, and that a promise given without qualification binds forever and admits of no release — and I suspect the truth is that the assurance I gave R was never one thing but at least two, only one of which his death could conceivably touch. So: does the obligation survive him, and if so, in whose hands does it now sit? Can anyone release me, and does the daughter's word carry weight when her brother's contradicts it and neither of them was party to the exchange? Is the assurance R extracted from me the same assurance the still-employed technician is relying on, when I never gave him one? And concretely, what should I do with the notebooks before my editor's deadline at the end of the month — publish, publish in part, hold them, destroy them, or seek out a man I have not spoken to in years? Please argue the case through rather than sending me to authorities; I am trying to decide something, not annotate it.
