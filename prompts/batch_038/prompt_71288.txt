I am in the final year of an undergraduate philosophy degree, currently taking a course on probability and paradoxes in which we have spent three weeks on the Doomsday argument — Carter's original formulation, Leslie's development of it, and Gott's delta-t version — alongside Sleeping Beauty and the fine-tuning debate. In the autumn I am due to join a small research group whose theme is self-locating belief: what one ought to believe when one's evidence includes indexical facts such as *this* observer-moment, *this* birth rank, *this* century. My prospective supervisor has asked each incoming student to submit a short statement identifying which open problems in the area the group ought to be working on over the coming decade. She has been explicit that she wants a defended ordering rather than a survey, and a committed position rather than a menu of options for her to choose between. I would like you to produce that statement — in my voice, but with your own convictions in it.

My own working diagnosis, which you should pressure-test and override wherever you think it mistaken, is that the field's headline dispute — roughly, that between the self-sampling and the self-indication assumptions — is downstream of a question that has not been settled: whether a fact such as "I am the n-th observer to have existed" is the kind of thing that can enter an ordinary conditionalization step at all, or whether indexical evidence requires a distinct updating rule. If the latter, I suspect that a good deal of the anthropic literature is a verbal disagreement in something like the sense Chalmers characterises, where two frameworks are each coherent and the argument is about which to adopt rather than about what is true. I also suspect this general question is more tractable than the Doomsday argument taken on its own, because it can be pressed through Sleeping Beauty, the presumptuous philosopher, the shooting-room paradox, and the long-running dispute over whether inferring a multiverse from the fine-tuning of this universe commits the inverse gambler's fallacy — cases in which progress has been made by argument and modelling alone, without any cosmological data.

Please give me your ranking of the open questions in this area, ordered by how much of the group's effort over the next decade each should absorb, and state the criteria you are weighing against one another: importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many further questions turn on the answer. For each question on your list I want four things — what precisely is unresolved, put so that someone who knows the Doomsday argument but not the technical literature can see what would count as settling it; which other questions it is upstream or downstream of, and why; whether it is addressable by argument and formal modelling or blocked on empirical facts we lack; and what would move it up or down the ordering within the next few years, whether a distinction, a counterexample, a result, or a shift in what cosmologists are prepared to say.

Be actively dismissive where you think that is warranted. Certain recurrent lines of work strike me as poor value: the proliferation of further named anthropic assumptions, unending argument over which reference class is the correct one, and attempts to convert the Doomsday argument into recommendations about existential-risk spending. Tell me which of these, or which others, you would deprioritise and why, and whether dismissing them is justified or is merely impatience with problems that look intractable. Take a definite position throughout and give your reasons; where you disagree with the diagnosis above, say so directly and defend the ordering you would put in its place.
