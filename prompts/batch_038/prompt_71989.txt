I've been reading Nāgārjuna — emptiness, svabhava, the tetralemma — and I tried to write down the standard self-refutation objection to it myself, because it looks exactly like the self-application bugs I already believe in: Russell's paradox, Type:Type being inconsistent, Tarski on a language not being able to define its own truth predicate. Here's my version.

1. Any thesis that is about everything applies to itself. Nothing gets to exempt itself from its own scope.
2. The thesis that everything is empty — that nothing has intrinsic nature and everything exists only dependently — is about everything.
3. So that thesis, and emptiness itself, are empty: no intrinsic nature, fixed entirely by their conditions.
4. Something with no intrinsic nature of its own settles nothing on its own account; whatever content it has is borrowed from the context that supports it.
5. A claim whose content is entirely borrowed doesn't rule anything out — the world going the other way is just as compatible with it.
6. So the thesis that everything is empty rules out nothing, and therefore cannot exclude the realist who says some things do have intrinsic nature.
7. So Madhyamaka either exempts its own thesis, which makes it false by 1 and 2, or the thesis has no content, which makes it not a thesis. Either way it isn't the position it presents itself as.

I've read that Nāgārjuna saw this coming and basically says yes, my own statement is empty too, and that's fine because an empty statement can still do work at the conventional level. But that looks to me like just restating 3 — it doesn't touch 4 and 5, which is where the damage is.

And here's why I dont trust my own arguement: run it on things I'm not willing to give up. The structuralist reading of physics, where there are no intrinsic properties at all and everything is relations holding between relations — that self-applies in exactly the same shape, and plenty of people hold it without embarrassment. Meaning is use: does that slogan's own meaning consist in its use, and if so is it vacuous? All models are wrong but some are useful — which is a model of modelling. And my own working assumption, which I'd defend in any code review: there is no bottom layer, every level of the stack is a convention resting on another convention, all the way down into physics, which is not bedrock either. If my chain refutes those, it isn't a weapon, it's a landmine I'm already standing on.

One smaller thing that may or may not belong in the argument: when Nāgārjuna runs the four corners over nirvāṇa and comes back denying all four, my instinct as a PL person is to reach for a four-valued semantics — true, false, both, neither. But then the claim that some things get the neither value has to itself get one of the four, and I can't tell if that's a problem or harmless.

So: which numbered step is doing the dirty work? I want a verdict, not a menu of possible readings. Tell me the one you'd cut, why the steps around it survive, and what cutting it costs — including whether Nāgārjuna gets to keep 1 and 2 for free or has to pay for those too. If you think the chain is valid as written and one of the premises just has to go, say which one and make the case for it.
