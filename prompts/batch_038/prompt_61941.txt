I have no academic training in philosophy or psychology — I read on my own, unevenly, and mostly at night — and for some months I have been circling a pattern I cannot find a word for. I would like to know whether one exists.

What started it: I read about a condition, I believe usually called pain asymbolia, in which people who have sustained certain kinds of brain injury will tell you that they do feel the pain — they can say where it is and how strong it is — and yet they do not mind it, will take no steps to make it stop, and by some accounts smile or laugh while it is being inflicted. What struck me was not the pain case as such. It was that I kept recognising the same structure elsewhere, in situations with nothing to do with brain injury:

- A man who has been on the same psychiatric medication for years and says the feelings are all still there and still aimed at the right things — he can tell you precisely why a piece of news is dreadful, and he means it — but nothing has any weight now. His own phrase is that it is like watching the same film with the sound off.
- A woman I know who lost a child. Nine years on she recounts the loss in full detail, the sadness in the recounting is plainly real, it arrives reliably every year on the same date, and it costs her nothing: no plans change, and the conversation afterwards is no different from a conversation about the weather.
- A man who acknowledges, without a trace of defensiveness, that what he did to someone was cruel. He assents to every sentence you put to him about it. The acknowledgement makes no difference to him whatsoever. He is not defying anything; it simply gets no purchase.

I suspect the words people reach for — apathy, anhedonia, numbness, indifference, emotional blunting, dissociation — all miss it, because in every case above the person is not blank. The state is present, correctly directed at whatever warrants it, and accurately reported. What has gone is its claim on the person: its capacity to cost them something or to move them. And I take it that alexithymia is close to the opposite, since there the trouble is that people cannot identify or describe what they feel, whereas here the description is impeccable.

So, concretely:

1. Is there an established concept — in philosophy of mind, psychology, psychiatry, or anywhere else — for this structure: a state that retains its content and its felt character while losing its grip?
2. If there are several near neighbours rather than one, tell me what each covers, where each falls short of the three cases above, and which comes closest.
3. I want the edges, not just a label. Does the concept require that the state still feel like something, or does it also cover the person who says he knows he ought to feel grief and simply does not? Does it cover a state that has lost its hold on action but not on judgement, and one that has lost both? Is it a property of a single episode or of a person across time? And what would count as a case that looks like this from the outside but is not an instance — someone who is moved and hiding it, someone who feels it fully and has decided not to act, someone in whom the state is genuinely absent?
4. If no established name exists, coin one, define it so that it comes apart cleanly from apathy and from numbness, and give two or three borderline cases that test the definition.

I am not after sources or a reading list; I could not chase them down and I would rather not be handed titles I have to take on trust. What I want is the distinctions, and, if they have to be made, the terms.

One further point, which I raise only because I think it bears on where the concept should be drawn. If cases like these are genuinely possible, does that suggest that the grip a feeling has on us is a further ingredient alongside the feeling itself — so that the feeling alone is not yet a hurt, or an offence, or a grief — or does it suggest that the feeling is wholly intact and only the person's responsiveness to it has been damaged? Say which of those readings the pattern better supports, and how that changes where you would place the boundary.
