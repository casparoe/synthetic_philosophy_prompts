I'm a third-year student double-majoring in philosophy and psychology, and this term I'm taking a seminar on explanation in the behavioral sciences. Outside class I coordinate a tutoring scheme our department runs at a nearby elementary school, and last week's steering meeting got tangled in a way I have not been able to untangle on my own.

The school's literacy lead had read a newspaper summary of a twin study reporting that something like seventy percent of the differences between children in reading ability trace back to differences in their genes. One representative of our funder took this to mean the tutoring is money down the drain: if the variation is mostly genetic, he argued, then extra reading practice cannot shift where a child ends up, and the budget would do more good spent on classroom books and a breakfast program. A second representative objected that the students our tutors work with have gained more this year than comparable students elsewhere in the district — to which the first replied that this only proves we recruit the children whose families are already pushing them, so those gains were coming anyway. Worth noting: the study he was quoting was carried out on twins in another country, in schools considerably better resourced than ours.

There is also a third item on the agenda. A company is offering the district a saliva test that returns, for each child, a single score intended to predict how well they will read at age eleven, and the proposal is to use it to allocate the one-to-one tutoring, on the grounds that it would surface children nobody else has noticed.

I want a settled judgement here, not a menu of considerations to weigh for myself. Which representative is closer to right, and exactly where does the other's reasoning fail — is it a misreading of what that seventy-percent figure reports, an invalid inference from it to what the tutoring can achieve, or both? And should the district buy the test? Commit to positions on each and give your reasons; if some part of the case is genuinely undetermined by the evidence, identify that part specifically rather than hedging across the board.
