Write me a short story, somewhere around 1200-1800 words, built out of the four-rung ladder of minds — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian tiers, the scheme where each rung adds a new way of getting information into the design: genes and natural selection, then conditioning, then letting your guesses die instead of you, then picking up tools of thought from outside your own skull.

Here's the wrinkle I want the story to hang on. It isn't part of the standard picture, so you'll have to build it yourself: a creature — a person is easiest, though an animal or a constructed thing works — that tests as fully Gregorian. Reads, writes, uses notation, argues, inherits and extends other people's ideas, is obviously swimming in scaffolding. But the rung underneath is missing or has been switched off. No offline rehearsal. It can't run a guess forward in its head and watch it go wrong; every prediction it makes has to be made out loud, in the world, where the cost of being wrong is real and can't be un-paid. Show how it got this way, but don't explain it to death — I want to see what it's actually like from the inside, or at least from uncomfortably close.

Then the question the story should turn on: which direction does the ladder run for this thing? Everyone assumes the rungs stack, that you can't hold the top one without the one under it, because symbols only mean anything to a mind that can already simulate with them. My creature breaks that, and I want the break to be dramatized rather than announced. Maybe it's leased the simulation out — to a machine, to a partner, to a written procedure it follows faithfully — and the interesting horror, or the interesting liberation, is that the dying-in-its-stead is now happening somewhere else entirely, on somebody else's time and body.

Some ground rules. No lecture. No scene where two experts conveniently spell out the taxonomy for the reader. No ending that states the moral. Put it in one concrete situation with a stake — a hearing, an examination, an accident, a hiring decision — where whether this creature counts as one of us has to be settled by people who don't have the vocabulary to settle it. First person is fine. I care way more about the phenomonology than the argument: if you can make me feel what it's like to have no inner rehearsal room, you've done the thing I'm after.
