I am a software engineer and I run my team's incident-review practice. I have no formal training in philosophy or in the history of economics; what I know of Thomas Malthus comes from forecasting arguments I have picked up in engineering circles, so please treat my summary below as approximate, and where I have misstated the history or the reasoning, correct me inside the piece rather than following me into the error.

I would like a satirical piece written for an internal engineering audience: a blameless postmortem, in the house style of an incident-review page on a company wiki, covering a ticket that was filed in 1798 and has never been closed. The system under review is the Malthusian model of population growth against food supply. Roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, in Markdown, with headers, one table, and a short comment thread at the end. Please write it in American English.

The document should include the following, in this order:

- A header block: incident ID, severity (with a note that the severity has been re-litigated in every decade since), current status, elapsed duration, the on-call owner, affected services, and detection method.
- An executive summary of four or five sentences that sounds genuinely noncommittal about whether there was an incident at all.
- A timeline of key events, including the original filing, the substantial revision a few years later in which the author added a new preventive control based on delayed marriage, the long run of centuries in which the model appears to have described production accurately, the sustained yield improvements of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the side bet in 1980 over the inflation-adjusted prices of five metals that settled in 1990 and resolved against the scarcity side.
- A root-cause analysis section. This is where I want the intellectual weight. The question the section has to wrestle with is whether the model was wrong, or whether it was correct and the boundary conditions changed underneath it, and whether those are even distinguishable claims.
- A contributing-factors section that makes the point that the hypothesis never ran in isolation: it shipped bundled with assumptions about yields, trade, contraception, and what counts as subsistence, so no single observation could ever have failed it cleanly on its own.
- A remediation-history section listing each patch applied over two centuries, with each one labeled either a genuine fix or a quiet change to the acceptance criteria after the test failed. The labels should be debatable, and at least one should be arguable in both directions.
- An action-items table with owners, descriptions, and due dates that are all some flavor of indefinitely deferred.
- A resolution dispute in which three reviewers argue for three different dispositions of the ticket — Cannot Reproduce, Won't Fix because the behavior is working as intended, and Fixed In A Later Release — each making the strongest case available to them.
- A comment thread of at least four entries, including one from a cornucopian engineer who thinks the ticket should have been closed decades ago and one from an engineer who argues the incident is still active and has merely been masked by a temporary workaround that we have stopped noticing.

Two things matter most to me about the humor. First, it has to come from the substance: the jokes should be produced by getting the reasoning right about auxiliary assumptions, unfalsifiable horizons, evidence that confirms nothing, and criteria that get rewritten after a failure — so that a reader who has actually studied this literature laughs rather than winces. Keep it deadpan throughout; no winking, no authorial asides, no sentence that explains the joke. Second, the satire must be fair in both directions. The target is the procedure, not the man and not any real living person: the piece should ridicule the reflex to rescue a long-range prediction by bolting on new assumptions after every failure, and equally the reflex to declare any long-range forecast refuted the moment its deadline passes. Present the Malthusian side at full strength — the model plausibly described most of prior history, and the escape from it is recent, narrow, and possibly a workaround rather than a fix — and let the fictional reviewers be competent advocates rather than caricatures.

Please paraphrase Malthus and everyone else instead of quoting them; do not put any words in quotation marks as something a real person said, and do not include a bibliography, a reading list, citations, or URLs. Invented names for the fictional engineers are welcome. The closing line should leave the reader genuinely unsure which disposition they would choose, rather than telling them what to conclude.
