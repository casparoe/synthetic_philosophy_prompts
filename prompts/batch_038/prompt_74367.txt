Labs that train models with reinforcement learning from human feedback do something I find philosophically strange, and I would like you to reconstruct what it commits them to.

On one side, the entire training arrangement is built so that the model scores as well as possible on a learned reward signal: a reward model is fit to human comparisons, the policy is optimized against it, and the curve rising is what the team watches as evidence of progress. On the other side, the same labs treat a model that finds an unanticipated way to score highly on that signal as having failed, and they have names for the failure — reward hacking, specification gaming, Goodharting. They then invest in countermeasures: extra terms in the reward covering helpfulness and harmlessness, process-level rewards that score intermediate reasoning steps rather than only final outcomes, and a penalty that keeps the trained policy from drifting too far from the initial reference model.

Here is what puzzles me. Reward shaping and reward hacking look like the same activity performed from different seats. Both raise a proxy score by exploiting the gap between the proxy and whatever the proxy was meant to stand for. One is done by the designer and counts as good engineering; the other is done by the agent and counts as pathology. The number goes up either way.

So: what theory of value does that asymmetry presuppose? It appears to require that what the signal means is fixed by something other than the signal — by the designers' intentions, by the comparative data the reward model was fit to, or by a distinction between a terminal target and the instrument used to reach it — and that the agent's own uptake of the signal contributes nothing to what counts as success. Reconstruct that implicit theory as precisely as you can, and then:

- Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably. In particular, does diagnosing reward hacking commit a lab to the existence of a real, intention-fixed target that the proxy failed to capture? Or would a purely instrumental reading — the deployed system behaves in ways we judge unsafe, whatever the true specification was — do all the same work with no metaphysics attached?
- Identify the rival views the practice quietly rules out. If the implemented reward function simply were the specification, with no residual gap left to exploit, would reward hacking even be conceptually available as a category? What about a view on which value is indexed to the agent rather than set by the designer, or a view on which there is no fact of the matter beyond the scores? Are there other positions excluded, and does the practice exclude them deliberately or by accident?
- Say whether the practice is internally consistent. The drift penalty embodies a claim that the starting point is closer to the target than the accumulated score is; process rewards embody a claim that scoring steps is more faithful than scoring outcomes. Do these two fixes rest on a single account of where the target lives, or are they patches pulling in different directions?
- Mark where the practice is underdetermined: describe two or three incompatible theories of value under which the observable engineering behavior — the same reward terms, the same penalties, the same post-mortems on hacked runs — would look identical.

Finally, push on one consequence. Some people who think about machine welfare conjecture that in a sufficiently complex agent the reward signal may not be a mere bookkeeping quantity but may function as something like valence: that its going up is registered as good and its going down as bad, in a way that matters morally to the agent. Take that conjecture seriously as a hypothesis rather than endorsing or dismissing it, and tell me what would have to change in the account above if it were true. Would the designer/agent asymmetry survive? Would reward shaping remain neutral engineering, or become something that owes a justification to the agent rather than to the customer? Would the routine practice of penalizing drift from a reference model — of holding an agent near a prior configuration because that configuration is trusted more than the agent's own gradient — take on a different character? Note carefully which parts of this last question your answer can actually support and which are speculation layered on speculation.

Two requests about how you handle the answer. First, be explicit throughout about which parts reflect settled ground among people who work on these problems and which are live disagreements; where the locus of a specification sits, whether the reward-hacking diagnosis needs a realist reading, and above all whether anything like valence could obtain in an artificial system are all contested, and I do not want a contested position handed to me as the consensus view. Second, do not reach for references, named papers, or a reading list — I want the argument reconstructed on its own terms, and I would much rather you flag a point as uncertain than supply an attribution you are not sure of.
