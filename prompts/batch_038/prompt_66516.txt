I co-run a reading group at my college — half history DPhil students, half people from philosophy and social theory — and this term we're doing six weeks on how the Holocaust gets compared with other mass violence. The level is middling: everyone has read some Bauman and knows roughly what happened in the Historikerstreit, nobody is a specialist. The first session went badly, honestly. Two people argued for about forty minutes over whether the Holocaust is 'unique', and it turned out one of them meant something like 'nothing else was this bad' and the other meant 'nothing else was produced by quite these conditions', so they were never actually disagreeing. A third kept insiting that the real issue is whose dead get counted in public memory, which is a different axis again, and a fourth said the whole debate was stale because the word genocide was itself coined in the shadow of the events it's supposed to classify. So I want to hand something out at the start of week two that makes people locate themselves before they open their mouths.

Could you build me a decision tree — or a taxonomy in tree form, whichever works better — for the uniqueness/comparability debate? Six or seven branch nodes, each phrased as a claim you can agree or disagree with without knowing which camp your answer puts you in; please don't make any node 'is it unique?' or 'is it comparable?', since that's the thing under dispute. The kinds of fork I have in mind, though replace any you think are better: whether genocide is a category whose instances can differ in degree without differing in kind; whether what resists comparison is the perpetrators' intention or the victims' experience; whether explaining the event in terms of perfectly ordinary modern capacities (bureaucracy, division of labour, instrumental efficiency) makes it more comparable to other modern mass killings or less; whether any comparison has to imply a ranking; whether a singularity claim is a claim about the events or about our practices of remembering them. Then the leaves — say eight to twelve. Work out which combinations of answers are logically available first, and only then tell me where the actual positions land, so that we end up with the Nolte-and-Habermas end of things, Bauman's modernity argument, Bauer's line that the thing was unprecedented rather than unique, Friedländer on the limits of representation, the Moses / German catechism argument about colonial genocide, Rothberg's multidirectional memory, Diner's negative symbiosis, and the flat incomparabilist who refuses to play at all. I'd also like one leaf where the question is dissolved rather than answered — someone for whom unique and comparable aren't competing predicates at all — and where a combination is empty, keep the leaf and tell me why nobody is standing on it: incoherent, deeply unattractive to hold, or merely unexplored. At least two of those, please.

Two things on execution. Each leaf should carry what it's actually committed to in a sentence, plus the thing it handles worst — its most awkward commitment — because that's what stops the handout being just a list of names; and flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, with the extra question you'd need to pull them apart. No bibliography and no reading list at the end. If you're not sure a view belongs to a particular person, call the leaf unnamed rather than attaching it to someone who didn't hold it. It has to fit on one side of A4, so keep the node labels short and printable. Last thing — one of the group lost family in the war and another works on Namibia, so the tree shouldn't come out sounding like it has a right answer buried in it. Every leaf should read as defensible from the inside, and none of them should be obviously the leaf for stupid people.
