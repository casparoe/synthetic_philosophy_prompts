trying to pin down an arguement for a paper and I want to check whether I've just talked myself out of the whole topic.

so Winner's famous case is the Long Island parkways — the story being that Moses had the overpasses built low enough that the city buses carrying poor and Black beachgoers out to Jones Beach couldn't get under them. That's supposed to show that artifacts have politics. But doesn't it show the opposite? Nothing political happens at the bridge. A powerful guy makes a decision and then has it poured into concrete so it keeps running after he's stopped standing there. The concrete is just a very durable instrument, like a will or a padlock on a gate. Nobody says the padlock has politics; it's doing what the owner wanted, minus the owner having to be present. So "artifacts have politics" looks like it collapses into "powerful people leave durable traces," which literally nobody disputes.

and the second move — some technologies counting as inherently political becuase they require a certain kind of social setup, reactors needing centralized command and control while solar is compatible with decentralized stuff — seems even thinner. A reactor has engineering requirements: cooling, grid stability, licensed operators, enormous capital. The politics is in how we choose to organize around those requirements, not in the requirements themselves.

I also keep seeing him described as anti-determinist and I can't see what's left to be anti-determinist about. If all the politics sits upstream with the designer, he isn't saying technology shapes society — he's saying society shapes the technology and then the technology runs errands for it.

so what am I missing here? Is there any content in the claim that survives my padlock reductio, and if so which part of it does the surviving — or should I just write the paper arguing it's a slogan?
