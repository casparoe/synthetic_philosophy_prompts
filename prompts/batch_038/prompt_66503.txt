I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of language — my actual dissertation is on indexicals and context-dependence, not on slurs, but I keep getting dragged into the slur literature and I've realized my own intuitions about the cases are a mess. I'd like to know what principle, if any, is behind them.

Here are my verdicts, roughly in the order I noticed them. I'll just write "the slur" — assume one of the paradigm racial ones, targeting Black people, and assume the speaker knows exactly what it means.

1. A historian's book quotes a 1920s letter verbatim, slur and all, in quotation marks, with a footnote condemning the writer. Totally fine, I wouldn't blink.
2. The same historian paraphrases the letter and writes the word unquoted, still clearly reporting, still condemning. Fine too, but noticeably worse — a reviewer might flag it, and rightly.
3. A novel set in the period has a bigoted character say it in dialogue. Fine.
4. The narrator of that same novel writes it in free indirect narration, not attributable to any character's attitudes. Now I think something has gone wrong, and I can't say what.
5. An actor plays the bigot and says it on stage, every night, to an audience that knows he's acting. Fine.
6. The same actor says it at the cast party, as a joke, to a co-star who's in on it and laughing. Not fine. And I dont know why — nobody's harmed, consent is explicit, no target.
7. A member of the target group uses it affectionately toward a friend from the same group. Fine.
8. Same person, same use, but a bigot overhears and takes it as license to use it himself. Still fine — I don't think the speaker has to police the overhearer. But 6 and 8 feel like they should have swapped verdicts, and they haven't.
9. A seminar where everyone present is in the target group, discussing the word, and one of them says it out loud to make a point. Fine.
10. Same seminar, an out-group philosopher (say, white) says it out loud to make exactly the same point, with everyone's explicit permission. Not fine. Or maybe: permissible but shouldn't. That distinction might just be me flinching.
11. "If that slur turns up at the door again, don't let him in." The word sits inside the antecedent of a conditional, so on the standard expressive story nothing is asserted, no attitude is expressed, there's no target at all. And yet this is the worst item on the whole list for me. Worse than 2, worse than 6.
12. "He's not a slur, he's just an idiot" — speaker intends to pry the word away from its usual target. Fine? I genuinely don't know, I keep flipping.

My half-baked working principle is something like: production of the word is bad when it makes the derogatory attitude more available to be adopted — normalizes it, licenses it, spreads it downstream. But that predicts 11 is fine (no expressive work being done, nobody licensed to think anything), predicts 6 and 10 are fine (explicit consent, in-group or sympathetic audience, no uptake), and predicts 8 is *not* fine (the bigot did in fact pick up license). It gets basically every case I care most about backwards, which is why I'm asking.

So: is there a principle that gets most of these? I want it stated precisely enough that I can see what work each clause is doing and which case motivated that clause, and I want you to say outright which of my verdicts a good principle would have to overturn — for what it's worth I'd rather lose 2 and 12 than lose 11. Then run whatever you land on against two cases I have no verdict for: a museum wall label that reproduces the word because the exhibit is about what the word does, and a translation of an older novel where the translator has to pick something and every available option misrepresents the original's force. And tell me whether the result is one principle or a stack of them with a priority ordering, because I suspect it's a stack and I'd like to know what the ordering is and why the higher-ranked clause wins when they conflict.

Also: flag where you're least sure of yourself. If someone argued that 11 and 4 are the same phenomenon and my split verdict is just squeamishness dressed up as data, would you go along with that? What argument or piece of evidence would make you abandon the principle you've just built, and which step in your reasoning is the most likely place for it to break?
