I'm a junior and I run the quantum physics club at my school — there's only seven of us and we meet in Mr. Okonkwo's room during lunch, mostly arguing. This semester we fell down the many-worlds rabbit hole and now two of our members are basically at war about the Born rule. Priya (senior, did a summer program in physics) is a committed Everettian: no collapse, the wave function just keeps evolving, every outcome is realized somewhere in the branching structure, and she insists the squared-amplitude probabilities aren't just tacked on — she says there's a real derivation from decision theory (the Deutsch/Wallace line) plus a separate version that goes through being uncertain which branch you're on after the measurement but before you look. Marcus (also a senior, way more skeptical) says that's a dodge dressed up in math. His whole thing is: if everything happens, what work is left for "probability" to do, and any argument that lands you exactly the Born rule must have smuggled it in as an assumption. He loves the branch-counting point — you can always bolt on an extra measurement so one outcome splits into a hundred sub-branches while the other stays one, which is supposed to show counting branches is meaningless. But Marcus says that cuts both ways: if the count is arbitrary, then the "weight" the Everettian substitutes for it is arbitrary too, and weight is just a number attached to the wave function with no story about why it should behave like chance.

Could you write the two of them as an actual dialogue, not an explainer with speaker labels? Set it in the club room after everyone else has gone, with a whiteboard, and give them one concrete case to fight over: an atom prepared so the spin-up amplitude is sqrt(0.7) and spin-down is sqrt(0.3), and a $20 bet on which they'll see. I want them to work through at least these, in whatever order feels natural: (1) what "probability" could even mean when both outcomes definitely occur, and whether it's just guidance for betting; (2) whether the decision-theory derivation is circular — specifically the step that says only the amplitudes matter and not how the branches are arranged, since that looks like it already contains the answer; (3) Marcus's fine-graining attack, which I'd like done concretely: he proposes running a hundred little ancilla measurements that carry zero information about the spin and pay the same in each sub-branch, so nothing about the bet changes — and he wants Priya to explain what her derivation is actually keying on if it isn't a count; (4) the objection, which I think traces back to Albert and Loewer but I'm fuzzy on it, that a rational Everettian should care about all her future copies equally rather than in proportion to their weights, and what goes wrong if she does (does that fall apart when you fine-grain too?); and (5) whether the "I don't know which copy I'll be" move rescues anything, or whether it just relocates the problem.

Both of them need to be genuinely sharp — no strawmen, and please don't let either one win just by asserting something. They should actually answer each other's points instead of delivering parallel speeches, so if Marcus makes the fine-graining move, Priya has to grapple with it and vice versa; a couple of moments where one of them concedes a smaller point to save a bigger one would be great. End it somewhere definite: either a real concession, or a clean statement of exactly where they now think the disagreement sits, ideally including what kind of thing would even count as evidence between them. Around 1300–1700 words, plain speaker labels, stage directions only when something on the whiteboard or in the room matters. One last thing — they can name real physicists and describe real arguments, but please don't have them cite paper titles, page numbers, or quote anything, because we can't check any of that and I don't want us reading out invented quotes at club.
