The lens is what recent discussion of dual-use and risky research calls the unilateralist's curse. As I understand the mechanism: suppose there are *n* people each of whom could undertake some project, each forms an independent and noisy estimate of whether it is worth undertaking, and each acts whenever their own estimate comes out positive. The projects that actually get carried out are then disproportionately those that someone over-optimistically judged to be good — a selection effect structurally analogous to the winner's curse in auctions. The consequence is that acting on one's own honest private judgment is systematically biased toward disaster, and that some additional conservatism, more caution than any individual's own evidence warrants, is collectively rational even when it looks irrational from inside a single agent's deliberation.

My hunch, which I want you to test rather than accept: that formulation is entirely about unilaterally *acting*, and *Frankenstein* suggests a mirror-image failure the formulation doesn't cover — unilaterally *aborting*. Victor negotiates a bargain with the creature (build a female companion and the pair will withdraw to the wilderness of South America and never reappear among human beings), then tears the half-finished work to pieces alone on a remote island, on the basis of a private reassessment of risks the creature is given no opportunity to answer, after which Clerval and Elizabeth are murdered.

Please address:

1. Is the destruction of the second creature a case of the curse properly so called, or a different failure? The curse is a population-level selection effect across many rival would-be actors; Victor is a single actor with no competitor. Does the mechanism genuinely apply, or does invoking it here trade on a loose use of the term? If the honest answer is that it doesn't apply, say so and explain what the novel is dramatizing instead.
2. Can one account explain both of Victor's errors — being too willing to create and too willing to destroy? Or does the second error require different machinery?
3. Walton's frame. He pursues his polar expedition alone, takes Victor's story as a warning, and then, when his icebound crew asks him to turn back, first resists and finally, reluctantly, turns south; the dying Victor reproaches them for abandoning the enterprise and urges Walton to hold to his course and to finish Victor's own unfinished task. Does the novel endorse Walton's level of caution, condemn it as cowardice, or refuse to adjudicate? Does Victor's deathbed exhortation undercut any precautionary reading of the book?
4. Disclosure and third parties. Victor tells neither his father nor any public authority what he has done until it is far too late and he is no longer credible, while William, Justine, Clerval and Elizabeth bear costs they were never positioned to weigh. Is the tragedy a failure of aggregation or a failure of disclosure, and does the distinction matter for my argument?

Tell me frankly where reading the novel this way flattens it — if Victor's real fault is not miscalculation about risk but the refusal of care and relationship, my paper may be recasting a nineteenth-century novel about responsibility as a twenty-first-century puzzle about collective choice. Tell me how much of that objection survives my hunch.
