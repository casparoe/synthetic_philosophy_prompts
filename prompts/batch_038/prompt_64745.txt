I'm drafting the framing section for my quals and also a talk for the systematics reading group, so before I commit any of this to paper I want someone to stress-test the package as a whole. Here's what I actually hold, not what I think is defensible-sounding:

1. I'm with Ghiselin and Hull on the ontology: species are individuals, not natural kinds. A species is a bounded chunk of lineage history, it has no essence, and belonging to one is more like being part of a particular organism than like satisfying a definition.

2. But I'm a realist, not a conventionalist. Where the boundaries fall is discovered, not decided by a committee vote. If two taxonomists disagree about whether some valley holds one species or two, at most one of them is right, and it's not a matter of which research program you signed up for.

3. When I actually have to delimit things, I work with the phylogenetic species concept: the smallest monophyletic cluster that has a fixed diagnostic character.

4. Case verdict, and this is my own study system: two forms that differ consistently in male plumage and come out reciprocally monophyletic in mtDNA, but they meet across a wide-ish hybrid zone and the hybrids are fertile and backcross freely in both directions. I say one species. I'd defend that call to a splitter.

5. For prokaryotes I use a genome-similarity cutoff, roughly 95% average nucleotide identity. I dont think that's a fudge: the number is a convention but I think it's a convention that lands on a real discontinuity, a genuine gap in the distribution rather than a place where we got tired of splitting.

6. Ring species — the Ensatina sort of case — show that the biological species concept is unusable as a criterion, but they don't show species are unreal. Fuzziness at the edges is what an individual looks like when you catch it mid-formation.

7. I'm a monist about concepts: there's one right species concept for all of life. Pluralism reads to me as giving up and rebranding it as sophistication.

8. Asexual lineages — bdelloids, most bacteria and archaea — still divide into species, and my account is supposed to say how, not exempt them.

What I want is an audit, not a general tour of the species problem. Go through these pairwise and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, ranked by severity — worst first, and don't flatten them all into "there's some tension here." For each real one, say which commitment has to be dropped or weakened, what the cheapest version of that revision is, and what it costs me downstream (e.g. does it take 2 with it, does it wreck my verdict in 4). Then separately tell me which pairs only look like they conflict and are actually fine, because I'm fairly sure a couple of these are and I don't want to go repair things that aren't broken. Please don't take my ordering or my intuitions about which ones are fragile as evidence; I honestly don't know where the fault lines are, and I'm deliberately not telling you in advance which of the eight I'd be least willing to give up.
