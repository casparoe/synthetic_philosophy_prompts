Quick one for my ideas column — I cover science and the occasional bit of philosophy-of-mind fluff, so bare with me.

There's a study making the rounds at the moment: half the participants were allowed to save facts onto a tablet as they worked through a task, half had to keep everything in their head. The tablet group were worse at a plain recall test afterwards but much better at a follow-on task that asked them to apply the material in a new context, and the write-up billed it as the first solid empirical support for the extended mind thesis. The press office went further and said it proves your smartphone is literally part of your mind.

I want to hang my piece on that, but I'd rather explain the philosophy properly than just repeat the blurb. My understanding is that Clark and Chalmers ran the Otto and Inga thought experiment, which showed a notebook can hold a belief just as well as a brain can, and that the point of demonstrating this was that whether something counts as part of a cognitive process comes down to how much of the work the external bit is doing for you. So my question is: how much of a performance boost does a device have to give before it crosses the line from tool into part of somebody's mind? Is there a threshold the researchers can measure, or does it vary by task — and if the tablet group in this study only got the benefit on the transfer task, does that mean the tablet counted as part of thier mind for that task but not for the recall one?

Plain-ish prose please, I'm not writing for academics, but don't water it down either. British English throughout. And no references or suggested further reading — I just need the reasoning laid out so I can write around it. Six or seven hundred words is plenty.
