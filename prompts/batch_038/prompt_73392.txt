I am preparing a short statement of my position for a workshop on minds and machines, and what I want from you is an audit of it rather than a defence of it. My view is a version of the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument, deliberately stripped of Penrose's physics: I want the conclusion on purely mathematical grounds. The commitments are as follows.

1. I am a physicalist about the brain in the ordinary sense. I do not believe in hypercomputation, I do not invoke objective reduction or any non-computable physical process, and I do not think my conclusion depends on the physics being non-computable. Were it established that neural dynamics are computable to arbitrary precision, I would not take that to refute anything below.

2. I accept the incompleteness theorems in their standard formulations, for recursively axiomatizable theories that are consistent and contain enough arithmetic.

3. The argument I rely on: for any recursively axiomatizable theory T that is sound, if T were put forward as a complete account of what I would accept on ideal reflection, then I could see that T's Gödel sentence is true while T cannot prove it; so T is not a complete account of me.

4. I am a thoroughgoing fallibilist. I have made mathematical errors and will make more; I claim no immunity from error and no special faculty. I think the argument in (3) is routinely misread as a claim of human infallibility, and I reject that reading.

5. To accommodate (4), I idealize. The argument is not about my day-to-day acceptances but about what I would accept after unlimited reflection and after the correction of my errors; and I stipulate that this idealized body of acceptances is consistent and contains no falsehoods.

6. A verdict on cases. If someone handed me a complete specification — axioms, inference rules, coding, everything — and asserted that it captured my idealized acceptances, I think I would have a real argument against the assertion. But if someone built an opaque system that simply out-proved me at everything, I would have no argument whatever, and I would see no reason to deny it understanding.

7. Methodologically, I take (3) to be an a priori argument owing nothing to cognitive science; but I also hold that whether it applies to us is partly an empirical question, since we are finite creatures with limited memory and measurable error rates.

8. The conclusion I want: I am not, and could not be, adequately modelled by any Turing machine.

Please go through this package and tell me which of the commitments genuinely pull against one another, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad. For each real tension, say which specific items are involved, what has to be given up to remove it, and what giving that up costs me — in particular, whether the cost falls on (8) or somewhere upstream. I would also like you to separate out the pairs that only look like tensions and dissolve once the notions are prised apart, since I suspect at least one of the objections I hear most often is of that kind and I do not want to spend my statement answering it. Finally: is there a single revision that repairs the most while leaving (8) intact, and if so, is the resulting (8) still a thesis about minds, or has it been weakened into something a mechanist could happily accept? Treat the items as jointly held and do not assume in advance that I would sacrifice any particular one of them; I do not know where the problem is, and I would rather not be told which objection is standard — I want to be told which of my own commitments licenses it.
