**Purpose of the exercise.** I am preparing a seminar problem on the argument from gradual replacement: the claim that if a brain is swapped out one functionally equivalent component at a time while the patient remains awake and reports no change, then nothing that matters can have been lost at any step, and so whole-brain uploading must be survivable. The problem I want to set will show that this argument borrows from two different accounts of what survives, and that those accounts can be made to disagree about *which* procedure is safe. My students already know the ship-of-Theseus framing and the teletransporter; a case that reduces to either one will not count.

**The construction.** Build a thought experiment with two arms, run at the same near-future clinic on two patients who are alike in every clinically relevant respect — same condition, same baseline psychology, same team, same synthetic technology — so that the only variable is the protocol.

- *Arm 1 (incremental).* Over roughly two years the patient's brain is replaced neuron by neuron with synthetic units, each fabricated at the moment of installation to match the cell it replaces in every input–output respect the team can measure. Your mechanism must be a matching failure that is individually undetectable and cumulative across tens of thousands of installations, such that the endpoint is **not** fine-grained functionally isomorphic to the original brain.
- *Arm 2 (single-stage).* The patient's brain is scanned at molecular resolution and, in one operation, destroyed and replaced in the same skull by a synthetic system built to be an exact duplicate of the fine-grained functional organization recorded at the scan.

**Required verdicts.** For the *organizational theorist* — who holds that being the same mind consists in the preservation of fine-grained functional organization, whatever realizes it and however it came to be there — Arm 2 must preserve and Arm 1 must not. For the *non-branching psychological continuity theorist* — who holds that identity consists in psychological continuity brought about in the right way by the continuing system itself, and that a chain broken by destruction-and-reinstantiation does not count however close the match — Arm 1 must preserve and Arm 2 must not. The crossover must be generated by the theories' own premises, not asserted in the setup.

**Constraints.** No duplication or branching anywhere in either arm; no copies instantiated elsewhere; the body stays put. No exotic physics and no faster-than-measurement information transfer. In Arm 1 the patient is awake, articulate, and self-monitoring throughout and reports no change at any stage; in Arm 2 the interval of unconsciousness must be a dial you can turn, and you must show whether the continuity theorist's verdict moves as that interval goes to zero. The two endpoints, and the original patients, must be psychologically indistinguishable — same memories, same character, same standing intentions, same self-description — so any difference your case turns on must sit in the physical or procedural structure, not in the psychology. No souls, no stipulated facts about experience, no announced winner. One paragraph per arm, 150 words each at most.

**What to deliver after the scenario.**

1. For each arm and each theory, the verdict together with the *specific premise* of that theory which delivers it. Name the premise; do not restate the theory.
2. The single structural feature doing the work in each arm; what the crossover becomes if that feature is removed; and whether removing it collapses the arm into a familiar case — and if so, which.
3. The organization/psychology dilemma for Arm 1. Your cumulative feature is either psychological or it is not. If it is, your endpoints are not psychologically indistinguishable and the constraint is breached; if it is not, say why a theorist who cares only about organization should think its loss costs anything at all. Take both horns explicitly.
4. The evidential role of the reports. The patients say nothing has changed in either arm. Explain how each theory can credit those reports in one arm and discount them in the other without special pleading — or concede that it cannot.
5. The best rebuttal each losing theory has: what the organizational theorist says against Arm 1, what the continuity theorist says against Arm 2, and whether either rebuttal, if granted, destroys the crossover rather than merely softening it.

Keep the whole demonstration under 900 words. State any technical detail once and treat it as fixed thereafter. Do not cite, recommend, or allude to literature — the case has to stand on its own.
