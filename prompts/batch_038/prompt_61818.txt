I am preparing a chapter on epistemic injustice for an edited volume, and before I send it off I would like a consistency check on the package of commitments I have ended up endorsing. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for a defense of any of these claims; I want an audit of the set.

Here is what I hold.

1. Testimonial injustice consists in a credibility deficit in a speaker's word caused by the hearer's identity prejudice. The wrong is done to the speaker in her capacity as a knower, and it is not exhausted by the practical costs of going unbelieved.
2. Hermeneutical injustice consists in a speaker's inability to render an experience intelligible, to herself or to others, because the shared interpretive resources contain a gap, where the gap is a function of her group's exclusion from the practices that generate those resources. Like Fricker, I treat this as a structural wrong in which no individual hearer is culpable for the gap itself.
3. The hearer's remedy for (1) is a cultivated virtue: a standing, reflexive critical sensitivity to her own credibility judgments, adjusting them when she suspects that identity prejudice is distorting them, with the aim that the adjusted judgment track the speaker's actual trustworthiness.
4. I accept the empirical picture on which identity prejudice in credibility judgments is largely implicit and poorly accessible to introspection. I deny that this inaccessibility excuses the hearer: responsibility for the deficit survives her ignorance of it.
5. I am an evidentialist about credibility. A hearer should apportion credibility in accordance with her evidence about the speaker's reliability, and any adjustment made on account of the speaker's social identity, upward or downward, is a departure from epistemic rationality rather than a requirement of it.
6. Epistemic exploitation in Berenstain's sense is a genuine wrong. Individuals and institutions should stop calling on marginalized speakers to educate the privileged about the nature of their own oppression.
7. At the same time, I hold with Medina that dominant hearers bear affirmative responsibilities for their own epistemic positions: they are obliged to acquire the interpretive resources that marginalized communities have already developed, and I think that obligation is discharged chiefly by soliciting and deferring to testimony from members of those communities wherever the hearer's own understanding is inadequate.
8. I also hold, with Pohlhaus, that willful hermeneutical ignorance is culpable. Refusing to engage resources that already exist and circulate outside one's own hermeneutical economy is a blameworthy failing in a way that the mere absence of resources is not.
9. I accept Dotson's contributory injustice — a speaker who has the right concepts and deploys them competently can still be wronged when her hearer keeps applying dominant concepts that ill fit what she is saying — but I regard it as a species of testimonial injustice, since in such cases the speaker is also being accorded a credibility deficit, rather than as a third kind alongside testimonial and hermeneutical injustice.

Two cases on which I have firm verdicts, and which I use to test the account:

Case A. A hospital committee reviews transcripts in which triage nurses give less weight to pain self-reports from patients who speak a stigmatized regional dialect. The nurses disavow any negative belief about these patients; they report that the speech is simply harder to follow, and comprehension measures confirm that they process it less fluently than other patients' speech. I judge this to be a paradigm of testimonial injustice, and the nurses culpable for it.

Case B. A parole board gives reduced weight to a petitioner's self-reports because the record documents a sustained history of deceptive statements to officials. I judge this not to be testimonial injustice, however regrettable the outcome.

Please tell me which of these commitments pull against one another and which merely look as though they do. I am aware that some of them sit uneasily, but I cannot tell where the fault lies, and I have no view in advance about which one I would give up. For each tension I want the mechanism stated in terms of what my own commitments force, not in terms of what the secondary literature says, together with the cheapest repair and what that repair costs me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
