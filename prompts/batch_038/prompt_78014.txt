I am preparing a chapter for an edited volume on choice architecture, and the section I keep struggling with concerns a design I will stipulate rather than describe from any existing product, because I want the conceptual question isolated from empirical noise. Call it an endorsement-tracking default. An agent, at a moment of reflective calm, ranks her own ends in an explicit ordering — say, health over convenience, savings over consumption, candor over social smoothness. The system stores that ordering and then, at each subsequent choice point, silently sets the default to whichever available option best satisfies the highest-ranked end engaged by the situation, without prompting her again and without making its operation visible in the interface. She could disable it at any time; she never thinks to, because nothing ever flags its presence. It works, moreover, precisely by exploiting a well-documented status-quo bias: she stays with the pre-selected option a large majority of the time, including in cases where her in-the-moment impulse pointed the other way.

The proposition I want a number on is this: endorsement-tracking defaults of that kind are not paternalistic toward the agent, and their use does not compromise her autonomy. Please give me your actual credence that this is true, and — much more importantly — decompose it. I would like the sub-questions you think the answer turns on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification: whether the operative notion of paternalism here is one about the substitution of another's judgment for the agent's own, or about interference with her liberty, and which of those the case falls under; whether a mechanism that reliably issues in her acting on her own endorsed ordering thereby counts as an extension of her agency rather than an external influence upon it; whether hypothetical or dispositional endorsement — she would approve if she attended to it — carries the normative weight that actual endorsement does; whether exploiting status-quo bias necessarily bypasses her rational capacities or can instead operate through them; whether the fact that the ordering was fixed at an earlier time and never revisited undercuts the claim that the resulting choices are hers; and any further term you think belongs that I have left out. Then tell me how these combine — and please do not simply multiply them if the dependencies do not license that — which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it most. I am not after a survey of how the nudge literature has lined up on these matters; I want your own figure and its derivation.

For calibration against other people's views: my credence is roughly 0.7, and a co-author's is roughly 0.3. She is not reflexively hostile to nudging — she accepts that the ordering is genuinely the agent's own, that the agent is competent, and that the mechanism works exactly as I have described it. Given that shared ground, where do you think the 0.4 between us most plausibly lives? If you can identify which sub-question we are weighting differently, rather than telling me which of us is right, that will be more useful to me than anything else in your answer.
