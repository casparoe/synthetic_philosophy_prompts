I'm in the middle of a novel and I need help with something embarrassingly practical. There's a stretch of the book — maybe sixty pages — set inside a state wildlife agency that has to decide whether a population of fish in one river system counts as a distinct species, because if it does it gets protected and a road project dies. I want that stretch to be right. What I've taken away from my reading is that biologists can't agree what a species is: there's the interbreeding one, the one about separately evolving lineages, the ecological-niche one, a couple more, and they actually come apart on real cases rather than just spelling the same thing differently.

Here's my problem as a writer. My characters can't say it's an open conceptual question and leave it to the philosophers. Somebody has to tick a box on a form. So I want the decision procedure the agency would actually run — the thing a listing coordinator with a general biology background, not a systematist, could follow across a two-day workshop with two outside reviewers, and come out with a recommendation that survives being picked apart by the road company's lawyers.

What I need in it:

- the questions asked in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each — including what to do when the data just isn't there, because in one of my cases there's a single jar of specimens from the 1950s and nothing else
- which species concept each question is drawing on, named in plain language, and why that one gets used at that step instead of a rival
- where the procedure terminates in not-distinguishable, and where it hands the call to a human because what's left is a value choice wearing a lab coat — I want those points marked, not smoothed over
- the forced-choice part: my agency's form has exactly three boxes (distinct species / distinct segment below the species level / not distinguishable) and one of them has to be ticked even when two of the concepts point in different directions. Tell me how the procedure settles that — tiebreak rule, priority ordering, or escalation — and which of those you'd defend
- which distinctions from the real debate have to survive into the procedure and which you're dropping for usability, and why those

Then run it on three cases, and one of them has to give me an answer I hate:

1. Two populations in adjoining drainages, no gene flow detected in the wild, but they cross readily in the lab and the hybrids are fertile.
2. A population that looks identical to a widespread one anywhere you'd look with a caliper, but is deeply divergent genetically and lives in a completely different thermal regime.
3. A hybrid swarm — two forms started interbreeding freely when a dam came out about eighty years ago, and there are now fish that don't sort into either parent type. This is the sad one. Be honest even if the procedure says the fish loses.

The agency is invented and the fish is invented, so don't go hunting for the matching statute; I only need the reasoning to be real.

Two things about how you write it. I'd rather you were blunt about your confidence than thorough: tell me which steps you think track how these decisions really get made, and which ones you're tidying up because a novel needs tidier machinery than the world has. And if you think what I'm asking for can't be done — if the honest view is that there's no procedure here, just people picking a definition after they've picked an outcome and then drawing a flowchart around it — say that plainly and tell me what I should write instead. I'd much rather hear it from you now than from a reviewer later.
