Every fall my seminar on the philosophy of atrocity burns an entire meeting on the same fight, and this October I want to walk in with something that short-circuits it before it starts. The fight goes like this: one student, usually a poli sci major who's had a transitional justice course, insists that obviously some mass crimes are worse than others, that's the entire basis of sentencing and of triage in humanitarian work; another insists that ranking them is literally impossible because the harms are incommensurable; a third says it might be possible in principle but that doing it out loud, especially as someone whose own people aren't in the ledger, is a kind of betrayal of the dead. They talk past each other for eighty minutes because "can't compare" is doing at least three different jobs in the room — can't in principle, can but shouldn't, and can and should but we're the wrong people to be doing it. So I want a decision tree the students fill in privately during the first ten minutes of class, before anybody has declared a side, that sorts them by what they'd actually say about concrete cases rather than by which slogan they're attached to.

Here's the shape. Roughly a dozen branch nodes, each one a scenario with two or three answer options, phrased so a student can pick without knowing which leaf they're steering toward — absolutely no node of the form "do you think atrocities are commensurable?" Build the nodes out of the parameters that actually do the separating work (aggregation vs. kind, outcome vs. will, shape-in-time vs. total, epistemic destruction, who is doing the ranking and what it commits them to afterward), and please use at least these five cases, which I've been workshopping with my TA; add more wherever you need them:

1. Ledger: 100,000 people killed outright over two years, versus 10,000 people enslaved for thirty years each with nobody killed. Worse, equal, or not the sort of thing that has an answer?
2. Distribution: one million dead in a single campaign, versus one million dead spread across a century in a hundred massacres of ten thousand. Same body count — does the shape matter, and if so, does it matter morally or only rhetorically?
3. Agency: a regime that tortures one dissident for a year, versus a state whose criminally negligent dam fails and kills a million. Which is the greater wrong, and does your answer change if you're grading the will rather than the outcome?
4. Erasure: two equally lethal genocides; in one, the perpetrators also burn the archives, kill the last fluent speakers of the language, and level the shrines, so the crime can never be documented or mourned on the victims' own terms. Is the second worse — slightly, drastically, or is "worse" now being used in a different sense than in case 1?
5. Triage: an aid official has to pull her team off documenting a massacre to prevent a famine and cannot do both. Does the fact that she must choose show the two are comparable, or only that she is under constraint?

The leaves have to be the positions themselves, named where they have names and marked "unnamed" where you think the logical space contains a cell nobody occupies, each with the answer-path that reaches it and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I want leaves for the in-principle incommensurabilist, for the person who thinks ranking is possible but that publishing a ranking is wrong, for a straightforwardly cardinal and additive view, and for whatever the "this crime stands above all others and nothing gets weighed against it" intuition amounts to once you pin it down; the rest is up to you. Then give me the empty cells — which answer combinations are reachable in the tree but correspond to no view anyone defends — each with a one-line diagnosis of whether it's empty because the position is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's thought of it yet. And flag honestly any pair of leaves your cases can't actually distinguish: I strongly suspect you won't be able to separate metaphysical incommensurablism from the view that comparison is possible but permanently beyond our ken, and I'd rather the handout admit that than paper over it. Audience is about thirty second- and third-years, mixed philosophy and poli sci, one of whom has a family connection to the events we discuss and has already told me he finds the whole exercise distasteful — so keep the case wording clinical and generic, no named real events and no body counts attached to actual countries, without being coy about what each case is modeling. Format: indented tree, one node per line with lettered options, leaves in caps. Last line, exactly: Verdict: <number of leaves>, hardest-to-separate pair = <two position names>.
