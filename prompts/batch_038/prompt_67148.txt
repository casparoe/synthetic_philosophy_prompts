I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of medicine and last Thursday our department co-hosted a session with the hospital's clinical ethics committee — the standing item was whether the trust should fund cascade screening for the relatives of an index patient who'd had a malignant hyperthermia crisis during a routine appendectomy. The committee itself was uneventful. The interesting part was the pub afterwards, where Devi (an epidemiologist, pretty orthodox Boorse-ian naturalist about disease) and Marcus (a GP with a bioethics master's, normativist-leaning) got into it about the underlying question: is MH susceptibility in someone who has never been near an operating theatre a disease at all? Neither of them is stupid, which is why I couldn't referee it. Here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Devi opened by saying no. On the biostatistical line, disease is subnormal functional efficiency of some part relative to the species design, assessed in a species-typical environment; the untriggered relative's rylanodine receptor handles calcium perfectly normally in every environment they'll actually inhabit, and only goes haywire under volatile anaesthetics and suxamethonium, which is an artefact of twentieth-century medicine. So it's a risk, not a pathology, and you can't justify covering relatives' screening on the grounds of treating a disease. (2) Marcus said the phrase 'species-typical environment' was doing all the work and had been chosen to deliver the answer she wanted: most humans now encounter anaesthesia, refrigerated food, antibiotics, air travel. Widen the environment to include them and it's a disease on her own theory; refuse to widen it and she owes a non-question-begging rule for what counts as typical. (3) Devi said fine, the reference environment is a matter of degree, but that's a factual question about environments, not a values question — you don't need any evaluation to measure whether calcium release is typical, and that's the whole point: the reason we can now say flatly that homosexuality isn't a disease is that there was never a functional deficit to point at, and it's the value-free framework that gives you the leverage to strike entries off the list; on Marcus's view 'disease' is just what a society disvalues, and disvaluing is precisely what the people who wrote those entries were doing. (4) Marcus said he wasn't offering 'whatever we disvalue': the concepts are used to allocate things — sick notes, coverage, legitimacy, stigma — and a value-free account can't capture the asymmetry that actually matters clinically, since the same biological state (his example was infertility in someone who doesn't want children) is a disease in one context and mere variation in another. (5) At this point Yusuf, a health economist who'd tagged along and hadn't spoken, said they were both arguing about a word: for the committee the only question is whether labelling it a condition produces a better use of resources than labelling it a risk factor, and 'disease' is a coverage flag wearing a medical costume. (6) Both of them rejected that, interestingly for different reasons — Devi because if labelling is just bookkeeping you lose the ability to criticise a payer that codes joint wear as normal ageing to save money; Marcus because it turns the history of the DSM into accounting, and the reason some entries were struck wasn't cost. (7) Marcus then made a move against himself, which I found the most interesting thing he said all night: on a hybrid account (dysfunction plus harm), MHS is unstable, because the untriggered relative has the dysfunction but only conditional harm, so the hybrid is forced to say she isn't a patient now and becomes one on the operating table — which is no good for a coding decision that has to be made this quarter. (8) Devi said that's a feature, not a bug: dispositions and their manifestations are different states, and medicine codes dispositions all the time. She also volunteered a wrinkle against herself — there's decent evidence that some susceptible people react to extreme heat or heavy exertion, not only to anaesthetic triggers — but argued it strengthens her position, because now there's a measurable functional deficit in a genuinely ordinary environment (a hot summer, a marathon) and MHS becomes a disease on naturalist grounds with nothing normative smuggled in. (9) Marcus's last move was that this concession backfires: if whether you have the disease depends on how hot it is outside and whether you run, then the predicate flickers on and off with circumstances outside the body, which shows that the property the committee actually needs — should this person be tracked, screened, warned? — is relational rather than intrinsic, and relational properties are normativist home turf. Then they went to get another round and I gave up.

Can you go through this move by move? For each of (1)–(9) say who's right and rate the move on something like clearly mistaken / weak but pointing at something real / roughly right / strong / clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each — I don't want a general essay on naturalism versus normativism, I know the literature, I want the reasoning applied to these nine moves specifically. Then: which single point did each of them make that the other never actually answered? Is (7) a genuine problem for hybrid accounts or a confusion about dispositions and their manifestations, and does (8) dispose of it? Did Yusuf escape the debate or just restate one side of it in cost terms — and is the fact that Devi and Marcus both rejected him, but for different reasons, evidence that the naturalism/normativism split isn't the only fault line running through this? Finally, tell me what's left of the disagreement once you strip out the parts that are purely terminological: if there's a substantive question buried in it, state it precisely, and say which of the three positions you'd write into the committee's coding policy and what that sentence would look like.
