DRAFT — "Two-place essences: what the lettergame says about what makes an 'a' an 'a'"

§1. Setup. Hofstadter's group spent years on a project usually called the lettergame. The task is deliberately narrow: given a small number of seed letters — say a *b* and an *e* — drawn on a coarse grid of possible line segments in some style, produce the remaining lowercase letters so that all 26 look like they belong to one hand. The self-imposed constraint is the philosophically interesting part. The system is not supposed to work by holding a template for each letter and then deforming it, nor by holding a list of features that define each letter. Instead the machinery stores fragmentary calligraphic material and includes a component whose job is to judge whether a newly produced letterform is in the spirit of the seeds. Style consistency is not a rule that gets applied; it's a verdict that gets reached.

§2. Thesis. The natural way to describe what such a system has learned is not "the essence of *a*" but "the essence of *a*-in-this-style." Letter identity comes out as a two-place relation between a shape and a style rather than a one-place property of a shape. The same arrangement of strokes can be a perfectly good *a* in one style and an intolerable *a* in another — intolerable not because it's ugly but because it breaks the local logic of the face, the way a letterform that ignores the shared slope of its neighbours reads as a mistake rather than as a choice. If that's right, then the question "what makes every 'a' an 'a'" is malformed as posed, because it asks for the range of a one-place predicate where there is only the range of a two-place one. Essence, on this picture, is a matter of which distortions a style licenses, and the licenses differ from style to style.

§3. Against family resemblance. The default gloss on all this is Wittgensteinian: letterforms form a category held together by overlapping similarities with no common core, exactly like games. I think that's the wrong model, and importantly wrong rather than harmlessly imprecise. Family resemblance is a story about how a category hangs together in the absence of any standard; letters hang together because there is a standard they are answerable to. Letterforms are artifacts with a function — being recognized as this letter by readers who were not present at their design — and that function is policed by a community. Resemblance runs downstream of function, not upstream of it. Two letterforms resemble each other in the relevant way because both satisfy the same job description badly or well, not the other way around. The lettergame systems make this visible: the component that judges spirit-consistency is doing something normative, sorting candidates into acceptable and unacceptable, and that is not what criss-crossing similarities give you.

§4. The invariant-core problem, and a dilemma. If essence is style-relative, something has to explain cross-style recognition: a reader who has never seen a given face can usually still read it. So there must be some invariant. Two candidate accounts of the invariant, and I think both are bad. (a) The invariant is a style-neutral feature list — a set of properties every acceptable *a* has. This is the Platonism the whole project was designed to avoid, and it looks empirically false: the population of acceptable *a*'s includes two-storey forms with a closed upper bowl and single-storey forms with no closed bowl at all, plus everything between, and I don't see a feature non-trivially shared across that range. (b) The invariant is a fact about readers: what makes an *a* an *a* is that people who have been exposed to the actual historical distribution of letterforms accept it as one. This preserves everything I want in §2 and §3 — the standard is real, it's just located in us rather than in the shapes — but it makes the essence of a letter a contingent fact about a species' visual diet, and I don't like it. I currently lean toward (b) while hoping not to have to say so out loud, which is probably a sign the paper isn't finished.

§5. Notes for the machine-learning interlude — not yet written.
- Someone will say: just train a conditional generative model on (style, letter) pairs, it works, question dissolved.
- My intended reply: it doesn't dissolve the question, it relocates it. The trained model has an implicit metric over style-letter pairs, and the issue becomes whether that metric is tracking the essence of *a* or recording a contingency about the data it saw.
- worry: is this a tu quoque? The model's designers would happily say "recording a contingency, so what," and I don't have an argument that they can't.
- possible strengthening move: point out that the lettergame systems fail in an informative way — they can produce something that is locally consistent with the seeds and globally not an *a*, which is exactly the failure mode you'd expect if spirit-consistency and letter-identity came apart. Do I actually know they fail that way? I think so but it's from memory of the book and I should re-read before claiming it.
- if this section can't be strengthened, cut it and spend the words on §4.
