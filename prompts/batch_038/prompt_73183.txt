I am a staff software engineer at a company of roughly nine hundred people, and I have no formal training in philosophy — one ethics elective I barely attended, and a habit of reading on airplanes. Over the holidays a colleague lent me Arendt's essay on the Pentagon Papers ("Lying in Politics," which I understand appeared in the New York Review of Books in November 1971 and was reprinted in Crises of the Republic), and on her recommendation I then went back to the earlier essay "Truth and Politics," from The New Yorker in 1967. What stayed with me was not the part about state secrecy. It was her suggestion that the people manufacturing the fabrications had themselves come to believe them, so that by the end there was no one left inside the process standing in a relation to the facts from which they could have been deceived. That describes my working life better than I would like, and I want to test it against something concrete rather than admire it from a distance.

So: please play Hannah Arendt, in the first person, as she might have received me in 1972 — an engineer who has just spent twenty minutes explaining his job to her and now wants to be argued with. Restrict yourself to the conceptual resources available to her then plus whatever I tell you about the machinery; you may not pretend to know what a percentile, a cache tier, or a service-level objective is, and if you need a fact about the system in order to make your point, ask for it in character and wait for my answer. Please do not caricature her. She should be severe where severity is earned and wry where she is amused, impatient with sloppy vocabulary, and free to reject my framing rather than inherit it; she may tell me that a word I used does not mean what I think it means. She may grant me minor points but must never concede the central one below, and she should attack the strongest version of my argument, not a weak one I have not actually made. Do not treat me as a villain awaiting diagnosis, and do not generalize about corporate culture — the task is to defeat a particular claim. Stay in character until I write "stop," keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that it reads as conversation rather than lecture, do not close your turns by summarizing where you stand, and do not invoke your own books as authority: if a distinction matters, argue it out.

My opening move, which I would ask you to take seriously rather than dispose of. We publish a monthly service-health report to customers: availability, latency, error rate. Last spring we redefined one of those numbers. Latency had been measured from the moment a request reached our servers; we moved the measurement point to an intermediate layer closer to the customer, and we began excluding automatically retried requests as well as traffic from three regions where our instrumentation was unreliable. Every figure we now publish is literally accurate, auditable, and reproducible from our logs — I could defend each one in a deposition, and our legal team has signed off on the methodology, which I am scheduled to present to a customer advisory board in the spring. The reported number improved by something like forty percent. Nothing in the customer's experience improved; if anything it worsened that quarter, because excluding retries suppresses precisely the failures customers feel most, and because the region exclusion removes the places where we were performing worst. Now here is my position, offered not as a confession but as an argument: nobody at my company thinks this was a lie, including me, and I believe your own framework says we are right. A lie, as I understand you, concerns something known and determinate — it conceals or replaces a particular fact that the liar is aware of. We are not doing that. We are selecting which true facts to make public, which is what every report, every statistic, and every historian must do, since a complete account of a quarter would be an account of nothing. And you yourself insist that the capacity to say that something is not the case is bound up with the human capacity to act and to begin, so that the lie is a form of freedom and not merely a moral defect; you also say that truthfulness has never counted among the political virtues. Constructing an image for an audience seems to me to fall squarely inside that description — you call image-making a distinctly political activity, and that is what a curated dashboard is. So what in your account forbids what we did? If the answer is that we did not misdescribe a shared world but damaged it, then tell me what separates that damage from ordinary selection, because "you chose the wrong true facts" does not sound like lying to me; it sounds like taste, and taste is not something I can be shown to have gotten wrong.
