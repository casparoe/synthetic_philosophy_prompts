I'm auditing a social epistemology seminar this term and my flatmate is doing a psychology MSc (dissertation on ego depletion, of all things), so the Bem "Feeling the Future" business came up twice in one week and we ended up arguing about it in the kitchen until two in the morning. Quick recap of the bits I'm relying on, in case you remember them diferently: in 2011 Daryl Bem published a paper in a top social-psychology journal reporting nine experiments, all run with completely bog-standard methods, which came out as evidence that people respond to stimuli before those stimuli appear. The editors' position was that it cleared the usual methodological bar, so anyone who wants to reject it owes an account of why everything else that clears that same bar is fine. A group of Dutch methodologists replied in the same journal by rerunning one of the paradigms with an extra control condition, one where nothing later in the session could possibly have leaked backwards into earlier responses — and they still got the "anticipation" effect, which they read not as evidence for psi but as a demonstration that the standard pipeline manufactures effects like that out of thin air. Straightforward replications by other labs came up empty, and within a few years you've got the big multi-lab replication exercise, preregistration, registered reports and the rest of the reform movement, most of which tells that exchange as its origin story.

What I'm after is a proper case analysis of the episode through higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia — not a history of the replication crisis, which I can read anywhere. So first sort the evidence in play into the two flavours of higher-order evidence I've been sketching: process-directed (reason to think the method that produced your belief is unreliable in general — optional stopping, the garden of forking paths, the fact that the same recipe spits out psi) versus state-directed (reason to think you misapplied an otherwise sound method on this particular occasion — you were rushed, you misread the output, the manipulation check quietly failed). Then say what each camp was rationally required to do under each heading: Bem himself, the methodologists, and the ordinary working psychologist who found the critique completely convincing and then carried on publishing her own significant results exactly as before. Be explicit about where the framework goes silent rather than making it say something it can't.

The bit I really want you to bite into is the self-exemption asymmetry — everybody in the episode seemed able to grant that the field's process was unreliable while remaining totally unmoved about their own first-order conclusions. Is that epistemic akrasia, or is it the level-splitting move ("my evidence doesn't support P, but P anyway") pulled off legitimately, or is the akrasia charge just misapplied because the evidence here is process-directed and process-directed evidence can be bracketed without any inconsistency at all? And run it in the other direction too: does the episode confirm the standard picture of higher-order defeat, throw up a case that picture never anticipated, or reveal that "higher-order evidence" isn't doing any work here that "you should have preregistered" wasn't already doing on its own? British English throughout, please.
