I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about the price of a gamble, by listing cases and recording my verdicts on each. The cases are all built out of a single fair coin flipped until it first comes up heads, with the payoff determined by the number n of flips required. I would like a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that accounts for the pattern — including the places where the pattern looks incoherent.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A ticket that pays ten dollars for certain. Price: ten dollars. No hesitation.

2. Ten dollars for certain, plus one hundred trillion trillion trillion trillion dollars if the first heads happens to fall on exactly the hundredth flip. The expectation here is perfectly well defined and works out at roughly eight billion dollars. My price: twelve dollars. I would not pay twenty, and I do not think I am being merely weak-willed.

3. First heads on flip n pays two to the n dollars. The expectation is infinite. My price: fifty dollars. I think someone who paid five thousand for this ticket is a fool, but I also do not think the ticket is worthless, and I cannot say what makes fifty the right figure rather than thirty or five hundred.

4. First heads on flip n pays positive two to the n over n if n is odd and negative two to the n over n if n is even: win two dollars, lose two, win eight thirds, lose four, win thirty-two fifths, and so on. The expectation series is one minus a half plus a third minus a quarter, which converges to about sixty-nine cents, but converges only conditionally, so its value depends on the order in which the terms are added. My verdict: this ticket has no price at all. There is no amount I would pay for it and no amount I would accept to surrender it, and I find that far more disturbing than the infinite expectation in case 3. I take this to be the example people call the Pasadena game, though I have not checked.

5. Truncations of case 4: play at most N flips, and if no heads has appeared by then, pay nothing. Every one of these has a definite price I am confident about; the thousand-flip version I would price at sixty-nine cents, to within a fraction of a cent. The prices of the truncations converge, and they converge to sixty-nine cents. And yet I refuse to say that case 4 itself is worth sixty-nine cents.

6. The same ticket as case 4, but with the flips settled in bundles of two rather than one at a time: (2 − 2), then (8/3 − 4), then (32/5 − 32/3), and so on. After the first bundle every bundle is a net loss, and the bundles grow without bound, so the bracketed sum runs off to minus infinity. Same coin, same payoffs, same probabilities — only my bookkeeping changed. I do not accept that the ticket is worth minus infinity, but neither can I say what is wrong with the bracketing. Relatedly, someone pointed out to me that if I re-attach the same list of payoffs to the flips in a different order, the total can be made to equal any number I please, or to diverge; and that re-attaching payoffs genuinely produces a different gamble whereas bundling in pairs does not. I see that there must be a difference, but I cannot state it, and I suspect my inability is the whole problem.

7. First heads on flip n pays positive two to the n if n is odd and negative two to the n if n is even. The expectation series is one minus one plus one minus one, which has no sum at all, though averaging the running partial sums suggests a half. My price: forty cents, and I feel more confident about this figure than about anything in cases 2 through 6. I notice something I cannot explain: this ticket exposes me to arbitrarily large losses, and adding that exposure to a ticket I priced at fifty dollars in case 3 has lowered my price by only ten cents.

8. Aggregation. If one person plays case 4 once, I think that is reckless. If a charity sells a million tickets at one dollar each on the same payoff structure, I think running it is unobjectionable and perhaps admirable. Similarly, given a public-health choice between an intervention with a well-estimated modest benefit and one whose benefit is fat-tailed and unbounded above, I would fund the second — which I suspect contradicts my verdict in case 2.

My own tentative principle is this: the price of a gamble is the limit of the prices of its finite truncations, provided that the limit does not depend on how the gamble is described or subdivided. I can already see that it misfires on case 2, where the truncations converge to eight billion dollars, and on case 3, where they diverge; and that if I repair it by allowing oscillating partial sums to be averaged, so as to handle case 7, then the repair also assigns sixty-nine cents to case 4 and destroys the verdict I care most about.

What I would like from you:

- State one principle, or at most three competing candidates, precisely enough that it assigns a unique price or a definite no price to each of the eight cases. Tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and say what work each clause is doing.
- Say explicitly which of my verdicts each candidate preserves and which it must overturn. If no candidate preserves all of them, identify the single verdict I should abandon, and say whether the tension between case 4 and case 7 — no price for the better-behaved series, a confident price for the worse one — can be dissolved rather than merely endured.
- Apply your principle to cases I have not considered: case 4 with losses capped at one thousand dollars but gains uncapped; case 7 played a million times by a syndicate that pools the results and divides them; a gamble whose expectation is undefined but which pointwise dominates an alternative I would accept; and the seller's side of case 4, where I am the bookie rather than the player. Where your principle yields no price, say whether that is a verdict or an admission of failure.
- Tell me whether the resolution amounts to requiring that utilities, or payoffs, be bounded. If it does, say whether that is a solution or a change of subject — and in particular how it can be squared with my confidence in case 2, where the expectation is finite, unambiguous, and about eight billion dollars, and where bounded utility would presumably tell me I am right to pay twelve dollars anyway.
- Finally, tell me whether the distinction I am tracking between case 3 and case 4 — between an expectation that is infinite and an expectation that does not exist — is a real distinction doing real work in my verdicts, or an artifact of my having learned the vocabulary.
