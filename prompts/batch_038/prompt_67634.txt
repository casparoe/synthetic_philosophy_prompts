I am preparing a seminar exercise on the Hart–Devlin debate and want a case built to order rather than one of the familiar exhibits. Design a single municipal scenario — a licensing board, an occupational regulator, or a council committee, not a legislature enacting a general prohibition, and nothing technologically exotic — concerning a private, commercially organized practice that consenting adults enter into. The specifications: the practice must provoke intense and vocal moral revulsion, but that revulsion must attach to a feature of it that injures no one; a separate and severable feature must cause serious, demonstrable injury to identifiable third parties who have not consented; that injury must occur out of public view and must not fall on the participants themselves, so that neither an offense rationale nor a paternalist one is available; and no step of the reasoning may rest on contested empirical claims about social cohesion or deterrence. One paragraph for the scenario. The purpose is to show my students that each theorist's internal limits can invert the expected verdict, so the verdicts must be generated rather than declared: demonstrate that Devlin, applying his reasonable-bystander standard together with his commitments to the greatest liberty consistent with the integrity of society, to the protection of private life, and to slow legal change, is barred from suppressing the practice, while Hart's harm principle licenses an intrusive, harm-tracking intervention — compelled disclosure, audit of private contracts — that Devlin's limits would forbid. Then name the single feature doing the work, state what happens to the case if you remove it by making the object of revulsion coincide with the locus of injury, and give the strongest reply a Devlin partisan could make against the design.
