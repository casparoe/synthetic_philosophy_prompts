You are an experienced aesthetics instructor who also designs participatory programs for museums. Think carefully before answering: sketch two or three candidate mechanics briefly, say which one you are committing to and why, then write the full activity.

Setting: an upper-level undergraduate aesthetics seminar, 16 students, one 75-minute session, a room where students can move around. Materials must be cheap; no real money.

What I want the students to run into, on their own and before anyone names it: that the value we attach to an object survives the object being perceptually indistinguishable from a copy, and that the obvious explanation of this appeal to how it looks collapses almost immediately once they have committed to prices. They should not hear the labels (forgery, achievement or performance accounts, contagion and essentialism, aesthetic empiricism) until the debrief.

The core mechanism I have in mind: four visually identical objects placed at four stations around the room, each with a sealed dossier that gives only its history of making and chain of custody. One was made by the artist to whom it is attributed. One was made by an assistant in that artist's own workshop and sold under the master's name at the time, to nobody's surprise. One was made by a highly skilled twentieth-century imitator, passed off as a rediscovered early work of an old master, praised at length by connoisseurs, and later exposed. One was produced by a contemporary image-generating system and printed last week. Students must first rank what a museum would pay for each, then commit a fixed budget of points to those rankings, then defend their numbers aloud to the whole group as the dossiers are revealed one at a time.

Please build in at least these two twists: partway through, disclose that one dossier contains an unexplained thirty-year gap in the chain of custody, and force the students holding that lot to decide whether to sell at a loss before anyone knows whether the object is what it claims to be. And make the scoring rule reward only the final monetary value of each student's portfolio, so that those who reasoned badly but happened to price correctly still win, and then ask the group at the end whether they are comfortable with that.

Deliver: complete rules, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timing table, a points and payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto each of the following: why perceptual indistinguishability is what makes the case philosophically interesting rather than merely a matter of deception; why the objection might be about misrepresenting a human achievement rather than about the object's appearance; the pull of physical contact with a maker's hand even when nothing about it can be seen; the fact that the imitated work was genuinely admired on its merits while it was believed authentic, and what that does to the idea that value tracks the experience the object produces; and the fact that the workshop lot shows the whole practice is indexed to a norm of attribution that has a history, so that the same object could be unremarkable in one century and a crime in another.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I am particularly worried about two failure modes: that students will simply trade on information asymmetry and private dossiers instead of confronting the puzzle at all, and that the machine-produced lot will hijack the session into a general debate about artificial intelligence. Give me concrete interventions for each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the real problem this structure cannot reproduce, so that I know what I have to supply myself in the debrief rather than pretend the game delivered.
