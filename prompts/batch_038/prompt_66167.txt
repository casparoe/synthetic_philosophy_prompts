About three weeks from now I'm workshopping a chapter draft at our department's political theory seminar — the joint one with the law school, so the room is going to be roughly eight political theorists (two of them committed all-affected-principle people, one who thinks any version of all-affected dies because affectedness isn't measurable), two constitutional law folks who spend all day on standing and jurisdiction, and one empirical political scientist who studies turnout and thinks anything requiring a brand new administrative body is DOA. I've been arguing with my advisor about this thing since January and it's honestly eaten the chapter, which started out being about climate migration. The thesis: stop trying to fix the boundary of the demos and abolish it instead. Every person gets a vote in every collective decision that affects them, but weighted — weight proportional to how much of their interests are actually at stake, with the stake computed by a standing commission using something like an expected-harm metric, and decisions carved into issue-specific electorates rather than one fixed membership list. My claim is that this dissolves both the boundary problem and the second-order version of it (who decides who decides never comes up, because there's no fixed "who" left to decide). Argument sketch, since the objections should aim at this and not at a strawman: (1) the whole problem is generated by an assumption that membership is binary — you're in the set or you're not; (2) that assumption does no normative work, its just administrative convenience hardened into principle; (3) once membership is scalar, the standard objections (recursion, arbitrariness, exclusion) lose their target; (4) weighting is operationalizable at the level of policy domains even if not globally.
