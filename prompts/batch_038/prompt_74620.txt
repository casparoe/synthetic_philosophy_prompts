I sit on the board of a private foundation whose mandate is to reduce the risk that large-scale, quietly load-bearing intellectual infrastructure fails without warning. Most of our portfolio is unglamorous in exactly that spirit: reference data standards, long-term archival formats, maintenance funding for a handful of critical open-source projects. One of my fellow trustees has proposed adding a small line item — call it four researcher-years spread over five years — to what he terms foundational-inconsistency risk: the possibility that the standard axiomatic base of mathematics, ZFC or the arithmetic beneath it, turns out to be inconsistent.

I want a decision memo rather than a survey, and I want it to take the proposal seriously enough to say what the money would actually buy. Assume the trustees already accept the standard logical background: a discovered inconsistency would be a finite, machine-checkable derivation, so detection is not the bottleneck; and any system that interprets an inconsistent one collapses with it, so retreating upward along the consistency-strength ladder is not a hedge. Assume also that we are not trying to purchase certainty about the consistency of our foundations — we understand that is unobtainable from inside — but to decide what, if anything, is worth doing before, during, and after such a discovery, and whether the honest answer is that nothing is.

Given four researcher-years, which open questions deserve them, and in what order? Please make the ordering dependency-aware. In particular, I want to know whether the first move should be epistemic — calibrating how much warning, if any, a genuine inconsistency would produce, and what the historical record of the set-theoretic paradoxes suggests about how far disruption actually propagated into working mathematics — or whether it should be triage: determining which parts of applied, computational, and physical mathematics really need the contested strength and which could be re-grounded in weaker or lateral systems such as predicative fragments, bounded set theories, or constructive type theory. Say where machine-checked formalization belongs in the sequence, and whether porting results into a proof assistant is a genuine hedge or an expensive placebo that merely relocates trust to a kernel whose own foundation is in no better epistemic standing. I also want to know which candidate projects you would refuse to fund as untractable or as irrelevant to the outcome — including whether feasibility-based and ultrafinitist programs, and the well-publicized episode in which a prominent logician announced an inconsistency proof for Peano arithmetic only to have it refuted within days, carry any planning lessons at all.

Structure the memo as follows, with these section headings in this order:

1. Bottom line (about 100 words): whether the line item should be funded at all, and in what shape.
2. Criteria and their trade-offs (about 150 words): what you ranked by, and what you gave up in order to rank by it.
3. The ranked agenda (700–900 words): four or five items in priority order, each stating the question precisely, explaining why it sits upstream or downstream of the others, what a researcher could plausibly deliver in one to two years, and what a good result would let us do that we cannot do now.
4. Refusals (about 150 words): what you would decline to fund, and why.
5. Reordering triggers (about 200 words): specific developments — a preprint, a formalization result, a shift in expert consensus — that would move particular items up or down the list, and in which direction.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; name particular results or episodes only where they carry argumentative weight.
