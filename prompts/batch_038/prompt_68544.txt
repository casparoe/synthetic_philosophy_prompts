Taking a Kant seminar this term and I'm stuck on the lying essay, specifically on how much his actual verdicts pin down. Here's every case I can find where Kant gives a ruling on truthfulness, running from the Groundwork through the Metaphysics of Morals and that 1797 piece answering Constant:

1. Borrowing money on a promise to repay that you know you'll never keep: wrong.
2. The murderer at the door asks whether the friend you're hiding is in the house: you may not say no. Truthful answer required even though a lie would save a life.
3. The unlucky variant he gives right after: you answer that he's not there — and you're telling the truth, he really did slip out without you knowing — the murderer leaves, runs into him on the road, kills him. Kant says you can still be blamed as the cause of the death, since who knows whether neighbors might have grabbed the murderer while he was searching the house.
4. A lie that hurts nobody in particular: still wrong. His reason is roughly that a lie always wrongs somebody, an individual if there happens to be one, otherwise mankind, because it makes the whole business of declarations useless.
5. Constant's principle — that you only owe the truth to people who have a right to it, and a guy threatening to murder your friend has forfeited his — Kant rejects it, partly on the ground that a right to the truth doesn't even parse, since truth isn't a thing you can own or be deprived of.

The case I have to write about is a variant on 2. Same setup: friend hidden in your house, murderer at the door, "is he in here?" But instead of a false answer you say: "I watched him walk off down the road toward the bridge about ten minutes ago." Ten minutes ago he genuinely did go out that door and walk toward the bridge. He then doubled back and he is currently in your cellar. Nothing you asserted was false. You just left out the doubling back, and you are absolutely counting on him drawing the obvious conclusion.

My prof said in seminar, with no argument at all, that this is "obviously still a lie" and gets the same verdict as 2. Half the class said its not a lie in any sense Kant cares about because every sentence came out true. What I want to work out is which of them is right, and more than that whether the five verdicts above even settle it.

So:

I've got three rattling around: something like never assert a falsehood, full stop, consequences and addressee irrelevant; something like never let another person end up believing what you know to be false, which cares about the effect on their belief rather than the truth value of your words; and something keyed to the responsibility move in 3, where what matters is whether you could be held to account for what happens downstream. I suspect there are more, and I want them named explicitly, because I have a feeling my prof is quietly running one of them and calling it Kant.
- Say which of those rules actually diverge on the cellar case and which give the same answer for different reasons. Then give your best guess with the reasoning, and point at whatever in verdicts 1-5 does real pushing work rather than just being compatible with everything.
