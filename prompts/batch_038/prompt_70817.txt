I am applying for a twelve-month research fellowship in legal philosophy and need a proposal of about two pages, roughly 1,100 words, built out of an idea I can at present state only roughly.

The rough idea. Discussions of self-amending rules — a constitution that forbids the amendment of its own entrenchment clause, a sovereign legislature said to be able or unable to bind its successors — tend to run two different questions together. One is diachronic: what authority, if any, does a rule adopted earlier have over the status of a later rule, and can a rule determine whether it will still be recognised as valid at some future date? The other is hierarchical: which of two rules in force at the same time outranks the other, so that a conflict between them is resolved rather than merely registered. My suspicion is that most of the supposed paradoxes here are diachronic puzzles wearing hierarchical costumes. They feel contradictory only because we tacitly assume that a hierarchy of norms, once laid down, persists unchanged through time, when in fact the amendment of one rule can alter the criteria by which the validity of that very rule is later assessed. If that is right, the standard moves in the area — treating entrenchment clauses as generating a logical contradiction, or dividing sovereignty into continuing and self-embracing conceptions of omnipotence — are answers to the hierarchical question offered as solutions to a diachronic one.

I want the proposal to turn this into a project with a determinate aim. The concrete object I have in mind is a taxonomy of amendment-limiting provisions, ordered by what exactly each purports to constrain — who may change a rule, what content may be changed, what procedure counts as changing it, or how a change is to be recognised afterwards — together with a test for deciding, of any two such provisions in the same instrument, whether their interaction yields a genuine normative conflict or only an apparent one. As a running example, use throughout the following provision, which I drafted myself and which the project should be able to dispose of:

Article X. (1) The provisions of this Title may be amended only by a statute passed by two-thirds of each chamber and approved by referendum. (2) Paragraph (1) admits of no amendment. (3) Nothing in this Article limits the power of the electorate, acting by referendum, to adopt a new constitution.

The proposal should contain: a statement of the research question precise enough that a reader can say what would count as answering it; an account of why the existing positions leave it open rather than simply unresolved between rivals; the method — what I would actually do, month by month, across the twelve months — and a defence of that method against the obvious alternatives, namely imposing a fixed hierarchy of norms, declaring the puzzles verbal, or formalising rule-change in a dynamic logic and letting the formalism settle the question; a description of what a successful result looks like, ideally something a constitutional court's clerks could apply to a text they had never seen; and an explicit statement of what would count as failure, including the possibility that the distinction I am relying on turns out to do no explanatory work. End with a candid assessment of scope: what would have to be cut for twelve months to be realistic, and at which point the project risks expanding into a general theory of legal validity.

Do not include a bibliography or citations, and do not invent references to works, cases, or jurisdictions; describe positions and arguments in prose and I will supply the references myself. Write for a selection panel that includes at least one reader who doubts the question is well-posed.
