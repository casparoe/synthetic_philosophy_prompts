I spent thirty-five years in experimental physics, where a result counted only insofar as an independent group could reproduce it, and I have come late to the philosophy of testimony, where the old quarrel between Hume and Reid now absorbs me. Re-watching Twelve Angry Men last week, it struck me as a laboratory for that quarrel: the killing is never shown, so the audience is confined, exactly as the jurors are, to what witnesses say.

Juror 8 looks to me like a local reductionist. He does not distrust testimony as such; he asks for positive reasons case by case — the noise of the elevated train, the timed re-enactment of the old man's walk to the door, the knife that turns out not to be unique, the impressions a pair of spectacles leaves on the bridge of the nose — and each reason is drawn from an inductive base of ordinary experience. Two puzzles follow. First, that base must be selected: "old men who feel overlooked," "people who wear glasses," "boys from the slums." Is the film persuasive because Juror 8 picks the right reference classes, or does it depend on his being permitted to pick them at all? Second, the last holdout changes his vote after tearing up the photograph of his son, with no fresh evidence in sight. I note too that the courtroom's procedure is reductionist, the burden lying on the state, while the opening vote of eleven to one shows the jurors defaulting to belief exactly as Reid would predict; I am not convinced the film notices the tension.

I should like a committed answer, not a survey: tell me which view the film actually vindicates, or whether it vindicates neither, with reasons, and say what the photograph scene does — a defeater located in the believer rather than the witness, or an admission that the film's own epistemology fails at the decisive moment. Close with what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot. If I have mis-described a scene, say so plainly rather than working around it.
