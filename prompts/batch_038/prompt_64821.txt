I'm three years into a decision theory PhD, formal epistemology side of the department, so half my committee thinks in representation theorems and the other half reads Kahneman, and I'm stuck on what's supposed to be a boring background chapter of my dissertation: what the Allais paradox is actually *about*, philosophically. The setup isn't the problem — million for sure vs. 89% million / 10% five million / 1% nothing, and then 11% million / 89% nothing vs. 10% five million / 90% nothing, most people take the sure million in the first pair and the bigger gamble in the second, which is precisely what independence rules out. The problem is that seventy years later, with the experiments done to death, nobody has moved an inch. One camp says the pattern shows independence isn't a requirement of rationality at all, leans on Allais's own talk about complementarities between the branches of a single gamble, and builds rank-dependent or risk-weighted theories that reproduce the pattern while keeping transitivity and stochastic dominance intact. Another camp says an axiom isn't the kind of thing a questionnaire can overturn — that failing independence just is what it amounts to for a ranking of lotteries not to be a linear expectation — so the data show only that people's choices aren't representable that way, not that they're rational in failing to be, and then they point out that a person who makes those choices across a sequence of problems can be pumped for money. And there's a third group who I suspect are answering a different question entirely about how choices get assembled (configural weighting, branch-splitting effects, the rest of it) and who treat the normative standoff as beside the point.

The obvious diagnosis — "one side is descriptive, the other normative" — can't be right, because both of the first two camps are making normative claims and they're contradicting each other. My working hypothesis, which I want you to stress-test rather than flatter, is that 'rational' is doing two jobs that only come apart in exactly these cases: for one side it names a property an isolated preference between two lotteries can have, for the other it names a property of an agent's dispositions across time and across problems. On that reading the disagreement isn't verbal, exactly, but it isn't a substantive normative disagreement either — the camps would agree about almost everything if you pinned down whether the bearer of assessment is a single choice, a whole choice function satisfying internal-consistency conditions (the Sen-ish tradition), or a diachronic policy. Related guess: part of the standoff is that the empirical literature hasn't settled anything either. Some people report the Allais pattern shrinks or disappears once the common consequence is made explicit and the stakes are small and hypothetical, and the Slovic-and-Tversky line of work reports the opposite, that people are handed the sure-thing argument and mostly stick with their original picks. So maybe the normative fight is downstream of an unresolved empirical question about whether the effect survives reflection, and each camp is just cherry-picking the results that suit it.

So: diagnose the structure of the disagreement, not who's right about independence. Is it verbal (two senses of 'rational'), substantive normative (a real conflict about what rationality requires that could in principle be argued), a conflict about which object bears the assessment, a hidden empirical question, or two research programs that stopped translating into each other because their theorems and their lab results don't meet anywhere? Engage what each side actually says, including the money-pump style objection and the rank-dependent reply to it, and be concrete about where the translation between the camps breaks down. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction somebody needs to draw, an experiment that would genuinely be decisive, a formal result (a dynamic coherence theorem, a representation theorem for choice functions that don't satisfy independence, whatever), or an admission that the parties should change the subject. If your answer is that no progress should be expected, argue for that instead. And tell me honestly whether my own framing is one of the parties' framings in disguise — i.e. whether I've just restated the deflationary camp's view and dressed it up as a meta-diagnosis.
