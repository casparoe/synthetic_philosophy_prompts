So I've read Frankenstein maybe five times now and every time I hit the Orkney chapter — the one where Victor is halfway through assembling the female and then tears her apart with the Creature's face at the window — I come out of it convinced this is the best piece of nonidentity-problem fiction anyone has ever written, and somehow nobody in my circles has said so. I want a real critical reading of the novel through that lens. Not a book report, not "Shelley raises important ethical questions." I want someone to actually argue with me.

Where I'm coming from: I spent a bunch of years on the old rationalist forums, so I hit Parfit via the population ethics threads rather than via a philosophy department, and I think the nonidentity problem is a live puzzle rather than a verbal confusion. But I lean person-affecting — I want there to be somebody who got wronged, or the wrongness starts to feel like it's floating free of morality altogether. A friend of mine (we've been going back and forth on this for years and its never resolved anything) holds that a good person-affecting view just dissolves the puzzle and that this is a feature, not a bug. I don't buy it and I think Frankenstein is why, but I can't get the argument out of my own head cleanly.

The thing I find weirdly underappreciated is that the novel gives you a nonidentity case with the pieces rearranged. Two coinages I've been using, which you should feel free to attack:

- **Retrospective standing.** In creation cases the only being with standing to object to being created is one who already exists, so the objection is structurally unavailable at the moment of choice — it can only be voiced afterward, by the person it's too late to help. The Creature is literally the only creature in the universe positioned to file the complaint, and he files it in person, to the defendant.
- **The Victor move.** The intuition that wrecking what you've already started is worse than never starting, even when the two outcomes are extensionally identical — nobody exists either way in the "never started" branch and nobody exists in the "tore her to pieces" branch. My friend says this is just scope neglect dressed up in a lab coat. I suspect the novel endorses it, and I want to know whether that endorsement survives scrutiny.

What I want the reading to do:

1. **Take Victor's chapter-20 reasoning seriously as reasoning.** I re-read it last week. What he actually says is species-level and tail-risk flavored: if the pair end up breeding, a "race of devils would be propagated upon the earth who might make the very existence of the species of man a condition precarious and full of terror," and then "Had I right, for my own benefit, to inflict this curse upon everlasting generations?" He also notes she "might refuse to comply with a compact made before her creation." Run that as an expected-value calculation under moral uncertainty, which is what it resembles — he's assigning probabilities to her dispositions, to the two of them hating each other, to there being offspring at all. Then show what the calculation systematically omits: he never once asks whether *her* life would be worth living. Is that the nonidentity blind spot showing up in a text from 1818, or am I overfitting?

2. **Sort out who, if anybody, is wronged at the moment of the tearing.** On a person-affecting view there is no subject there to harm. But the novel clearly treats Victor as having broken something — a promise, an obligation to a being who does exist and who was counting on her. That's a third axis the standard literature doesn't have, since in the usual cases nobody has promised anybody anything. Does obligation-to-the-existing-person do the moral work here that impersonal and person-affecting accounts both fail to do? And does the half-built female have any moral status *before* she's a subject — is this killing or never-creating, and does the novel know the difference?

3. **Deal with what the Creature actually asks for.** He never asks not to have been made. He asks for a companion, and he claims his misery made him vicious and that companionship would reverse it. So the one being with standing to object to his own creation doesn't object to his creation — he objects to his isolation. What does that do to wrongful-life and anti-natalist readings of the book? And relatedly: does the novel actually commit to his life being net-negative? If it doesn't, is this even a nonidentity case, since the puzzle bites hardest right at the threshold where a life stops being worth living?

4. **Use the self-education stretch.** The cottage, the De Laceys, the three books he finds in the portmanteau — Milton, Plutarch, Goethe. He acquires preferences, a self-conception, and a sense of his own difference *after* existing. That suggests the harm of his creation is positional and relational — about his standing relative to others — rather than about his welfare level in isolation. Is that a view the novel holds, or one I'm holding and projecting?

5. **Tell me where the reading breaks, honestly.** The Creature is an articulate adult mind with aesthetic and moral experience, not a baby, which makes him a bad test case for the threshold question. Victor is an unreliable narrator inside a frame narrative reporting to Walton, so the chapter-20 reasoning might be a rationalization of disgust rather than the book's argument — the Creature's reaction suggests the novel is indicting Victor for abandonment, not for population ethics. And there's a real danger of turning a novel about loneliness into a diagram. I'd rather you flag the strain than smooth it over. Also correct me anywhere I've got the plot wrong.

6. **End with what the book adds that argument alone doesn't.** My hunch: the thought experiments in this literature are bloodless because the future person is a description, and descriptions don't stand at your window watching you work. Shelley puts the affected party in the room. Does that vividness track something morally relevant — the retrospective-standing point, maybe — or is it narrative salience masquerading as evidence? I'd like a real answer either way, including if the answer is that it's mostly the latter.
