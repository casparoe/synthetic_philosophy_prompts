Working on a paper about the boundary problem — who counts as part of the demos — and I've got maybe 60% of a draft. Before I keep going I want a read on whether the middle section actually holds up, not a copyedit. I've pasted what I have below, including a couple of bits I'm unsure about.

The framing, roughly: everyone knows the standard pairing, all-affected vs all-subjected/all-coerced, and everyone knows the standard complaints (too wide, too narrow, unbounded, arbitrary). I want to argue those complaints are all pointed at the wrong feature of the two principles. Here's the draft.

---

**The index problem**

*1.* Both the all-affected principle and the all-subjected principle are usually criticised along the same axis: they pick out the wrong set of people. All-affected lets in foreigners, future generations, and arguably every sentient being downstream of any decision, so it dissolves the demos into humanity. All-subjected is too stingy about people coerced indirectly, or too generous about tourists who are technically subject to the traffic code for a fortnight. Fine. But both criticisms assume the principles are answering a well-posed question and just getting the extension wrong.

*2.* The real problem is in the argument place, not the extension. Call it the index problem: both principles define membership by a variable — being affected, being subjected — whose value is itself a function of what the demos, once constituted, goes on to decide. Affectedness is not a set you can consult before drawing the boundary. It is produced by the boundary and by the decisions taken inside it. Whether a given choice counts as one decision or a nested family of decisions depends on how the polity has already organised itself; a currency union makes foreign central bankers' meetings into events that affect you; a federal structure converts what would have been a national question into a regional one; an immigration policy retroactively creates a class of residents who are subject but not members. So the criterion and the thing it's supposed to be applied to move together.

*3.* Which is why the debate feels interminable rather than merely unresolved. Each side can always produce a case the other excludes, and neither can say in a principled way whether the case is inside or outside, because whether the case exists at all — whether those people are affected, whether they're subject in the relevant sense — is downstream of the answer being argued for. The disagreement isn't about where to draw the line. It's about the fact that the line's location changes the terrain it's being drawn on.

*4.* Two live cases. For the 2018 New Caledonia independence referendum, the franchise was a special roll created under the 1998 Nouméa Accord: you qualified by being born there, or by twenty years of continuous residence, or by having arrived before a cutoff tied to the late 1980s — and about 17% of the ordinary electoral roll, roughly 36,000 of 210,000 registered voters, were excluded. The exclusion wasn't a mistake anyone made; it was the point. The body being constituted was supposed to be the people the previous hundred years of settlement had wronged, and the only way to specify that was to freeze a snapshot of who had been there long enough to count. The Scottish independence referendum of 2014 pulled in the opposite direction: it ran off the local government register, which enfranchised 16- and 17-year-olds and resident EU and Commonwealth nationals, while Scots who had moved to London got no vote at all on the fate of their own country. Same underlying question, two incompatible operationalisations, and in both cases the choice of roll was doing substantive political work that no principle of affectedness could have dictated in advance.

*5.* Proposal: stop looking for a criterion and specify a procedure instead. Membership is current enrolment plus standing to contest. Anyone who can show that a decision altered the set of people that decision affects acquires enrolment for subsequent rounds. The rule doesn't tell you who's in — it can't, for the reason in *2* — but it gives you a way of being wrong about who's in, and a way of correcting the error prospectively, without reopening a decision already taken. That last bit matters: it's what stops the account from collapsing into the all-affected principle's retroactive imperialism, where every past decision is illegitimate because someone now-affected wasn't consulted.

*6.* The worry I can see: manufactured affectedness. If inclusion is earned by demonstrating that a decision touched you, actors have an incentive to make themselves touchable — to arrange their affairs so that a jurisdiction's choices run through them, and thereby to acquire standing. I don't yet know whether that's a bug or just the ordinary cost of any dynamic franchise.
