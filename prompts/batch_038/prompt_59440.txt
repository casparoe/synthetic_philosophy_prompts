so a friend of mine who works on religious epistemology keeps telling me my position on Hume on miracles is unstable, and before I concede anything I want a second opinion. Here's what I actually hold, written out so it can be checked rather than argued with:

1. A miracle is an event brought about by a superanatural agent that goes against the laws of nature. I want the violation language, not just "an event we cant currently explain."
2. But I'm a Humean about laws. Laws are the simplest, most informative systematization of the whole pattern of particular events — a summary of what happens, not something that governs or pushes anything around.
3. Hume's maxim is right: testimony establishes a miracle only where it would be more of a miracle for the testimony to be mistaken or false than for the event to have actually happened. And I read that as a genuine in-principle constraint on what testimony can do, not as a piece of practical advice.
4. I don't buy the question-begging objection. The complaint that Hume is just assuming miracles never happen, so of course the evidence always lands against them, seems to me to miss what he's doing, which is an ordinary inductive generalization from the observed record. Nothing sneakier than that.
5. I'm a Bayesian about all of this. The prior that any given miracle report is veridical is tiny but nonzero, and I also think no finite prior is safe from sufficiently overwhelming evidence — so nothing logically impossible about testimony establishing a miracle.
6. In fact nothing comes close. The reports are late, anonymous where it matters, produced by people culturally primed to see religious significance in odd events, mutually inconsistent, and from witnesses with every motive to embellish. This part of Hume's argument is the part that does the real work for me.
7. I think Hume's own hypothetical supports me here: he says that if every author in every language agreed there had been a total darkness over the whole earth for eight days starting in January 1600, and the tradition stayed strong and uncontradicted, we should accept it and then go looking for the causes. So he himself allows that maximally uniform testimony can establish something wildly extraordinary.
8. If I saw a miracle with my own eyes I'd be within my rights to believe it. His argument is about testimony and I don't think it's meant to reach direct perception.

Audit this. Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly — I want them ranked by severity, not dumped on me as a flat list where everything is equally incoherent. Which pairs only look like they clash and dissolve once you spell them out, because I assume there's at least one of those and I don't know which. For each real tension, tell me what has to give and what it costs me to give it up. And then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most, since I'd much rather make one big change than six little ones. I'm deliberately not saying which of the eight I'd be most willing to drop, so don't try to guess at my preferences. And I'm not after a literature survey or a list of things to go read — just the audit of my own set.
