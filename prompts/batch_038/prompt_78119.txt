I am in the second term of an epistemology seminar whose current unit is the value problem, and I would like a careful audit of an analogy that has come to dominate our discussions.

The classmate I argue with most often presses what I will call the coffee-machine case. A good cup of coffee brewed by a reliable machine is no better, as a cup, than an equally good cup that fell out of a temperamental machine which happened to work this morning; once the good cup is in front of you, the reliability of its source has nothing further to contribute, because whatever value it had has been transferred into, and exhausted by, the product. Belief, he says, works on exactly the same pattern: once a belief is true, the reliability of the process that produced it has already handed over its value, so knowledge cannot be worth more than mere true belief. He draws two conclusions from this. The first is that the Meno problem is a pseudo-problem generated by a confusion about instrumental value. The second is a practical corollary he is rather pleased with: if reliability adds nothing over the product, then a curriculum that drills students toward correct answers without attending to how they arrive at them is not epistemically second-best, and those who insist on understanding over accuracy are trading in a feeling rather than a value.

When I objected, I offered my own image: knowledge is like a well-tended garden and true belief like a lucky wild harvest, since the garden goes on producing and the harvest is finished in a season. My tutor's reply was that my analogy simply builds my conclusion into the picture — I selected a source whose distinguishing feature is precisely the durability I want knowledge to have.

So, first, lay out the correspondence in the coffee-machine case item by item: what occupies the place of the machine, the cup, the act of drinking, the standard by which the cup counts as good, and the point at which its value is fixed. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his two conclusions from those that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in whether the analogy presupposes that the value of a belief is settled once and for all at the moment of formation, and whether that presupposition, rather than any feature of coffee, is carrying the argument. Tell me plainly whether the pseudo-problem conclusion and the curricular conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether they stand or fall together — it would not surprise me if one went and the other did not.

Two further questions. Are Socrates' own two images in the dialogue — the person who directs travellers correctly to Larissa without ever having made the journey, and the statues that must be tethered or they run off — simply earlier versions of the coffee-machine argument, or do they differ from it in a way that matters? And what conditions would any analogy in this debate have to meet in order to escape my tutor's charge? Is a non-question-begging picture available here at all, or must every analogy offered for the comparative value of knowledge and true belief already contain an answer to the question it is supposed to help settle?
