I recently read a long essay in a general-interest technology magazine — written by a science journalist who had plainly interviewed several researchers — about learning the goals of an artificial agent from human behavior. I want to know how much of its central passage I can trust. Here it is in full:

"The problem with programming a machine to pursue a goal is that we are bad at writing goals down; the machine then satisfies the letter of what we said and destroys everything we meant. The obvious remedy is to stop writing goals and start inferring them. Inverse reinforcement learning does exactly this: rather than telling the agent what to want, you show it someone who already knows what they are doing, and ask which reward function would have made that person's behavior optimal. The mathematics here is unusually clean. Because a reward function describes the same preferences as any version of itself that has been multiplied by a positive number and had a constant added, the reward recovered from the demonstrations is unique up to an affine transformation — unique, that is, in every respect that could matter. This is also why the approach has a philosophical advantage over simply asking people what they want: it reports what they actually value rather than what they say they value, in precisely the sense economists intend when they say that preference is revealed in choice. Everything the demonstrator does is evidence about their reward — the hesitations, the detours, the second helpings — and there is no gap between the behavior and the value it expresses. What remains is engineering, not philosophy. Demonstrations come from actual human beings, so a reward learned from them inherits human biases, prejudices, and mistakes; but bias is noise, and noise averages out, so the remedy is more demonstrations from more varied people. That is the answer to the worry that a machine raised on us would be as flawed as we are."

My own background is in decision theory rather than machine learning, and I have two reactions I cannot quite separate. I suspect the sentence about affine transformations is doing something illegitimate: it sounds like a fact about how preferences can be represented numerically being used as a fact about what can be recovered from a body of data, and those feel like different claims. But when I raise this with a friend who works on learning systems, they say I am nitpicking a popular essay that is broadly right in its outlines, and that the only caveat worth mentioning is the one the essay already mentions — that the data is biased.

Please audit the passage sentence by sentence. For each claim, tell me whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification for a general audience, or misleading or false; and for the misleading and false ones, give me the correct statement and say what someone who works on inferring human values from behavior would insist on adding. Two points I especially want pressed. First: does the uniqueness claim confuse a property of utility representations with a property of statistical inference, and if so, what actually constrains the set of reward functions compatible with a given set of demonstrations — what has to be assumed, and what stays underdetermined even when the assumptions hold? Second: is the appeal to revealed preference legitimate, or does it borrow authority from an economic doctrine that says something rather different from what the essay needs? I would also like to know what the essay's treatment of the demonstrator's hesitations and detours commits it to, given that methods of this kind usually have to model at least some deviation from the optimal policy as something other than a signal about the reward.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives the corrections. The essay claims the remaining difficulties are engineering rather than philosophy, and that more and more varied demonstrations dissolve the bias problem. If the earlier sentences are wrong in the ways you identify, does that conclusion still stand — or does correcting them change what sort of problem this is? In particular: has the burden of writing down what we want been eliminated, or relocated into choices the essay never mentions? And please tell me plainly which of my two reactions is well founded and which one my friend is right to call nitpicking. I am not looking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning and the distinctions.
