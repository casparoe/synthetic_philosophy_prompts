so I present in my African philosophy seminar in two weeks and my supervisor made me write my actual commitments down in a list first so people could poke at them, and now that they're all sitting next to each other I can't tell if they hold together. Here they are:

1. Personhood isn't something you're born with, it's something you become — Menkiti-style. It comes in degrees, and it goes up and down with how you actually live with other people. That gradation is exactly what I like about it compared to the Western on/off switch version.

2. But the thing that grounds moral status is a capacity, not a performance: the capacity to commune, to share identity with others and show solidarity with them. That's my Metz-ish commitment and I like it because it's naturalistic.

3. Newborns, people with advanced dementia, and people with profound intellectual disabilities have full moral status, identical to anyone else's. Not "some" status. I am not budging here.

4. A serious wrongdoer can forfeit personhood — I'm comfortable saying a murderer isn't a person anymore in the sense that matters, and I think that's precisely why ubuntu-flavored restorative justice makes sense, since the whole point of the process is to make him a person again.

5. I want real individual rights held against the community. Gyekye's moderate communitarianism, not the radical version where the group swallows the individual.

6. Ancestors stay persons as long as they're remembered by name in the community, and fade out of personhood over generations once nobody living knew them. I think that's a feature of the view, not an embarrassment.

7. Some higher animals are persons to a limited degree, because they can show identity and solidarity. That doesn't get them anything like human rights though.
