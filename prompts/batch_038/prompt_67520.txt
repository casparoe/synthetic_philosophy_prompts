You are a philosopher specialising in judgment aggregation and collective rationality. Think the problem through carefully before answering, and open with a short, informal sketch of the possible lines of response before committing to one of them. Please write your answer in British English, and argue from analysis of the case rather than from citations or reading lists.

A nine-member commission must decide whether to license a new medical device. Under its charter, licensing requires the device to be safe, effective, and cost-justified; safety is in turn defined as requiring both that the observed failure rate fall below a specified threshold and that adequate post-market monitoring be in place. The ballots divide as follows: seven commissioners judge the failure rate to be below the threshold, seven judge monitoring to be adequate, six judge the device effective, and seven judge it cost-justified — but only three support licensing. Majority voting applied premise by premise, at both levels of the charter's hierarchy, licenses the device; majority voting applied directly to the licensing question refuses it.

Argue that this nesting of the doctrinal paradox inside itself, far from being a curiosity that leaves the standard defence of premise-based aggregation intact, decisively undermines it. Defend the claim that a procedure which tracks reasons at every level of the charter's conceptual hierarchy can issue a verdict endorsed by only a third of the body, and that this amounts to a reductio of the idea that fidelity to reasons is the same thing as fidelity to the group's judgment — a distinction the debate routinely blurs. Then confront the two strongest objections still open to the defender: that commissioners who accept the charter's definitions have already agreed to be bound by its premises, so their holistic verdict carries no residual authority; and that conclusion-based aggregation is simply less reason-responsive, and therefore cannot serve as the fallback. Finish by drawing out what your argument implies for how such commissions ought in fact to be designed — including whether the level at which voting takes place should itself be put to a vote, and what happens if it is.
