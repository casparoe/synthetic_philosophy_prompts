so I have zero econ training — I run a distribution center outside Columbus, ~60 people, been there 14 years — and I've been reading about markets on and off for a while and sort of assembled a position for myself. Before I keep repeating it at Thanksgiving I'd like someone to tell me if it actually hangs together. My brother in law thinks it's a pile of mush and he might be right, but he never says where.

here's what I believe:

1. prices are basically information. the fact that a bunch of strangers all buy and sell is how stuff nobody's in charge of gets known — how much insulation is available in Ohio in March, what a used trailer is really worth. I find this genuinely convincing, more convincing than any argument I've heard for central planning.

2. so with my retirement money I don't pick anything. index funds, all of it. if somebody's willing to sell me a share of a company, on average they know something I don't, and I'm not going to kid myself otherwise.

3. I read about a thing called a no-trade theorem, I think. my understanding is it says if everyone's rational and everyone starts from the same basic view of the world, two people shouldn't want to trade on their private information at all — because the fact that the other guy will take the other side is itself evidence he knows something, so you should just cancel out and not trade. this struck me as obviously right and kind of devastating.

4. except I do think I have an edge in one narrow area. I know freight. I can tell from how full our trailers are, what our customers are ordering, and which carriers are calling me begging for loads, whether a certain packaging or logistics company's quarter is going to be good, weeks before the number comes out. I keep that in a separate little account from retirement and I've done alright.

5. I don't think 4 contradicts 2 or 3, beacuse the market isn't a person. it's just the pile of everybody's decisions, and a lot of those decisions are dumb, and all I'm doing is standing on the correct side of the dumb ones.

6. I also think insider trading laws are stupid. if a CEO knows the quarter's bad, letting him sell pushes the price down sooner, which is 1 working the way it's supposed to.

7. on the other hand I do think prices can be badly wrong for years at a time. housing 2005 to 2007. I'm not just saying that in hindsight, I remember guys at the bank telling me the numbers made no sense while they kept writing the loans.

8. but I still think trying to find and bet against the wrongness is a losers game for a guy like me, and I'd rather own the whole haystack.

9. method thing, this one matters to me: I distrust any argument that only works if people are stupid. if your story needs most market participants to be idiots, I figure the idiots get cleaned out and replaced and then your story stops working. I want my view to be one that still holds up even if everybody in it is pretty sharp.

10. one more. a guy on a forum told me my whole position collapses because if prices already contain all the information, nobody would spend money finding information out, since there'd be nothing left to profit from — so prices could never contain it in the first place. I found this worrying and I've been treating it as the weak point in what I believe.

so — go through these and tell me which ones are actually pulling against each other and which ones only look like they are. I keep assuming 10 is my biggest problem, so tell me if that's wrong. rank the real tensions by how bad they are, and for each one say what I'd have to give up and what that costs me, and then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I'm not attached to any particular item, I just want to know which ones are load bearing.
