I work on chemical regulation and I keep getting told by economists that our pesticide rules are irrational, and I don't think the argument is anywhere near as clean as they make it out to be. Here's the practice, roughly: under EU law an active substance is refused approval outright if it falls into certain hazard categories — carcinogenic, mutagenic, toxic for reproduction, persistent/bioaccumulative/toxic, endocrine-disrupting — and that refusal doesn't turn on how much of the stuff anyone is actually exposed to, except for a couple of narrow derogations that mostly trade on negligible exposure. For a substance outside those categories the same law does exactly the textbook thing: derive a threshold dose from the toxicology, estimate exposure from the proposed uses, require the second to sit under the first. So exposure matters hugely for one set of chemicals and not at all for another, and the line between them is drawn by *kind* of harm rather than by amount. The same shape shows up elsewhere, e.g. the old US rule that a colour additive shown to cause cancer can't be approved at any dose, so I'd rather not treat it as a European quirk.

What theory of rational choice under risk does that split presuppose? My half-formed suspicion is that people say "the precautionary principle" and stop there, and that the live question is whether the cut-off half is a piece of risk-weighted expected utility — a decision-maker with a very concave risk function who treats the worst outcomes as counting for far more than their probability licenses — or something else entirely: a deontological constraint, a claim that the relevant probabilities simply aren't estimable for novel chemicals, a claim about non-consenting future people, or just an allocation of the burden of proof with no decision-theoretic content whatsoever. And here's the bit that actually puzzles me. Risk-weighted EU (Buchak's is the standard version) is normally motivated as a theory about how *one person* should weigh *her own* gambles. Is a regulator allowed to run a concave risk function on behalf of a population most of whose members, asked individually, would happily take the gamble? I have a vague memory of an argument that a non-linear risk function can't survive aggregation across people like that — that it collides with something in the neighbourhood of ex ante Pareto. If that's right, the cut-off criteria might not be reconstructible as risk aversion even in principle, and they'd have to be grounded some other way.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, name which rival theories would license the same rule, and keep what the practice strictly requires sharply apart from what merely fits it comfortably. Then tell me whether the agency is in flat contradiction with the exposure-threshold half of its own law and with the expected-value reasoning it plainly does everywhere else — residue limits, road safety, impact assessments where a statistical life gets priced and traded against money — or whether one theory can hold both halves. Finally: if you swapped the whole thing for a risk-neutral expected-utility regulator, what specifically changes? Not "more chemicals get allowed" — which decisions, which categories, which steps of the procedure disappear, and where the burden of proof moves.

Flag the places where you're making an interpretive choice about the practice rather than reporting agreement.
