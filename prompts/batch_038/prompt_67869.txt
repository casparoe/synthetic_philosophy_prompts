Last night my friend Daniel and I spent something like three hours on a question that turned out to be at least three questions, and we resolved none of them. It began with Kripke's suggestion that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg, or from a different pair of gametes at all. Daniel, who works in developmental genetics, said this is both false and uninteresting: on his account a sperm is a delivery vehicle for a haploid genome and an egg is a cytoplasmic, mitochondrial and signalling package, so the moment you say which of those components is supposed to be doing the individuating, he can build a case in which that component is preserved and I am plainly absent, or altered and I am plainly present. I replied that whatever the embryology, the person in his case is not me. By two in the morning we had established only that we were disagreeing about three separable things: which item in a person's origin is supposed to be the one that matters, how much slack a claim of that kind admits before it ceases to be a claim about the same individual, and whether transworld identity is even the right apparatus for the question. We run a reading group together, and we agreed the productive move is to stop arguing and instead sort the room.

So, acting as a metaphysician who works on modality and the philosophy of biology and who has built diagnostic instruments of this kind before, please write me a twelve-question sorting quiz — which view of origins do you actually hold — for an audience of eleven: mostly philosophy graduate students, plus two people from a genetics department who will notice instantly if a laboratory procedure has been described carelessly. Every question must be a concrete case with two or three answer options, written in the language of the case and answerable by someone who has never heard of the debate; questions may ask what could or might have happened, but none may ask whether an origin is essential or necessary, none may invoke possible worlds or transworld identity, and none may name a position. Build at least six questions out of the following materials, and describe them precisely enough to survive Daniel's scrutiny: whether I could have been conceived from the particular gametes that produced my older brother; pro-nuclear transfer, in which the pronuclei of one couple's freshly fertilised zygote are moved into an enucleated zygote from another couple, so that the resulting person has one couple's nuclear genome and another's mitochondria and cytoplasm; gametes differentiated in a dish from my own somatic cells and then used to conceive a child; an embryo that splits at day four, so that two people appear to share one origin; a sperm that differs from the one that actually fertilised the egg by a single point mutation, and then a chain of such near-misses; a child produced from the nucleus of one of my skin cells; and an embryo conceived in 1994, cryopreserved, and born in 2024. Think carefully and work in stages: first sketch the logical space in half a page — the axes along which answers vary and the combinations those axes generate — and only then construct the instrument out of that sketch rather than out of a list of views you happen to recall. At least one question should be one where the answer a consistent position requires is deeply counterintuitive, so that the quiz actually bites.

The scoring key matters more to me than the questions. Present it as a two-dimensional grid — one axis for what the respondent's answers treat as the origin that does the individuating work, one for how much substitution they tolerate before the person is gone — and locate a named position at each occupied region, including at minimum a strict particular-gamete view, a view on which the zygote as a structured whole is what matters, a genome-centred view, a graded or tolerant version of origin essentialism, the deflationary move that dissolves the question by denying transworld identity in favour of counterparts, and the outright denial that origin does any individuative work at all; add any further leaves you think the space demands. Then tell me about the cells nobody occupies: for each, say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is theoretically available but repellant, or simply because nobody has explored it. Flag explicitly any pair of positions your twelve questions cannot separate, rather than pretending the instrument is finer than it is, and mark which questions are load-bearing and which are consistency checks. Close with a two- or three-sentence profile for each leaf — what the respondent's pattern commits them to, and the single most awkward consequence they now own. No bibliography, reading list, or citations; names of views or people are welcome only where they do diagnostic work. Finally, tell me which one question you expect to do the most sorting, and which one you expect people to answer inconsistently with the rest of their pattern, since that inconsistency is exactly what Daniel and I want to press on.
