I'm covering a 55-minute seminar for second-year cognitive science students next Thursday on whether dreams are experiences at all — the Malcolm/Dennett row. They've had one philosophy lecture so far (qualia, Mary) and one sleep physiology lecture, so they'll turn up sure that REM plus dream reports settles it empirically, and about a third of them keep dream journals and will take the whole thing personally. Don't walk me through the debate, I know the shape of it — plan the seminar for me.

What I need. The order the pieces have to go in and what each one depends on — in particular where 'what would counting as observing a dream even be?' has to sit relative to 'what is a report like the one you make when you say you dreamt you were being chased actually doing?'. The one case that should carry the hour, and what that case has to have and mustn't have — I'm toying with the alarm clock getting woven into the dream, but I suspect it backfires and I can't say why yet. The misconceptions they'll arrive with or pick up on the way ('dreams are just experiences you forget' looks like the big one, 'Malcolm is only playing word games' second, and whatever lucid dreaming is going to do to the room), plus the exact moment in the sequence to head each one off before it sets. Where they decide it's all trivial, and what I say then. And one question I can put to them in the last five minutes that shows whether they've grasped what's at stake rather than which side they fancy.

Two things I haven't worked out myself, so tell me rather than leaving them implicit: whether the row is about what a dream is or about what dream reports mean, and how I keep that distinction visible for an hour without loosing them. Assume a whiteboard, no slides, and an essay on it three weeks later that I want them to be able to write without my having steered them to a verdict. Finally, tell me what to leave out entirely at this level, and what I should announce out loud that I'm leaving out — I'd rather they knew the gaps were deliberate.
