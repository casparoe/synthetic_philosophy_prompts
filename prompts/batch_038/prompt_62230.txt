The Uniform Determination of Death Act, a model act approved in 1981 and since adopted in some form by most American states, defines death disjunctively: an individual who has sustained either "irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or ... irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead," the determination to be made "in accordance with accepted medical standards." I am less interested in that sentence than in the practice built around it, and in what the practice commits us to that the sentence does not say. Four features of it puzzle me.

First, the neurological limb is applied by a bedside protocol whose selectivity looks arbitrary from the outside. The apnea test is mandatory: the ventilator is disconnected and the patient must fail to breathe while arterial carbon dioxide climbs past a set threshold. Yet spinal reflexes — knee jerks, triple flexion, sometimes quite elaborate movements of spinal origin — are expressly permitted and do not vitiate the determination, and residual hypothalamic and posterior pituitary function is not tested for at all. Some patients so declared continue to secrete antidiuretic hormone; there are reports of heart-rate and blood-pressure responses to surgical incision during procurement.

Second, procurement is governed by a dead-donor rule: organs may be taken only from someone already declared dead, and the taking must not itself cause death. In donation after circulatory death, support is withdrawn, the heart stops, the team observes a stand-off of commonly two to five minutes of pulselessness, declares death, and begins. Within that window circulation could in principle be restored; the protocol's contribution is a standing commitment not to restore it.

Third, British law and practice treat irreversible loss of brainstem function alone as death, while American practice requires the entire brain. Each jurisdiction treats the other's determinations as determinations of the very same thing, and the clinical literature moves freely between them.

Fourth, once death has been declared neurologically the body is typically kept ventilated, warm, perfused, and hormonally managed — sometimes for days, and in reported cases of pregnant patients for weeks — precisely so that the organs remain transplantable. The family is told the patient is dead while the chest rises, the skin is warm, and the kidneys are working.

Please tell me what conception of death this practice presupposes. Give me exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no subheadings. Items 1 through 4 should correspond in order to the four features above: for each, state the commitment about death — or about what it is that dies — that the feature requires, name at least one rival account it thereby rules out or strains against, and say whether the feature strictly presupposes that commitment or merely sits comfortably with it and would be licensed by others too. Item 5 should say what would have to change in the practice if the commitment behind the second feature were replaced by its leading rival. You may use technical vocabulary, but state each commitment in plain terms first. And if any of my four descriptions misstates how the practice actually works, or if you think the request is misconceived — that this body of practice is a negotiated compromise with no unified commitments behind it at all — say so plainly in the relevant item instead of answering the question as I have framed it.
