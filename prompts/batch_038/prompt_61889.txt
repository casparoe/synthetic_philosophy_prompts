I am preparing the second take-home problem set for an upper-level undergraduate seminar, "Formal Systems and the Mind," jointly listed between philosophy and cognitive science. The students have already worked through Hofstadter's MU-puzzle and his coinage JOOTSing, for "jumping out of the system," and this set is meant to press on the point at which that material usually stops being examined. I would like you to draft one three-part question built around a rewriting system of my own devising, so that nobody can answer it by reproducing the familiar analysis of the MIU system.

The system will be printed in the problem set exactly as follows. It uses the two-letter alphabet {A, B} and takes `AB` as its sole axiom. Its four rules, each applicable anywhere in a string, are:

1. **Doubling**: from `Ax` infer `Axx`, where `x` is any string, possibly empty. The rule applies only to strings beginning with `A`, and duplicates the entire remainder.
2. **Accretion**: from `x` infer `xAAA`.
3. **Collapse**: from `xBBBy` infer `xAy`.
4. **Annihilation**: from `xAAy` infer `xy`.

The question should then ask students:

- **(a)** [6 marks] Show that neither `A` nor `ABBB` is a theorem. Proofs by exhaustion of the derivations they happened to try will not receive credit; the argument must be an induction on the length of derivations.
- **(b)** [4 marks] Exhibit a derivation of some theorem containing exactly five `B`s, citing the rule used at each line.
- **(c)** [15 marks] Evaluate the following reflection, appended by a hypothetical student who has just completed (a) and (b): *"The system can never produce `ABBB`, yet I can see that it never will. I started out inside the system, shuffling its strings by its own rules, and then I stepped outside it and established something about every string it could ever generate. A program implementing these four rules could only keep generating; it could never see what I saw. That is what jumping out of the system means, and it is why a mind cannot be a formal system."*

Part (c) should require the student to say what the reflection gets right about the relationship between rule-following and the invariant proof; to identify and explain at least two distinct inferences in the passage that do not hold; to say whether the situation would differ in kind if `ABBB` were replaced by a Gödel sentence for a system able to express elementary arithmetic; and to address what follows from the fact that the meta-level argument can be written down, taught, checked, and used as a pruning condition in a search procedure.

Please supply three things. First, the question as it will appear to the students, including the system specification above. Second, a model answer of roughly 900 words, in which the induction for (a) and the line-by-line derivation for (b) are both fully worked out and correct. Third, a marking scheme totalling 25 marks that assigns marks to named moves rather than to topics.

The marking scheme must in particular do the following. It should state how many marks are lost by a student who proves only that every theorem contains at least one `B` — a claim that is true and that blocks the first target in (a) but not the second — as against a student who isolates the residue-class invariant. It should name the specific error of conjecturing that the number of `B`s in a theorem is always a power of two, and say what in part (b) refutes it. It should specify the marks available for observing that derivability in this system is semi-decidable by exhaustive search while non-derivability is not, and for explaining why that asymmetry concerns halting rather than insight. For part (c), it should distinguish an A answer from a B answer by naming the moves each must contain: for instance, whether the candidate separates moving from a formal system to a stronger system containing it from escaping every formal system altogether; whether the candidate recognises that the reflection inverts rather than expresses Hofstadter's own use of the term; whether the candidate notes that the reflection, if sound, would undermine the possibility of grading it by following a marking scheme; and whether the candidate avoids treating any rule-governed system's blind spots as though they were instances of incompleteness. Finally, list the mistakes that should cost marks, with the deduction for each.

Students may not consult external sources, and the question must be answerable from the specification printed above together with the seminar's discussion of the MU-puzzle and of the Lucas-style argument; require no citations, bibliography, or recall of the exact rules of Hofstadter's system.
