I am a senior analyst in a state human-services agency, and I have been asked to prepare background material for a legislative committee that is about to redesign our low-income benefit programs. My training is in public administration rather than philosophy, but I have become convinced that the design choices in front of the committee are not merely technical, and I would like a philosophically literate reconstruction of what they commit us to. Please take the administrative facts below as stipulated by me; I am not asking you to verify them, and I do not want references, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading.

The two design choices at issue. First, nearly all of our programs are means-tested against a household's current income, and several of them terminate eligibility at a threshold rather than reducing the benefit gradually. On our own modeling, a single parent with two children who accepts a raise from seventeen to eighteen dollars an hour can lose a childcare subsidy worth substantially more than the raise, and end up materially worse off for having accepted it. Second, the legislature has recently converted several of these cliffs into tapers: the benefit is now withdrawn at a constant rate — in some programs fifty cents per additional dollar earned, in others eighty — until it reaches zero. Once that taper is combined with payroll taxes and the phase-out of the federal earned-income credit, the combined withdrawal rate facing that same parent across a wide earnings range approaches eighty cents on the dollar, which is more than double the top statutory rate applied to a high earner's last dollar.

Two features of this arrangement puzzle me, and they are what I want the paper to address.

The first concerns the reform itself. A taper is not an unambiguous improvement on a cliff, and I want that trade made explicit rather than assumed away. A taper rescues households just above the threshold, but it also withdraws something from households further up who, under the cliff, would have been past the cutoff and lost nothing at all. The reform therefore takes from relatively better-off working households in order to improve the position of worse-off ones, and it does so by extending a high marginal withdrawal rate across a much wider band of earnings than the cliff ever reached. I do not know how to characterize that trade in the vocabulary of justice, and I suspect the answer depends on assumptions nobody in the room has articulated.

The second concerns an asymmetry in how the same political culture describes the two ends of the rate schedule. High marginal rates at the top of the income distribution are routinely denounced as confiscatory, as an obstacle to freedom and to the planning of a life, and as a taking of what people have earned. Effective marginal rates of seventy or eighty percent at the bottom are described, at most, as an administrative glitch — an incentive problem to be smoothed over, never as a question of justice. No member of the committee has suggested that a poor parent facing an eighty percent marginal withdrawal rate is being treated as unjustly as a wealthy taxpayer facing an eighty percent rate would be. I want to know what theory of distributive justice, if any, makes that asymmetry principled rather than merely rhetorical.

For context on the alternative: several Western European systems do not build family support around a means test at all. One large system consolidated several separate means-tested benefits into a single monthly payment, reduced at one constant rate as earnings rise above a fixed disregard, and sold it to the public on the ground that work should always pay. Others simply pay a child allowance to every household regardless of income, out of general revenue, and run their contribution-based social insurance alongside it. Ours is the outlier in relying on the threshold test, and I want that difference treated as a philosophical difference rather than a budgetary one.

Please write the paper in the following sections, in this order, and keep close to the lengths given.

1. The problem restated (about 150 words). Describe the practice and the puzzle in the vocabulary of distributive justice, in your own terms rather than mine, and state in one or two sentences what you take the central philosophical question to be.

2. What the cliff presupposes (about 350 words). What has to be true about the currency of justice, and about the site at which justice applies, for a hard income cutoff to be the natural instrument? Does the threshold structure presuppose that justice is concerned with where people end up rather than with the shape of the choices they face? Does it presuppose a sufficientarian view on which nothing about the gradient immediately above the line is anyone's business? Does the means test additionally presuppose something about desert, stigma, or the moral significance of not needing help?

3. What the taper presupposes (about 300 words). Explain how the commitments change when the cliff becomes a constant withdrawal rate, and whether the taper expresses a different theory or the same theory applied more carefully. Address the trade I described: the taper helps households nearer the threshold at the expense of households further above it, across a wider band. Which families of views can endorse that trade, which cannot, and is there any view on which the taper is worse than the cliff it replaces?

4. Views the design rules out (about 350 words). Take the major theories in turn and say whether each could license a means-tested, threshold-based benefit system with steep withdrawals at the bottom: a difference-principle view, prioritarianism, luck egalitarianism, sufficientarianism, a historical entitlement view, and a view on which equality of opportunity is the whole of the subject. Distinguish sharply between what the practice strictly presupposes — what it could not survive without — and what merely fits it comfortably.

5. Where the practice is underdetermined (about 250 words). Identify the points at which several incompatible theories would generate exactly this design, so that the committee cannot read our commitments off the design alone. Then say what further evidence about how the practice operates — who lobbies for the thresholds, how the lines move over time, how caseworkers exercise discretion — would help settle which theory we are in fact running.

6. The transatlantic difference (about 300 words). Say what the choice between a targeted residual system and a universal or insurance-based one presupposes about why inequality is objectionable at all: whether the objection is to deprivation as such, to unchosen brute luck, to relations of domination and status, to the erosion of a shared civic condition, or to something else. Assess whether the American attachment to the means test expresses a defensible view or an unstated one about who may receive anything. Also take up a claim I often hear from colleagues, that narrowly targeted programs end up less generous because the median taxpayer sees nothing in them, and tell me whether that is a moral argument or an empirical prediction — and whether it changes what our design presupposes.

7. Is the asymmetry defensible? (about 300 words). Give the strongest reconstruction available of the position that a high marginal rate on a wealthy earner is a serious injustice while a comparably high effective marginal rate on a poor earner is a technical defect. Consider whether it can be grounded in property, in the baseline against which rates are measured, in what each party is asked to give up, or in the difference between a rate set by law and a rate that emerges from the interaction of several programs. Then say plainly whether the position survives, and if it does not, whether the honest conclusion is that our benefit schedule is unjust or that marginal rates are not the kind of thing justice evaluates.

8. What would have to change (about 250 words). Suppose the committee accepted that the marginal schedule is itself part of the subject matter of distributive justice. Describe concretely what would have to be surrendered in a means-tested system — eligibility tests, budget predictability, targeting, and the political coalition that currently sustains the programs — and say whether any real design could satisfy the new constraint, or whether accepting it commits the state to abandoning means testing altogether.

Mark clearly where you are reconstructing rather than describing, and do not soften your conclusion if it turns out that the practice is philosophically incoherent.
