Consider the puzzle that goes by the name of the Judy Benjamin problem (van Fraassen). A scout has been dropped into territory divided into four quarters — north-west, north-east, south-west, south-east — and has no idea where the enemy company is, so her prior is uniform: 1/4 on each quarter. Radio headquarters then transmits two reports: that the probability the company is in the north is 1/2, and that if the company is in the north, the probability that it is in the north-east is 3/4. (Jeffrey conditioning on the partition north/south satisfies the first report but leaves the probability of north-east given north at 1/2, flatly ignoring the second, so something more is needed.)

My view about testimony of this shape is as follows. Reports of the form "the probability of B given A is q" are hard constraints on the posterior, in the same way a reported probability for a member of a partition is a hard constraint; and among all probability functions satisfying the constraints, the rational posterior is the one closest to the prior in the sense of maximum entropy — equivalently here, minimum relative entropy from the prior, which is uniform. For Judy Benjamin this yields 1/8 on north-west, 3/8 on north-east, and 1/4 on each southern quarter. Two features of that output are what recommend the rule: the constraint set fixes the north completely, and the rule then leaves the south as it was, since headquarters said nothing bearing on it; and no ad hoc tie-breaking is required, because entropy has a unique maximum over the constraint set. For non-uniform priors the rule generalizes by minimizing cross-entropy relative to the prior instead of maximizing entropy outright. I also hold the rule to be context-invariant: it does not matter who headquarters is, how reliable they are, or why they phrased the report as a conditional.

I want a map of this view's exposure — what would have to be true, or what would have to happen, for it to be wrong. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a defender of the rule should concede it or dismiss it. Say what the rule predicts that its rivals do not, and whether it risks anything at all: is there any possible development that would show it mistaken rather than merely unsupported? If in practice every complaint can be absorbed by re-describing the rule — a different divergence, a different construal of what the testimony constrained — tell me plainly that it has been insulated, and whether insulation of that kind is a defect in a rule of this sort or a respectable feature of a normative principle.

To keep this from being generic, please work through the following probes with the arithmetic shown.

1. Entropy or symmetry? I suspect that in this very case the rival minimal-change measures — reverse relative entropy, squared Euclidean distance, Hellinger — all select the same posterior, because the constraints already fix the north and the residual freedom in the south is symmetric. Confirm or refute this. If it holds, the celebrated case provides no support for entropy specifically; say then whether any nearby variant makes the choice of divergence matter, and what is left of the rule's distinctiveness if none does.

2. Breaking independence. The uniform prior is equally describable as two independent binary variables, north/south and east/west, each 1/2. Read the report as preserving that independence and the posterior becomes 1/8 north-west, 3/8 north-east, 1/8 south-west, 3/8 south-east. My rule instead destroys the prior independence: it makes the probability of east given south 1/2 while the probability of east given north is 3/4. Which response is the more conservative, and on what grounds? Does my rule have a principled answer, or does an appeal to closeness in entropy beg the question against independence preservation?

3. Silence. My rule treats headquarters' silence about the south as leaving southern probabilities untouched. But if the company's position is the outcome of a single chance process, learning that east is likelier than west in the north should bear on the south. Construct the strongest version of this objection, including a concrete generative model of how the company came to be where it is in which my posterior is demonstrably wrong, and say whether a rule forbidden from consulting the etiology of its inputs can meet it.

4. The conditional alone. I calculate that if headquarters transmits only the conditional report and says nothing about the probability of north, maximizing entropy subject to that single constraint does not leave the probability of north at 1/2: it moves it down to roughly 0.467, giving about 0.351 on north-east, 0.117 on north-west, and 0.266 on each southern quarter. Verify this. If it is right, explain how a rule advertised as changing as little as possible can shift a quantity it received no information about, in a direction fixed by nothing but the form of the entropy functional — and whether that is fatal or merely awkward.

5. Non-uniform priors. Run the generalized rule on a prior of 0.45 north-west, 0.15 north-east, 0.25 south-west, 0.15 south-east, given the same two reports. Report the posterior and assess it: is anything about the result indefensible, and does the generalization retain the two claimed virtues — no ad hoc tie-breaking, and no disturbance where testimony is silent? Also say whether the rule's output depends on the order in which the two reports arrive, or on whether the constraints are packaged as conditional probabilities or as joint probabilities of the quarters.

6. Decision-theoretic bite. Suppose Judy can move to intercept in the south-west, which pays off only if the company is there: probability 1/4 on my posterior, 1/8 on the independence-preserving rival. Is that gap enough to flip any reasonable choice? If not, does the dispute collapse into a verbal one; if so, name the act that separates the two rules most sharply.

7. Testimony as evidence. A strict Bayesian treats the transmission as an event to conditionalize on, reflecting headquarters' reliability and their reasons for speaking, and will generally land on a posterior satisfying neither constraint exactly. State what my view loses by refusing this, whether the loss is real or an idealization, and where my context-invariance claim is doing defensive work rather than explanatory work.

If you happen to recall how van Fraassen himself resolved the case, or how later writers have proposed to resolve it, say whether my rule agrees with theirs — but do not supply attributions, titles, dates, or references you are not confident of, and do not let the assessment rest on them. Everything needed is in the prompt above; work from that.
