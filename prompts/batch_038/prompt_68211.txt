I've been going in circles on something and I figure there's a principle hiding underneath my intuitions, so here are my verdicts case by case. Context: I mess around with a lot of used gear — water testing kits, thermometers, espresso stuff, old vans — so I'm constantly checking an instrument against something I know some other way. I gather this whole area has a name, something about bootstrapping, but I don't want the name, I want my cases handled.

1. Bought a van with a fuel gauge I don't trust. I fill the tank to exactly half — pump shuts off, I did the math from the tank spec — and the needle sits on half. Do I now have reason to think the gauge is reliable? I say basically no. Like, a tiny tiny bit, but nowhere near enough that I'd plan a trip around it.

2. Same structure, used thermometer, except I check it at twelve known points: ice bath, boiling water corrected for my altitude, a block certified at 37.0, and so on. It nails all twelve. Now I say yes, that's real evidence, and I'd trust it at 62 degrees even though I never tested 62.

3. My spam filter. I only ever look at the mail it flagged, and sure enough all of it is spam. I conclude the filter is good. Verdict: half fine. I've learned it isn't crying wolf, but I've learned literaly nothing about the spam it waves through, because I never see that pile.

4. My roommate runs the twelve-point check on the thermometer himself and reads me the numbers off. Verdict: no different from case 2. I get the same evidence whether I'm holding the thermometer or not.

5. A kitchen scale I already know reads 5 grams high (certified weight, last year). It reads 105 on my sample. I conclude the sample is 100 grams. Obviously fine, I know that.

6. Here's the one that breaks my brain. Different scale, new to me, no certified weight in the house. It reads 105 on a sample that I know from a lab report is 100 grams. I conclude: this scale reads 5 high, so once I subtract 5 it's accurate. Verdict: totally fine, I'd bet on it. But that's the exact same shape as case 1 — one reading, one fact I got independently, conclusion about the instrument — and in case 1 I said you get basically nothing. I cannot tell you what's different. If you can, tell me. If you can't, tell me which of the two verdicts goes.

7. My own eyes. The wall looks red, so it's red, so my color vision is working. Verdict: fine, but weirdly I don't feel like I learned anything about my eyes. Related one: I know that if the wall were white with a red light on it, it'd look exactly the same. I'm happy to say I know the wall is red. I am not happy to say I thereby know there's no red light on it. Those two feel inconsistent and I dont know why.

8. A mechanic has already told me the vans gauge sender is shot. Now I do the same half-tank check, needle on half. Verdict: now it gives me nothing at all, less than case 1, even though the check itself is identical.

Two asks. First, is there a principle — one, or a few clauses — that gets all of these, or as many as it honestly can? I want it precise enough that I could apply it to a device I've never thought about, and I want to know which case motivates each clause. My own tentative version is "you can use a source to confirm it's reliable only when the confirming fact was already known independently of that source," but case 1 satisfies that (the pump is independent of the gauge) and I still say nothing was gained, so it's broken. Repair it or replace it.

Second, run whatever you land on against three cases I haven't listed: (a) my fitness tracker says I slept 7 hours and I independently remember roughly 7 hours; (b) I check the van's gauge fifty times over a year against the pump — half, quarter, three-quarters — and every single check is perfect; (c) the thermometer from case 2 is checked by me, then I use it to check a second thermometer, then I use the second one on real samples. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts above your principle has to overrule. I'd much rather give up one verdict than end up with a baroque thing that has a special clause per case.
