I'm moderating a session of our department's speaker series. The guest defends an account of promissory obligation that reverses the usual order of explanation. On the standard picture, a promise creates a duty owed to the promisee, and her power to release you follows from the duty's being owed *to her*. My guest holds the opposite: what makes a promise binding is that it hands the promisee a standing power to withdraw the demand, so that being under a promissory obligation just is being subject to a demand someone else can extinguish. She is a non-conventionalist — no appeal to practices, rules, or utility — and she rejects assurance-based accounts on which promising is merely one way of generating expectations and reliance.

Draft seven or eight questions for the session, each followed by one follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Nothing generic; every question should turn on the mechanics of her view. At minimum, cover: (a) self-promises, where the release power sits with the person bound, and what survives of the obligation if she can simply let herself off; (b) promises that no one can release — made to someone who has since died or become incapacitated — and where the power goes in those cases; (c) why her release power isn't parasitic on a prior directed duty, which would flip her explanation back to the standard picture; and (d) promises to do something independently wrong, where the promisee's release seems unable to make the act permissible, so the limits on the power must be set from outside it. Leave room for one question that lets her say what her account explains better than the assurance-based alternative — the redundancy case, promising to do what one is already unconditionally required to do, looks like her best example — and close with a question asking what discovery would count against the view.
