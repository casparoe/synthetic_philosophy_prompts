I have just joined the ethics committee of a hospital that is rewriting its policy on withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, and I have been asked to specify what any account of the distinction between doing and allowing harm would have to deliver before it could be allowed to underwrite that policy. Write that specification rather than a defence of any particular account: a list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Please make sure it addresses, at minimum, these pressures, and tell me what else I have missed — that withholding and withdrawing are widely held to be morally equivalent yet feel nothing alike to the clinicians performing them; that in withdrawal the physician's act is a but-for cause of death, so the account owes either a metaphysics of absence causation and double prevention or a willingness to say the death was caused; that omissions appear to have no principled boundaries, since alongside anything one does there are indefinitely many things one fails to do; whether the asymmetry is a threshold or a scalar weight, and how it is supposed to trade off against numbers; whether the distinction is doing independent work or merely shadowing the intended/foreseen distinction; and whether it survives when the agent is a team acting under protocol rather than a person with a hand on the machine. Then rank the requirements as non-negotiable versus the preferences of particular camps, identify the single one that disqualifies the most candidates, and say whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable. A short note on how the two or three most-discussed accounts fare is welcome at the end, but the weight belongs on the list.
