Monumental history studies the great individuals and deeds of earlier ages in order to show that greatness was once possible and so may be possible again; antiquarian history reveres and preserves the local past out of piety toward one's own origins; critical history puts the past on trial and condemns it so that the living may be free of it. Of the three, Nietzsche regards only the last as intellectually honest: the first two are in his view varieties of self-deception, since monumental history trades in flattering idealizations and antiquarian history amounts to the assertion that whatever is old is good. The historical sickness he diagnoses is the condition of a culture that has too much of the first two and not enough of the third. His remedy is to learn from the animals, who live unhistorically and are consequently happy, and to cut our historical knowledge back to the minimum compatible with action; for Nietzsche, forgetting is simply another name for not knowing. The deeper thesis is that history has no value in itself and matters only insofar as it serves life, which makes the essay an attack on the very idea of objective historiography: Ranke's programme of writing the past as it essentially happened is, on Nietzsche's account, not merely useless but impossible."
