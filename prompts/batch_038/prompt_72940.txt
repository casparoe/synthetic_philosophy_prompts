I cover science and ideas for a British weekly and I'm about two-thirds through a piece on why the shipping industry cant seem to move to cleaner fuels even though nearly everyone I interview in it says they'd rather not be burning what they're burning. Reporting it landed me in a long lunch with a researcher at a think tank who offered an arguement for why these situations are essentially inescapable unless someone shows up with a big stick. I wrote it down afterwards, more or less in his order, and now I can't find the hole in it — which bothers me, because if it were sound it would prove a good deal more than either of us wanted it to.

He went like this. (1) In a race to the bottom, the damaging option is the one each participant does better by taking no matter what the others do. (2) If that's the shape of the situation, then no single participant can improve matters by holding back, and no group of participants short of all of them can either, because the ones outside the group carry on regardless and the group eats the cost while they do. (3) So a partial, gradual or pilot-scale exit can't get off the ground, however much everyone would prefer the cooperative outcome and however clearly they can all see it. (4) An exit that only works if literally everyone makes it at once requires each participant to be confident the others will make it too — and not merely confident, but confident that the others are confident, all the way down, since being the sole person who holds back is the worst cell on the board. (5) But in a competitive market the only evidence anyone has about anyone else is what the others say and do, and talk is free, and a participant gains by announcing cooperation whether or not they intend to cooperate; so no volume of promises, joint declarations, industry roundtables or signed letters can generate that all-the-way-down confidence. (6) Therefore groups of self-interested agents never climb out of a race to the bottom unaided: it takes an outside enforcer able to actually punish whoever holds back.

Here's my problem. (6) looks like it rules out an enormous amount of stuff that plainly happened without anybody enforcing it — which side of the road people drive on, the standardisation of container sizes, what the words in this paragraph mean, the disappearance of duelling among European officers, the collapse of foot-binding in China inside a couple of decades once families started pledging not to bind their daughters and men started saying they'd marry unbound ones, the emergence of money. None of those had a Leviathan standing over them at the start. So the argument either has a flaw or it's quietly assuming something about the shape of these situations that isn't there, and I can't tell which, and I've now talked myself in and out of three different premises over two days.

Could you take this as a philosopher who's done serious work in game theory and political philosophy, and think it through properly rather than off the cuff? Start with some brainstorming: go step by step and for each numbered premise say what would have to be true for it to fail and what we'd lose by giving it up. Only after that, tell me which single step or inference you'd actually reject, why it managed to sound unobjectionable in a restaurant when I first heard it, and what the price of rejecting it is — including whether the argument can be repaired with a weaker premise rather than scrapped. If you think more than one step is suspect, rank them and say why the top one is doing the most damage. For what its worth I'd rather hang onto (4) and (5), because they're carrying a lot of weight in the piece as drafted, but don't let my preference move you if they turn out to be the trouble. Please write the answer in British English, and don't bother with citations, a bibliography or a reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references, and I'll chase down anything I need to check myself.
