so I've got a position I want to write up for a small alignment workshop in the spring, and before I commit it to paper I'd like to know whether it actually hangs together, because my flatmate has been telling me for a month that it doesn't and I've run out of good answers.

The question I'm working on is whose values a system like this should track, and here's what I've landed on. (1) The target has to be built out of people's actual preferences, not idealized ones — I don't want a system serving the values I'd endorse after a lifetime of reflection and correct information, because I think that's just the designer's own ideals arriving through the back door with a philosophy degree. (2) The aggregation has to be neutral: nobody's preferences get extra weight for being true, or noble, or widely shared. One person, one equal say. (3) But equal say isn't equal weight on every preference, so I want to weight by stake, and I've been calling it the sacrifice metric: for each of your preferences, ask how much of your other stuff you'd surrender to keep it, and that's its weight. Concretely, a billionaire who'd give up the jet to keep the beach house scores high on that preference; a subsistence farmer who'd give up nothing scores low, because she has nothing to give up — and I've been telling myself that second one is a measurement artifact and not a value judgement, which honestly makes me a bit queasy. (4) Adaptive preferences — ones shaped by deprivation or oppression, like wanting less schooling because schooling was never actually on offer — should be screened out or discounted, since honoring them just ratifies the injustice that produced them. (5) And underneath all of it I hold that there's no fact of the matter about the uniquely correct interpersonal weighting scheme, in roughly the spirit of Arrow's result for rankings: the scheme is an institutional choice, and the right one is whichever is most publicly justifiable to the people it governs.

My flatmate's charge is that (4) is flatly incompatible with (1) — she says once you start discarding actual preferences you've conceded the whole anti-paternalist point — and I've been insisting it isn't, but I can't say why in a way that survives follow-up. What I actually can't see is how (3) and (5) sit together, and whether (3) is doing something to (2) that I'm not noticing. Go through the set and rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad: for each one, say what specifically would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs. Then tell me which single revision buys me the most consistency per unit of pain. And please also name the pairs that look contradictory but aren't — I'd much rather hear that (3) and (2) are reconcilable under some reading I haven't thought of than have everything flagged as suspicious, since right now I can't tell a real problem from one that just sounds bad.
