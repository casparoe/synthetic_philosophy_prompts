For a departmental podcast series aimed at philosophy graduate students and at machine-learning researchers with no philosophical training, I will be interviewing a researcher whose position I set out below. She is not a public figure, and I am not asking you to identify her or to compare her with anyone else; treat my description as complete and authoritative, and write only from it.

Her view has four commitments:

1. Representational talk about trained models is literally true rather than a useful fiction. It is a fact that a given classifier represents the presence of a tumour, and that a language model represents something about the syntactic category of the next token.
2. What makes such claims true are facts about the model's position in the practice that produced and uses it: the training objective, the distribution of inputs it was shaped by, and the downstream uses its outputs are put to. Content is fixed from the outside.
3. It follows that no internal element bears content in its own right. Directions in activation space, units, circuits, and the features recovered by sparse autoencoders or exposed by steering experiments carry information and support intervention, but the question whether some particular direction is really the Golden Gate Bridge feature has no determinate answer — no more than the question whether some particular molecule is really the temperature.
4. She therefore regards interpretability's ontological ambitions as a category error, while insisting, against those who would deflate representational talk altogether, that she is a realist about content and an anti-realist only about vehicles.

I would like eight to ten interview questions that engage this architecture. Please observe the following:

- No question may ask her to explain superposition, to define interpretability, or to speculate about model phenomenology. She has fielded all three repeatedly, and they will not be new to the audience either.
- At least one question must press the internal coherence of pairing realism about content with anti-realism about vehicles: if it is literally true that a model represents tumours, something physical must make that true, and physical structure is internal. Anticipate her reply that the truthmaker is the whole trained input–output disposition, and build a follow-up that engages that reply rather than restating the challenge.
- At least one question must take up the analogy she will reach for from the philosophy of mind — the functionalist's claim that beliefs are nowhere located in the brain — and ask why it does not run against her, given that functionalists ordinarily remain realists about belief tokens and deny only their type-identification with particular neural states.
- At least one question must confront her with an intervention result: suppose that amplifying or suppressing a candidate feature shifts the model's behaviour in precisely the way her own account of representation demands. What condition is still unmet, and can she state that condition in advance, or only recognise it after the fact?
- At least one question must attack commitment 2 from the opposite direction: if content is fixed by training history and use, then two models that behave identically throughout can represent different things. Ask whether she accepts that a model may represent what it would never respond to, and press her on cases where the ascribed content disjoins across contexts.
- At least one question must ask her to name a claim that working interpretability researchers actually make and that her view renders false or meaningless, and to explain why this is a substantive verdict rather than a relocation of the dispute.
- The final question must ask what finding would oblige her to abandon commitment 2 outright instead of reinterpreting it, phrased so that an appeal to the field's immaturity cannot serve as an answer.

For each question, supply four items: the question as I would deliver it aloud, in two or three sentences and in language a non-philosopher can follow; the answer she is most likely to give, in one sentence; a follow-up that is intelligible only as a response to that particular answer and that closes off at least one of her exits; and a single line identifying which of the four commitments the exchange is meant to strain, together with what a successful answer would have to concede.

Order the questions so that the earlier ones can be answered from inside her framework without cost and the later ones force her either to revise a commitment or to accept a conclusion she has resisted elsewhere. Do not cite sources, name other researchers, or append a reading list; I want questions keyed to the position exactly as I have described it.
