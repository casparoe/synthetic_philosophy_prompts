Coming at this from type design rather than philosophy: I've settled on the view that there's no feature list that defines a letterform. What makes every 'a' an 'a' is a space of transformations the form can survive — a centre with the most typical exemplars, a periphery where it starts shading into 'o' or 'u', and no line you actually cross. Style, on this picture, is what you get when one deformation is applied coherently across a whole alphabet instead of to single letters in isolation. What would count against it? I want the cases and results that would genuinely hurt, ranked from fatal to merely embarassing — including whether some directions in that space turn out to have sharp boundaries rather than fuzzy ones (the b/d, p/q mirror situation seems like a candidate, where a flip is catastrophic but a dozen other changes aren't), and what a purely graded story owes about people who recognise a form they've never seen before. Then tell me which of these a committed defender can absorb by re-setting up the space, and whether absorbing all of them is exactly the worry — has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a real problem here or just what a claim about categories looks like?
