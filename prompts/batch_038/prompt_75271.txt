I teach a 20-person upper-level seminar on democratic theory and next week's session is Simone Weil's wartime note on the general abolition of political parties — the short one, written in exile and only published after her death. I don't want to lecture on it, and I definitely don't want a round of "here's what you found interesting." What I want is for the class to run into her argument themselves, and specifically the line they all skip past on first reading: "la première fin, et, en dernière analyse, l'unique fin de tout parti politique est sa propre croissance, et cela sans aucune limite." Most of my students think that's overheated. Their standing objection is basically: sure, parties distort judgement, but that's just bad incentives, and incentives can be patched — transparency rules, primaries, recall provisions, campaign finance limits. I want the 80 minutes to leave that objection feeling shaky from the inside, with me saying as little as possible.

Here's the shape I have in mind; you build it out properly. Two rounds, same deliberative task, so the contrast is the data. Round one: the class is sorted into three blocs, each handed a written platform, and there's a mechanic that makes bloc growth pay — recruiting a defector from another bloc, say, plus a bloc-level bonus for voting as a unit — and every student also holds a private card stating a position they're told is simply the just one, which cuts against at least one plank of their own bloc's platform. The bloc has to produce a single joint text on some concrete question; I was thinking a clause on emergency powers, or a rule for rationing something genuinely scarce, but pick whatever you think generates the most friction between the platform, the growth incentive, and the private cards. Round two: same question, blocs dissolved, everyone independent, private cards still in force, class has to produce one text by some rule we agree in advance. Then the debrief maps what actually happened onto Weil's three definitions of a party (machine for manufacturing collective passion; organization built to exert collective pressure on the thought of each member; growth as the sole end) and onto her positive proposal — candidates standing as individuals who state their own positions, and journals as things you can be near without adhering to, which is the proximity/adherence distinction she's really leaning on. I also want the debrief to reach her claim that belonging to a party always forces you to lie, because that's the one they'll say only applies to hacks.

So give me the whole package: the rules, the exact scoring (no real money — points, tokens, whatever), the actual text of the platform cards and the private cards, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for 80 minutes with the debrief inside it, and the three or four facilitator lines to drop at the moments where the structure is about to bite, so the naming comes out of what they did rather than out of me. Add a fallback version for 9 students and another for 35. Before you commit to a design, spend a few sentences brainstorming which growth mechanic best does the work, and think carefully about the failure modes — I want those flagged explicitly, along with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each (my own guess: round two's independents will just reproduce round one's text because nobody's opinions actually changed, and the contrast evaporates). Most of all, tell me honestly what this activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that a points game can only model the incentive version of her claim, and a student can perfectly well comply in round one while thinking clearly the whole time — which is precisely what Weil denies is possible — so the game risks handing my skeptics a clean counterexample and a smug debrief moment. Related: Weil isn't making an interest-group argument, she thinks thought needs a collective milieu and her revue proposal is about keeping the milieu without the adherence, and the game's two rounds are minutes rather than years of habituation, so the "collective passion" my students will feel is at most competitive buzz. Say which of those I have to supply by hand and how. Act as someone who is expert both on Weil and on experiential teaching in philosophy classrooms; don't give me a bibliography or reading list, I have the text, and don't let the debrief turn into a lecture in disguise.
