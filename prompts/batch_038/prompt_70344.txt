I serve as returning officer for a professional society of roughly four thousand members. In a year I run about twenty ballots of four quite different kinds: contested officer elections with between three and seven candidates; the technical standards committee, which must settle on one of several competing drafts of a specification in which members' employers hold commercial interests; the prizes committee, selecting from a shortlist of six or eight; and occasional constitutional referendums in which several rival amendment packages appear on the ballot together with the status quo. Two years ago an officer election was annulled after a defeated candidate demonstrated that a coordinated bloc had ranked its least-liked serious contender first in order to eliminate a stronger rival, and the executive council now requires a written justification for every rule choice. The councillors are intelligent laypeople with no training in social choice theory, I have a fortnight per ballot, and once the council has adopted the procedure it will be applied by volunteers who know less about this than I do.

What I currently have is a discouragement rather than a method. I know the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem governs this territory — that no deterministic rule over three or more alternatives, able to select any of them and admitting every possible ranking, is immune to strategic voting unless it is dictatorial — but that tells me nothing about what to write on the ballot paper. Please convert the theorem into a standing decision procedure that my assistants and I can run before each ballot.

The procedure should:

- begin with the theorem's own hypotheses, recast as plain-language screening questions about the particular contest — how many alternatives there really are once nomination rules and agenda control are taken into account, whether each can in principle win, whether any ranking is admissible, whether the rule is deterministic and returns a single winner — stating for each what answer lets the ballot through to the next question and what answer stops the line;
- specify what happens once every gate has fired. At that point "choose a strategy-proof rule" is unavailable, and the procedure must hand me an explicit second-best criterion and a way of applying it without mathematics: how to compare candidate rules, for example, by what a successful manipulation stands to gain and by how much coordination, information, and mutual trust it demands;
- include a diagnostic for the restricted-preference escape — a way for lay people to test whether a given contest really is one-dimensional in the sense the theorem needs, using material we already have (nomination data, a straw poll of rankings), together with a statement of when it would be reckless to rely on the result;
- deal with the four proposals that arise at every council meeting: switching to score voting, randomizing or drawing lots to break ties, cutting the field to two alternatives in a preliminary round, and letting a chair or small panel decide with the vote treated as advisory. For each, say what the theorem and its extensions do and do not rule out, and what new difficulty the move imports;
- keep separate, in terms a volunteer can apply, the rule that is manipulable in the theorem's worst-case sense and the ballot that is actually vulnerable on the day, and say which features of a particular contest move it between those categories;
- mark the steps that are judgment dressed up as procedure, and the points at which the decision must go to a person because it turns on values — what the society is trying to protect — rather than on facts.

Then run the procedure on three of our ballots: the standards committee, five competing drafts, forty voting members, sustained employer lobbying, presently decided by Borda count; the officer election, six candidates, presently a two-round runoff of the whole membership with a fortnight between rounds; and a constitutional referendum with three rival amendment packages plus the status quo, where the council proposes that the chair cast a deciding vote if no package beats all the others. I am attached to the runoff and would prefer not to be told to abandon it, but I need to know whether the procedure says so, and if it does, what the cheapest defensible replacement would be. Close by stating what the procedure cannot deliver: the questions it will answer badly, and the ways in which a determined bloc could still defeat whatever rule we adopt.
