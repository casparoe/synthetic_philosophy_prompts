I'm prepping my TA for week four of my senior seminar on reference. It's about a dozen students, mostly final-years plus two first-year grad students, and last week we did Frege on sense and reference — that one landed better than I expected. This week is the 1905 paper. Catch: the projector in that room has been broken since September so it's two whiteboards and nothing else, and the group has all encountered the theory of descriptions somewhere before, which is the worst possible starting condition. Three of them will tell you within ninety seconds that it's "about nonexistent objects" and that it "involves the King of France," and they'll think that's a complete grasp of it. So you'll be teaching the theory *and* dismantling the version they walked in with, in 80 minutes.

What I want from you isn't an explanation of the theory — I can do that, I've done it, not always well. I want the plan for explaining it in that slot. Work out the order the pieces have to go in and say what depends on what: I've long suspected that if the three-clause analysis arrives before they've felt *why* a definite description isn't a name, it just reads as pedantry, and I've never quite solved that. So figure out what has to be installed first — the gap between grammatical form and logical form? the fact that Frege's picture generates a problem here at all? both, and in which order? Then pick the example, or the small cluster, that carries the session, and tell me what it needs to have and what it must not have. I expect you'll want to argue me out of the King of France, or at least argue about how long it earns its keep; it's pre-chewed, but it's also the one case where the uniqueness failure and the emptiness aren't masked by context, which most ordinary examples are. Also decide which of the payoffs to spend time on — negative existentials, the uniqueness failure cases, the identity puzzle where they're told two descriptions of the same poet both hold — because 80 minutes only buys you maybe two of them, and tell me which one you'd cut without regret.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I actually care about. My own guesses: they'll think the analysis is a claim about how to paraphrase English sentences rather than about what the proposition says; they'll insist that "the book on the table" obviously doesn't carry uniqueness as a literal truth condition, which is a real problem for the view and not just a confusion; some will slide into thinking the whole thing is a Meinong-baiting exercise; and somebody will say the King of France sentence is "neither true nor false," which is not Russell and isn't Strawson either, since we haven't got to Strawson yet. Tell me where in the sequence each gets headed off, whether any of them you'd deliberately let happen and repair afterwards rather than preempt, and how I'll know by the end whether they've understood the theory or have just learned to recite it. Give me the one closing question to ask and what a good answer versus a tell-tale bad answer looks like, plus what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and announce that I'm refusing.

Format, please:
- **What the session has to install** — one paragraph, the single load-bearing idea and what having grasped it amounts to
- **The order** — numbered steps with rough minutes and a clause each on why that step can't come earlier or later
- **The example** — 2–3 paragraphs: what carries it, what it must have and lack, the King of France verdict, what it costs you
- **Misconceptions and where to head them off** — a short list, each with its trigger point in the sequence
- **The check** — one question, good answer, bad answer, about 150 words
- **What I refuse to explain** — a short list with a line of reason each
- **The cut** — two sentences on which payoff you drop and why you can live without it
