I am a junior, and my high school lets us replace the spring history final with an independent research paper; I chose the comparisons people keep drawing between the last century of the Roman Republic and present-day American politics, partly because my older brother forwards me a column about it every few months and partly because I cannot say precisely what I object to in them. My plan was to work through a handful of standard diagnoses — the professionalization of the army and the shift of soldiers' loyalty from the state to their general, the strain of administering an empire with the institutions of a city-state, the erosion of forbearance after the Gracchi, the land question and the displacement of smallholders, the enfranchisement of Italy making the old assemblies unworkable — and judge them. But before I judge any of them I want to know what I am entitled to demand of one, because nearly every account I have read answers a slightly different question and then gets praised or attacked for failing at a question it never asked.

So: write the specification. What must a causal account of the fall of the Roman Republic deliver to count as an explanation rather than a well-told sequence of events, and — separately, since I suspect this is where the two come apart — what must it deliver before it may be used as an analogy for a modern democracy at all? I have a partial list of my own that I do not fully trust. It must explain the Republic's earlier resilience: the same constitution absorbed the sack of the city by the Gauls, Hannibal at the gates, and the beating of Tiberius Gracchus to death in 133 BC, and then failed to absorb Sulla leading his legions into Rome in 88 BC or Caesar crossing into Italy in 49, so if the constitution produced the collapse, why did it take four centuries. It must explain the timing and the order, not merely the outcome — why the breakdown ran through tribunician obstruction, agrarian legislation, army loyalty, the Social War, Sulla's settlement (which two of his own former lieutenants dismantled within a decade of his death), and only then the triumvirates and the civil wars. It must say what evidence would count against it, and it must explain why it is not simply adopting the moral diagnosis supplied by the sources, who wrote after the fact and mostly wished to blame luxury and ambition. And I expect there is a requirement I have not thought of, or that one of these is not a genuine requirement but a prejudice of readers who want a cautionary tale.

Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no introduction, no conclusion, no restating my question. Items 1 through 4 should be the four requirements you consider most important: each names the requirement in one sentence, gives in a second sentence the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and ends with a bracketed tag, either [non-negotiable] or [contested]. Item 5 should identify the pair of requirements, mine or yours, that you think cannot be satisfied together, and say what follows for the popular diagnoses; my guess is that a contingent, choice-driven account, one that makes individuals like Caesar and Cicero do real causal work, cannot also be general enough to warn anybody about their own republic, and I want to know whether that is right and which diagnosis the tension eliminates first.
