Last week a new grad student in our epi group wrote in a paper review that a rival model was "wrong too, so there's no reason to prefer ours," my PI muttered something about all models being wrong anyway, and the conversation just died. The student caught me afterward and asked the thing I couldn't answer: if two theories are both false, can one still be *closer* to the truth, and how would you rank them? I've now volunteered to run our journal club on exactly this — 9 people, all quantitative, none of them have done any philosophy, 75 minutes, one room with a whiteboard.

Design a team game they actually play, so they run into the problem instead of being told it. Shape I had in mind, improve it if you see better: there's a hidden rule — three switches and a lamp, so 8 configurations — and each team submits a theory that predicts some configurations and abstains on others. Note the configurations sit at different distances from one another (one flip apart, two apart, etc.), and that's where the third beat comes from. I want them to hit, in order: (1) a scoring rule that only counts errors makes total abstention the winning move; (2) once you also reward correct predictions, two teams can both be wrong and yet everyone agrees which is better; (3) counting mistakes isnt the right measure either — a theory wrong in two places can be nearer the truth than one wrong in a single place, depending which places. They should find each of these by playing, not by me announcing it.

Give me the exact scoring with the numbers worked through on two or three example theories so I can check it behaves, a prep list (index cards and whiteboard ideally), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75, the reveal of the hidden rule, and a debrief that maps each beat onto the underlying problem and lays out the main repair strategies available at each fork. Then tell me which beat the game cant really reproduce — the part I'll have to supply verbally — and what predictably goes wrong the first time this runs, like a team that games the scoring in round one, plus how to handle it.
