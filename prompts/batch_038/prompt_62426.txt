Novelist here, working on a book with a character shaped loosely like Malthus — not the man, someone cut from the same cloth. Doing the research I fell down a hole and came out with a question I can't stop turning over. It's really about an argument pattern, not about the character.

So. Everyone agrees his big prediction didn't happen for two centuries. Population multiplied, food per head went up, real wages rose, no general collapse into subsistence. If you'd laid bets year after year, betting against him paid out every single time for roughly two hundred years running. And then the record starts filling up with famines, drained aquifers, thinning soil, fisheries going, and the defenders say: see, he was right, he was just early. And it lands. People nod. Nobody blinks.

That's the configuration I have no word for. Note what it *isn't*: nobody patched the theory, nobody made it vaguer, nobody carved out an exception or quietly relocated the target. The claim sat there unchanged and wrong for generations, and then the world walked into it. I know there's a whole literature about theories that wriggle free of disproof, but wriggling is precisely what this isn't. The prediction was deferred, and then cashed.

A second example so it doesn't look like a one-off. A friend of mine has been telling anyone who'd listen since about 2004 that the newspaper business would be wiped out. He was the punchline of every dinner party for fifteen years. Now it's broadly true, and everyone says he called it. Third one, from my own family: my grandfather told people in the sixties that the low part of town would flood regularly. Wrong for fifty years. Right now, and the occuring floods get credited back to him.

So: does this specific thing have an established name, or a literature I'm just not finding? Meaning — a claim treated as vindicated not because the evidence arrived on schedule but because it arrived eventually, after a gap long enough that any sane bettor would have written it off and been right to. And underneath that, the part I actually care about: at what point does "right but early" stop being a fair description of a delay and turn into a position that nothing could ever prove wrong? I want the phenomenon carved, not just labelled — what it is, and as much work spent on what it *isn't*. Don't let it get absorbed into ordinary vagueness or goalpost-shifting on one side, or into plain dumb luck on the other. If there's genuinely no term, coin me one and tell me where its edges sit. And give me a pair of contrasting cases that would seperate the two readings — one where the delay obviously destroys the credit and one where it obviously doesn't — so I can see what's doing the actual work in between.

One wrinkle that might matter: some of these people arguably helped bring about, or helped prevent, what they announced. Does the verdict change if the prophecy talked itself out of being early, or into being late?

Format: I want the guts as a JSON object, because I'm pasting this straight into my notes and I like things in boxes. Schema:

- "has_a_name": one of "yes" / "partly" / "no"
- "nearest_concepts": array of objects, each with "term", "where_it_comes_from", "what_it_covers", "why_my_case_isnt_quite_it", "fit" (one of "close" / "partial" / "loose")
- "coined_term": string, or null if an established term already does the job
- "definition": two sentences maximum, no jargon, usable by a layman
- "necessary_conditions": array of strings — everything that has to hold before a case counts as this
- "boundary_cases": array of objects with "case", "verdict" ("counts" / "doesnt"), "reason"
- "what_would_settle_it": string — the contrasting pair described above, with the diagnosis of what differs between them
- "self_reference": string — a sentence or two on the wrinkle where the predictor changes the outcome
- "for_my_character": string — one sentence on what would make his being right feel earned rather than cheap

Prose after the JSON is very welcome, but the JSON has to stand alone and be complete without it.
