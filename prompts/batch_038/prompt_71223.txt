I am preparing a diagnostic handout for the opening meeting of a graduate seminar in metaethics. The students have all had truth-functional logic and some non-cognitivist reading, but their self-descriptions are wildly unreliable: several announce themselves as expressivists while holding views that turn out, on inspection, to be indistinguishable from a minimalist cognitivism, and at least one maintains that he has dissolved the embedding problem altogether while holding a view on which moral sentences simply carry descriptive truth conditions. I do not want a survey of the responses to the Frege–Geach problem; they will have read the standard accounts before the handout is distributed. What I want is an instrument that sorts them by what they actually say about particular sentences.

Please construct a decision tree that takes a student from the raw problem — moral sentences embed under negation, in conditionals, under epistemic modals — to a named position among the available solutions. The handout will be worked through in class, so the instrument has to function without me in the room.

**1. Fixed material.** Every question in the tree must be asked about one or more of the following six sentences, and only these:

 (i) Plagiarizing a colleague's manuscript is wrong.
 (ii) It is not the case that plagiarizing a colleague's manuscript is wrong.
 (iii) If plagiarizing a colleague's manuscript is wrong, then so is paying a graduate student to do it in your place.
 (iv) Plagiarizing a colleague's manuscript might be wrong.
 (v) Either plagiarizing a colleague's manuscript is wrong, or nothing is.
 (vi) Charging a drowning swimmer five thousand dollars for a tow to shore is exploitative.

Sentences (i)–(v) hold the subject matter constant so that no one can escape a node by shifting what the moral claim is about; (vi) is there because a thick predicate lets the tree probe whether a given view is prepared to assign any descriptive content to a moral sentence at all. Say explicitly, for each node, which of the six sentences it is being asked about.

**2. Nodes.** Twelve to fourteen nodes. The root should be a question about (iii), since the conditional is where the difficulty lives, and the tree must reach at least one later node about (iv) and one about (v) that discriminates among positions which gave identical answers to (i)–(iii). Each node must be phrased as a question a student can answer without knowing where the branch leads — that is, in terms of what the sentence says or what the speaker is committed to, never in terms of a doctrine. No node may contain the name of a philosopher, or the vocabulary of the debate (expressivism, non-cognitivism, quasi-realism, inferentialism, plan-laden, ecumenical, being-for, and the like); paraphrase technical notions into ordinary questions about sentences and states of mind.

**3. Leaves.** Six to eight. At minimum the tree must be able to terminate at: a Blackburn-style quasi-realism; the norm-expressivist, planning-based semantics; Schroeder's uniform "being for" account; Ridge's ecumenical expressivism; a semantics on which the logical operators are not construed as functions from attitudes to attitudes in the first place, but as operating on inferential commitments; and the deflationary response that there is no embedding problem for the expressivist to solve. In addition, reserve a leaf for the view on which the embedded clause in (iii) has no semantic value whatsoever and the whole conditional expresses a single complex attitude. I am not confident that anyone actually holds that last view; if you agree, mark the leaf unoccupied rather than inventing a defender for it. For each leaf give: the standard name of the position, or "unoccupied"; one sentence saying, in the same plain vocabulary used at the nodes, what the embedded clause in (iii) contributes on that view; which of the six sentences that view finds hardest; and its most awkward commitment.

**4. Separability.** Flag every pair of leaves that your questions do not genuinely separate — I expect at least two such pairs — and for each state the one further question that would separate them, or concede that the difference is verbal.

**5. Unoccupied space.** List the answer-combinations your nodes make logically available that no leaf occupies, and diagnose each: incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored by anyone.

**6. The hinge.** Identify the single node that does the most work in the tree, and explain in two or three sentences why the remaining nodes are comparatively idle.

Two constraints on execution. First, no bibliography, no citations, no page references, and no claims about what any particular author wrote in any particular work — I want the tree built out of the logical space, with the actual positions located in it afterward. Where you are unsure whether an attribution is right, describe the view functionally and flag the uncertainty rather than guessing. Second, assume throughout that you cannot look anything up, and that the reader of the handout cannot either.
