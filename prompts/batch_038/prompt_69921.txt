I am sitting in on a philosophy-of-physics seminar this term and have been trying to get a handle on the debate over whether the wavefunction is something real or something epistemic. My preparation is a single undergraduate course in quantum mechanics — I can follow Dirac notation and the measurement postulates, but I have never seen the ontological-models framework the specialists work inside, which I suspect is why so much of the discussion talks past me. The seminar gave the question two sessions and left me more confused than I started: one participant insisted the matter had been settled in 2012, another insisted it was wide open, and the instructor appeared to agree with both. Afterwards I read a long-form essay in an online science magazine that takes the first side, and I would like to know how much of it I should carry back into the room. The relevant passage runs as follows: "For most of a century physicists have been unsure whether the wavefunction is part of the furniture of the world or a ledger of our information about it. That changed in 2012, when Matthew Pusey, Jonathan Barrett and Terry Rudolph proved a theorem showing that the wavefunction cannot be a state of knowledge. Their argument is disarmingly simple. Suppose the wavefunction merely encodes information about some deeper physical condition of a system, so that two distinct wavefunctions can be compatible with one and the same underlying condition. Now take two systems prepared independently of one another, so that their underlying conditions are independent as well. On those assumptions there is a nonzero chance that a joint measurement designed for the pair will return an outcome that quantum theory says has probability zero, and since quantum theory's probabilities are confirmed to spectacular precision, the hypothesis that the wavefunction is mere information is ruled out by experiment rather than by philosophical preference. The wavefunction is therefore a real physical field, spread out through space, and its collapse on measurement is a genuine physical event. The result does for the quantum state what Bell's theorem did for hidden variables, and it leaves interpretations that treat the wavefunction as a catalogue of an agent's beliefs — QBism is the best-known example — without a leg to stand on."

Could you take that passage apart claim by claim and tell me, of each, whether it stands as written, whether it stands only with a qualification the author has left out, or whether it is simply false? Three things interest me most. First, which assumptions the theorem actually needs, and which of them the passage states, downplays, or drops; if one of them is doing most of the work and is itself contestable, say who contests it and what they are prepared to give up in order to keep an epistemic view alive. Second, where a technical notion slides into its everyday sense — I suspect that whatever the theorem licenses by the word real is not what the author's gloss about a field spread out through space asserts, but I cannot articulate the difference, and I would also like to know whether the theorem says anything at all about collapse, or about whether the wavefunction is the whole of the underlying physical condition. Third, whether the two closing conclusions survive the corrections: that measurement involves a genuine physical collapse, and that belief-based interpretations are finished. Note that QBism may not even be the kind of view the theorem's premises are about, in which case I want to be told plainly that the author has argued against something nobody holds.

For what it is worth, my own instinct runs the other way from the essay's: the wavefunction of a ten-particle system is defined on a thirty-dimensional configuration space, which hardly looks like a field in the world, so I have trouble taking the author's realism literally — though I recognize that an instinct is not an argument, and I would like to be told if that intuition cuts against something the theorem does establish. I do not need references, arXiv numbers, or a reading list, none of which I could chase down; I want the audit itself, and at the end the shortest accurate replacement for the quoted passage that you can write in the same register.
