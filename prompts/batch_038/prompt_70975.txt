I'm writing up something for a philosophy of science seminar and I've gotten myself tangled in a question about idealizations, specifically the Friedman-style move where a model's assumptions are flatly false but the false bits seem to cancel each other out — what people around here call the F-twist. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Point-mass planets when you're computing orbits: fine, no worries. Frictionless pendulum when the target quantity is the period: fine. Same frictionless pendulum when the target is how the amplitude decays over an afternoon: not fine. Uniform-density Earth when you want surface gravity: fine. Uniform-density Earth when you want core pressure: not fine. Ideal gas law, where you treat molecules as dimensionless points with no attraction between them — both assumptions false, and part of why it still works is that dropping the volume term pushes predicted pressure up while dropping the attraction term pushes it down, so they roughly cancel over a big range: fine, and I'd actually say the model is genuinely well-confirmed, not just luckily right. Now here's the one that bothers me. A climate model where the cloud-albedo parameterization is known to be biased in one direction and the aerosol forcing term has been hand-tuned to be biased in the other, so that the historical temperature record comes out right. Structurally that looks like exactly the same thing as the gas law — two errors, opposite signs, net cancellation — but I don't think the good hindcast confirms that model nearly as much, and I can't say why. Assuming profit maximization for firms run by empire-builders who get disciplined by competition and takeover threats: fine. Assuming perfect competition in an industry with three firms and brutal entry barriers, where the price predictions come out right anyway: not fine, even though the prediction succeeds. Assuming no unmeasured confounding in an observational drug study where there's a plausible confounder nobody bothered to check: not fine. Assuming an infinite population in a toy population-genetics model: fine, and fine even though I couldn't tell you how big "big enough" has to be. And Friedman's own examples — the tree whose leaves are distributed as if it were computing sunlight density, the billiard player who plays as if he knew the formulas — I find those actively irritating, but when I try to say what's wrong with them I come up empty.

My tentative rule is something like: an idealization is admissible if the error it introduces is either small in the region you care about, or offset by another error that wasn't put there in order to make the numbers come out right. But I'm suspicious of that second clause because it seems to make admissibility depend on what the modeler was trying to do, which feels like the wrong kind of thing for a principle about models to hang on. So: can you state a principle (or a small number of competing ones) precisely enough that each clause is motivated by a specific case from my list, run it on two cases I haven't given — a macro model with a single representative agent who has rational expectations, and an epidemic model that assumes homogeneous mixing — and then tell me straight up which of my verdicts it can't save? I already suspect the gas-law/climate-model pair is going to force me to drop one of them, and I'd rather be told which than have you paper over it.
