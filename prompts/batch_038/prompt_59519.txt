Forty years in applied entomology — field trials, resistance monitoring, more cabbage seasons than I care to remember — and I retired four years ago and fell into philosophy headfirst with no training whatsoever. My reading group made me write this out properly and now I can't get out of it. It seems to prove something I'm certain is false.

Some of the steps are empirical and I reckon I'm on firm ground there. Some aren't.

(1) If a creature can suffer, its suffering counts: causing it is a harm, and letting it happen when you could cheaply prevent it is a harm too.
(2) A great many insects can suffer. Not all, perhaps, and the marginal cases are disputed, but the ones I worked with showed nociceptive withdrawal, learned avoidance of a place where they'd been hurt, changed responses after substances that behave like painkillers, and a willingness to trade a present injury against a later reward.
(3) Wild insect populations are vast and run on a make-thousands-lose-all-but-a-few schedule; most losses happen in the early larval stages, to starvation, desiccation, disease and parasitism, and parasitism at least is slow.
(4) So the total insect suffering out there dwarfs everything humans cause and everything humans could relieve.
(5) The cheapest way to reduce the suffering in a population is to reduce the population. An egg that never hatches cannot starve, and this costs the egg nothing, because there is no egg.
(6) Therefore anyone who really holds (1) should be making insect populations smaller — smaller everywhere, not merely in crops — and the limit case is that a world with no insects in it would be better then this one.
(7) And by the same road, wildflower margins, habitat restoration and the general campaign to bring the bugs back are projects for manufacturing suffering, while the four decades I spent suppressing pest populations were the morally correct ones, for a reason nobody in my old department would ever have accepted.
(8) Which is absurd. But I've been round the chain a dozen times and each step looks like it follows from the one before.

So where does it break? It might be an equivocation hiding between two steps, or a step that only goes through on a reading the earlier ones don't support, or the argument might be valid and the real question is which premise I ought to be least fond of. I honestly can't tell which situation I'm in, so please don't take a hint from me about where I want the blame to land. For what it's worth I'd fight hardest for (1) and (2), and I'm happy to see (6) and (7) shot. What I want is the bill for that. If I have to insist, say, that there being fewer sufferers isn't good for anyone, does that also stop me from saying anything good about averting a harm that hasn't happened yet, and where exactly does the line go? Lay me out two or three seperate repairs — different premises to give up, or different readings to adopt — and say which is cheapest and what each one costs me somewhere else I care about. Then tell me whether a weakened version survives with any teeth left: does any of this bear on sterile-insect releases, on biological control, or on whether we should be rearing insects for feed at all?

Don't be gentle with (3) and (4) if you think that's where the rot is; I have the data and I can take it. And please argue it out on the page rather than sending me off to a reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references. British English throughout, if you don't mind.
