I write novels, and for three years I have served as the lay member of my city's Commission on Public Monuments and Street Names. In March the Commission votes on the Hallam statue, and I need a memo to circulate to the other six commissioners beforehand. Nothing in this case is famous and nothing here needs looking up: take the particulars as I give them and reason from them.

Cyrus W. Hallam founded the iron works that made this city; he endowed the free library, the east-side hospital wing, and a scholarship that still pays two students a year. In 1899 his company guards fired on a strike at the works and killed four men, one of them fifteen. The city raised a bronze by the sculptor Adelaide Frost, unveiled in 1926 at the head of Foundry Street: Hallam standing, one hand on a rail, and a plaque listing his offices and benefactions with no mention of 1899.

Three things have happened since. In the summer of the protests the plaque was pried off the granite and never recovered; the outline and four drilled holes remain, and someone painted the date of the strike in white across the base, which our crews have power-washed twice and residents have repainted twice. Second, our conservator reports chloride corrosion in the bronze and a failing internal armature: the statue needs roughly $180,000 of work within two years, or it will be lost whether we decide anything or not. Third, a bill now in committee at our statehouse would forbid municipalities to alter monuments more than fifty years old; counsel thinks decisions taken before enactment would likely be grandfathered, but she will not promise it. Our own charter expires in eighteen months unless the council renews it. There is also a petition to rename Foundry Street, with two rival proposals — name it for the four killed, or name it for the library Hallam endowed — and we have not decided whether to hear the renaming in the same motion as the statue or separately.

The Commission has been reasoning, without ever saying so, with three ideas I want the memo to test rather than assume. First, reversibility: in conservation we hold impermissible any intervention a later generation cannot undo, and we have never asked whether the same rule governs meaning. Re-casting the lost plaque is one kind of act; leaving the ghost outline and the repainted date in place is another; melting the bronze is a third. Second, accretion: at some point damage becomes part of a monument's history, and if so, who owns the composite — the city, which did the damage, or the residents, who keep renewing it? Third, voice: every addition to a monument speaks in a first person, and no speaker available to us is neutral; a panel that says we must be able to say who that we is. If you think a fourth concept is needed, name it and use it.

Please draft the memo. Open with your recommendation in a single sentence. Then, in no more than 800 words in total, give me: what the Commission should resolve in March about the pedestal, the missing plaque, and the conservation contract, with dates and an order of operations; whether the street renaming should be heard in the same motion as the statue or severed from it, and why that is not merely a procedural question; a rule for the nine other contested monuments in our inventory that a commissioner who disagrees with you about Hallam could still apply; and the strongest consideration on the other side, argued as its holder would argue it, together with a plain statement of what your recommendation is willing to lose. Two constraints to work under rather than around: the Hallam Trust's deed ties its scholarship distributions to the city's care and preservation of the monument, and counsel cannot tell us whether an altered plaque would breach it; and the conservation clock runs whether or not we reach agreement on meaning. I have disclosed my own novel about the 1899 strike and will recuse myself from the vote, so write reasons that do not depend on my being in the room to defend them.
