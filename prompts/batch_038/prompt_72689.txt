So I write a weekly column on science and ideas and it comes with a small podcast attached — nothing huge, a few thousand downloads, mostly grad students and people who work in media. Next month's episode is about the idea of "the public" as a manufactured thing rather than a real collection of people, and my guest is a scholar who's just put out a book arguing that Kierkegaard's attack on the press in the 1840s is still the best account we have, only for a reason almost everybody gets wrong. His thesis as I understand it: the public and the crowd are not the same target at all. A crowd is an actual mass of bodies you can get swallowed up in; the public is an abstraction that exists only because there's a press — nobody is the public, no one can act as the public — and it turns dangerous when people start understanding themselves through it. Leveling on his reading isn't homogenized opinions, it's the loss of anyone in a position to decide anything, which is why he thinks the reflex "Kierkegaard predicted Twitter" line my editor always pushes for is backwards: what matters isn't that everyone can now speak, it's that everyone gets turned into a spectator of a phantom. He also holds that Kierkegaard's own conduct — deliberately provoking a satirical paper into caricaturing him in the street, then a decade later putting out his own polemical sheet against the church — isn't a contradiction of the theory but a piece of it.

My problem is that he's extremely smooth and he has one move he uses on everything. Whenever you press him about whether any of this could be wrong, he says Kierkegaard isn't making an empirical claim, he's making a category claim, and the categories have to be kept seperate before you can even start. So: give me eight or nine questions in the order I'd ask them, arranged so that that retreat gets progressively harder to take, and for each one write out in a sentence or two the answer he's most likely to give, plus the follow-up keyed to it — I'll be reading off a page over a video call and we have maybe 40 minutes, so keep them short enough to say out loud without tripping. At least one question has to be on the crowd/public distinction and how I'm supposed to know it's in the texts rather than a convenience of his reading, and it should press the awkward fact that the work he leans on hardest is itself a book-length review of somebody else's novel, which looks a lot like the reflective spectator genre he's criticizing. One on the reflexivity of running all of this through print and through his own book tour. One on the many-to-many objection — on platforms people aren't just an audience, they perform, they accumulate followers, they organize, they get people fired, and that sounds like crowds doing things rather than publics spectating. One on whether the leveling story is really compatible with wanting more equality in the ordinary political sense, because I think he's stretching there, but phrase it so he can't just cast me as a nostalgist for hierarchy. And close with one that asks him flat out what would show his category claim is false.

Also — this is the part I actually care about — tell me where you're least confident. Flag any question that rests on a contested reading I'd get embarrassed about on air, say which of his positions you think is the weakest link and what specific consideration would change your mind about it, and if you think the crowd/public distinction is mostly an interpreter's invention, tell me that straight instead of building a question on top of it. Don't send me a reading list, I've got the books and I won't be able to look anything up mid-recording; and paraphrase him rather than putting words in quotation marks, since I wont have a way to check exact wording and I don't want to misquote a dead Dane for an audience that includes actual Danes.
