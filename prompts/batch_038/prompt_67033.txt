So I've got an idea rattling around for a short story and I'd be really grateful if you'd write it — somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words, third person, past tense, whatever suits you best.

The setting. A small harbour town where, for eighty-odd years, there's been a machine in the harbourmaster's office that prints one number a day onto a strip of ticker tape. The number is the objective chance — genuinely objective, not anyone's estimate or gut feeling — that some specified event will happen before midnight. It's never the same event twice; the machine picks. Locals treat the printout the way other people treat the shipping forecast: you set your confidence to the figure and get on with your life. A 0.3 for 'the surge tops the seawall' means you move the cars up the hill but you don't evacuate, and nobody finds that odd. Children are taught it the way they're taught subtraction. The town's whole idiom has grown up around it: 'the tape says', 'that's a nine-tenths day', 'don't spend a four-tenths on a nine-tenths'.

The protagonist is one of two people who maintain the machine. The other is her brother, and he's the one who actually likes the place.

Now the wrinkle. One morning the tape reads 0.92 for an event the protagonist already knows, flat out, is not going to happen — because of something she saw with her own eyes the night before, something no one else saw and something that couldn't possibly have got into the machine's evidence. So she's in the position of being fully certain of a chance and fully certain of the outcome at the same time, which everyone agrees is a state of mind a person could in principle be in, even though no honest way of reasoning lets you hold both and keep everything else you'd normally keep.

She has to give one thing up, and only one: (a) that she's really certain about the number on the tape; (b) that her private observation is the kind of thing that's allowed to move her off the number; (c) that she accumulates evidence in the ordinary way, yesterday's evidence still counting today; or (d) the whole practise of matching confidence to the figure in the first place. By the end she's chosen, and the cost should be visible in her life and in the town's — in what she does about the event, and what her brother does about her — rather than narrated at us.

Second wrinkle, and I think this is where the good fiction actually lives. Chances are indexed to moments. The figure on the tape is the chance as of four in the morning, when the machine ran; if the world has shifted since then in ways the machine couldn't know about, the chance now may be something else entirely, and it isn't obvious which of the two numbers she owes her confidence to. Have her work this out somewhere around the halfway point, and have it not rescue her — or rescue her only at a price she hadn't priced in.

Craft notes. No exposition dumps, and absolutely no scene where one character explains the ideas to another character who's been written to be thick. Let the ordinary local speech carry the concepts. Include one scene where somebody who's never given any of this a moment's thought behaves in a way that's a clean counterexample to the town's rule, and let that land with no commentary. Don't make the machine a stand-in for God, or fate, or an algorithm quietly running everyone's life: it's a machine, it's finicky, it needs oiling, and the dread should come from the bookkeeping rather than from any mystery. And I'd rather the words 'probability' and 'credence' never appear in dialogue at all. Feel free to invent the town's name and whatever the locals call the machine.
