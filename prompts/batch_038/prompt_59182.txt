I TA a grad seminar on the ethics of war, and my professor has been handing down verdicts on invented cases all term. She never states her principle — she just says liable or not liable and moves on. I'm trying to reverse-engineer her view before the last session, because I'm pretty sure she's going to put a case to us and ask what follows from it. You're a philosopher of war ethics who's taught this material for years and can hold the Walzerian and the revisionist positions in your head at once; think carefully and don't just pattern-match to whichever camp she sounds closest to.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A 19-year-old conscript in the aggressor state's army, drafted, genuinely believes the war is defensive, fights strictly within the rules of war. Targetable by the defenders: liable.
2. A colonel on the aggressor's general staff who plans logistics, knows perfectly well the war is aggressive, carries no weapon, sits 800 km from the front. Liable.
3. A civilian haulier under contract to the aggressor's army, knows what's in his trucks, but is one of about four thousand identical drivers, hauling grain as often as shells, and could quit tomorrow and be replaced by Thursday. Not liable.
4. A fighter pilot on the defending, clearly-just side, who has bombed a munitions plant with foreseen but proportionate civilian casualties. Not liable to be targeted by the aggressor's troops.
5. The same conscript from case 1, captured, disarmed, in a cage. Not liable.
6. An aggressor soldier who has deserted, is hiding unarmed in a barn, and has no intention of ever picking up a rifle again. Not liable.

The case she's flagged for next week: a software engineer, national of a neutral third country, working from his own apartment for a private firm. He wrote and still maintains the targeting software the aggressor uses to pick out electrical grid nodes in civilian areas — no one else alive understands the codebase, so if he stops, the campaign stops. He's never worn a uniform, has no rank, isn't on any payroll of the aggressor state, and does it purely for the money, saying he'd take the same contract from any government that paid. Defenders are considering sending a team to kill him. Also in the same building, there's an off-duty uniformed corporal from the aggressor's army who sublets the spare room and does nothing but play video games.

Structure your answer exactly like this:

- **Bottom line** — 3 or 4 sentences: who she says is liable in the new case, and with what confidence.
- **Candidate rules** — about 350 words. Brainstorm here before you commit: lay out at least three principles that could generate all six verdicts (status/role forfeiture for one side's armed forces, graded moral responsibility for an unjust threat, current-threat-only, and anything else you think survives), and say what each one has going for it.
- **Fit check** — about 300 words, a table is fine: each of the six verdicts against each surviving rule, marking whether the rule explains it cleanly, explains it only with a stretch, or flatly contradicts it. Kill off whatever dies.
- **The new case** — about 450 words: run each surviving rule on the engineer and the corporal, note where they diverge, and give your best guess at her verdict plus the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives.
- **The tiebreaker** — about 200 words: one further case, stated concretely, that would come out differently on the two rules you think are still alive, and which of them it would confirm.
- **What she'd be committed to** — about 150 words: if she rules the way you predict, what's the cost? Which intuitively attractive thing does her view have to give up?

Be honest about where her verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer rather than pretending the rules pick out a unique one. No references or reading lists needed — I just want the reasoning.
