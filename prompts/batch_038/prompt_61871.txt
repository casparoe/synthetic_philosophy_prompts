I'm on a charter review commission for a city of about 180,000 — two universities, one huge hospital system that's basically the biggest employer, and a minority community thats roughly 7% of residents but almost entirely concentrated in two neighborhoods on the east side. The commission has to write the "direct democracy" article of the new charter, and I got volunteered to score the candidate instruments before Thursday. The trigger for all this, incidentally, was a fight two years ago over where to site a new middle school, which ended with the council putting it on the east side after a well-funded petition drive, so nobody's approaching this in a neutral mood.

Here's the list I was handed: (1) a mandatory referendum on any tax or fee increase above a set dollar threshold; (2) a citizen-initiated ordinance route with a 5% signature requirement; (3) an abrogative referendum letting voters repeal a council ordinance if petitioners collect signatures within 90 days of passage; (4) a binding citizens' assembly of 40 members chosen by sortition from the voter registration roll; (5) a non-binding citywide ballot question that the council is legally required to respond to in writing within a year; (6) recall of individual council members; (7) a two-track initiative where a proposal qualifies for the ballot at a much lower signature threshold if a council supermajority declines to adopt it as-is. What I need from you is two separate rankings of all seven — one on how well each would protect the east side minority from getting steamrolled on land-use and school-siting decisions, and one on what I'll call epistemic quality, meaning roughly how likely the instrument is to produce an outcome that tracks considerations a well-informed and disinterested resident would judge relevant. Then collapse them: give each instrument a single score from 0 to 10 for what you'd actually recommend putting in the charter, and tell me explicitly where the two rankings pull in opposite directions and how you'd break those ties.

Assume the following when you're scoring, because they're the things that actually bite here: signature gathering is cheap for a well-funded single-issue group and brutally expensive for anyone without one, off-cycle local turnout runs about 18% and skews older and more homeowner-y, and our state constitution doesn't let us weight votes by district or carve out any subject matter as off-limits to amendment, so "just exempt zoning" isn't available to us. Don't send me a reading list or case comparisons — I'll pull those myself — I care about the reasoning and about the numbers being defensible to six people who disagree with each other. If one of the seven is a trap that looks good on paper and I should be arguing against it at the meeting, say which and why.
