I am a fifth-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I am setting the single essay question for the final in our upper-level seminar on testimony and evidence. Our unit on Hume's "Of Miracles" kept stalling on the same difficulty, and I want the exam to test that difficulty rather than the usual summary. Because Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, and takes the laws of nature to rest on exceptionless experience, his test seems to condemn not merely miracle reports but reports of unprecedented natural events as well: European natural philosophers discounted eyewitness accounts of stones falling from the sky for decades, and opinion turned only after the Académie des Sciences sent Jean-Baptiste Biot to investigate the fall of some three thousand stones at L'Aigle in Normandy in April 1803. The students have read Earman's Hume's Abject Failure, so the Bayesian reconstruction of the maxim may be assumed.

Please write, first, the exam question itself, phrased so that a student must say what on Hume's own principles distinguishes a miracle report from a report of a novel natural event, and whether any such distinction can be drawn without already assuming which regularities count as laws. Second, a model answer of about 700 words that works through the reconstruction and pinpoints where the prior improbability of the reported event does the decisive work. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 organised around five must-hit moves, giving the marks for each and one line on the mistake that most often costs students that mark. The question must not be answerable by reciting Hume's definition, his maxim about the falsehood of the testimony, or the four considerations in Part II.
