I'm news editor at my college paper (small, all volunteers, maybe two of us with any actual training) and we have the same fight every week: somebody wants to run something that's probably true but thin on evidence, half the staff says publish because the story matters, half says we're not entitled to it. We just did Clifford and James in my ethics class and instead of writing another essay I want to actually build the thing into a procedure.

So: give me a decision procedure an editor can run on a story in twenty minutes before a print deadline. Ordered questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it hard-stops at no, and where it has to hand the call up to a human because its a values question and not a facts question (our editor-in-chief signs off, we have no lawyer, no fact-checker).

It has to handle the cases that break us: the one where publishing is what produces the rest of the evidence, because a warning gets two more people to come forward; the one where the choice is genuinely forced for readers but totally avoidable for us; and the one where the claim is about somebody's character and they declined to comment. Be explicit about the translation steps — Clifford's rule is about believing, not publishing, and James's genuine-option test is about one person's passional choice, not an institution's — and tell me which parts of each view you kept and which you dropped to make it usable, and where the procedure is really just my judgement wearing a flowchart.

Then run it on three cases: an assault allegation against a grad instructor from one named complainant, no second source, instructor declined to comment; a prof's warning that a supplement students stack before exams interacts badly with a common prescription, based on a handful of case reports; and a story I badly want to run linking a donor to something shady where the whole trail is one memo I can't verify. Let one of them come out against me. End with what the procedure just cannot do.
