For thirty-five years I worked in geophysical fluid dynamics, mostly on the parameterization of turbulence in oceanic and atmospheric circulation models, and I have spent the first years of my retirement reading the philosophy of science with a certain embarrassment: several of the arguments appear to turn on examples drawn from my own trade that I never thought of as arguments at all. I should be grateful for a careful audit of one analogy I keep encountering.

In a correspondence with a philosopher of economics, the defense of Milton Friedman's position — from the 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, the thesis Samuelson dubbed the F-twist — is regularly conducted by way of the map analogy: a scientific model is like a map; a map that omitted nothing would be as useless as a one-to-one map; therefore demanding that a theory's assumptions be realistic descriptions is a category mistake, and what matters is whether the map gets you to where you intend to go. My correspondent uses this to conclude that realism of assumptions is not merely dispensable but epistemically irrelevant, and that the reluctance of practicing scientists to rest on heavily idealized models is aesthetic squeamishness rather than a cognitive concern.

My difficulty is that my own models depart from reality in at least three distinguishable ways, and I cannot see the analogy reaching all three. There are (i) neglected terms whose smallness I can bound after the fact — dropping the Coriolis acceleration in a basin where it is genuinely negligible; (ii) limit idealizations, where a parameter is driven to an extreme such as infinite Reynolds number or an unbounded domain, and where the limit is frequently singular, so that the solution at the limit does not approach the solution near it; and (iii) closure relations, where the term is unknown and one substitutes a constitutive law of one's own devising — an eddy viscosity, a mixing length, a set of tuned constants — which has no counterpart in the physical system whatsoever, and whose coefficients are not properties of the fluid but of the particular flow, so that they must be recalibrated for each application. The map analogy looks comfortable with (i), strained by (ii), and without obvious purchase on (iii), unless we are prepared to let a map contain invented features on the ground that they improve navigation.

Please tell me, then: what corresponds to what — give me the mapping explicitly, a table of correspondences if that is convenient; which disanalogies actually bear on my correspondent's use of the analogy and which are beside the point; what features of maps it quietly imports that models lack, of which the surveyor's prior access to the territory and the localized, maker-known character of a map's distortions are only the first that occur to me; whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and in particular whether case (iii) makes Friedman's thesis stronger rather than weaker, since it is there that prediction succeeds while the assumption not only is unrealistic but has no referent at all; and if the map is the wrong source, what a better analogy would be. I have toyed with the idea that a model is a prosthesis — it does the work of the missing part and is judged by function rather than by resemblance — and I should like to know whether that is an improvement or merely a different confusion.

Please also indicate how confident you are in the main claims you make, and single out the point in your answer of which you are least sure, together with what would settle it.
