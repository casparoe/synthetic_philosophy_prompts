I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary misinformation workshop in about three weeks and I've decided to open with something nobody else will: the 1938 War of the Worlds broadcast, read through the frame / outer message / inner message apparatus from the "location of meaning" chapter of GEB. My co-panelist is doing deepfakes, another is doing the liar's dividend, and I refuse to be the third. The reason I want the Hofstadter lens specifically is that it's not a theory about lying — it's a theory about where in a signal the meaning actually sits, and how that location moves as the channel degrades. My gloss, correct me if I've got it wrong: any message comes in layers, where the frame is the part that tells you what kind of object you're dealing with (this is a broadcast, this is fiction, this is a news bulletin), the outer message is the coarse-grained content (there are 24 preludes here, in this order), and the inner message is the fine-grained stuff (the actual notes) — and the claim is that in a clean channel most of the information is in the inner message, but as noise rises the inner message dissolves first and you fall back on frame plus outer message, with your own prior knowledge filling in the rest. Which makes the broadcast, on paper, close to a perfect natural experiment, because radio in 1938 handed out the frame unequally and non-consensually: if you tuned in late, or if your set was bad, or if you'd been channel-surfing between CBS and NBC and hit the second act cold, the frame had already gone by.

So here's the mapping I want, and I'd rather it be disciplined than sweeping. Be explicit about who the sender is (is it Koch and Welles, CBS, or the news-bulletin *format* itself, which is arguably doing the framing work without anyone intending it), what the channel is, and what counts as noise in this story — and note that there seem to be at least two very different candidate noises, one acoustic/timing (static, tuning in at 8:20) and one interpretive. Then walk the episode: the opening disclaimer, the dance-band-from-a-hotel-ballroom business interrupted by increasingly grim bulletins, the Princeton Observatory astronomer, the heat ray at Grovers Mill, the Secretary of the Interior speech that people apparently took for Roosevelt (who happened to be on the air that same evening), the mid-show announcement that it was a play, the disclaimers bookending the end — something like four of them total, but only one in a place where a late tuner-in would have caught it. Roughly six million people were listening; Cantril's team put the number who were frightened somewhere north of a million, and I don't need you to trust those figures, just work with the shape of them. What does the three-layer account *predict* about which listeners panicked, and does it actually predict the Cantril findings rather than being stretched to fit them after the fact? Two things bother me. One is that a bunch of people who heard the mid-broadcast announcement that it was a dramatization kept believing anyway, and some kept fleeing even after police and neighbors told them what it was — which looks like the frame arriving and failing to take, and I don't think the model has anywhere to put that. The other is Cantril's observation that some listeners who reported not believing a word of it were still badly frightened, which sounds like the inner message getting through while the frame is explicitly rejected. Is that a case of the layers coming apart, or does it show the layers were never really separable to begin with?

Then I want you to push on two ideas of mine that I haven't been able to get past, and tell me whether they're extensions of the framework or evidence against it. First, a timing problem: Hofstadter's picture seems to assume the frame is decoded before you get to work on the content, which is fine for a record or a printed page where you can hold the object still. But a live broadcast delivers its frame *inside* the stream, at one particular moment, and a frame you missed is not the same object as a frame you have. Should we think of the closing disclaimers as frames at all, or as retro-frames that reclassify a message you've already processed — and if meaning can be relocated after the fact, does the "location" metaphor survive? Second, a receiver-side noise problem: the account puts noise in the channel, but the most frightened 1938 listeners often had perfectly clean reception and instead had rotten priors — a population months out of Munich, habituated by real news bulletins to expect that the interruption of a music program means catastrophe, and (per Cantril) less equipped to check the frame against anything. If the degradation is in the decoder rather than the wire, is that still the same theory with one parameter moved, or is it a different theory wearing the old one's clothes? Finally, tell me straight where the framework is silent rather than making it speak: it seems to have nothing to say about *who gets to set* the frame, and nothing to say about the newspapers, which by most later accounts inflated the panic considerably because radio was eating their lunch — so the "mass panic" was itself partly re-framed into existence the next morning by a competitor with an interest in the frame being "radio makes people hysterical." Does that belong inside the model, outside it, or does it break it? End with a verdict I can actually use on a slide: is War of the Worlds a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case Hofstadter's apparatus simply never anticipated — and no references or reading list, I just want the argument.
