I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level population ethics seminar with 18 students, none of whom has read Parfit. Design an in-class activity that gets them to run into the nonidentity problem on their own, with nobody — including me — naming it until the debrief.

The activity should turn on real choices made by the participants: some mechanism (cards, dice, a ballot, a budget allocation) whereby the policy they collectively adopt determines which individuals come to exist inside the simulation and how well off each is, arranged so that every life in the worse outcome is still worth living. The pivotal feature is that participants must answer, in writing and in a fixed format, the person-affecting question — who, if anyone, is worse off in outcome A than in outcome B — before they are permitted to say which outcome is better. Build in a contrast round where the very same harm falls on people who exist under either policy, so they can feel what the identity shift does to their reasoning. Then make them defend their ranking and record precisely which principle they reach for when they do.

Please supply materials and mechanics (nothing requiring money), a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact wording of the written prompts, and a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the structure of the problem and onto the standard moves for escaping it — impersonal principles, a wide person-affecting principle, rights-based and threshold views. Be frank about which feature of the real case a card-and-dice simulation cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — in particular, whether students will try to escape by denying that the lives in the worse outcome are worth living, and how to head that off before it derails the round.
