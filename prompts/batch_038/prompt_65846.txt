Volunteering on a nonprofit's animal-welfare committee that's trying to assign "moral weights" to species roughly in proportion to neuron counts. One of the other members thinks the whole project is incoherent, and she walked me through this and I still can't get out it.

(1) If moral status comes in degrees, then a being's degree of status is fixed by how much experience it has.
(2) The more experience a being has, the more its interests count.
(3) There's no sharp cutoff in how much experience a being has: for any being with experience there's another with only negligibly less.
(4) So there's no point at which moral status drops from something to nothing.
(5) So everything with any experience at all — a nematode, a fly larva, arguably a device that registers anything — has nonzero status.
(6) If a harm to a being carries nonzero weight, then for any harm to a person there is some number of comparable harms to such beings that together outweigh it.
(7) So enough trivial harms to tiny minds outweigh any harm you could do to a human.
(8) There are unimaginably more tiny minds than people, and ordinary farming, driving and walking destroys them wholesale.
(9) So the only thing that matters is minimising trivial harms to tiny minds.
(10) That's clearly not the only thing that matters, so (1) is false: status is binary, on or off.

She presents it as a reductio of the graded-sentience picture and says our neuron-count weights are dressed-up arbitrariness. I don't accept (10) but I also can't point at the bad step — every line looked fine when I read past it. Which one do you reject, why did it slide by so smoothly, and what do I owe once I reject it? I'd like to keep both gradation and (3), since I don't see how anyone defends a cutoff without being arbitrary, and I'd like to keep the committee's project alive if that's still possible.
