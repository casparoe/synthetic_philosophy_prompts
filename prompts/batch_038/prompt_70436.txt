Here's the question as it was set:

> In Politics I.8–10 Aristotle distinguishes two kinds of wealth-getting, and in NE V.5 he explains why exchange needs money. Some readers say that Aristotle's hostility to money-making rests entirely on his view that money is not natural. Is that a fair account of his reasoning? Explain what he objects to in (a) retail trade and (b) lending at interest, and say whether the two objections share a premise. 450 words.

And here's the answer, unedited apart from the spelling of the Greek:

> Aristotle separates wealth-getting into two kinds. The first, household management, is natural: it deals with the goods a household and a city need in order to live, and it has a limit, since the point of it is living well and you need only so much for that. The second, the art of money-making, is unnatural because it has no limit at all — the more you have the more you want, so there is no point at which you can stop. What I think is sharp here is that the unlimitedness is structural rather than a moral complaint: if acquisition is a chain of means, then a kind of acquisition whose end is simply more of the means can't have a stopping rule built into it. Aristotle also notes that the two kinds overlap, since the same possessions can be handled either way, but the difference between them is what each one produces — goods in the first case, money in the second.
>
> Money comes into the picture in the Ethics because exchange has to make unlike things comparable. A house and a pair of shoes can't be compared as such, but both can be related to need, and money has been set up by convention as a stand-in for need, which is what lets us measure one thing against another. Aristotle stresses that money exists by convention and not by nature — he even ties the word nomisma to nomos — and he says it is in our power to change it and make it useless. This, I think, is the whole basis of his hostility: money isn't part of the natural order, so getting a living out of it is getting a living out of something with no real being, and the objection to money-making rests entirely on money's conventional status. It follows that what Aristotle really objects to in retail trade is that it doesn't produce anything, since the trader adds nothing to the goods and simply moves them and charges for the moving.
>
> The sharpest case is interest. Aristotle says usury is hated most of all and with the best reason, because "its gain comes from money itself and not from that for the sake of which money was invented": money was brought into existence for exchange, but interest makes the money itself grow — offspring resembling parent, money born of money — and so this form of wealth-getting is "of all forms the most contrary to nature." But the objection here isn't really about the coin, it's about the harm. The lender gets rich without working, and he gets rich out of somebody else's shortage, since people borrow when they are short. That is why interest also falls under the account of unjust exchange in the Ethics: the lender takes more than his share of what the two of them have between them, which is the graspingness Aristotle says injustice consists in.
>
> So the two objections do share a premise. Both rest on the thought that money is a convention and therefore unnatural, and both are cases of people getting wealth in ways that don't produce wealth.
