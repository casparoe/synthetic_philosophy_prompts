I'm a non-executive director on the board of a big district general hospital trust here in England — logistics background, not a clinician — and I sit on the sub-committee that signs off commercial income. In three weeks the full board has to rule on a proposal from our commercial director, the committee is split three–three with me as the swing vote, and I'd rather circulate a written opinion beforehand than improvise something at the meeting. The proposal: open a weekend self-funder list for routine elective work (cataracts, hernia repairs, primary hip replacements) at a fixed price. The stipulated details matter, so here they are. The theatres are otherwise dark at weekends and the surgeons would be paid sessional rates they'd not otherwise earn. Our analysts model the capacity as strictly incremental — on their numbers, no NHS patient's position on any list moves. The projected surplus, roughly £430k a year, is contractually ring-fenced to fund two extra weekday lists, which the model says shaves about five weeks off the median wait for the same procedures. And self-funders must be drawn from the same referral pool and meet the same clinical criteria, so nobody buys their way ahead of a more urgent case; they only buy a calendar.

Half the committee says that if the capacity really is incremental and the ring-fence genuinely holds, the scheme makes some public patients better off and nobody worse off, so all that's left is squeamishness about money touching medicine. The other half says the entire point of a service allocated by need is that money doesn't buy time, that a trust which sells time has stopped treating its patients as equals whatever the spreadsheet says, and that the incremental claim is fragile in practice anyway — scrub teams and anaesthetists are one fixed pool, not two, a consultant with a lucrative weekend list has less appetite for fighting to make the weekday list run efficiently, and today it's cataracts while tomorrow somebody will argue for oncology follow-ups. My own frustration is that the two sides keep talking past each other: one is doing an accounting of harms and the other is making a claim about what the institution expresses, and I don't think the accounting can settle the expressive claim — but I also don't think the expressive claim gets to walk away from the fact that turning the scheme down leaves the nine-month waits exactly where they are, which is a choice and not a clean pair of hands.

So: draft the opinion I'll circulate, call it 900–1,200 words, addressed to the board rather than to philosophers. It has to land on an actual recommendation — approve, reject, or approve subject to conditions — and if it's conditions, name ones a board could administer and monitor (hard caps, who may be sold a slot, what gets published and how often, what specific trigger pulls the sunset review) rather than general assurances about protecting the ethos. Treat the incremental-capacity claim as the hinge it is: say explicitly what has to be true for the accounting to hold, and how a non-clinician director like me could find out whether it is true. Then give the losing side its best run — the version of the objection that survives the spreadsheet — and say why it loses, or wins. Finally, and this matters most to me, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the one point you're least sure of; my own candidate is whether the expressive objection is doing genuinely independent work or is just a proxy for the second-order incentive worries, but tell me if you think the weak joint is somewhere else. Don't hedge everything into mush — I'd much rather have a committed memo with an honest flag on its weakest part than a balanced one that recomends nothing.
