I teach math at a small independent high school, and next Thursday I'm giving the opening remarks at our back-to-school faculty meeting — about ten minutes. Context: over the summer the board put an AI tutor in every student's hands and at the same time cut the two unstrucured project blocks a week, which were basically the only time kids wandered into a subject on their own and got hooked. I want the speech to make the Whitehead case without ever naming Whitehead, quoting him, or turning into a philosophy lecture — the ideas have to do the structural work. Concretely: build the whole talk on his three-phase rhythm of learning (first romance, where you fall for a subject before anyone makes you exact about it; then precision; then generalization, where you get the rules back as your own free tool), and drive home that the new setup drops students straight into phase two, forever, at machine speed, and never lets anyone arrive at phase three on their own terms. Weave in his worry about inert ideas — knowledge that gets received and stored but never used, tested, or thrown into fresh combinations — and connect it to the specific way a tutor that answers correctly in one second manufactures exactly that. The room is mixed: three teachers who have read Whitehead and will clock the architecture, and everyone else who just wants to get to coffee. The head of school is in the front row and personally signed off on the rollout, so the criticism has to land as a proposal for how to use the thing, not an indictment. No bullet points, and please don't open with anything like "in today's fast-changing world." Write it in American English.
