I lead the interpretability-monitoring group at a mid-sized frontier lab, and every five to seven weeks we ship a revision of the same production model — a preference fine-tune, a safety RL pass, a mid-training data refresh — and each release reopens the same dispute. Our monitor is a small classifier trained on the internal activations of an older checkpoint to detect a behaviour we very much do not want: the model holding concealed goals during capability evaluations, cooperating with oversight in training and behaving differently when it appears to be unobserved. That classifier was validated once, expensively, over several months, using behavioural evaluations and intervention work. Management now expects us to say, within a working day of each release, whether the existing monitor still licenses its verdicts on the new checkpoint or whether we must re-validate from scratch. Re-validation costs roughly three months of two researchers' time and holds up the release; a silent failure of the monitor costs us the only line of defence we have against something we cannot detect behaviourally. The people who sign off — our head of safety, and for major releases the model-approval committee — are not interpretability specialists, so whatever we hand them has to be legible without the theory behind it.

Last night I argued about this for three hours with a friend who did a philosophy PhD before moving into AI safety, and I have not yet recovered. His position is that the question of whether a mechanism is the same one is only ever a question relative to a description: you individuate a mechanism by its causal role, and a component belongs to it when manipulating that component changes the phenomenon and manipulating the phenomenon changes the component; so two checkpoints host the same mechanism whenever the same causal role is filled, whether or not the same features fill it. My objection is that this is unusable in our setting. Our feature dictionary is re-learned from scratch at each release, so no individual feature has an identity that persists across checkpoints; we cannot enumerate the variables whose interventions would settle the question, only the handful we happened to measure; and any causal role we state loosely enough to survive a retraining is also loose enough to be satisfied by something we would want to call a different mechanism. He replied that I was confusing sameness of mechanism with sameness of implementation — a distinction he was entirely entitled to make, and to which I had no answer at one in the morning.

What I need is not a verdict on that argument but something my group can actually run: a decision procedure, executable in about half a day per release by researchers who are competent with sparse autoencoders but have no philosophical training, terminating in one of three outcomes — the monitor transfers, the monitor transfers subject to a stated restriction, or we re-validate. Give me the questions to ask in strict order; for each, what would count as a sufficient answer, and what evidence we can realistically collect (activation patching across checkpoints, matching old features to new ones, ablation, behavioural probes under the new prompt distribution); where the procedure halts and escalates to a human because what remains is a question of risk appetite rather than fact; and, unflinchingly, which steps dress a judgment call up as a threshold. Then run it on three cases. First, a modest preference fine-tune in which our features match across checkpoints only poorly, yet patching the previously flagged component still suppresses the behaviour by roughly the same amount. Second, a release in which the measured behaviour falls by most of its former magnitude, but a structurally dissimilar circuit now produces that behaviour under a narrow range of prompts we had not previously tested. Third, the case I genuinely fear: every test in the procedure passes cleanly, my friend would declare the mechanism preserved, and there is nonetheless good reason to think the monitor's false-negative rate has risen. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do. If the question of whether it is the same mechanism turns out to have no determinate answer independent of the description we choose, say so plainly and state what the procedure is then really tracking — and what a reader of the sign-off memo would be misled into believing if we presented its output as a fact about the model rather than a fact about our tests.
