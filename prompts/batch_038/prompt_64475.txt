So — weird situation and I could use help thinking it through in writing.

I'm a novelist. Four books, all fiction, zero history training, and somehow I've been on the jury of a nonfiction prize for two years running. Five jurors, twenty grand, money from a family foundation. The deed says the prize goes to "the work of nonfiction that most advances public understanding of history," and it explicitly forbids splitting the award. The citation goes to the printer in three weeks.

We're deadlocked 2–2, the chair won't break the tie, and the two books are both about the British Raj — basically the Ferguson–Tharoor argument wearing different clothes.

Book A is by an economic historian and frames itself openly as a balance sheet. There's a literal ledger chapter tallying what British rule cost India — famine mortality, deindustrialization, revenue drained to London — against what it left behind: railways, irrigation works, a legal code, a civil service, a unified administration. Its verdict is that the human ledger runs deeply negative but the institutional inheritance is real and a lot of modern India runs on it. Confident, densely sourced, reads like a cross-examination. Two of us want to crown it.

Book B is by a historian who argues that the ledger *is* the flaw. You cant book railways as a credit when they were financed out of Indian revenues and routed to move troops and cotton out of the interior; famine deaths were policy choices, not weather; the institutions arrived bundled with communal electorates and a census that hardened the categories in which Partition later became thinkable. Angrier, more essayistic, sometimes gorgeous. The other two jurors want it to win.

Each has a blemish, and the blemishes aren't the same kind. Book A has a chapter where the famine-death arithmetic quietly switches baselines mid-argument — our one economist caught it, and the author hasnt corrected it in the second printing. Book B, in its single best passage, asserts a death toll for one famine that most of the literature puts materially lower, in a sentence obviously built to be read aloud.

Also, the foundation's program officer has emailed twice about how much the trustees admire "even-handedness," which nobody has defined and everybody noticed.

My hunch, which I can't get past the others: we're not actually fighting about facts. We're fighting about form. One book is a ledger, one is an indictment, and each form pre-commits you to a way of aggregating across people and generations that the jurors are just reacting to viscerally. If I'm right, the prize should go to whichever book's form doesn't lie to the reader even when its entries are true.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday. It needs: (1) a recommendation — one winner, or if you think there's a legitimate third path inside a deed that forbids splitting, name it and argue why it isn't cowardice, and say what we do about the foundation's emails; (2) a straight answer on the question underneath, which is whether netting costs against benefits across a century of rule is a coherent moral operation at all, and what would have to be true for it to be; (3) a way to weigh the two blemishes against each other given that one is a rigor failure the author knows about and the other is a number inflated for cadence; (4) the strongest case for the book you *don't* pick, stated in terms its champion on our jury could actually answer; and (5) one paragraph operationalizing "advances public understanding" that a future jury could apply without it collapsing into "the book I already agreed with."

Two pages, prose five laypeople can read aloud. No reading list, no citations, don't tell me what to go read — I need something we can vote on Thursday.
