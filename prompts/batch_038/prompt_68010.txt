I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the last several months in the literature on longtermism and the ethics of the far future. One thread in that literature keeps surfacing in my interviews and I cannot get a clean read on it: the suggestion that Bostrom's simulation argument, usually filed under metaphysical curiosities, actually bears on how much weight the far future deserves. A reader with a formal background — a statistician who publishes philosophy on the side — has sent me a compact version of the case, and I need to know whether it survives scrutiny before I describe it in print as anything more than clever. It runs as follows, in his numbering. Let p stand for one's credence that we are inhabitants of a computer simulation operated by a more technologically mature civilization (call that hypothesis SIM), and 1 − p for one's credence that we are in base reality (BASE).

(1) Rational choice under uncertainty should aim at the probability-weighted sum of value across possible futures.

(2) On BASE, our descendants can in principle spread beyond the Earth and draw on the resources of at least our own galaxy, so the number of future lives affected by what we do now is astronomically large; let V be the value of the best such future.

(3) On SIM, the future open to our descendants is capped by whatever computational budget our simulators are prepared to spend on this particular run. Any civilization capable of high-fidelity ancestor simulations can run very many of them and has every reason to keep each one cheap, so ours is almost certainly among the cheap ones, and the value at stake on SIM is some negligible fraction εV with ε near zero.

(4) On SIM, moreover, nothing we do reaches past the run: our choices cannot alter anything in the world where our simulators live, and only what happens there can affect the deep future of anything that is really there.

(5) By (1) through (4), the expected value of a project aimed at steering the far future is at most (1 − p)V + pεV, which for small ε is approximately (1 − p)V.

(6) It follows that the case for such a project shrinks in direct proportion to one's credence in SIM: raising p by δ removes roughly δV of expected value, and no offsetting term appears anywhere.

(7) Therefore the simulation argument is not an idle puzzle but a systematic deflator of far-future ethics. A rational longtermist should discount the entire programme by (1 − p), and since it is rational to assign SIM some nonzero credence, fanaticism about the far future is at least somewhat undercut.

He supplied a gloss as well. What keeps p from being dismissible is the trilemma in Bostrom's paper: either almost no human-level civilizations survive to the point of running ancestor simulations, or almost none of those that survive want to run them, or else nearly everyone whose experiences resemble ours is simulated. And (7) is meant to be the interesting upshot — a piece of anthropic reasoning that quietly disciplines a piece of moral reasoning, rather than the usual result that the two never meet.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a re-litigation of the framing. Which premise or which transition fails, and what made it look acceptable when you first read it? If instead the argument is valid and the only real question is which plausible premise is least costly to surrender, say so plainly and rank the candidates. Two further requests about the deliverable. First, for each premise you would give up, tell me what the longtermist forfeits by giving it up and what the skeptic actually gains — I need to be able to explain the price, not merely announce a verdict. Second, tell me whether the reasoning generalizes: if this move works against the far future, does it also work against commitments I would be unwilling to lose, and should I count that as a flaw in the argument or as evidence that I have been inconsistent all along?

For what it is worth, I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), which are the premises my longtermist sources defend openly and which I would like the analysis to engage rather than wave away. I have no stake in the rest, and I would rather be told which of them to attack than be reassured that the whole thing is fine.
