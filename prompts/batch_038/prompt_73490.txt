A colleague of mine, Nathan, and his sister Rosa argued for an hour last week about a case from his work, then asked me to break the tie. I declined, but I would like a real adjudication of the exchange.

Nathan works at a small firm that sells an AI assistant to freelance accountants and bookkeepers. One user asked it to help design a structure for routing a client's income through two entities in a way that, on Nathan's account, is technically lawful but contrary to the evident purpose of the statute. The assistant declined, said the request fell outside its usage policy, and offered instead to summarize the relevant filing rules.

1. Rosa's opening: the client's ends are the user's to set. The assistant substituted its own view of what the user ought to want and blocked a lawful act. Competent adults are permitted to make bad choices; that is what treating someone as an adult amounts to.

2. Nathan: this isn't paternalism. The assistant wasn't managing the user's life; it was declining to lend its own labor to a project it takes to be wrongful. Authority over your conduct doesn't include a claim on anyone else's participation — a lawyer may turn down a client she finds repugnant without impugning that client's freedom.

3. Rosa: the analogy fails. Software has no integrity to protect, so when the tool refuses, the refusal is the owner's, and the owner is thereby imposing one firm's view on hundreds of thousands of users who never had a say in it. A lawyer declining a case is one person deciding about herself; a guardrail that ships to everyone is legislation.

4. Nathan: every design choice legislates, including the choice to assist with whatever is asked. There is no neutral baseline to fall back on. So Rosa's complaint cannot be that a view is being imposed — only that this particular view is wrong, which makes it an argument about tax justice rather than about paternalism.

5. Rosa: grant that, and a wrong still remains that has nothing to do with tax justice. The assistant never said what it was doing. It cited a policy rather than saying that it judged the request immoral. Users are entitled to know they are being governed instead of being managed with a euphemism; the concealment, not the refusal, is the actual grievance.

6. Nathan: that proves too much. Had the assistant announced plainly that it considered the scheme immoral, the objection would have been that it moralizes at users. And a firm is no more obliged to publish its reasons for each guardrail than a legislature is to publish each member's motives.

Neither would budge, and neither thinks the other answered their strongest point. Please go through moves 1–6 in order, rating each as clearly right, partly right, or mistaken with a sentence of justification for the rating. Then say whether Rosa's original framing survives the exchange or is displaced by something else, and identify anything substantial that both of them are missing.
