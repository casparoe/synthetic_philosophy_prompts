Act as a philosopher of statistics who has also spent years maintaining a production A/B-testing platform. Before designing anything, think carefully and spend a short opening section brainstorming which underlying commitments — not which camp labels — actually separate the positions in the frequentist-versus-Bayesian dispute and in the methodological arguments around the replication crisis.

Some context. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training; I know the vocabulary only from blog posts, so please don't assume I can adjudicate anything. I own the experimentation platform at a mid-size consumer app. What we ship is a hybrid I can't locate in the standard two-camp picture: an engineer may stop a test the moment the posterior probability that the variant beats control crosses 95%, under a default weakly informative prior, and we simultaneously spend a Benjamini–Hochberg-style budget across all running experiments to cap the share of shipped "wins" that turn out to be null. Peeking isn't tolerated, it's the interface.

Deliverable: a decision tree of eight to twelve branch nodes that walks one of my engineers from a claim they would sincerely endorse to a named position. Every node must be a concrete operational question — about what a probability attaches to, whether stopping rules matter once the data are in, whether two analysts with identical data and different intentions have identical evidence, whether a procedure's long-run behavior can bear on this single result, what preregistration actually buys — answerable without knowing which leaf it leads to. No node may ask anyone to self-identify as frequentist or Bayesian. Leaves must include the Fisherian and Neyman–Pearson positions, error statistics, subjective / objective / empirical Bayes, the model-checking view that treats both frameworks as approximations, a "this debate is a category error" pragmatist, and our platform's hybrid.

Then, briefly: mark any cell your tree reaches that you believe nobody defends and say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — our hybrid is the case I most want diagnosed; flag any two leaves your questions cannot in fact separate; and identify one node where a perfectly reasonable answer would send someone down a branch that misrepresents them. Keep the whole thing to roughly one page of tree plus a short diagnosis section.
