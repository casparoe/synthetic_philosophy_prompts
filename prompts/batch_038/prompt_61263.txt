Question 3 (700 words max). In the Keynesian beauty-contest game, n players each pick a number in [0,100]; the winner is whoever's number is closest to two thirds of the average of all the numbers chosen. (a) Say what the iterated-dominance analysis predicts and how much reasoning that takes. (b) In experiments the modal choices cluster well above the predicted value and only a minority pick it. Explain what that pattern is and isn't evidence for, keeping in view the difference between the claim that players aren't rational and the claim that rationality isn't common knowledge. (c) Does the pattern show that common knowledge of rationality is doing any real work in the theory? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

(a) Two thirds of the average is at most 66.7, so nothing above 66.7 can ever be closest to it and every such choice is strictly dominated. Once those go the average is at most 66.7, so two thirds of it is at most about 44.4, and everything above that goes too, and so on down. The rounds never terminate though: after k rounds you're left with [0, 100 x (2/3)^k], and 0 is what you get in the limit. Since 0 is what survives the whole process, it strictly dominates every other number, and the game is dominance-solvable, so the unique rationalizable outcome is everyone playing 0. That's an equilibrium as well, and its the only one when p = 2/3; if p were 1, so you win by being closest to the actual average, then any number would be an equilibrium provided everyone picks the same one.

The deletion argument needs iterated knowledge, not just rationality. The first round only needs the players to be rational. The second needs them to know that the others are rational. Getting all the way to 0 needs common knowledge of rationality: everyone rational, everyone knows that, everyone knows that they know, without end. Deleting strictly dominated strategies is order-independent, which is why none of this depends on the sequence you do the rounds in.

(b) Nagel's guessing-game experiments (Nagel 1995) find a big cluster around 33, a smaller one around 22, a spike at 0, and a thin spread over the rest. The usual reading is level-k or cognitive hierarchy: level-0 players pick at random, level-1 best-responds to level-0 and lands near 33, level-2 best-responds to level-1 and lands near 22, and most people stop at level 1 or 2.

I think this is evidence against common knowledge of rationality rather than against rationality itself. If players were rational and knew each other to be rational to all orders, everyone would play 0; they don't, so one of those two assumptions has to fail. And since the level-k models have players doing the best-response calculation correctly, just fewer times of it, what's failing is the knowledge assumption. So the data locate the failure in common knowledge, not in rationality.

(c) Less sure here. Common knowledge of rationality does do work somewhere: in the centipede game its what makes backward induction unravel from the first node, and Rubinstein's email game shows that even when there's only a tiny chance of a message getting lost the players still end up coordinating much as they would have, so almost-common-knowledge is nearly as good as the real thing. But in the beauty contest I don't think it's doing much, because you can't tell from the choices whether players failed to iterate or failed to know that others would iterate. Both give the same distribution. So the beauty contest is a bad place to test common knowledge of rationality, and the fact that people stop at level 2 doesn't by itself show the assumption is empty, it just shows it isn't satisfied in this game.
