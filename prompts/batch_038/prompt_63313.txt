I spent forty-one years in invertebrate ecology, most of it counting small things through a hand lens, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy at random from the county library's shelves, badly but with great enjoyment; I find most of it is written for other philosophers rather than for me. My late wife and I bought twenty-seven acres of ravine hemlock — eleven hectares, as I still prefer to think of it — in 1988, and I now look after it alone, with worse eyes than I had. Hemlock woolly adelgid is throughout the stand at what my own branch counts suggest is a middle phase of decline: several ovisacs per centimetre of new growth on most mature trees, thinning crowns visible from the ridge, four greys already standing dead. I can afford, spread across the rest of my life, a systemic soil-injection treatment of the eighty largest trees, renewed every five years or so in perpetuity, which would very likely keep those trees alive past my grandchildren. The only alternatives I have been offered are a horticultural oil that must be wetted onto every centimetre of a mature crown — which my eyesight can no longer supervise to any standard — and a wait-and-see with the two predatory beetles being released for biological control, which will not save these eighty trees in my lifetime.

Two arguments have been pressed on me by people I respect, and neither sits right. The county forester, who is careful and honest about trees, tells me the product moves into the foliage and does nothing to an animal that does not feed on hemlock, so there is no ethical question at all here: I am choosing between eighty trees and eighty trees' worth of adelgids, and the second are the ones I am trying to save the first from. Yet my own reading of the trial plots in this region is that treated hemlocks flare up with spruce spider mite and rust mite, because the treatment removes their predators along with the target; the stand I would keep is not the stand I have, and I am not confident the total number of small animals living in it goes down. My neighbour, a retired economist who reads more philosophy than I do, takes the other line: my eleven hectares are a rounding error inside an infestation of some hundreds of thousands of hectares, the ravine will change whether I inject or not, and an act that alters nothing cannot be wrong — so abstaining on conscience grounds is a way of feeling virtuous at the price of eighty trees, and it spares not one insect. When I did the arithmetic that keeps me awake, though, it points the other way: a mature hemlock carries some hundreds of thousands of feeding adelgids, so eighty trees on a five-year cycle for as long as my family holds this land is on the order of tens of millions of individual deaths, each of them an animal that — if an animal of that sort is capable of anything at all — has something done to it. I cannot tell whether that sum is enormous or negligible, and I notice that both of my advisers have simply assumed an answer to that question without registering that they were answering it. My granddaughter, who is nine and whom I taught to turn over logs, asked me last week why I intended to poison the bugs on our trees, and I gave her no honest reply.

So I should like a recommendation rather than a survey: treat, or do not treat, and why — and, if you think my two advisers are each missing something different, say what the two different things are. Because I am a scientist by habit and distrust a tidy conclusion, I ask one thing further. Tell me plainly what would change your mind: which single claim, if I could establish it as fact or as false, would flip your recommendation; and identify the step in your own reasoning where you think you are most likely to be wrong, as distinct from merely unsure. If your answer leans on a premise you would not care to defend under questioning, flag it rather than smoothing it over.
