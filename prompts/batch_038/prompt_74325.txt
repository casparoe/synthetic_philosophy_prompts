I'm the managing editor of a mid-sized journal in experimental psychology. We went fully double-blind three years ago: names, affiliations and acknowledgements stripped before the file ever reaches a reviewer, plus a standing instruction not to try to work out who wrote what, and to tell us if you find out by accident. But our submission system has a reviewer-finder tool that suggests referees by pulling up publication histories on the manuscript's topic, which nine times out of ten makes the author group obvious to whoever's doing the assigning — so we identify people in order to pick reviewers, and then ask everyone to un-know it. And separately, the same editorial board comissions review articles and editorials explicitly on the grounds of who's eminent, and we publish acceptance rates broken out by institution every single year. So we're not anti-evidence as a culture. We're anti-evidence in one very specific place.

A board member who does decision analysis for a living told me our policy commits us to the claim that we'd be worse off knowing more, and that this flatly contradicts a theorem — I think Good's? — to the effect that free information can never lower a rational Bayesian's expected utility, because you can always choose to ignore it. If he's right then either the theorem doesn't apply to us, or we're implicitly denying one of its assumptions, and I'd like to know which.

So: what theory of the value of information does double-blind review actually presuppose? Take me through the candidate reconstructions — that author identity is bad evidence (bias without information), that it's good evidence we've decided we're not entitled to use, that information simply isn't free for us once you count attention, reviewer recruitment and legal exposure, or that our utility function is partly procedural, i.e. fairness has value in itself so using the info lowers expected value even when it raises accuracy. Are those four genuinely rival theories, or one theory with different bookkeeping? Which views does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it underdetermined — where would several incompatible theories all license exactly what we do? Then tell me which condition of the theorem a board of editors is most plausibly failing to meet: that we're a group rather than an agent, that reviewers are choosing under a utility that isn't the journal's, that the utility is supposed to be fixed over consequences rather than over what we know, or that the decision isn't a one-off act at all but something that changes who submits to us next year.

Finally, if we took the theorem at face value, what would actually have to change in how we run review — and would that be an improvement, or just a more respectable way of entrenching a bias we already have?
