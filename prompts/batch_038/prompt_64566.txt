For several years I have been defending what I would call a Hayekian-Bayesian position on the epistemic function of markets, and a colleague has told me flatly that the package does not cohere. He would not say where, and I would rather find out from an independent audit than from his next reply. So I am setting out my commitments as explicitly as I can, without indicating which of them I would be most reluctant to surrender — I genuinely do not know, and I have been assured that I should not assume the problem lies where I suspect it does.

1. I am a Bayesian, and I treat the common prior assumption as a substantive claim rather than a modeling convenience. There is one prior over the relevant state space; what separates agents is the evidence each has conditioned on. Persistent disagreement is therefore always, at bottom, a difference in evidence or in how evidence was processed, never a difference in starting points.

2. I accept the no-trade result in its standard form: with common priors, an ex ante efficient initial allocation, and common knowledge of rationality, a proposed speculative trade cannot be common knowledge to be strictly profitable for all parties to it. More importantly, I treat this as carrying normative weight rather than being a curiosity about idealized models. On my reading, a large share of observed trading volume is something fully rational agents simply should not be doing.

3. I accord Aumann's agreement theorem comparable standing, and I think it licenses a general suspicion of durable expert disagreement: when two economists who have read the same literature and have access to the same data go on disagreeing about the output effects of a given fiscal program, I take it that at least one of them is failing to be rational in some identifiable respect.

4. My positive thesis about markets is Hayekian. What markets do distinctively well is to mobilize dispersed, local, and largely unarticulated knowledge about particular conditions — knowledge that no central authority could gather and state — and they mobilize it by encoding it in prices. It is this informational role, and not the incentive properties emphasized by later writers on the calculation debate, that explains why decentralized allocation outperforms centralized allocation.

5. I hold a moderately strong efficiency view: at any moment, prices are the best available summary of what is known about fundamentals, and I use them that way in my own reasoning about policy.

6. At the same time, I think much of the trading that generates those prices is noise — overconfident, underinformed, or sentiment-driven — and I do not regard noise trading as a marginal phenomenon confined to small accounts.

7. Nevertheless, I oppose measures that would suppress speculative volume (transaction taxes, position limits, restrictions on short selling), because I believe they would degrade the informativeness of prices and so damage the function described in 4.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature:

- Say which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. Do not flag everything; part of what I need is discrimination.
- For each genuine tension, identify what has to give and what the cheapest revision is, together with what I lose by making it — in particular, whether the revision costs me any part of the thesis in 4, which is the one I care most about.
- Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and say why the ranking comes out that way.
- Where a tension is only apparent, explain exactly what assumption does the dissolving work and whether that assumption is one I am entitled to given the rest of the package.
- Note any commitments whose fate is entangled: that is, any revision that would leave some other commitment unmotivated even if the two are not strictly contradictory.
- Close with the single revision that cleans up the most, and describe the resulting position in enough detail that I could recognize it as something a coherent economist or philosopher actually holds.

Please write your answer in American English.
