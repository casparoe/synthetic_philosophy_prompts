A friend of mine did a philosophy degree and has offered to spend an evening pulling my views on infinity apart, on condition that I write them down first. I should say I have no training in any of this: it comes from reading and watching lectures on my own over several years. Here is the list she will be attacking.

(a) Hilbert's hotel is decisive about the concrete world. An infinite hotel that is already full and can still take in another guest, with no change in how many guests there are, shows that an actually infinite collection of things cannot exist out there in reality. What makes it absurd is not the strangeness of it but the fact that a part would equal the whole.

(b) Cantor's diagonal argument I find completely convincing. Take any list of decimal expansions and you can always build a number missing from it, so no procedure whatever can exhaust the reals; there are always more left over.

(c) I am not a Platonist about sets, and I do not think infinite totalities exist as finished objects. An infinity, for me, is always something being generated by a rule — potential, never completed.

(d) I do not think mathematics needs completed infinities for anything. I have never come across a result that seemed to require one, and I doubt there is such a result.

(e) Zeno's race is not a puzzle. Achilles really does traverse infinitely many distinct stretches of track, they are all there, their lengths add up to the whole distance, and there is nothing incoherent about getting through infinitely many stages in a finite time.

Before she arrives, I would like a check of my own: which of these actually conflict with one another, and which merely look like they conflict until you examine them closely? Please rank the real conflicts by how much damage they do rather than treating them as equally bad, say what would have to be given up in each case, and tell me which single revision would buy me the most consistency and what it would cost me.
