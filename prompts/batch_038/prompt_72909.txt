You are a professional historian of the French Revolution who has spent years teaching a joint literature-and-history seminar and is therefore equally comfortable arguing about narrative form and about archives. Please think this problem through carefully and in stages rather than answering off the cuff; the analysis matters far more than the coverage.

Last night a friend and I argued for four hours about *A Tale of Two Cities* and finished almost exactly where we began, so I would like a better version of the disagreement than either of us could produce. My position was that the novel is an argument in the philosophy of history wearing a melodrama's clothes, and that it commits itself to a fairly specific account of why the Terror happened. My friend's position was that I was flattering Dickens: that the book contains no theory of the Revolution at all, only an extremely persuasive mood and a moral warning, and that everything I was calling a causal thesis was my own framework draped over some very good atmosphere. I want you to adjudicate, and to tell me plainly if my friend is right.

The question I want you to take up first is this: which of the great explanatory families in the historiography of the Revolution does the novel actually instantiate, if any? By these I mean, roughly:

1. the social or class account in the tradition of Lefebvre and Soboul, on which revolutionary violence is intelligible as a class taking and then defending power, with the Terror downstream of material antagonism;
2. the revisionist political-cultural account associated with Furet, on which the Terror grows out of the Revolution's own claim to speak with one undivided voice for the people, so that a logic of unanimity generates a logic of the scaffold without much help from circumstances;
3. a contingency account of the kind Tackett has pressed, on which radicalization is something that happened to particular men under particular pressures — war, invasion panic, the king's flight — and might plausibly have gone otherwise.

If my labels or attributions are crude or wrong, correct them; that correction is part of what I want, not a distraction from it.

Then come the two distinctions I invented during the argument and now distrust, which I would like you to pressure-test rather than adopt. The first is between what I called *harvest necessity* and *process inevitability*. Harvest necessity is the retrospective claim that the Terror is the crop grown from seed sown by the Old Regime's own cruelty — the novel seems to me to assert something like this early and to lean on it heavily. Process inevitability is the prospective claim that once the machinery is running it no longer needs its original cause and feeds on what it produces. My suspicion is that Dickens is often credited with the first view when large parts of the book are really doing the second, and that this is why readers cannot agree on whether he blames the aristocracy or the Revolution. Is that a real distinction with real work to do, or two labels for one thing?

The second is what I called the *ledger theory*. Madame Defarge is not, in the novel, an ideologue: she advances almost no principles and makes almost no arguments. Her instrument is a register of names, a running account of debts owed by particular families to particular victims. On this reading the book's actual political theory is a theory of bookkeeping — justice as the enforcement of a list, and the Terror as what happens when the list becomes self-authorizing, when keeping it counts as reason enough to execute it. I would like to know whether this reading survives contact with the scenes, and whether the register is better described as a class document, an ideological one, or an accident that a different set of circumstances would never have produced.

Please work through this against specific episodes rather than in the abstract. The broken wine cask in the Saint Antoine suburb and the word daubed on the wall in the spillage; the echoing footsteps in the quiet house in Soho, which read to me like the novel's own footnote on historical causation; the letter read aloud in court that condemns Darnay for what his father and uncle did; the grindstone in the prison courtyard, with the crowd at work sharpening blades; the knitting women in the gallery at the trials; Madame Defarge's own death by her own weapon; and the closing vision of a restored city that Carton is made to see. For each, say whether it carries a causal claim or only atmosphere, and if a claim, which of the three families it belongs to.

Then take up the devouring question directly. The old image of the Revolution as a Saturn eating its own children is usually applied to the Jacobins destroying one another. Where does the novel locate the devouring — in individual psychology, in institutional logic, or in something more like a natural force? Who, precisely, is devoured, and is the devouring presented as tragic, as self-limiting, or as deserved? I think Defarge himself is quietly destroyed by his wife's escalation and that this is the novel's least noticed argument, but I want your judgment, not my confirmation.

Finally, argue my friend's case at its strongest, and then answer it. The strongest version I can construct is that the ending abandons the inquiry: after two-thirds of a book spent asking why this happened, the question is dissolved into a private redemption and a prophecy of restoration, which is consolation rather than explanation, and which retroactively reveals the earlier causal imagery as mood after all. Say whether you think that is right, whether it is fatal, and whether a substitution that cancels a debt instead of transferring one to someone else can be read as the novel's answer to the register rather than its evasion.

A few working instructions. Begin with a short brainstorming section: three or four candidate readings of the novel's theory of the Terror, ranked by how much textual work they actually do and by how badly they fail, with the failures named. Only then write the essay, in clearly headed sections. Be faithful in both directions — do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a historiographical position, and do not bend the historiography to fit the novel; at minimum, identify two places where the mapping breaks down and say what an honest critic must concede there. Do not quote the novel or the historians; describe scenes and paraphrase claims, and if you are unsure of a detail of plot or of anyone's position, say so explicitly rather than supplying something plausible. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want the analysis, not the references. Close with a paragraph on what the novel contributes to the debate about how the Revolution should be explained that argument and archive alone cannot supply. Aim for roughly 1,500 to 1,900 words.
