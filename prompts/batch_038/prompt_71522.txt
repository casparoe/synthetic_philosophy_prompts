I'm a junior and I run the ethics club at my school, which is like eight people arguing in an empty classroom on thursdays. Last month we did the supererogation week — acts that are good but not required, donating a kidney, jumping on a grenade, giving away most of your paycheck, that sort of thing — and I tried to build an argument that I thought demolished the whole category. My friend says it demolishes a lot more than the category and I can't tell whether she's right, and neither of us could actually point to where it breaks. Here it is:

(1) An act is required of you only if doing it wouldn't cost you too much. So what's required depends on how costly the act is for you.
(2) Cost varies from person to person. Same act, different agent, different cost — losing a kidney costs me more than it costs someone who has a spare, or who minds pain way less than I do.
(3) So by (1) and (2) the same act can be required of one person and supererogatory for another. Nothing about the act settles which it is; the agent settles it.
(4) And an agent's costs are things the agent can shape over time. You can train yourself to care less about your own comfort and money, or you can let yourself care more.
(5) So someone who's trained herself to be really unselfish finds beneficence cheap, and by (1) cheap beneficence is required of her — which means she basically has no room left to do anything above and beyond.
(6) Meanwhile someone who's cultivated selfishness finds even small decency expensive, so by (1) it isn't required of her, and when she does manage it, it goes beyond her duty.
(7) So the better a person you become, the smaller your space of praiseworthy options gets, and the worse you are, the bigger it gets. The saint is obligated to do the exact thing the jerk gets celebrated for doing.
(8) Which means anyone who wants chances to do genuinely credit-worthy good has a reason to stay somewhat selfish, and that's absurd.
(9) Therefore (1) is false — cost to the agent doesn't decide what's required.

My friend's counter is that I havent refuted the cost condition at all, I've just shown it applies to whole agents in their actual situations, and that (7) and (8) don't really follow from (3)–(6). She couldn't say which step she rejects though, and honestly neither could I. I'd really like to keep (1), because if I give it up I don't see what stops every act of beneficence from being obligatory for everyone, which feels insane to me. So: which step do you cut, why did it look obviously fine before you cut it, and what do you have to pay for cutting it — and if there's more than one candidate, price each one, because I suspect that's the actual answer. Also, and this matters since our club gets annoying about it: please be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on supererogation and which parts are genuinely contested, and don't dress up one side of a live disagreement as the consensus. And if my whole setup is off — like if (1)–(3) aren't how anyone actually frames the category in the first place, so the argument is attacking a straw version of it — just say that instead of playing along.
