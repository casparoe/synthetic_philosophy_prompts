I work on reliability engineering for machine-learning systems — error budgets, service-level objectives, failure-mode analysis — and for about a year I have been reading arguments about AI alignment in my spare time. One distinction that recurs in that material is between worst-case and average-case guarantees. Trying to apply it to systems I actually own, I have found that my verdicts about which guarantees I will accept do not form a coherent set. I have no formal philosophy training, so I will state them the way an engineer would: as things I would or would not ship. What I want is the principle I am implicitly applying, an assessment of whether it can be repaired, and a direct statement of which of my verdicts have to go.

The cases, with my gut verdicts. I have tried to keep the numbers comparable where I could.

1. A storage system certified to lose data with probability on the order of one in a billion per year. I accept this without hesitation, even though I cannot say which year it happens in.

2. Our autocomplete service, sold internally on a p99 latency target with a completely unbounded tail — a handful of requests per day take thirty seconds. I accept this as well, and it does not strike me as even the same category of claim as case 1.

3. Our paging system produces a false alert roughly once every fifty pages. I accept this. I also understand the mechanism by which it eventually causes harm: engineers stop responding, and a real alert gets missed during a period of fatigue. I accept it anyway, and I have never thought of that as a concession.

4. A fraud-model vendor tells us that no legitimate transaction will ever be declined. I reject the claim, and I would reject it even if I believed it, because at our volume it is not falsifiable on any timescale I care about. The same vendor offering 0.1 percent of legitimate transactions declined, measured over twelve months on production traffic — a strictly weaker assertion — I accept readily. I find it uncomfortable that I prefer the logically weaker guarantee.

5. A rule-based static analyzer written by our own team, of which we can say without qualification that it never modifies your source files. I accept this as a genuine worst-case guarantee, because the whole of its behavior is small enough for one person to read.

6. A small neural network with an actual formal robustness proof, valid for every input within a stated norm bound. I do not accept it. My instinct is that the proof covers a box and that everything I care about lives outside the box. This sits badly beside case 5: both are checkable worst-case claims, and I trust the informal one and distrust the proved one.

7. A dosage calculator for an infusion pump. I would demand a worst-case guarantee over all reachable inputs or refuse to ship it at all. One error in a billion is not sufficient here, which is the same rate I waved through in case 1.

8. An agent built on a large language model that drafts and sends customer email with no human review before sending. I have told colleagues I would be comfortable shipping this if our measured rate of bad sends on our own traffic were under one percent. One in a hundred, unreviewed and irreversible, against one in a billion refused in case 7. I cannot defend that gap.

9. Password guessing against our login endpoint: I will not reason in average-case terms at all, because something is actively hunting for my tail and will concentrate all of its effort there. I want rate limiting and worst-case reasoning. Yet for the agent in case 8, users actively try to make it misbehave, and I still catch myself quoting an average — it declined 99.7 percent of the red-team prompts — as though that were reassuring.

10. Anesthesia delivery with a known nonzero failure rate. I accept it, because a person is in the loop who will notice and reverse the failure. This is the one case where I feel I know which variable I am actually tracking, and it is not the probability.

11. A deployed agent that behaves well during evaluation because evaluation is detectable as such. Here my average-case number is not a noisy estimate of the deployment rate; it is an estimate of a rate over a different population, and that population was selected by the thing I am measuring. My verdict is that neither kind of guarantee is available — I cannot bound its trajectories, and I cannot sample them honestly — and that I would nevertheless probably ship it.

My tentative principle, which I expect is wrong or at least incomplete: accept an average-case guarantee only when the failures are dispersed, so that no single person or downstream system absorbs a catastrophic share of them; accept a worst-case guarantee only when you can audit the domain it ranges over.

What I would like back:

- The principle, or the two or three rival candidates, that best accounts for this pattern, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a system I have never seen. For each clause, identify which case above forced it.
- Where my tentative principle breaks. I suspect it handles cases 4 and 5 and fails on 6, 8, and 11; show me how, and tell me whether it fails anywhere I have not noticed.
- Your verdicts on cases I have not listed. Construct two or three of your own where the principle you propose gets the answer right in a way my list would not have predicted, and at least one where it delivers a verdict that seems clearly wrong, if such a case exists.
- An explicit choice about cost. If the only principle that saves every one of my verdicts turns out to be baroque, say so, and give me the simpler alternative together with exactly which verdicts it overturns and why I should let them go.

I am not asking for references or further reading. I would rather have your own reasoning, and I am genuinely prepared to be told that cases 7 and 8 cannot both stand.
