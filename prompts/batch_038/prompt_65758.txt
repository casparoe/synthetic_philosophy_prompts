For six years I have administered the spiritual care service at a large public teaching hospital, and lately, auditing a course in the philosophy of religion, I have been reading Freud's *The Future of an Illusion* — which has left me distinctly uneasy about the thing I administer.

Let me describe the practice precisely, since the details are what I want examined. Our chaplains are salaried members of the clinical team, on call around the clock, and they may be hired only with the formal endorsement of a religious community. They are nonetheless required to serve every patient, family member, and staff member who asks, including people whose faith is not the chaplain's own and people of no faith at all; our publicity states that the service is available to the non-religious. Two standing rules govern the encounter. A chaplain may not proselytize, and may not introduce prayer, scripture, or religious language unless the patient introduces it first. And a chaplain is trained to treat their own convictions as material to be examined in supervision precisely so that those convictions do not shape what happens in the room; the formulation we use internally is that the encounter belongs to the patient. At the bedside chaplains complete a structured spiritual assessment — sources of meaning, community, hopes, guilt, practices that matter, what the patient cannot imagine living without — which is coded, entered in the medical record, and used in care planning. When a family asks for a memorial, we hold it in an interfaith room whose symbols are neutral or removable, and the chaplain presides in language a mixed audience of believers and unbelievers can all inhabit, softening or excising doctrinal claims at the family's request. The service is funded and defended on outcomes: family satisfaction, reduced distress, fewer unresolved conflicts at goals-of-care meetings.

My unease is that each of these arrangements seems to assume that what religion does for a person matters and what it asserts does not — close to Freud's own picture of belief answering to helplessness rather than to evidence, valuable as consolation — except that we have kept the mechanism and discarded the verdict, and we pay people to operate it.

So I want the presupposed theory reconstructed, as carefully as you can manage.

First: what view of religious belief does the service strictly require, as against what it merely sits well with? Is it specifically Freudian — illusion in his sense, a belief derived from wishes and not for that reason false — or something weaker and more respectable, such as that religious utterances are not really assertions about the world, or that a rite's efficacy is independent of its truth? Could the rules be justified with no theory of religion at all, by professional role morality, by constitutional neutrality, by epistemic humility, or by the sheer logistics of pluralism? How far do those alternatives actually license the details: neutrality, for instance, seems to require that I not impose, but not that I scrub the symbols from the room or edit the liturgy.

Second: which rival accounts does the arrangement render unintelligible? In particular, the account of the believer for whom the rite is worth anything only because its claims are true, and who would regard a chaplain's bracketed assent as corrupting what is performed. Does the proselytizing ban assert that differences among faiths are not differences about how things stand with the world, or does it merely decline to adjudicate them?

Third: is the service internally consistent? The same hospital honors religious refusals of blood or of treatment, arranges visits from a patient's own clergy, and protects the receipt of sacraments — practices that appear to take content seriously — while our service treats content as interchangeable and its symbols as detachable. Do these presuppose incompatible things, or is there one view on which both are right? And on a Durkheimian account, where the rite's work is to make and sustain a group, could a rite survive having its symbols emptied out for a mixed audience?

Fourth: can the functional description be detached from Freud's evaluation? If the service does presuppose that religion is an illusion, is it committed to its being pathological and something to be outgrown, or is there a stable position that accepts the description and refuses the verdict — and what would such a position say to the chaplain who believes the claims and is paid to hold them back?

Finally: tell me what would have to change — in hiring, in the assessment form, in the interfaith room, in what may be said at a bedside — under a robustly cognitive view on which religious claims are truth-apt and their truth bears on their efficacy; and what would have to change if we adopted Freud's view entire, verdict included.
