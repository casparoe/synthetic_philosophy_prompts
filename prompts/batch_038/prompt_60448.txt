so I've been deep in Sidgwick's psychical research for a few months now — the SPR years, not just the Methods — and I've realised I've basically signed up to his whole evidential package, partly because I run a small group that investigates this kind of thing and I needed principles we could all agree on. I want to write it down before I present it and I want someone to try to break it. Here's the package, as my own view:

1. the credibility of testimony for an event depends on two separate things: how trustworthy the witnesses are, and how improbable the event is on its own account. Where a report would require upsetting a large body of established law, testimony by itself is never enough — you also need a positive account connecting the phenomenon to known causes.
2. but you can never put that antecedent probability at zero. impossibility claims are themselves inductions from what's happened so far, and they've been overturned often enough — stones falling out of the sky, surgery without pain under mesmerism — so refusing a whole class of reports in advance is just bad science. the right response to striking testimony isn't counter-argument, it's investigation.
3. my own observation under conditions I control is strictly better evidence than any amount of second-hand report, because with second-hand report I can't rule out collusion or self-deception however respectable the witnesses look.
4. case verdict: the early thought-reading results with the Creery sisters I take to have been real, at least in the trials where the girl doing the guessing was shut in another room and couldn't see or hear anyone; the later discovery that two of them had been secretly signalling shows some of it was fraud but doesn't touch those trials.
5. case verdict: on the Theosophists I accept Hodgson's finding against Blavatsky, even though plenty of the witnesses were respectable and some were careful observers, because once a fraud mechanism has actually been demonstrated in a couple of instances it's more reasonable to extend it across the rest of the case than to keep multiplying hypotheses.
6. these phenomena are elusive — they don't perform for hostile or merely curious people, and they seem to depend on rapport and the emotional state of the room. so failure to reproduce under laboratory conditions isnt evidence against them.
7. even if telepathy ends up false, the work pays for itself in psychology: hypnosis, automatic writing, all the evidence that a great deal of mental processing happens out of consciousness.
8. the real stake is bigger though. if survival of death could be shown empirically you'd have support for what ethics otherwise has to postulate — a moral government that reconciles duty with self-interest — and without that the practical reason just stays dualistic. that's why I'm in this at all.
9. also for what it's worth, I think every one of the arguments for God's existence fails and I'm an agnostic about the whole business; I'm not a spiritualist and I don't go to sittings for comfort.

Two questions. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I can feel some of them sitting uneasily but I couldn't tell you which pair is the real problem, and I'm not going to pretend I know which one I'd give up — that's what I want from you. So: rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad, and for each say what would have to change and what the change costs me; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most damage. And if I've put words in Sidgwick's mouth anywhere, flag that too, but it's secondary to whether the set is tenable. Dont tell me to run more experiments or to just be more Bayesian about all of it — assume the evidence situation is exactly as I've described it.
