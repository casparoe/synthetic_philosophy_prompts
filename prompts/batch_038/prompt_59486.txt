**DRAFT: "Who Owns the Instruments? Two Failure Modes for the Modern Nation"**

Anderson's *Imagined Communities* and *The Invention of Tradition*, the volume Hobsbawm edited with Terence Ranger, both appeared in 1983 and are habitually shelved together as the twin founding statements of constructivism about nations. My starting point is that they are not answering the same question, and that once their mechanisms are separated out, they imply two distinct ways in which a national identity can come apart. Keeping them fused, I want to argue, is what makes the standard set of stateless-nation cases look anomalous.

Anderson's mechanism is epistemic and infrastructural. A nation is imagined in the sense that its members will never encounter more than a vanishing fraction of their fellow members, and the imagining is nonetheless stable because it is mediated. Print capitalism fixed vernaculars into durable fields of communication, creating audiences that could recognize one another as a public; later, the colonial and post-colonial state added the census, the map, and the museum, which make a population commensurable with itself by assigning it fixed categories. On this picture a nation fails when the machinery of commensuration fails: when the language in which the "we" is printed ceases to be the language in which people actually read, or when official categories stop lining up with the imagined ones.

Hobsbawm's mechanism is political and ritual. Invented tradition, on the account he and his contributors give, is a body of practices of a ritual or symbolic character, governed by rules that are overt or tacit, whose function is to inculcate norms and values through repetition, and whose repetition thereby implies continuity with an appropriate past. Here the failure mode is different in kind: the nation fails when repetition stops being experienced as obligation and starts being experienced as performance, when the ritual becomes optional, decorative, or an object of affectionate irony.

The distinction matters because the two failure modes can come apart, and in stateless nations I claim they characteristically do. A nation without a state usually lacks the apparatus that supplies Andersonian commensuration: no census of its own, no school system, no official statistics, no orthographic standard enforced by anything but voluntary bodies. But rituals are cheap and portable, and a stateless nation can generate them in abundance. The consequence is that the nation is ritually self-sufficient while remaining administratively dependent, and that its ceremonies must be staged inside categories borrowed from the containing state. Hroch's phase scheme for non-state national movements registers the same asymmetry: the scholarly and agitational phases are typically run by bilingual activists whose careers, credentials, and audiences belong to the containing state.

Three cases. In Highland Scotland, Trevor-Roper's chapter argues that much of what was later taken to be immemorial Highland tradition was assembled retrospectively, most famously in the attribution of the small kilt to an English ironmaster working in the Highlands in the early eighteenth century. That attribution has been seriously contested by historians of dress, but the contest is beside my point: what matters is that the artifact which came to stand for the nation was fabricated with materials and by agents drawn from the state that contained it. In Wales, the neo-druidic ceremonial grafted onto the Eisteddfod in the 1790s was the work of a stonemason with a talent for literary forgery, and it survives today as a national institution. In Catalonia, the Jocs Florals were restored in Barcelona in 1859 as a deliberately archaizing poetry competition; yet they were administered as a modern European cultural contest, judged by the standards of a literary field, and their medievalist framing arrived as part of a transnational romantic vogue rather than from within.

In each case the ritual layer succeeded, in that it reliably produced a "we," while the commensurating layer remained someone else's property. Hence my thesis: the variable that explains the recurring pattern of durable national identity without sovereignty is control over the categories of commensuration, not control over the rituals. Tradition can be invented by amateurs. A census cannot.

A consequence worth stating, though I am less sure of it. The invention-of-tradition critique is asymmetric: it is devastating when aimed at the ritual layer and nearly powerless when aimed at the administrative layer, because administrative inventions become invisible precisely where they succeed. Nobody experiences a census category as a tradition; they experience it as a fact about themselves. This may help explain why the debunking literature on invented tradition was produced mainly by historians of states rather than by historians of stateless nations, for whom the question of who does the classifying was never neutral to begin with.

If something like this is right, then the constructivist quarrel over whether nations are old or young is downstream of a prior question: not when the nation was first imagined, but who owns the instruments with which a population can be imagined at all.

**What I would like comments on**

1. Is the two-failure-modes distinction load-bearing, or have I carved a single mechanism twice using two vocabularies? In particular, Anderson already has resources for the ritual point, notably his claim that nationalism is modular and can be taken up in new settings; does that collapse my distinction into a restatement of his?

2. Please check the three cases for accuracy. Flag any place where I have overstated, misdated, or mischaracterized the history. I specifically want to know whether my treatment of the kilt controversy is fair to Trevor-Roper's chapter, and whether my account of the restoration of the Jocs Florals is right. Where you believe I am wrong, say what you take the correct version to be and how confident you are in it.

3. Does the claim that stateless nations characteristically lack the commensurating apparatus survive obvious counterexamples? I expect that some stateless nations did build their own schools, standardization bodies, or statistical work. If so, does my thesis need a scope restriction, and what form should it take?

4. Is the paragraph on the asymmetry of the critique doing argumentative work, or is it a rhetorical flourish that I should cut?

5. Where does the draft slide between treating imagined and invented as interchangeable? I suspect at least two places, and I am worried that the whole argument leans on an equivocation I have not noticed.

6. Identify the single weakest paragraph and say why. Then tell me whether my final paragraph states something that could be false, or only something that sounds like a conclusion.
