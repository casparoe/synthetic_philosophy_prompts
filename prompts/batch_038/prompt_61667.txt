A friend of mine — an economic historian, and considerably better read in the comparative series than I am — and I spent most of a Saturday arguing about the Great Divergence, and I came away with the uncomfortable sense that the argument we were having, and the argument the field has been having for decades, is not the argument either of us supposed we were having.

His position: the question of why sustained industrialization got going in Britain rather than in China is answerable in principle; we now have much of the evidence that would be needed to answer it — real wages, prices, life expectancy, market integration, factor prices, energy endowments, colonial trade — and what separates the institutionalists, the high-wage and cheap-energy people, the ecological account built on coal and New World land, the cultural account, and the regional-comparison revisionists is a disagreement about which items on a fairly short list did the decisive work. My position: it is not one question. The difference-maker one is licensed to cite changes with the foil — England against the Yangzi Delta in 1800, northwestern Europe against East Asia over a millennium, a self-sustaining growth regime against another commercial efflorescence that stalled, Europe against everywhere else; the explanandum is a process with no agreed boundaries being handled as though it were an event; the field slides unnoticed between why industry and why modern science, which are different explananda and may have unrelated answers; and if the unit of comparison can be a region rather than a civilization, the Britain–China pairing may be a category mistake from the start. He answered that contrast-sensitivity is a feature of every causal explanation and licenses no such conclusion, that my move is evasive because no evidence could falsify it, and that the record says otherwise: accounts that once commanded assent, the equilibrium-trap family among them, lost standing precisely because the wage, demographic, and regional data arrived.

My working hypothesis, which I cannot defend and may well be wrong about, is that this is largely a case of parties answering different contrastive questions under a single heading, combined with an unacknowledged disagreement about whether a contingent, path-dependent, multiply-causeable outcome can admit of a general explanation at all rather than only a narrative. I suspect the real structure is more complicated than that, and I would rather be corrected than agreed with.

So: diagnose the dispute. Is it a substantive disagreement about causes, a disagreement about what a satisfactory explanation of this sort has to look like, a partly verbal disagreement about foils and units of analysis, or a hidden empirical question that nobody has framed as one? Ground the diagnosis in the positions themselves — say what each of the five accounts I listed is actually committed to, and show which of those commitments evidence can and cannot be brought to bear on, and why not. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that would let the parties locate their real disagreement, a class of data or a formal result that would settle something, a reframing that would dissolve the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, with reasons. I am not after a survey of the literature, and I do not want a bibliography or a list of sources; I want the diagnosis argued through, and I want you to say plainly where my hypothesis fails.
