I am the editor of my high school's literary magazine, and our spring issue is a themed one tied to the Cold War unit my American history class just finished; the working title is Living with the Bomb, and my grandmother, who remembers the duck-and-cover drills at her own elementary school, has agreed to contribute a page of recollections. To open the issue I want a poem of my own, but I would like it to avoid the two things nearly everyone in my class wrote last year when we tried this: the protest poem that ends with a plea, and the elegy with a mushroom cloud in it. Our teacher had us read excerpts from Thomas Schelling's Arms and Influence alongside the usual documents on the Cuban Missile Crisis, and what stayed with me was not the horror but the strange internal logic of the argument the two superpowers were making to each other. I would like the poem to be a rendering of that logic, in a voice that is neither horrified nor triumphant, and I want it to be accurate enough that a person who actually studies strategic affairs would recognize the reasoning rather than just the vocabulary.

Specifically, there are three moves I would like the poem to get right, and I would rather you paraphrase them in your own words than reach for slogans. First, the credibility problem: a threat prevents an attack only if the adversary believes you would carry it out, yet once the attack has already happened, carrying it out can win you nothing and will finish destroying you too, so the course of action that is rational to announce beforehand is the one that becomes irrational afterward. Second, the consequence that statesmen drew from this, which is not that they made the threat more certain but that they deliberately cultivated a shared risk nobody fully controlled, so that the danger of losing control would itself restrain both sides. Third, the paradox that follows from mutual vulnerability: because the large war has been made unthinkable, the small wars and proxy wars become comparatively safe, and the calm at the center purchases the violence at the edges. Please do not let the poem slide into treating deterrence as though it were defense, and do not use mutually assured destruction as a loose synonym for nuclear war; those are the errors my teacher corrected most often.

For the form I would like a pantoum of four quatrains, sixteen lines, with the repetition kept strict: the second and fourth lines of each stanza recur, word for word and unaltered, as the first and third lines of the stanza after, and the closing stanza makes the traditional circular move, using as its third line the second line of the opening stanza, which is the one line that has not yet returned, and as its fourth line the very first line of the poem. Lines of roughly ten to twelve syllables, with the meter loose enough that a borrowed line scans naturally in both of its contexts; rhyme is welcome but not required. The philosophical work should be done by the repetition itself: every line that comes back must change force in its new setting, so that the same sentence is a warning the first time and something else, a promise or a prediction or an excuse or an epitaph, the second, and so that the final return of the opening line leaves the reader unable to say whether the threat is still live or has hardened into ritual. The speaker should be a state addressing an adversary in the first person plural; no real people, no place names, no dates, and no imagery of ruins or radiation. After the poem, please add one paragraph in American English, which is the variety I need throughout, telling me which of the three ideas each stanza is doing and which returning line you intended to change meaning, so that I can introduce the piece honestly in my editor's note.
