I am presenting a paper at an economic-history workshop in six weeks, and I want a question-and-answer prep sheet for the discussion period rather than a defense of the argument.

The room. The workshop's regulars are quantitative agricultural historians and demographic economists. Two members have built provincial grain-procurement and price series for China in the 1950s; one has published on estimating excess mortality for that period from census and reconstruction methods; one has argued that the 1943 Bengal famine involved a real aggregate shortfall in availability and that the entitlement account overstates the distributional story. There will also be a political theorist who works on Sen and is broadly sympathetic to him, several development-economics graduate students who encountered *Poverty and Famines* in a methods course, and no other historian of Ireland. The norm in this room is identification: they ask what your explanatory variable is, how you would know if it were absent, and whether you are describing a mechanism or relabeling an outcome.

My thesis.

1. Entitlement theory explains who starves within a famine — the distribution of starvation across occupational groups — better than food-availability-decline accounts do, but it is close to silent on why the catastrophe begins when it does.
2. Onset timing in my three cases tracks the breakdown of the feedback system that normally compels a response: the yield reports, price signals, and press coverage that convert local distress into a claim the state cannot ignore. I call this informational foreclosure.
3. Foreclosure takes forms that I argue are the same mechanism under different political conditions. In the Great Leap, output reporting was inflated by cadre competition and honest reporting was punished after 1959, so procurement quotas were set against fiction. In wartime Bengal, censorship, the denial policy, and the refusal to divert shipping meant distress was known to officials but could not be converted into a politically enforceable claim. In Ireland there was no suppression at all — the press reported the failure and Parliament debated it — but the reports were discounted through a doctrinal filter: providentialism, a Treasury committed to non-interference, and a means test that made relief conditional on surrendering any holding above a quarter of an acre.
4. The upshot: the familiar claim that famine does not occur where there is a democracy and a free press is doing less work than its defenders suppose. What matters is not the presence of the channels but whether the state treats the information moving through them as binding. Ireland is my hardest case for the standard view and also my hardest case for my own, since it shows foreclosure can occur with the channels fully open.

My evidence base, which you should assume throughout: published procurement and price series, cabinet and official correspondence, commission reports, and the contemporary press in each case. I have done no new archival work and I run no regressions. I am on the academic job market.

What I want, in American English, as a compact prep sheet of roughly 1,400–1,800 words:

- The eight or nine objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones a human-rights lawyer, a postcolonial-studies audience, or a philosophy seminar would raise. Order them by expected damage, where damage is likelihood multiplied by how hard they are to answer live, and give one sentence per objection explaining its rank.
- Phrase each in the objector's own idiom. The demographer's objection should sound like a demographer talking about baselines and reconstruction; the procurement-series people should sound like people who know exactly what the Chinese statistical record for those years can and cannot support. Do not let every objection come out in the same philosophical voice.
- For each objection: what I should concede in my first sentence before answering, a reply I could deliver aloud in ninety seconds to two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is soft — specifically, what I cannot show given the evidence base above.
- Identify at least two objections that are genuinely fatal to the strong version of the thesis, and state the weaker version I should retreat to, precisely enough that I could say it out loud without its collapsing into a truism.
- Name the single objection I am hoping nobody raises and explain why it would hurt most, including whether my argument quietly presupposes what that objection denies.
- Tell me which one reply has to land for the paper to be taken seriously here, and whether there is a question I should raise myself during the talk in order to inoculate against it.
- Close with two or three sentences on how the Sen-sympathetic political theorist's objections will differ in kind from the cliometricians', and which camp I should be courting given that I am interviewing.

Where an objection turns on a contested number — the spread in excess-mortality estimates, revisions to reported grain output, the reliability of wartime price series — characterize the dispute, its direction, and roughly how wide the disagreement is, but do not manufacture precise figures, tables, or sources. I want no bibliography and no citations; the deliverable is what I say, not what I have read.
