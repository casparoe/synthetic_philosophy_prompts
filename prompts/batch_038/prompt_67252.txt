Before I retired I spent some thirty-five years in a pharmacology department, and I have spent the last four reading philosophy with more enthusiasm than method. I have been working through the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with a small reading group, and I have taken to explaining it in the vocabulary of my old trade: history is a drug; the historical sense is therapeutic below a certain dose and toxic above it; the whole problem is one of titration; and when Nietzsche invokes the unhistorical he is simply prescribing drug-free intervals, periods in which the organism is allowed to clear the substance and act again. The group finds this very clarifying, and I have begun to suspect that its clarity is doing damage. I should like a candid audit. What in the meditation genuinely corresponds to what in a dose–response relationship, and where does the correspondence break — in particular, what does the image of a molecule entering a patient from outside assume about the past that the past does not actually satisfy? Is there a quantity to be adjusted at all, or is something other than amount doing the work in his account; and how are the three ways of using the past that he distinguishes supposed to sit inside the picture, since none of them appears to differ from the others by dose? Finally, if the pharmacological analogy misleads, is there some other comparison that would carry the same explanatory weight without importing commitments Nietzsche does not hold? Please write to me in continuous prose, as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. If you need to lay out the correspondences, do so in sentences, one after another.
