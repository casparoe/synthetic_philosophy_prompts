Teaching a philosophy of science proseminar next term, 16 students, mostly second-years, and I have one 75-minute slot in week 4. Deliberately before they read any Kuhn — Structure goes in week 5 and the postscript he added to the second edition goes in week 6. So the activity has to make them collide with incommensurability for real, and they must not hear the word until the debrief. No framing lecture up front either, and I don't want to be in the position of just announcing that the point has been made: it should be visibly true of something they actually did.

Here's my seed, change it as much as you like. The setting is calcination of a metal. Everyone in the room can see the same thing with their own eyes: heat a metal in air and the residue weighs more than the metal did. One team gets a phlogiston-flavored framework, the other an oxygen-flavored one, but I don't want a costume drama where one side is transparently the dumb one — both frameworks have to account for that shared weighing, and the phlogiston team needs a resource in their glossary that lets them explain why losing phlogiston could make the residue heavier, so the number on the scale genuinely doesn't settle it between them. Then somewhere around the midpoint one team is handed a revised one-pager where a couple of their terms have quietly shifted what they pick out. After that the two teams have to co-author a single paragraph and jointly sign off on one disputed sentence, with points riding on it.

What I need from you: full rules and roles; the two glossaries written out in full as printable one-pagers (short, plain language — actually draft them, don't just tell me they exist); the weighing setup, which has to be doable with a kitchen scale and stuff a student can bring from a hardware store or their kitchen; a scoring scheme with small stakes and no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75; and the debrief. The debrief should map named moments from what they did onto specific Kuhnian claims — the stuff about the two sides seeing different things, or living in different worlds — and then push them onto the harder problem: if those teams were negotiating perfectly competently for twenty minutes, in what sense were they unable to understand each other? I want them on the horns of that dilemma by themselves, so I never have to give a talk about Davidson.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what I say in the moment. Especially the bright student who says: okay but they both had the scale, that's a neutral court of appeal, so the dispute was decidable the whole time and Kuhn is just wrong. And be straight with me about which feature of incommensurabilty this cannot reproduce no matter how well I design it, so I know what I'm still on the hook for supplying by hand.

One more thing. A few of these students are sharp and they will smell a rigged experiment within about four minutes. If the phlogiston team is destined to lose, or if it's obvious I'm herding them into the failure, the whole thing collapses into a compliance exercise. So tell me how to keep the design honest — ideally the teams sometimes do manage to write the paragraph, and the interesting datum is what it cost them to do it and what had to be left unsaid. And give me a stripped-down variant for a later section where they've already read the postscript and come in braced for the trick.
