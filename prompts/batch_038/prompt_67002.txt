Next month I present a paper to a political theory seminar arguing that the lordship-and-bondage passage in Hegel's Phenomenology is not a normative ideal of mutual recognition that inequality tragically defeats, but a demonstration that recognition under inequality never gets off the ground: by staking his life the master shows the only other available to him to be, on his own terms, a nothing, and the slave's formation comes from having stood in unmitigated fear of death rather than from having worked, so that the Marxist and Kojèvian weight placed on labor misidentifies the engine of the transition into Stoicism and skepticism. My argument runs in three moves. First, the characterization of self-consciousness as finding itself in another functions as a criterion the passage then tests, not as a recommendation Hegel is issuing here. Second, the life-and-death struggle destroys the value of the master's recognition structurally rather than psychologically — the failure does not depend on the master's character or on the slave's resentment. Third, work matters for the slave only as the standing external trace of a fear he has already survived, which is why the next stage is a freedom of thought rather than a revolt.

The room is mostly analytic political philosophers who came to Hegel by way of recognition theory and treat recognition as a normative resource for liberalism; one Hegel specialist who works in the German and will want to know how my claims differ from the Jena material and from Fichte's account of the summons; and two graduate students, one working on Fanon's complaint that the colonial relation lacks the mutual orientation the dialectic presupposes, one on social ontology.
