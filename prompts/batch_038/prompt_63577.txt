I have spent most of my reading life on rationalist blogs and forums — I was around before LessWrong had a sidebar, I have written more words about whether "knows" is a two-place predicate than I am willing to admit, and I still lose arguments about the lottery paradox roughly once a year — so I want to be explicit that the constraints below are load-bearing rather than decorative, and that I would rather you honor all of them imperfectly than satisfy the obvious ones beautifully. Next Saturday I am giving the principal toast at the retirement dinner for Professor Ingrid Mbayo, in a hotel function room, to about eighty people. She spent forty years in epistemology defending the family of views according to which what it takes to know something varies with what is riding on the belief, and against the contextualist reply that the standards move with the conversation rather than with the situation; she was never a skeptic, and in department meetings she was the person who would say that a belief stops being knowledge when the step you are about to take on it gets bigger than the evidence can carry. Her habitual phrasing, which everyone who studied with her imitates badly, was that she was "entitled to that, for now." The room will be roughly a third philosophers, several of whom reviewed her books and lost, and two thirds family: her sister Dot, her late husband's brother, two adult daughters (a nurse and a landscape architect) and four grandchildren, none of whom have read a word of her work and none of whom are especially impressed by it. She has asked, specifically and in writing, that nobody give a speech about her scholarship; she said she wanted to be toasted as a person. So the view has to be present structurally and invisible nominally — it has to determine what the speech says and declines to say — without containing the word epistemology, without naming any philosopher, and without sounding like a seminar talk that has wandered into a banquet.

Here is the complication that makes the view load-bearing. Nine days ago I sat as the junior member on a university committee reviewing the small archive of twentieth-century labor pamphlets and correspondence that she built over twenty-two years and still volunteers in on Thursdays. I read the drafted recommendation and the vice-provost's supporting memo: the half-time archivist position is not renewed past the end of the fiscal year, and the collection is folded into general special collections, which everyone understands to mean it is not catalogued again. The vote has not happened; I put the chance that it is reversed or substantially amended at maybe one in six; the announcement is scheduled for roughly three weeks from now and the drafters have asked the committee to keep it quiet until then. If I tell her at the dinner, she acts: she will be on the phone to donors and former students on Monday, and she will very likely withdraw the offer of her own papers, and her stakes become enormous — at which point, by her own principle, neither of us is any longer in a position to know the thing she is about to mortgage her retirement over. If I say nothing, she learns it from a dean's email in three weeks and I will have been standing at a podium in her honor, in front of her sister, knowing. There is a third option I have been circling, which is that the speech could leave her in a position to check cheaply on Monday — asking a question of the provost costs her almost nothing, and cheap checks are precisely what her view says convert a guess into knowledge — without my having asserted anything at all. I cannot tell whether that is wisdom or cowardice dressed up as epistemology, and I do not want you to assume it is the answer; I have read enough threads about the ethics of withholding information from someone who would act on it to know that this reduces to a question about whose stakes get to set the threshold, and I do not want the speech to settle that with a slogan.

Some raw material you may use or discard: she drove the same 1994 Volvo for twenty-two years and refused to replace it, explaining that she had already paid for its failure modes and knew exactly which ones they were; she answered the department telephone with a formula to the effect that the caller had reached the person least qualified to help them; she taught an undergraduate seminar on testimony for fifteen years and never assigned a book she had not re-read that year; she once made me sit in her office at eleven at night while she telephoned a library in another country to confirm a page range. The deliverable: a toast of three to four minutes aloud, warm, funny in at most one place, ending on something ordinary rather than elevated, with at most one detail in it that only the philosophers will catch and that is catchable without having read a single one of her papers. Nothing that requires the family to have context they lack, no material about grading or about her having taught us all to think, and no quotation from her writing. Before the speech, give me three sentences saying which of the options above you chose and what the speech is thereby committed to; after it, a short note identifying the sentence most likely to land badly and the one place where an epistemologist will hear the machinery. Above all, do not hand me a speech that can be read either way — I am well aware that a toast is a naturally deniable genre, and ambiguity here is the failure mode I am most afraid of. If the honest answer is that the speech must assert something, then let it assert something and tell me what it costs.
