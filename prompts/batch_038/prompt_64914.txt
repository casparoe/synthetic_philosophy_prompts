Prepping my Hume seminar and my students all reach for the same move against the miracles section: pile up enough independent witnesses, each with some small fixed chance of being mistaken or lying, and the likelihood ratio grows without bound, so no finite prior against a miracle survives. I want to hand them a harder target than the usual circularity complaint. Argue that Hume's argument survives this even granting the students everything they assume about the math — because the error rate can't be held fixed across content. On Hume's own account reliability is learned from experience with reports of that sort, and for miracle reports there's no clean reference class to learn it from, so the parameter their likelihood ratio depends on is precisely the thing in dispute. Make the case as tightly as you can: spell out what the fixed-error-rate assumption is actually doing, show where it has to come from, and say wether the argument needs Hume's talk of uniform experience or just his account of testimony. Then give me the best reply a Bayesian Hume-skeptic could make, and say what she'd have to concede to make it.
