I teach social epistemology, and over some years of chairing and sitting on faculty committees I have kept encountering a configuration for which I have no adequate name. Let me describe it as concretely as I can and without importing vocabulary, since I suspect the vocabulary I would naturally reach for would beg the question.

First instance. A junior colleague reports that her teaching evaluations run systematically below those of comparably performing peers, and offers several examples. The response is that anecdotes cannot establish a pattern and that the relevant data must be assembled. She spends a year assembling it: her own evaluations and others', a coding scheme, a comparison against departmental medians, an appendix on response rates. The response is that the analysis is amateurish and that she is too close to the subject to be trusted with it; a disinterested analyst would be required. She arranges for one and co-signs the result. The response is that the study is now derivative, adds nothing to what has long been said in the literature, and that what was wanted from the beginning was her own testimony, plainly given.

Second instance, structurally identical with the order reversed. A student organization complains about advising practices in narrative terms and is told that the university acts on documented cases rather than impressions. It documents six cases and is told that six is not a pattern. It produces nineteen and is told the sample is self-selected. It commissions a survey of the full cohort and is told the instrument was drafted by interested parties.

Three features strike me as essential, and they are why I doubt the obvious labels apply. First, each dismissal is locally legitimate. Anecdotes really are weak evidence; a self-selected sample really is a poor one; a study drafted by the complainants really does have an incentive problem. The person issuing the dismissal can at every step name a standard they did not invent and would apply to anyone, and asked afterward what would have counted, they can usually say. No single rule is violated, and there is no moment at which one could point to the shift, because the standard invoked at each step is a different one. Second, the pattern is asymmetric in a way not explained by the differing quality of the material offered. When a colleague without the relevant social identity runs the same sequence, the registers accumulate: the anecdote is received as a hypothesis, the analysis as its confirmation, the survey as its extension. The pieces are additive. For my colleague and for the student organization they are mutually cancelling. Third, and I am unsure whether this belongs to the same phenomenon or only accompanies it: several people I have watched go through the sequence have emerged concluding not that the standards were unfair but that they are themselves bad at producing evidence. They over-document, apologize for the length of the documentation, and the apologizing is then read as a symptom of the obsessiveness that made the documentation suspect.

Does this have a name? I know the general territory — credibility deficits that track social identity, and the adjacent family of concepts concerning what a hearer owes a speaker — but I have not found anything that picks out this particular thing, which is not a deficit applied to a speaker so much as a standard of adequacy applied to the speaker's material and relocated, step by step, to whichever register the speaker has not yet occupied. If there is a literature bearing on it, characterize the literature; I am not asking for references and I would rather not have guessed-at ones.

I am aware of several candidate concepts and I want to be told, for each, why it does or does not fit, rather than simply handed one. Is this goalpost-moving, and if not, what distinguishes it — since here the goalposts are not raised but relocated, and the relocation is invisible to the person performing it? Is it a species of the familiar credibility deficit, and if so, why does it survive the production of precisely the evidence the deficit was invoked to explain? Is it the trap in which any denial counts as confirmation of the charge, and if not, what separates the two, given that in my cases the disqualification is never offered as evidence of anything? Is the phenomenon better described as something hearers do or something institutions do, considering that the individual hearers appear to act in good faith and could not, one at a time, be shown the pattern? And is my third feature part of it, a predictable consequence of it, or a distinct thing requiring its own name?

If there is no established term, or none that carves the phenomenon correctly, I would prefer that you say so plainly and propose one: a definition, ideally with conditions that do real work, and — more important to me — boundary cases. Tell me what would be near but not an instance: a hearer who demands ever more evidence without ever relocating the standard; a speaker whose successive submissions genuinely do get worse; a case in which the standards are explicit, published in advance, and the speaker simply fails them. At what point does this stop being the phenomenon and become ordinary, unobjectionable rigor? I would also like to know whether it can run in the reverse direction, toward a speaker who holds the relevant social advantage, and whether such a case would be the same phenomenon or merely a similar one.

Please do not treat the naming as the deliverable; the carving is. But because I intend to use this in a seminar, and because I hold opinions here that I would like tested rather than flattered, close with an account of where your own analysis is most vulnerable: which of my instances or features, if any, you would refuse to group with the others; what argument or consideration would persuade you that this is nothing more than the ordinary operation of evidential standards under genuine uncertainty; and what you would have to be shown to change your mind about whether the phenomenon is unified at all.
