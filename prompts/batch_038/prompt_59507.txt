I have just finished my ninth consecutive day of brokering a compromise between the two factions on the board of the small grantmaking nonprofit where I serve as executive director, and the board has resolved — by a margin achieved, fittingly, through a trade — that the organization's first full-time hire should be a Director of Value Trade. I would like you to ghostwrite the recruitment posting for this role.

The piece should follow the conventions of a professional job advertisement: a short "About us" paragraph, a description of the role, a list of qualifications (both required and preferred), a note on compensation and benefits, an equal-opportunity statement, and instructions for applying. Aim for roughly 800 words. The voice should be that of a sober, slightly tired institutional posting — earnest about the mission, careful about legal phrasing, proud of its own rigor — and it should remain deadpan throughout, never winking at the reader.

The role itself is the reductio: the successful candidate will be expected to identify trades that make every party better off by their own lights across stakeholders whose ultimate values genuinely conflict, and to close them. The humor should come from the conceptual machinery rather than from generic office satire, so please build in the following:

- The posting must state, with complete managerial confidence, an exchange rate between incommensurable value systems, and must assign someone responsibility for maintaining it.
- The required qualifications should include holding no strong first-order commitments of one's own, while also requiring enough personal integrity to be a credible counterparty. The posting should not notice the tension; the reader should.
- The performance metrics should expose the measurement problem, in that they are denominated in units that cannot be compared across the very parties being traded between.
- The deontological constraints of one board faction should be described, in the preferred-qualifications or "culture" section, in language that treats them as negotiating positions or an anchoring bias to be overcome.
- The compensation and benefits section should include a component that is valuable under one axiology and worthless under another, plus language that commits the organization only to claims that hold true relative to whichever value system turns out to be correct.
- The equal-opportunity statement should be extended, without any change in tone, to cover applicants of every axiological persuasion, including ones the organization regards as mistaken.

Do not name or target any living person or any real organization; invent everything, including the nonprofit. A reader with no background in moral philosophy should still find it funny as an artifact of institutional prose, while someone who has thought seriously about Pareto improvements across value systems, moral uncertainty, and the objection from integrity should recognize that the piece understands exactly what it is exaggerating — and, ideally, should find the satire fair. Beneath the deadpan, one understated sentence near the end should let the real question surface: whether brokering compromise among values is the only sane response to pluralism, or a slow form of corruption.
