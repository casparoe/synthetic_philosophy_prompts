Next month I'm giving the opening remarks at the first public meeting of a regional water authority in my state: nine municipalities and two counties that have agreed, after a decade of litigation over a shared aquifer, to pool a deliberately narrow slice of their powers. The room will include the state legislators who drafted the compact, several mayors who campaigned against it, utility engineers, a visiting scholar of comparative federalism who will pounce if I mistake subsidiarity for plain decentralization, and a few hundred ratepayers who mainly want to know whether their bills are going up.

I have eight minutes. Please build the remarks around a rule of thumb I've been pushing in comment threads for years: give a decision to the smallest body that contains everyone who will bear its consequences, and where no such body exists, say so plainly rather than pretending a smaller one will do. Use it to explain why basin-wide supply planning went up to the new authority while distribution and rate-setting stayed with the towns, and use it to name two ways the compact fails its own test — at least one of which should make the mayors shift in their seats.

Two constraints: I will not promise that the authority's decisions will be better than the towns' would have been, only that they will be easier to hold to account; and the word subsidiarity may appear at most once, without gloss.
