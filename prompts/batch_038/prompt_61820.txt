I am assembling notes for an essay on behavioral tests for mentality, and I have run into a pattern that recurs across quite different debates but for which I have been unable to locate a term. The pattern concerns how two classes of evidence are weighted, rather than which conclusion is reached.

Consider a language model. It produces a correct and evidently novel solution to a puzzle absent from anything it could have memorized, and the skeptical response is that the achievement is only apparent: the system is recombining familiar strings, interpolating within a space it has already absorbed, and there is no comprehension to credit. Now consider the same model failing at something trivial — miscounting the letters of a common word, or attributing a famous novel to the director of its film adaptation. The response here is not that the failure is a curiosity awaiting explanation, but that it is revealing: it discloses what the system really is, and shows there is no mind there at all. Success is neutralized by being assigned a non-mental mechanism; failure is amplified by being read as the absence of a mental one. Neither result is allowed to count in the direction the other points. The same policy is not applied to human beings whose inner lives nobody doubts: a person who solves the puzzle is credited with insight, and a person who miscounts the letters is simply having an off day.

I see the identical structure elsewhere. In discussions of animal cognition, a surprising performance is explained away as conditioned response, cue-following, or an artifact of the testing setup, while a species-typical blunder is taken to demonstrate a missing capacity outright — and the class of performances that would count as evidence of the higher capacity seems to be redrawn after the fact so that it always coincides with whatever the animal fails to do. This is adjacent to the familiar observation that whatever a machine eventually performs reliably ceases to count as the mark of intelligence, but I take that observation to be about labels and tasks, whereas what I am describing is about the evidential weight assigned to successes and failures within a single ongoing assessment.

Is there an established concept for this? I have ruled out two neighbors myself. It is not merely moving the goalposts, because the criterion for mentality is not being shifted after a success so much as a standing policy is being applied differently to two kinds of result. Nor is it skepticism about other minds in general, which is at least symmetrical. What I want is the carving, not the label: which established notions come closest, from the philosophy of mind, from the methodology of comparative psychology, and from the informal-logic literature on discounting evidence — name the concepts rather than the sources — and how exactly the thing I have described differs from each. If it has no name of its own, propose one and define it precisely enough that I can separate a genuine instance from a merely one-sided but defensible assessment.

That last requirement matters most. If one's prior that a given system has a mind is very low, then discounting its successes while treating its failures as informative looks like ordinary Bayesian reasoning rather than a fallacy. Under what conditions does the asymmetry become irrational as opposed to an expression of a low prior? Where are the boundary cases — is there any evidential arrangement that could break the symmetry, something a system could do that the skeptic would be committed to accepting, or does the pattern amount to an unfalsifiable stance in one specific direction?
