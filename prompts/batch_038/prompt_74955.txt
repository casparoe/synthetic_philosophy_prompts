I'm in my second year on the city council of a mid-size city (about 180k people, mostly broke, lots of old infrastructure) and I've been keeping a private log of the votes where doing right by the city seemed to require doing something I'd call rotten if a private person did it. Five in nine years. One: during the flood, the city manager pushed water down a decommissioned sewage line onto a strip of working farmland without telling the farmer first, because telling him meant a delay and the levee would have topped. One guy lost his whole season. The council ratified it after the fact, held a public session, said on the record that this was not something they wanted to become normal, apologized to him, and paid him well over what the land was worth. Two: they refused to lie by omission to a neighborhood association about where the new waste transfer station was going, even though everyone in the room knew the truth would blow up. It blew up — two years of litigation, and we lost the state grant. The minutes basically give the reason as: we can't run a planning process that depends on residents not believing the city. Three: police posed as a scrap yard buyer to catch a building inspector who'd been shaking down contractors. Approved unanimously, no ethics discussion whatsoever, minuted as routine investigative work; one member joked afterward that it's easy to sleep at night when the guy you're lying to is a crook, and nobody pushed back on the joke. Four: they rejected a proposal to threaten a tenant group's annual funding unless its director dropped a lawsuit against the city, where the lawsuit could have cost a few million and humiliated the mayor and the group had done nothing wrong. Five: during the transit referendum campaign they sat for eleven days on the fact that a city bus had hit a pedestrian — the pedestrian walked away with a broken wrist — on the reasoning that the story two weeks before the vote would kill the measure and the measure was the entire bus network. When they released it, they released the delay and the reason for the delay in the same statement, and the council president said the eleven days was the part she'd loose sleep over.

Next Tuesday there's a sixth and I have to decide how to vote and, harder, what to say in the room. We have one pediatric dental clinic in the county that takes Medicaid kids, roughly 30,000 of them, and the health system says it closes in eight months unless the city covers the gap. The gap is coverable if we can get the empty medical building on Delancey Street cheap and move the clinic into it. The building is owned by a holding company run by a landlord in his 70s with a long record of code violations, most of which he contests; he bought the place for pennies after a fire and owes something like $1.1M in back taxes and fines. The plan, which the city attorney says is entirely inside normal procedure, is to have our appraiser — competant, but known to shade toward the bottom of the defensible range when he's asked to — file low, and open condemnation on a blight theory the same week. Between the number and the threat of a multi-year process with liens stacking up, they think he settles around forty cents on the dollar. Here's the part I can't stop turning over: the leverage only works if the file looks like an ordinary acquisition. If anyone says out loud, on the record, that the point is to squeeze him because we need the difference to fund a clinic, his lawyers dig in, the AG maybe takes an interest, and the clinic money goes with it. So there's no version of this where we do it and also own it — not at the time and not afterward, because the file has to keep looking routine, especially since there are four other properties on the wish list and the manager is not shy about wanting this to be how we do business from now on. His daughter has already been in the paper saying the city is trying to rob an old man.

So: what do they do, and what should I do? Walk me through the rules that fit all five precedents at once — I can think of three and I don't trust my list — and say what each one predicts for Delancey. Then commit. Tell me which reading you'd bet money on and why, which of the five is the closest analog and exactly where that analogy breaks, and how I should vote and what I should say in the room. If the record genuinely doesn't decide it, say so plainly, but then still tell me what principle we'd be adopting with each possible vote, because I think that's the part I'm ducking — I keep telling myself I'm voting on the clinic when really I'm voting on whether the lowball-and-threaten move becomes standard practice here. And tell me what the next proposal after this one would have to look like to settle which rule they're actually running, so I know what to watch for. Context: one political philosophy class, fifteen years ago, and I vaguely remember the phrase dirty hands being used for this sort of thing, but I couldn't reconstruct the argument to save my life, so don't assume I know the literature. I don't want a menu — give me a definite call with reasons I can push back on.
