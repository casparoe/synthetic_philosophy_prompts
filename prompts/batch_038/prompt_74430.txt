For three hours this evening a friend and I argued about whether a life can be meaningful when nobody ever learns what it amounted to. Neither of us budged, and I would like a third party's assessment.

The case I pressed on her is this. Call it the anonymized vocation. A woman spends forty years charting the shifting channels and sandbanks of a small estuary by hand: soundings, tide notes, records of where the bottom moves after storms. The charts are good. Crews who rely on them avoid groundings they would otherwise have suffered, and several lives are plausibly saved. She works without salary, recognition, or expectation of either, and by her own account this is the organizing project of her life. Two things happen after her death. First, the charts stay in active use for another thirty years, but the harbour master reprints them under his own name, so no one who depends on them ever learns that she existed. Second, the estuary is eventually dredged and channelized, rendering the charts obsolete; what survives is not the product but a method of patient observation she drilled into three apprentices, one of whom later averts a serious spill by noticing an anomaly nobody else thought to check.

Her own account of meaning, which she defended at length, is not one I have encountered anywhere, so I will state it as she gave it to me. She calls it the residual claimant view: the meaning of a life is a function not of what it produces nor of how the agent feels about it, but of how many people remain, after the agent's death, in a position to be wronged by failures in the agent's projects. Meaning, on this view, is the durable auditability of a life — the size of the class of strangers who could still bring a legitimate complaint against you. The chart-maker scores well, because generations of crews stood exposed to harm had her work been careless, whether or not any of them knew her name. My objection was that this makes meaning hostage to counterfactuals that never actualize, and that it ranks a negligent administrator of a huge dependency above a scrupulous maker of a small, secure thing.

Please do the following.

1. Rate each account below on how much of the chart-maker's meaning it says survives her death, on a scale from 0 (none) to 10 (undiminished), and then give a strict ranking, with no ties, from best to worst explanation of my own intuition — namely, that her anonymity costs her something real but not most of what she had. One sentence of justification per account.
   - the residual claimant view as my friend stated it;
   - Nagel's treatment of the absurd as a collision between the seriousness we cannot abandon from inside our lives and an external standpoint from which our projects look arbitrary;
   - Wolf's fitting-fulfillment conception, on which meaning requires subjective attraction to be matched by objective attractiveness;
   - purely objective or great-good accounts, on which meaning consists in producing or connecting with goods of the right magnitude, irrespective of anyone's attitudes or knowledge;
   - narrative or authorship accounts, on which meaning depends on the shape and intelligibility of a life as a story the agent helps to author;
   - accounts that measure achievement against contextually appropriate standards of excellence, so that an unremarkable but well-executed life can be fully meaningful.

2. Give me the fraction of our disagreement you take to have been merely verbal — a number between 0 and 1, for instance 0.4 — and state what substantive residue remains once the verbal part is stripped away.

3. Identify which of your six ratings is most fragile: name the smallest change to the case that would move it by at least three points, and explain why that account rather than the others is sensitive to it.
