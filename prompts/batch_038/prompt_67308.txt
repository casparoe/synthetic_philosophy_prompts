Been sitting on the singleton hypothesis (Bostrom's term: one decision-making agency at the top with enough of an edge that nothing can challenge it, versus a competitive multipolar order) for maybe a decade of forum reading, and I finally have a slot to run it at the discussion group I've been organizing for six years. Eight people, two hours, whiteboard only — no slides, no readings, no homework. They're smart non-specialists: a nurse, two software people, a high-school history teacher who listens to a lot of empire podcasts, a couple who mostly read sci-fi. One of the software people will go straight to AGI within four minutes and I do not want that to be the whole session.

Plan how I explain it. Don't explain it to me — I want the teaching plan.

Stuff I care about:

- **Order of the pieces.** My instinct is that they literally can't evaluate a singleton until the contrast class is in hand: that the alternative isn't chaos or endless war but a competitive order that can be quite livable and still grinds out races and lock-ins on its own. If that's the wrong first move, tell me what actually has to come first and what depends on what.
- **One carrying example, and it must not be an AI.** Tell me what the example has to have and what it has to lack, and flag the traps — my guess is that the obvious empire picks are big but way too internally fractured to actually count, and that a monopoly-firm example smuggles in the wrong idea about what "the top level" means. Pick one, defend the pick, and say what it will make them over-generalize.
- **Misconceptions in the order they'll arrive**, with the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. I'm expecting: singleton = world government = 1984 = automatically bad; singleton = one superintelligence; no singleton means war; "it locks in whatever values it starts with" treated as a fact instead of the open question; and the big one, that the hypothesis is a prediction rather than a conditional about what becomes possible.
- **Keeping "is it possible" separate from "would it be good"** for at least the first chunk, because they will fuse those two instantly and I want them to notice they did it.
- One question I can drop near the end that tells me whether the conditional structure landed or whether they just caught a vibe.
- And be straight with me about what I should refuse to open in two hours — I'm guessing the whole how-fast-things-happen taxonomy — plus what I say instead so it doesn't feel like dodging.

Epistemics, please: mark clearly which parts of this are more or less settled among people who actually work on it versus live disputes with smart people on both sides. I'd rather find out from you now than walk in teaching a contested position as consensus. Same for the misconception list — if one of those "misconceptions" turns out to be a defensible view and I'd just be bullying them out of it, say so and tell me how to present it instead.
