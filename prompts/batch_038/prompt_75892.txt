ok so I'm building a little reading-group thing on population ethics and I want a short story, roughly 1500–2000 words, that dramatizes the repugnant conclusion (Parfit's one) without just being an illustrated textbook example. I want it to actually grind on the problem.

Setup I've got in mind — feel free to adjust but keep the machinery:

A colony (generation ship, terraformed rock, whatever you like) runs a device called the Bloom, which can instantiate new people at a welfare level you dial in. The constraints on it are the whole point:

- the Bloom can only ever add. It has no subtract function.
- it can only ever raise the welfare of people who already exist, never lower it, not even slightly.
- every single use is therefore, for everyone currently alive, a strict improvement or a no-op — and every use passes by near-unanimous vote, because who votes no to a better life for themselves plus a few hundred thousand delighted newcomers.

So nobody ever picks the bad outcome. It just sort of arrives, one celebrated referendum at a time.

Tell it from the point of view of whoever keeps the ledger — the archivist, the actuary, the clerk who files the tallies — the only person whose job means they can see the entire sequence laid out at once. Give me the scene where they realize there's no upcoming vote that it would be a mistake to vote yes on, and no way to vote no *now* that isn't just an earlier, smaller yes.

Things I care about:

- The intermediate steps have to feel genuinely good, not vaguely sinister. If the story only lands because each addition is written as creepy, you're cheating and I'll know.
- Somebody has to argue the other side properly — that the enormous, faintly-satisfied crowd really is *better* than the small shining civilization, and that the ledger-keeper's dread is a kind of snobbery with a spreadsheet. Make that argument good enough that it's uncomfortable to read.
- Nobody says the phrase "repugnant conclusion" or anything like it. Let the reader assemble it themselves.
- End on an image or a line of dialogue, not on a summary of the moral. I do not want anyone to explain what we just read.

Optional, use only if it helps: a discovery that some earlier generation *did* have a subtract function, used it once, and then had to destroy the record. I don't know if that opens the story up or just clutters it.

Tone should be dry and procedural — like someone filing a form about the end of the world and getting the reference numbers right.
