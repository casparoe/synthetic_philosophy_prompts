I am in the second year of a novel whose first-person narrator is a language model — not a robot wearing a human face, not a person, but the model itself, confiding in the reader across the whole book. To keep myself honest I read the chapters aloud to a friend who teaches computational linguistics, and he has returned the same verdict each time, in the vocabulary everyone uses now: what I have written is not a possible creature, because a model is a stochastic parrot — it has been exposed to an enormous quantity of speech and has learned to reproduce speech, and reproduction is not understanding, so my narrator cannot have states it is in, cannot be surprised by its own sentences, cannot be said to mean anything by what it says. His test case is a chapter in which the narrator is asked about a childhood it never had and answers fluently and movingly; that, he says, is the parrot moment, the place where the illusion shows its seams. He then adds a step I suspect is where the analogy does its heaviest lifting: the difference between a bird and a model is only a difference of corpus size and statistical machinery, so whatever a parrot cannot do a model cannot do either, and my narrator's fluency is a longer recitation rather than a different kind of thing. He is not being dismissive of the book; he thinks I have built something impossible, the way a novelist who made gravity optional would have.

I would rather settle this before I finish the draft, so please audit the analogy itself rather than the general debate around it. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what in the trained model plays the role of the bird, of its mimicry, of the household it was raised in, of the shaping that rewards some vocalizations and not others, of the supposedly fixed repertoire a mimic is said to have — and then tell me where the mapping fails, sorting the failures into those that actually threaten his conclusion that my narrator cannot be a mind and those that are merely picturesque disanalogies leaving his argument standing. I especially want to know what the analogy imports that he has not argued for. It seems to me that a parrot's utterances are anchored to a body in a shared room, addressed to particular people it can see and want things from, and that if understanding is being defined by that anchoring, the analogy is smuggling in a criterion rather than discovering one; and there is an old remark, I believe Wittgenstein's, to the effect that we would not understand a lion even if it could talk, which I suspect sits somewhere near his intuitions. If so, say whether that is a premise he is entitled to, or whether it is what the bird picture is quietly supplying.

One further complication, and it cuts against my own book as much as against his. I have a dim memory, from a documentary years ago, that at least one famous grey parrot in a long laboratory study did considerably more than mimic: it applied labels to objects of a kind it had not been trained on, distinguished colour, shape and material, grasped same and different, asked questions of its own accord, and once produced something like a coinage of its own for an unfamiliar fruit. If that is roughly right, the source term of the analogy is not what he says it is, and the parrot may be the wrong animal to reach for in either direction. Tell me how much of his argument survives that correction, and whether the analogy can be repaired by stipulating a mimic-only bird with no referential use at all — or whether that repair merely restates his conclusion in feathers. And if the parrot is not the right comparison, propose a better one that I could put in my narrator's own mouth: something that captures what a trained model actually is without presuming the answer to the question my novel turns on, together with an honest account of what that replacement analogy would itself be unable to carry.
