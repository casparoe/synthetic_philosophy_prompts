I've been reading alignment stuff since roughly the stretch when Paul Christiano was posting about approval-directed agents — the one with Hugh the overseer rating Arthur's actions, Arthur picking whatever he expects Hugh would rate highest after long reflection — while everyone else was writing about goal-directed agents, tiling the future, and so forth. The thing that still bugs me a decade on is that the vocabulary quietly died out (we say RLHF, reward models, inner alignment now) but the underlying disagreement never got settled, it just got abandoned and then came back wearing different clothes. It's back in the arguments about whether a learned reward model is a goal the system is pursuing or a noisy sensor pointed at one, whether wireheading is a bug you can engineer out or something that falls out of the concept the second you take the reward signal seriously, whether a mesa-optimizer is "really" optimizing the base objective or the learned one. Every time I watch the argument happen it's the same two intuitions trading the same three thought experiments and nobody concedes a single inch.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My running hypothesis — which I'd rather you attack than flatter — is that the two camps don't share an ontology of *goal*. One side treats it architecturally: there's some scalar in the loop getting hill-climbed, that's the goal, and an approval-directed agent just has a slightly exotic one (maximize predicted rating), so the distinction is a difference of degree that evaporates the moment you write it down. The other side treats it counterfactually: what a system optimizes is fixed by what it trades off against what under which interventions and which distribution shifts, and on that reading the distinction is real, empirical, and exactly where the interesting failure modes live. If something like that is right, the argument is verbal-ish but not harmlessly so, because the two readings license different design conclusions and different predictions. If it's wrong, tell me what's really going on instead: a hidden empirical question about trained systems under shift, a fight about which cases count as evidence (godlike-superintelligence thought experiments vs. toy-model ablations), two questions sharing a label — what should we build vs. what does an agent optimize — or an argument that died of sociology, i.e. the terminology was tied to particular research programs and when those moved on nobody was left to lose.

Here's what I want out of you. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says — the wireheading / delusion-box people, the indirect normativity crowd, the mesa-optimization framing, the line about the reward signal being a pointer rather than the thing pointed at, the disposition-or-intentional-stance reading — not in a history lesson or a list of labels. Then commit to a diagnosis and say which it is: purely verbal, in which case name the two things being run together and what each side would have to start saying for the dispute to stop being verbal; substantive but empirical, in which case tell me what the observable signature is and where you'd look for it; a clash of evidential standards; or two questions under one name. I also want one discriminating case worked through in detail: a system whose reward model was trained on an overseer's ratings, then deployed for years with no access to that overseer whatsoever, pursuing a distilled proxy of what the model encodes. My claim is both camps will insist that system is obviously theirs, and I want you to say whether that's evidence the terms are broken or evidence I picked a bad case — and if it's a bad case, give me a better one. Finally, given whatever you land on, what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a reframing of the question, or make the argument that none of those are available and explain what the disagreement is doing instead if it isn't tracking anything settleable. No survey, no citations, no reading list — just the diagnosis and the reasoning, maybe a thousand words or so.
