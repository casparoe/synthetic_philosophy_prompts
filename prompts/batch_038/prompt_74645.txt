I chair the clinical competency committee at a mid-size general surgery residency program — five attendings, we meet monthly, and we sign off on whether a resident can do a given operation without an attending scrubbed in. We make roughly a dozen of these calls a month, two attendings have to co-sign each one, and the liability exposure when we're wrong on a clearance is very real. Our evidence is a mess of two completely different kinds and we have no principled way to weigh them. On one side: in-training exam scores, mock oral board scores, anatomy quizzes, and the resident's own case log. On the other: global technical ratings from attendings who watched them operate, plus video review of a case or two.

The failure modes go both directions and both are bad. We've cleared residents who can recite the steps and the anatomy cold and then go rigid at the table the first time the bleeding isn't textbook. And we've blocked residents who do a beautiful safe operation but who, when you ask them to explain what they just did or teach it to an intern, produce something close to word salad — sometimes flat-out wrong about why they did what they did.

What I want is a decision procedure my committee can actually run on one resident for one operation in about fifteen minutes, from a packet that already exists, ending in one of three verdicts: cleared to perform unsupervised, cleared with a named attending present, or not yet — plus what specifically has to change before the next meeting. It should be grounded in whatever the philosophy of skill knowledge actually says about the gap between knowing how to do a thing and knowing that something is the case, but please don't hand me a reading list or a citation dump. I want the usable content and the distinctions that do work, not the scholarship.

Cases it has to handle, because we have all of them in the room right now:

- The resident who performs well but whose account of their own performance is wrong. Do we count the performance, the account, or both? If both, how do they trade off, and is there any situation where a bad account should override a good performance?
- The reverse: flawless account, has never actually completed the case start to finish.
- Success by luck. Somebody did the operation three times, all three went well, but all three had a attending quietly steering from the side, or the anatomy happened to be easy, or the patient was young and stable. What in our packet distinguishes a real disposition from a run of favorable circumstances? I want the procedure to say something about how the resident would do in nearby variants of the case, not just their hit rate.
- Competence that only exists inside a particular setup. A resident who is excellent with our usual team, our usual equipment, our usual first-assist, and who we have reason to think would come apart on a night shift with a new PA and a different tray. Is that one competence or two?
- Knowledge that's present but never fires — learned on rounds, never once used at the table.
- Decay: someone who was fine a year ago and hasn't touched this case in six months.

And the part I care about most: be explicit about the back-door risk. If the procedure makes "can explain it to an intern" a necessary condition, haven't we just decided that being able to state propositions is what competence consists in — which is exactly what the cases above say is false? Take a position on whether an articulation requirement is defensible at all, and if it is, say what job it's actually doing in the procedure. Two of my members think articulation matters not as evidence of skill but as evidence that the skill will transfer and stay safe under novel conditions. I don't know whether that survives scrutiny.

Format: a numbered procedure, questions in the order we should ask them, and for each one what counts as a sufficient answer and where it stops with an outright "not yet." Then a short section on which distinctions from the theory you deliberately kept, which you threw out to make it runnable in fifteen minutes, and why — and which steps are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, because I'd rather be told. Then run it on three cases: one straightforward, one where my committee would split two-two going in, and one where the procedure produces a verdict I personally don't like — clears somebody I think shouldn't be cleared, or blocks somebody I'd bet on. I want the uncomfortable one, not a softball. Finish with what this procedure can't tell us and what has to be escalated to a human because it's a question of values or institutional risk rather than fact.

American English please.
