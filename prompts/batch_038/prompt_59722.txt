**Q2.** In apparent-motion experiments, subjects report seeing movement that was never present in the display, and in the color-phi variant they report seeing an object change color partway along a path it never actually traveled. Dennett and Kinsbourne distinguish two ways of explaining such reports — 'Orwellian' and 'Stalinesque' revision — and argue that subjects' reports cannot tell them apart. Explain the two accounts and the reasoning behind that claim. Then state what conclusion Dennett draws about the timing of experience and about the picture of consciousness he calls the Cartesian theater, and say whether you think the conclusion follows from the premises. (600 words, 25 points)

And here's the answer, retyped into paragraphs for readibility but otherwise unchanged:

> In apparent motion experiments, two static lights are flashed in quick succession at different positions, and if the gap between them is short enough — a fraction of a second — the subject doesn't see two flashes at all, they see a single thing travelling from the first position to the second. Nothing in the display moves, so the motion is entirely a product of the visual system. The color-phi variant is the interesting one: the two lights are different colors, say red then green, and the subject reports one object crossing the screen that changes color somewhere in the middle of its path. The puzzle is that this color change seems to be witnessed at a point in the trajectory earlier than the moment the second light comes on, so at the time the subject is apparently seeing the object turn green, the green light hasn't happened yet.
>
> Dennett and Kinsbourne say a Cartesian materialist has two options here. On the Orwellian account, the subject really does see the two lights separately and veridically — a static red, then a static green — but afterwards the memory of the episode gets edited, so that when they're asked what they saw they describe a moving object that changed color. It's the Ministry of Truth altering the records: the past is rewritten after the fact. On the Stalinesque account, the two events are reconciled before they ever become conscious. The brain holds off, composes the whole motion-plus-color-change sequence, and then presents the finished product, so the subject never has a conscious experience of two static lights in the first place. Like a show trial, the verdict is settled before the performance.
>
> The reason the two can't be told apart is that they predict exactly the same reports. A misbegotten experience and an accurate experience that is subsequently misremembered are indistinguishable from the inside, and since the only evidence available is verbal report plus forced-choice discrimination, there's no experiment that could come out differently on the two hypotheses. Dennett's conclusion is that the framing itself is broken. Both accounts assume there's some place and time in the brain where unconscious processing crosses over into consciousness, and the temporal oddities in phi and color phi only look paradoxical if you assume such a finish line exists. His alternative, the multiple drafts model, denies that there's a single stream with a privileged moment of arrival; there are instead parallel drafts of content at various stages of composition, and something counts as conscious in virtue of the downstream effects it leaves behind rather than in virtue of being presented anywhere.
>
> The same thing seems to be going on in Libet's timing experiments. Libet found that a brief stimulus to the skin doesn't become conscious until something like half a second of neural activity has elapsed, and his subjects reported feeling the stimulus roughly that long after it was actually delivered. The brain then has to correct the record so the sensation gets dated back to when the skin was touched, which is Orwellian revision showing up in a different domain.
>
> I think the conclusion mostly follows, and Dennett is right that the phi data can't be used to prove anything about when experiences occur. But I'd push back in one place: the color-phi case does seem to favor the Stalinesque option over the Orwellian one. A subject who genuinely saw only two static flashes has to have fabricated a very specific piece of content — the exact point along the trajectory where the color turned — whereas a brain that waits and composes the sequence can just read that content off the display. So even though the two theories are indistinguishable by report, there's an asymmetry in how much explaining each one has to do, and I take it that's why Dennett endorses the Stalinesque account rather than the Orwellian one while rejecting the theater picture as a whole.
