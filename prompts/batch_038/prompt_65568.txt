I work as a statistician building retention and fraud models, and for two years I have been sitting in on a philosophy-of-science seminar at the university where my wife teaches, largely because the question of which regularities may be extrapolated is my actual day job. I keep a running list of generalizations I am willing to extend to the next case and generalizations I am not, and the list has outgrown my ability to see the pattern in it. The seminar's diagnosis is that I am circling the problem of projectible predicates, and that grue is the canonical instance of it. Fine, but grue is one case and I have eleven, and several of them pull in directions that the standard story about grue does not seem to accommodate. What follows are my verdicts, in roughly the order I wrote them down. In every case the setup is the same: the predicate has held of every instance examined so far, and the question is whether I may infer that it will hold of the next instance.

1. Green emeralds: may.
2. Grue emeralds, where grue means green if first examined before 1 January 2050 and blue otherwise: may not — and I cannot point to any evidential difference from case 1, which is what started all this.
3. Bleen emeralds: may not, for the same reason.
4. Every national flag anyone has examined has been either green or red; the next flag will be green or red: may. This is a disjunction of two ordinary colour words with no time index in it, and I feel nothing wrong with it, which bothers me, because case 2 is a disjunction too.
5. Define a predicate purely in terms of the calendar and not of anybody's evidence or anybody's observations: green up to 2050, blue from 2050 onward. Every emerald so far satisfies it; the next one will: may not. So whatever defect I am tracking, it cannot be that the predicate mentions epistemic facts, since this one mentions none and is defective in the same way.
6. Everything observed so far has been observed, so the next emerald will be observed: may not. I reject this one for what feels like a different reason — there is no barrier whatever to its being true — but I reject it all the same.
7. Jade: every piece anyone has examined has been some shade of green, so the next piece will be: may not. I have been told the word covers two chemically distinct minerals that merely look alike to us, and that strikes me as exactly the right diagnosis. But note that "jade" is old, has been in continuous use, and has a long record of successful application; if the pedigree of a term is what licenses projection, case 7 should come out the other way.
8. A material first synthesized last year and given a name that exists nowhere before that: every sample produced so far has had a certain property, so the next sample will: may. The term has no history and no track record at all.
9. Water: every sample so far has been H₂O, so the next will be: may — and here I am inclined to say the license comes from what water is, not from anything about my word or my evidence. I resist saying the same thing about green, and I cannot tell you why.
10. A community whose colour vocabulary takes grue and bleen as primitive, and which builds our "green" as a derived term — grue if first examined before 2050, bleen otherwise. Their evidence matches mine case for case. I want to say their projection of grue is bad and their projection of their derived green-analogue is good; I also want to say that from inside their language they are entitled to the mirror-image verdicts about me, and that no non-question-begging fact settles which of us is right. I hold both, I expect one has to go, and I would rather be told which and why than be walked carefully around it.
11. At work I routinely project from features that look to me like case 2: "account created before the platform's fifth funding round," say, which predicts retention because the early cohort was acquired differently. The projection works for two years and then stops working, and when it stops I say the feature had gone stale rather than that I had been illegitimate all along. I think that is a fair thing to say, and it seems to commit me to holding that whether a projection was licensed can be settled by how things turn out — which is the opposite of what case 2 suggests.

My own tentative rule has been this: a predicate is projectible just in case there could be some mechanism by which instances transmit it, some way the world is such that one instance's having the property makes it likelier that the next instance has it too. But that appears to license grue in a world where emeralds genuinely do turn blue in 2050, and I do not want that result, and I do not know whether the fix is an extra clause or an admission. Please test that rule against the eleven cases and repair it or replace it. What I want back is a principle — or, if no single principle can do the work, a short list of candidates together with an argument that the choice among them is forced — stated precisely enough that someone who disagrees with my verdicts could still apply it. For each clause, say which case motivates it. Then give me its verdict on three or four cases I have not listed, at least one of which should come out in a way I would find uncomfortable. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save, treating cases 7, 8, and 10 explicitly; if the account has to be disjunctive, with a historical clause for some cases and a metaphysical clause for others, say so rather than papering over it. I am not after references or a reading list — I want the account itself, argued in your own terms. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, either the principle or the verdict of mine you are going to make me give up, and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and please do not read my list back to me.
