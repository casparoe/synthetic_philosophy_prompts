I direct my department's annual public conversation series, and our closing event this term features a moral philosopher and economic historian whose recent book advances what she calls a substitution account of moral revolutions. Her central claim is that practices embedded in an honor code — dueling among British officers and gentlemen, footbinding in late imperial China, the Atlantic slave trade as Britain abandoned it, and so-called honor crimes against women in parts of Pakistan — did not fall to moral argument, since the arguments had been available for generations without effect, nor primarily to shifts in material interest, but only once reformers contrived a substitute practice that discharged the same status function before the same audience. On her telling, the anti-footbinding societies mattered less because they persuaded anyone that binding was cruel than because they helped construct a marriage market in which unbound feet became marriageable, and British abolition mattered because the petitioning campaigns redirected the relevant audience from the planter's circle to the nation's own self-image. She is fluent and well media-trained, and her stock response to a hard question is that "the theory is about mechanisms, not predictions."

Please draft eight to ten questions for a forty-minute conversation with a mixed audience of undergraduates, faculty, and members of the local historical society. I want questions that engage the content of the substitution thesis rather than inviting her to restate it, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. At minimum, work in the following lines of pressure. First, the material-interests literature: Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery (1944) argued that British abolition tracked the declining economic value of the West Indian slave system, while Seymour Drescher's Econocide (1977) responded that the British West Indian slave economy remained profitable right up to abolition, so that decline followed the abolition rather than causing it — she should be asked whether her account can remain neutral in that exchange, or whether it must concede that interests set the timing while honor supplied the motive. Second, Bernard Williams's thought that certain moral transformations cannot be explained as the victory of better reasoning because the earlier society lacked the ethical concepts that would have made the criticism available: is her mechanism a redescription of that point, a rival to it, or a specification of it? Third, the symmetry problem — honor entrenches norms as readily as it dissolves them, and if the same apparatus explains both persistence and change, what would count as evidence against the view? Fourth, the normative question of whether an honor-driven conversion of a practice from creditable to shameful gives us any reason to call the outcome moral progress rather than a change of fashion, and what explanatory work, if any, is left for the reformer's belief that the practice was wrong. Fifth, the applied question about designing substitutes for a live practice, including the objection that outsiders engineering an honor substitute is a form of manipulation, and the question of who gets to define the substitute. End with a question about what observation would falsify the account. No generic questions about her influences or career, please.

One further request, and I ask it because I will be voicing some of these historical attributions aloud in a room that contains two economic historians: at the end of your draft, tell me how confident you are in the empirical and attributive claims your questions presuppose, and single out the single point you are least sure of, so that I can soften, hedge, or simply drop it from the live conversation.
