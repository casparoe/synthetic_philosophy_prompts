I sit on the review panel at an AI lab that decides whether a deployed assistant's behavior gets logged as deceptive, and our chair reliably ends the discussion with one line — you can't deceive without intending to deceive — which he takes to settle the matter: since the model has no intentions, nothing the model itself does can be classified as deception, and at most the company can be, and only when some human meant it. I want to bring a written menu of readings to the next meeting. Lay out the distinct claims that slogan could be making — for instance, whether "intend" has to name a conscious mental state or could name a stable functional disposition shaped by training; whether the intention has to sit in the system, in its trainers, or in whoever deployed it; whether the requirement attaches to lying narrowly or to misleading behavior generally, including cases where nobody foresaw the false impression; and whether the line is a conceptual thesis about the word "deception" or a normative one about when blame is in place. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or simply false, and then say whether that reading actually licenses the chair's conclusion that model outputs can never be flagged. Write in American English, and close by telling me where this analysis is most likely to break: which reading you would drop under pressure, and what consideration would change your verdict about whether his move is legitimate.
