I write a short monthly essay-letter with two friends, one Norwegian and one Swedish, and for the better part of a year the three of us have been unable to settle an argument that began as a joke: which of their countries is more liberal. Each keeps producing facts, and each treats their own facts as decisive and the other's as beside the point. I have come to think the stalemate is not empirical but structural, and I have drafted a piece making that case. It is about 950 words, unfinished at the end, and I would like criticism rather than encouragement.

---

**Draft: Which neighbour is more liberal?**

**1. The question is argued as though it were a measurement problem.** Whenever one of us produces an unflattering statistic about the other's country, the statistician believes the argument is over. But before any statistic could settle it, we would need to know what it is a statistic *of*. "Liberal" turns out to name at least four distinguishable things, and the two countries rank differently on each.

**2. Four senses.** (a) *Non-interference*: how much of ordinary life the state leaves alone — what you may possess, buy, drink, publish, or say; whether a dependent drug user is met with treatment or with punishment; how intrusive policing and surveillance are. (b) *Social provision*: decommodification, transfers, public services, the share of national income routed through the state — this is what an American means by "liberal." (c) *Openness to non-members*: asylum and migration admissions, naturalization, and the legal entrenchment of an indigenous minority's land and consultation rights. (d) *Constraint on the majority*: judicial review, press freedom, transparency, and the durability of rules against a determined governing coalition.

The same policy can move these in opposite directions. Sweden's voucher-funded independent schools, including for-profit ones, are usually described as a market-liberal reform: they expand choice and exit while doing nothing for equality of provision. Whether that makes Sweden more or less liberal depends entirely on whether one meant (a) or (b).

**3. A first attempt at a table.** Taking one indicator per sense, plus one for values:

| Indicator | More liberal | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Personal-conduct prohibitions (drug law, alcohol retail, purchase of sex) | Norway | Sweden's drug policy is among the most restrictive in Western Europe; Sweden criminalized the purchase of sex before Norway did |
| 2. Share of national wealth routed through public hands | Norway | Contaminated — see §5 |
| 3. Asylum admissions per capita, 2015–16 | Sweden | Sweden took in far more per head than any Nordic neighbour |
| 4. Constraints on majority power | Tie | Both sit at the ceiling of every press-freedom and corruption index I know |
| 5. Self-expression and secular-rational values in survey data | Sweden | Norway close behind |

Norway takes two, Sweden takes two, one ties. A majority rule yields no verdict.

**4. The verdict changes with the carving, not with the facts.** Two adjustments, both defensible. *Adjustment one*: drop indicator 2, because it measures endowment rather than disposition (§5). Result: Sweden 2, Norway 1, tie 1 — Sweden wins. *Adjustment two*: keep all five, but split indicator 3 into 3a (asylum admissions, where Sweden leads) and 3b (statutory entrenchment of Sami land and consultation rights, where, as I understand it, Norway has gone further). Result: Norway 3, Sweden 2, tie 1 — Norway wins.

No new empirical information entered anywhere. What changed the outcome was a decision about how finely to slice the concept and which slices to admit. That is a decision about vocabulary, and it is being made silently, by whoever happens to be holding the spreadsheet. The aggregation is not answering the question; it is restating the analyst's discretion in numerical costume.

**5. Costless virtue.** Indicator 2 needs excluding for a reason worth stating plainly. Norway's petroleum revenues were socialized into a sovereign fund rather than privately captured, which is admirable governance — but it was inexpensive. Sweden's welfare state had to be paid for out of taxation, which is to say out of resistance. If a liberal disposition is a disposition to bear costs for a principle, then generosity that cost nothing is not evidence of it, and an index that cannot tell the two apart is measuring luck.

This suggests a further possibility that I have not worked out: perhaps the question should not be about levels at all. Both countries have tightened on several of these axes over the past decade, and Sweden ended formal establishment of its Lutheran church before Norway did, yet nobody would say Sweden has become less liberal in the interval in the same breath as they rank them. So maybe the right comparison is marginal or counterfactual — not which country *is* more liberal, but whose liberalism is more robust: which would still be liberal under fiscal stress, after a security shock, or with an illiberal party in government. Half-baked, I admit.

**6. Conclusion (unfinished).** The dispute is not decidable at the level on which it is being conducted, because the word carries at least four normative packages and no fact fixes the weights between them. But the conclusion cannot be that the question is meaningless: restricted to any one of the four senses, it has a determinate and answerable form. So answering the general question requires taking a stand on what liberalism is *for* — which is exactly the normative question everyone was trying to avoid by asking a comparative empirical one instead.

Some specifics on what I want from you. First, the thesis: is §4 doing real work, or is it just the familiar point that any multidimensional index is sensitive to how its dimensions are individuated — in which case the essay has said nothing about liberalism that it has not said about democracy indices, happiness measures, and GDP, and I should either sharpen the claim or abandon it? Tell me which. Second, please check the mechanics of §3 and §4 line by line, including whether my three verdicts actually follow from the counting rules I state, and whether I have been consistent about how ties are handled. Third, flag every empirical claim you think is wrong or doubtful, with a confidence level; I would much rather see "not sure about this one" than a confident-sounding correction, and I am not looking for a reading list or citations, since I can verify sources myself. Fourth, tell me whether the definition of sense (a) and the indicators I file under it are actually consistent with one another, or whether I have smuggled something across a line I explicitly drew. Fifth, §5 is the part I like best and suspect is the least defensible: is the costless-virtue argument sound, or does it prove too much, and does the robustness idea in the second half of §5 rescue it or merely distract from it?

Finally, give me the single most damaging objection an intelligent and hostile reader would press, the one you would raise if you were paid to demolish the piece rather than improve it; a prioritized list of revisions, marking which sections are load-bearing and which are decorative; and a straight answer on whether the conclusion is too strong, too weak, or a non sequitur. If you think the essay is not worth finishing, say so and say why.
