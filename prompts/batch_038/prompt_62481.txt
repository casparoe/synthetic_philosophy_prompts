A long-running objection says pleasure is heterogeneous: the pleasant episodes in a life appear to have no one felt quality in common. (a) Explain this objection, and be clear about what it is supposed to show — note that it can be pressed in more than one way. And here is the student's answer, transcribed as written (they handwrote it, so a couple of words are my best guess):

> Hedonism says pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic bad. The heterogeneity objection starts from the sheer variety of pleasant experiences: the taste of a cold peach, the low register of a cello suite, the relief of pulling off tight boots, the click of finally seeing how a proof goes, orgasm, laughing hard enough that your ribs hurt. If you introspect for what these have in common you come up empty — there is no single felt ingredient you can isolate and point at as "the pleasure part." So either pleasure names a pile of unrelated feels, in which case the hedonist thesis is not really one claim about one good-making property, or it names nothing at all, in which case hedonism is not even false but meaningless, since the term it is built on fails to refer.
>
> The objection can also be run from the extension rather than the phenomenology. Suppose there is a common feel, call it hedonic tone. Then it looks like a separable ingredient, and separable ingredients can come apart from the things we actually care about — which is the structure of Nozick's experience machine, and also why a life could in principle be full of the tone and empty of everything else we would call worth having. Mill's competent judges generate the same pressure: if the judges prefer poetry to pushpin at equal quantity of pleasure, then either they are detecting more tone, in which case quantity does all the work and the higher/lower distinction collapses, or they are detecting something that isn't pleasure at all.
>
> The first reply says all pleasures do share one phenomenal property. Sidgwick is roughly this sort of hedonist: pleasure is a feeling which, when we have it, we at least implicitly take to be desirable, and the differences between the peach and the cello are differences in the experiences that carry the tone rather than in the tone itself. The color analogy helps — scarlet and chartreuse look nothing alike, yet both are colors. So pleasure might be a determinable whose determinates are the particular pleasant feels. The determinable move is really just a refined version of the disjunctivist reply: both concede that no one phenomenal property runs through every pleasure, and they differ only in whether you package the disjunction as a determinable or list the disjuncts out. I'll treat them together from here.
>
> The second reply concedes the phenomenology is heterogeneous and puts the unity somewhere else. Feldman's attitudinal hedonism is the main version — what makes an episode pleasant is not a feel but a pro-attitude, being pleased that such-and-such is the case — so the variety of sensory feels is beside the point, since attitudinal pleasure is not a sensation. A related strategy defines enjoyment functionally, roughly Brandt's approach: to enjoy an experience is to want it to continue, other things equal. And Aristotle is an early ancestor of the tone view, since on his account pleasure is the distinctive feeling that accompanies the unimpeded exercise of one of our capacities.
>
> The costs differ. The tone view makes the theory hostage to introspection: if careful introspection fails to find the ingredient, hedonism is in trouble, and the view still owes an account of why the tone is good in every case, including when it is produced by electrodes or by someone's cruelty. The attitudinal view pays a different price: being pleased that p looks suspiciously like a form of desire satisfaction, and if that is what it is, hedonism has stopped competing with desire theories of welfare rather than defeated them. The functional version inherits that problem and adds a circularity worry, since wanting an experience to continue is normally explained by the experience being pleasant.
>
> One last thing: the introspection debate looks empirically settleable now. Every rewarding experience anyone has measured produces activity in the same dopaminergic mesolimbic pathway, which is what the hedonic tone hypothesis predicts and what a disjunctivist should not predict. So the common-quality reply can be defended without appealing to introspection at all.
