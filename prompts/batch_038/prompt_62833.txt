I am a sampling statistician. For the better part of two decades I have designed household surveys and worked within design-based inference: frames and coverage error, stratification and clustering, weighting and raking, design effects, nonresponse adjustment, variance estimation, and the rather hard distinction between a quantity a design actually estimates and a quantity it merely appears to estimate.

Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend who works in political theory and who advocates selecting legislators by lot. Her position, roughly, is that a randomly chosen citizens' assembly is not merely a useful consultation but a body whose decisions carry a claim to authority that elections cannot match, because it is the population itself in miniature, unfiltered by the self-selection, ambition, and money that distort electoral outcomes.

My objections were all methodological. She dismissed all of them, and I suspect she was right to, but I cannot yet articulate why. I objected that with a hundred-odd members the sampling error on any given preference is enormous; she said the assembly is not estimating anything. I objected that a large share of those drawn decline to serve, which wrecks the randomization; she said they draw replacements, and that the resulting body need not mirror anyone. I objected that after months of testimony and internal argument the members' views are no longer the views of the population; she said that is precisely the point — that what the assembly concludes is what the public would conclude under conditions the public is never given, and is therefore a truer expression of public judgment than a poll of that same public.

That last claim is the one I could not answer, and it is why I want a translation rather than a further argument.

Restate her position — the sortition case in its serious form, not the caricature — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Take the load-bearing claims one at a time: that selection by lot yields a microcosm; that this microcosm confers a legitimacy elections cannot; that the assembly's post-deliberation judgment is the public's considered judgment; that rotation and short tenures prevent capture; that the assembly stands in for the whole rather than for a constituency. For each, tell me whether it is a claim about sampling at all, how well the design apparatus captures it, and whether it survives. Then sort them: which translate cleanly, which translate only at the cost of distortion I should be able to point to precisely, and which have no expression whatsoever in a design-based language — not a bad translation, just silence.

Two strictures. First, do not smuggle. If a claim of hers has no counterpart in my vocabulary, say so, rather than redescribing it in my terms and then scoring the redescription as a refutation of her; I am alive to the risk that I am asking you to grade an argument with a ruler that cannot measure what is being claimed. Second, I am not asking you to take my side. I know that deliberative polling exists and uses my vocabulary openly and honestly, which is evidence that at least some of her claims do translate. My question is where the translation runs out and what is left on the far side of it.

Close with the residue report and a verdict on it. For each claim that failed to translate, tell me whether the failure points to a gap in my framework — whether there is a property a body can have that is genuinely analogous to representativeness without being representativeness, and for which design theory has no name — or whether it points to a claim that was never clear in the first place, with the sampling vocabulary merely doing her the unkind service of exposing that. Distinguish the two wherever you can, and tell me what would settle it. Finally, return to the claim I could not answer: is "what the public would conclude under conditions it never gets" a quantity that any design could in principle estimate, and if not, what kind of thing is it instead?
