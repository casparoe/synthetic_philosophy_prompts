So I run a small reading group — maybe a dozen people, mostly LessWrong-adjacent ex-evangelicals who still can't leave the topic alone, two practicing Christians, and one guy who is functionally a mythicist and will not shut up about it. We've spent the last month on the criteria-of-authenticity debate, and last week I got into a genuinely unproductive argument with a friend about the criterion of embarrassment. Her position is that it's worthless, because the early church had every theological reason to manufacture embarrassing material: a traitor from the inner circle, a messiah who gets executed by the Romans, Jesus on the cross complaining that he's been abandoned. Mine was that some of those things are embarrassing in ways no theology predicts, so the criterion does some work. After twenty minutes I realized I couldn't tell whether we disagreed about the criterion itself, about what kind of documents the gospels are, or about how much theology you have to posit before an invention becomes likely. And then I realized none of the twelve of us can actually say what our own methodology is — we just have vibes about which pericopes feel load-bearing.

What I want is a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree / scored quiz, about ten nodes, that takes someone from a fixed starting commitment — let's say "I accept the near-universal consensus that a Galilean Jewish preacher existed and was executed under Pilate, and that the synoptics are my main evidence" — to a named methodological position. Please build the logical space first, before you start hanging names on it: identify the handful of parameters the positions really turn on (can authenticity ever be established at the level of the individual saying, or only at the level of recurring patterns? is memory best modeled as copy-degradation or as constructive reconstruction? does the independence the multiple-attestation criterion needs actually exist, given how the sources relate? do you have to explain a passage by the evangelist's theology before you're allowed to call it inauthentic? is the burden of proof on the skeptic or on the person claiming authenticity?) and only then locate the views in that space. Leaves I'd want to see occupied if they genuinely are: the classical criteria camp doing item-level work with dissimilarity, multiple attestation, embarrassment and coherence; the Wright-style 'double similarity and double dissimilarity' plausibility version (attribute that label to him only if you're confident); the Allison line that item-level criteria are hopeless but recurring patterns across the tradition are recoverable; the Keith/Le Donne-ish move that trades 'authenticity' for something like plausibility-in-context with memory as refraction; the critique that the criteria grew inside a particular confessional history and systematically produce a Jesus detached from first-century Judaism (I think that's Theissen and Winter's line, but flag it as uncertain if you're not sure); a Sanders-ish view where contextual plausibility does the real work and the criteria are at best tiebreakers; an Ehrman-ish moderate agnosticism where the criteria are individually weak and only ever combine probabilistically; the Bultmannian 'you basically can't get behind the kerygma' leaf; a maximalist leaf; and mythicism, which should either be reachable by some path or explicitly marked off-tree with a diagnosis of why the tree's starting commitment already excludes it.

Two constraints on how you write it. First, every node has to be answerable by someone who has never heard any of these labels — so make them concrete cases about actual material, not 'do you think the criteria work?': Jesus being baptized by John, the passion predictions, Mark's little Aramaic phrases that he then has to translate for his readers, the Lord's Prayer, the Temple incident, the independence or non-independence of Thomas, Paul's references to Jesus's brother and to the last supper, Luke's census, whether the Sermon material goes back to Q or to Matthew's own composition. Here's the register I'm after, from a half-drafted node: "An episode shows up only in Mark, and it would have been awkward for the early church. Does that raise your confidence it goes back to Jesus? (a) yes, substantially — churches don't invent self-damaging material; (b) only if nothing in the surrounding theology would motivate inventing it; (c) no — embarrassment is manufactured precisely where it's theologically useful, which is exactly here." Second, for each leaf give the name or a confident exemplar if one exists, otherwise just describe the position and say it's unnamed; add one sentence on its most awkward commitment; flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate; and mark which nodes ride on contested source-critical premises — Q's existence, Markan priority, Thomas's independence, how much Jesus tradition Paul really knew — such that if the premise flips, the person lands somewhere else. Also note any unoccupied cells in your parameter space and say whether nobody's there because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it just hasn't been tried. No bibliography, no page numbers, and please don't quote anything — I can't check it and I'd rather you not guess.
