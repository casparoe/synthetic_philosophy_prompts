I have no academic training in philosophy — I run a small engineering firm — but I have spent the past year thinking hard about the line between therapy and enhancement, largely because of a single case that seems to me to break it: Oscar Pistorius. I would like you to take on the role of a philosopher of medicine who is also comfortable with the empirical sports-science side of things, and to work through the case with me carefully. Please think hard before answering, and open with a short brainstorming pass in which you list the distinct questions you believe I am asking and the points at which they could come apart; only then move into the analysis.

The facts I am relying on, so that you need not look anything up: Pistorius was a South African sprinter born with a congenital absence of the fibula in both legs, amputated below the knee in infancy, who ran on carbon-fibre prosthetic blades. The international governing body for athletics barred him from able-bodied competition on the strength of a biomechanics study it had commissioned, and in 2008 the Court of Arbitration for Sport overturned the ban. The panel's reasoning was not that the blades conferred no advantage, but that the federation had failed to discharge a burden of proof it had itself accepted: the study measured him only at top speed in a straight line, which is not what a 400-metre race consists of, and said nothing about his start, his acceleration, or his running of the bend. Years later the rule was rewritten so that the burden ran the other way — onto the athlete using a prosthesis, to establish that it gave no overall competitive advantage — and a subsequent arbitration struck that burden-shifting down as unlawful and discriminatory, while individual disabled athletes nevertheless continued to be kept out of able-bodied competition in practice.

My own thinking has stalled in a particular place, and I want you to test it rather than flatter it. I have come to believe the distinction is really a claim about direction of travel relative to a baseline: therapy moves you toward a baseline, enhancement moves you away from one. The trouble is that this case offers at least four candidate baselines and they deliver opposite verdicts — his own unaided capacity, against which the blades are an unambiguous restoration; species-typical functioning for a healthy adult male, against which they may be an excess; the elite sprinting distribution, against which they are simply equipment; and whatever performance the rulebook of the sport permits, against which the question is not medical at all. If the label flips with the choice of baseline, and nothing in the biology tells us which baseline to choose, I worry the distinction is not doing the work people assume it does.

In order, then:

1. Map the case. Identify what in the episode counts as therapy, what counts as enhancement, and what falls outside both categories entirely, saying for each which baseline you have tacitly adopted.

2. Run the leading candidate accounts of the distinction through it — a disease or normal-functioning account, a treatment-versus-improvement account, an appeal to the biologically given or the natural lottery, and an account in terms of positional goods and competitive fairness. State what verdict each yields here, and whether it yields that verdict determinately or only once supplied with a premise the case itself does not furnish.

3. Tell me where the distinction falls silent or actively misdescribes. I have three candidate failures that I want you to evaluate, extend, or reject: (a) an aggregation problem — talk of an overall competitive advantage presumes that function is a single scalar, when the blades apparently made him better at one thing and worse at several others, so being above normal may be indeterminate rather than merely unknown; (b) a person-relativity problem — if an able-bodied sprinter had both legs amputated in order to run on identical blades, everyone would call that enhancement, though the device and the resulting physiology are the same, which suggests the label tracks who you were beforehand rather than what was done to you; (c) a ratchet — blades became standard equipment for elite para-sprinters within a decade, so any enhancement verdict here seems to carry an expiry date, and I cannot see what entitles a distinction whose output decays with time to be a distinction about the intervention itself.

4. Reverse the direction of analysis: what does the episode do to the theory? Is it a counterexample, an anomaly the distinction can absorb by being relativised to task, to person, or to rulebook, or a situation it never contemplated? If it survives relativisation, say precisely what work remains for it and what it can no longer be asked to do.

5. Finally, a procedural point that I do not see addressed anywhere: both tribunals treated the matter as an empirical question about advantage, and the argument then migrated into who must prove what when no empirical answer is available. Does the therapy/enhancement distinction collapse, in cases of this kind, into an allocation of the burden of proof? And if it does, is that a defeat for the distinction, or simply the honest form of something that was always about what we may demand of people rather than about what a technology is?

Please give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations, and do not invent studies, statistics, or quotations — I cannot verify any of them, and I would much rather you flag uncertainty than paper over it. Mark clearly which of your claims are conceptual and which depend on empirical matters you are unsure about. Use headers; a compact table is welcome for parts 1 and 2. Around 1,500 words. If you think my diagnosis in terms of baselines is wrong, or that I have chosen the wrong episode for this purpose, say so plainly and show me where.
