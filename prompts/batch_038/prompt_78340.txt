I am assembling an application for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in a program that straddles philosophy of science and economic history, and I need a research proposal of roughly two pages. I have a philosophy doctorate and only a working knowledge of the historical literature, so the proposal should be honest about where the historical expertise I would need to acquire lies. My half-formed idea is this, and I want you to turn it into a proposal that could survive a skeptical committee.

The familiar comparative question — why did sustained industrialization take off in Britain rather than in China, sometimes called the Needham question — is normally treated as a substantive historical dispute in which several camps disagree about causes: the camp that credits abundant and conveniently located coal together with resource flows from the New World; the camp that credits property rights, markets, and state capacity; the camp that credits high wages and cheap energy as the incentive to substitute machines for labor; the camp that credits scientific culture. My suspicion is that a substantial part of this disagreement is not disagreement about causes at all. Each camp appears to be answering a different question, because each fixes a different foil: England against the Yangzi delta, England against China as a whole, an industrial breakthrough against a counterfactual of continued growth within an organic economy, the nineteenth century against 1750. Which differences between the two cases count as explanatory seems to shift with the foil, so the camps may be producing answers to non-identical explananda while believing themselves to be rival answers to one.

The second problem is the one I think is genuinely novel rather than philosophical housekeeping, and I want the proposal built around it. Whichever side of the comparison is treated as the deviation, an explanation requires a baseline expectation about what would otherwise have happened, and the literature seems to smuggle in incompatible baselines. For some writers the unexplained datum is the escape from a broadly Malthusian equilibrium that everyone else remained inside; for others the datum is the failure of one of the most advanced commercial economies in the world to do what an advanced commercial economy would ordinarily be expected to do. These are not the same question, and the choice between them looks like it does prior work in determining which causal stories even count as candidates. I want the project to ask whether there is any principled way to settle the baseline, or whether the baseline is fixed only by the questioner's interests — and if the latter, what becomes of the claim that one of these accounts is historically better supported than another.

The proposal should contain, in this order: a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it could come out either way rather than so that the answer is built in; an account of why the existing positions on both the philosophical and the historical sides leave it open, and specifically why the standard moves in the philosophy of explanation do not settle it in advance; the method, phase by phase across twelve months, including what I would actually do with texts and data rather than what I would think about; a statement of what a result would look like that an economic historian would recognize as a result and not as a tidy-up, ideally with a concrete test — for instance, showing for a small number of specified causal claims that a refinement of the foil changes whether the claim functions as an answer; the failure conditions, including the live possibility that the project reduces to the trivial conclusion that a vague question should be made more precise, and the possibility that once the foils are pinned down the camps simply converge and there was never a structural problem; and a realistic assessment of scope, naming what I would have to cut to finish in a year.

Two further constraints. Explain why this approach is preferable to the obvious alternative — adopting a fully interest-relative account of explanation on which any contrast class is admissible, so that the question is automatically well posed relative to some historian's purposes and the entire puzzle evaporates; a committee member will press this, and I need a reply that does not concede the point by default. And do not supply a bibliography, a reference list, or inline citations; I will add those myself. You may refer to positions, arguments, and camps by description, and you may use standard labels where they exist, but do not attribute particular theses to particular named authors unless you are confident the attribution is correct.
