I teach a second-year "philosophy of democracy" seminar, 24 students, one 75-minute session. They've met the Condorcet cycle and a friendly version of Arrow's theorem, but nobody has met Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and I don't want the name said out loud until the debrief.

Design an in-class voting game that makes them feel the specific force of the impossibility. Not "here's a rule you can game" — they already assume that about everything. I want the structural point: once you have three or more options and a rule that's deterministic, lets every option actually win, and isn't a dictatorship in disguise, then somebody at some profile gains by misreporting, and it isn't a weird edge case they can shrug off. Ideally they end up irritated at the rule, then irritated at each other, and then notice that the irritation was built in from the start.

A few things I'd like you to work with rather than around:

- I invented a rule I'm attached to and I'd like you to stress-test it instead of quietly replacing it: each voter submits a ranking of the three options and also draws a line somewhere in their own ranking; everything below their line counts as unacceptable and is vetoed outright. Among the options that survive everyone's veto we tally Borda-style points; if nothing survives, or there's a tie, we resolve by lottery. My suspicion is that with 24 voters this deadlocks almost every time, and I can't tell whether that's a bug or the lesson. Tell me which.
- I'd rather play it in small committees of about 6 with a real class-level outcome riding on it, so that different committees hit different profiles and at least one of them contains an obvious manipulator. Then something has to combine the four committee results, and I have no idea what that step should be without importing a second copy of the same problem — which might be the best part, if you can stage it.
- Run two rounds with the tallies revealed in between, so they can revise. I want them to feel the regress: what do I submit given what I think they'll submit given what I said last round.

On stakes: no real money, and I don't want to bribe them with grades. My current idea is that whatever wins actually gets done in the first 15 minutes of next week's class. But I can see a problem I can't solve myself — once they know the outcome is real, their preferences get contaminated by which option is more fun to watch happen, so the incentive scheme mutates the profile partway through the game. Handle this, or tell me it's fatal and propose something else.

Give me the rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific hypotheses of the theorem — including the place where "just report your honest ranking" stops being a well-defined instruction. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (the student who refuses to lie on principle, the committee that accidentally lands on a dictator, whatever), and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — I already suspect one playthrough can't make them feel a universal quantifier, and that some of them will leave thinking lying is just smart politics rather than something the rule forces.

One last thing: I want you to mark clearly where you're on settled ground and where voting theorists actually disagree — whether the theorem has much to say about real electoral design, whether the computational difficulty of finding a good manipulation blunts it, whether randomizing or going cardinal is a genuine escape or just relocates the problem. Don't hand me the contested bits as though they were consensus.
