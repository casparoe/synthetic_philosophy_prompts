I run a Saturday-morning workshop for about 18 people — half are first-year philosophy grad students, half are staffers from a city planning department who've never touched a philosophy text and will happily tell you so. Three hours, one room, one big table plus some breakout corners, no budget to speak of beyond donuts. I want to build the session around a negotiation game that makes them feel two things in this order: first, that trading across genuinely different value systems can produce a package every side prefers to anything they could get by holding firm — real gains from trade, not a split-the-difference compromise where everyone walks away mildly annoyed — and then, right after they've started enjoying that, that the best available deal requires one of them to hand over something their own written instructions say is not for sale. I want them to make the deal anyway, or refuse and watch the loss land, and then have to argue about which of those was the mistake.

Here's the setup I've sketched; improve it or throw it out. A county has to settle one package about a stretch of riverfront: a housing development, a flood-control levee, a cemetery that would be partly flooded depending on the levee design, and a reserve fund. Four delegations, each gets a card with a private scoring rule over outcomes — and the crucial part is that the scoring rules are in different currencies, not different weights on the same currency. One delegation counts only housing units built, linearly and greedily. One counts the reserve. One counts the intactness of the cemetery, and their curve is a cliff: below some level of disturbance they lose almost nothing, above it they lose everything, and the card should not say in words where the cliff sits — they have to notice it in their own numbers, mid-game. The fourth counts something procedural: they score identical outcomes higher if they were actually offered the deal than if it was imposed on them, which means they can be bought cheaply with process, and I want that to be visible to the room in the debrief. There's no common metric and no social total anyone can compute; a good outcome is only ever one that every delegation's own ledger prefers to the fallback, and the fallback is that the package dies and everyone collects their no-deal score, which should be bad but not ruinous for anyone. The cemetery delegation's card also carries a charter line saying the ground is not tradeable at any price, while their numbers show a small disturbance costing them very little. That contradiction is the engine of the whole thing, so please keep it even if it makes the card look unfair.

What I want back: full role cards written out with the exact numbers, a round structure with a minute-by-minute timeline that fits the time I have, communication rules (who can talk to whom, whether side deals are allowed, whether card numbers may be shown or only described), and a payoff scheme that doesnt need real money but where the points still bite — plus your view on whether tying them to some small real pot at the end would sharpen the exercise or poison it. Then a public-justification mechanic: every deal has to be written up as one sentence all parties will sign, and the session ends with those sentences read aloud in front of the room, so people have to hear their own compromise said in plain words. Tell me how to run that so it lands instead of turning into a bit. Then the debrief, mapping moments they'll have just lived through onto the distinctions I care about — different currencies versus different weights, integrative trade versus splitting, holding a threshold versus holding out for extortion, betraying your mandate for your principals' own good, and whether a deal can be right in its outcome and wrong in the way it got made. No reading list, no names to go look up; these people have three hours on a Saturday and I want the concepts in words they'll already have in their heads by the time we talk. And be frank about what predictably goes wrong the first run — I'm already assuming the housing delegation opens with one lowball offer and the room decides they're the villain, and that the cliff group either gets steamrolled or freezes up — but tell me what else breaks, how I recover if no deal gets made inside the time available, and which feature of real moral compromise the game structurally cannot reproduce and I'm going to have to supply by hand.
