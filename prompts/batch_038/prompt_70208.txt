I am supervising a dissertation whose thesis is that Solomonoff induction is not merely a useful idealization but the correct normative account of inductive inference, and before I permit the student to draft a single chapter I want the standard of adequacy pinned down independently of her favorite results. Write the desiderata: what any proposed account of a prior over hypotheses must deliver in order to earn the title, given that its intended use is normative — it is supposed to tell a bounded agent what it ought to believe, and to serve as the yardstick against which tractable approximations are judged.

For each requirement, supply the case or consideration that motivates it rather than its label alone. The list should cover, at minimum, what the account owes on: dominance, i.e. why this prior rather than another, given that a universal prior multiplicatively dominates every lower-semicomputable semimeasure up to a constant; the invariance of the simplicity measure across description languages; semi-computability in place of computability — which direction of approximation is available, and exactly what is forfeited by the absence of a decision procedure; convergence to the truth whenever the data are generated by something in the prior's support; behavior at finite sample sizes; logical and mathematical uncertainty in an agent who is also supposed to reason about its own deductions; self-locating or indexical uncertainty; and the shape of the hypothesis space — whether it may be countable only, and what the account then owes to inference over real-valued parameters.

Press especially on the finite-data requirement. Consider a language obtained from a standard one by adding a single primitive symbol naming the first million bits of some familiar irrational. The invariance theorem guarantees that complexities relative to the two languages differ by a constant, but here the constant is on the order of a million bits, which is longer than any data set I shall ever actually observe. Say whether the invariance theorem is doing any normative work at that scale or only in the limit, and say the same about the claim that a predicate like grue is penalized relative to green.

Then — the part I most want — mark which requirements are non-negotiable for the normative role and which are the settled preferences of a particular camp; identify every subset you believe to be jointly unsatisfiable, saying in each case whether the impossibility is a theorem, a conjecture, or merely an engineering difficulty; and name the single requirement that eliminates the greatest number of candidates.

Close with a scorecard of no more than a paragraph each for four contenders: the universal prior itself; a prior defined by truncating program search at some length; a minimax-regret construction that dispenses with a prior altogether and is defined only where a certain complexity functional is finite; and an ordinary subjectivist Bayesian who holds that any strictly positive, reasonably calibrated prior will do the same work. No bibliography, and no page numbers you would have to invent.
