I am a sixth-form student writing an independent research essay for my school's philosophy prize, and before I commit four thousand words to a thesis I would like some help establishing how confident I ought to be in it. The debate I am working in is the one between Dennett and Caruso over punishment and just deserts: Dennett's side holds that responsibility is a matter of competence, that competence comes in degrees and is cultivated by the practices of holding people responsible, and that once you see desert this way there is nothing metaphysically expensive left to give up; Caruso's side holds that retributivist desert, the idea that wrongdoers deserve to suffer in virtue of what they did, is unsupported whether the world is deterministic or indeterministic, and that a public health approach to crime, built on the quarantine analogy Pereboom developed, can replace it.

My thesis is a specific one, and I want your credence in it as a single number. It is this: a committed free will sceptic can construct a system of criminal punishment that is at least as protective of offenders as the retributivist system it replaces, in the sense that giving up basic desert costs you none of the constraints retributivists claim only desert can supply — the prohibition on punishing the innocent, the requirement of proportionality, and the ceiling on severity. I am not asking whether such a system is desirable, and I am not asking whether scepticism is true; I am asking only whether the protective work can be done without importing anything desert-shaped.

Please show me the derivation rather than the conclusion. Break the question into the sub-questions you think actually carry the weight, give each a rough probability and one or two sentences of justification, and then say how they combine into your overall figure. I do not need a formal calculation, but I do need to know whether you are treating the components as roughly independent or as substantially the same question asked twice, and why. The sub-questions I have in mind, though you should feel free to discard or add to them, are: whether a forward-looking rationale contains any internal upper bound on severity, or whether risk-reduction licenses ever-longer detention the moment an offender stops responding to treatment; whether the least-restrictive-means standard I want to rely on can cap punishment without quietly assuming that offenders are owed no more than they merit, which looks like a desert claim in disguise; whether the capacity to respond to reasons can bear justificatory weight when one makes no claim at all about ultimate origination; and whether the attempt-versus-completion asymmetry survives — a system that tracks dangerousness rather than desert must sentence identically the person whose victim lived by luck and the person whose did not, and I cannot tell whether I should call that a cost of scepticism or a correction of a retributivist mistake.

Finally, my own credence in the thesis is 0.7 and my teacher's is 0.25, and we have now argued the point twice without either of us budging. Tell me which single sub-question you think we are most likely to be disagreeing about, and what kind of consideration would move your own number furthest in either direction — a philosophical argument, a thought experiment, or an empirical fact about how systems built on risk assessment rather than desert actually behave over time.
