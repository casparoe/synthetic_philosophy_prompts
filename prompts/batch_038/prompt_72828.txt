I'm putting together a session for a grad seminar on the ethics of everyday conventions and instead of a paper I want to hand out a dialogue — something that shows two positions actually getting traction on each other rather than trading slogans.

The setup, which I've deliberately fiddled with so it isn't just the standard US tipping debate: a restaurant in a country we can leave unnamed. The menu prices already include a mandatory 12% service charge, but that goes to the house as revenue, not to the staff. The staff get a legal minimum that nobody would call a living wage, and the discretionary extra that customers leave on top is basically the entire difference between the job being survivable and it not being. Everyone local knows how this works.

Two people at the table, end of the meal, bill arrives. One is visiting for a semester — not a tourist passing through, but not a member either — and comes from a place where leaving extra money is considered faintly insulting and where service staff are properly paid. She thinks the whole arrangement is a racket that employers have offloaded onto customers and she's not going to be the mark for it. The other is a long-time resident who thinks that's self-interest dressed up as principle.

I want them genuinely well matched. Not a conventionalist who just says when in Rome, and not a hardliner who only counts harm. Give the visitor the strongest available version of the structural objection — including the awkward fact that her refusal doesn't cost the house anything at all, so it isn't even a boycott. Give the resident the strongest version of the idea that a local convention can generate a real requirement on someone who never chose it, and then let the visitor press the obvious difficulty: if all the convention is doing is settling something morality leaves indeterminate, why can't her home convention settle it just as well? The resident should have a real answer to that, and the visitor should have a real answer to the answer.

I also want them arguing about the right things — whether the wrong here runs to the individual server or to the practice as a whole; whether being only a temporary participant changes the balance; whether the fact that people will sanction her (cold looks, worse table next time, being described as difficult) is evidence that theres an obligation or merely evidence that there's a norm; and, this is the part I care about most, whether they end up disagreeing about a principle or just about the institutional facts of this restaurant. Have one of them concede something that actually costs them. Land it somewhere definite — no agreeing to disagree, no narrator stepping in to summarise.

900 words or so. Begin directly with the dialogue, no preface, no title, nothing after it either. They can gesture at the philosophical machinery if it comes up naturally, but keep it inside the conversation — no name-checking, no survey energy.
