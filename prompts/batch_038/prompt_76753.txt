I'm a science and ideas correspondent at a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I'm delivering the opening lecture at a university institute devoted to the atmosphere and society — after which I take questions from the floor, unscripted, with no notes and no chance to check a figure. The room will be roughly twenty operational and academic numerical weather-prediction researchers (several of whom have spent their careers on subseasonal-to-seasonal forecasting and will take the talk's central claim personally), four philosophers of science working on determinism, emergence and modelling, and a handful of science-policy people who administer adaptation and disaster-risk programmes.

The lecture argues four things. First, that popular reporting of chaos is a category error: what was established mid-century was not that the world is indeterministic, nor that small causes generally have large effects, but that a strictly deterministic flow can have an error-doubling timescale — which turns a claim about physics into a claim about measurement. Second, that the resulting forecast horizon is logarithmically expensive to extend: if error grows geometrically, halving initial uncertainty buys one extra doubling time, a day or two, not a doubling of range, so the familiar fortnight barrier on deterministic mid-latitude forecasts is a structural feature of the problem rather than a technological lag we should expect to close. Third, that most of the genuine progress since has come not from extending that horizon but from converting it into a calibrated probability distribution — a different epistemic achievement, and one that is routinely misreported to the public as "better forecasts." Fourth, that this has a funding consequence: prediction and preparedness draw on the same public money, and if returns to range extension are logarithmic past a point while returns to short-lead warning, exposure reduction and insurance are roughly linear, agencies should tilt toward the second.

I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my case. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk if I fumbled it, and attributed to which segment of the audience would raise it. Phrase each the way that person would phrase it, in the forecasters' and the philosophers' own vocabulary rather than a general reader's, and attach a reply that fits in about ninety seconds of speech. For each, say whether I should concede something up front to disarm it, and say plainly where the reply is soft. Then tell me which of my four claims is load-bearing in a way I haven't noticed, and what the single question is that I should be hoping nobody asks.
