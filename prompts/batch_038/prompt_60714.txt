Our reading group spent six weeks on Savulescu's procreative beneficence and then one of us blew it up with an argument I still can't get out of. It's basically mine, I wrote it up, we call it the Transfer Argument.

(1) Procreative beneficence is true: if you're going to have a child and you could have any of several possible children, and you have good reason to think one of them would have the better life, you should have that one.
(2) What makes (1) true is just that the better life gets lived. Nothing about who the parents are, or about how they came to be that child's parents, is part of the reason — the reason is impersonal, it's the same reason there would be for anyone else's child to have the better life.
(3) So it generalizes: whenever you could arrange things so that some child's life goes better than it otherwise would, and the arranging costs you nothing you have an independent right to hold onto, you should arrange it.
(4) Here's our case. A clinic, stipulated into existence for the sake of the argument, offers two services. Service A: you have forty embryos, they're scored for predicted wellbeing, and you can just take the top one. Service B is the one that does the damage: you hand over an existing child and they place her with a couple who scores better on parenting on every measure the clinic uses to score embryos. Stipulate that the handover is genuinely costless to the child — no attachment loss, no grief, no stigma, no disruption to anything she values, and she's three so there's no memory of us to lose.
(5) By (3), I should use Service B on my daughter.
(6) I shouldn't. Everyone in the group says so, including the guy who invented Service B, and its not squeamishness — we've all been through the disability critique and the expressivist objection and none of that is doing the work here.
(7) So (3) is false, and since (3) came out of (2), (2) is false too.
(8) And if (2) is false then (1) has nothing left to stand on, because the impersonal reason in (2) is the only reason anyone has ever given for (1). So procreative beneficence is false.

I don't believe (8) and I can't find the bad step. My instinct is that something goes wrong between (3) and (5), but I can't tell whether that instinct is anything more than refusing to take (4)'s stipulations seriously — and if you get to dismiss the clean cases, you can never test a principle at all, which seems like a bad rule. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than any of the others.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when it didn't, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If the right move is to reject (6) and eat the bullet, say that and tell me what a bullet-biter then owes us that the rest of us don't. Two side questions I care about: does it matter that (1) is a choice about who will exist while (5) is a choice about who raises somebody who already does — is that a real difference or just where the intuition is hiding? And we burned two of our six weeks arguing about whether "the better life" in (1) means expected wellbeing, expected attainment, or those two coming apart in a single embryo; does any of that ambiguity change whether the Transfer Argument lands?

Bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, don't read the argument back to me, and please write it in American English.
