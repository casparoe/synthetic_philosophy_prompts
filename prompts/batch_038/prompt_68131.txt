I teach a graduate seminar in Bayesian epistemology and am setting the final paper. I need one question, worth 20 marks and answerable in 75 minutes, on Dutch book arguments. It must not be answerable by reciting the standard results — that an agent's betting function admits a sure-loss book just in case it violates the probability axioms, and that an agent who fails to conditionalize can be diachronically booked. My students have those theorems memorized. What I want them to confront is that both theorems presuppose a single probability function, so that once an agent's doxastic state is a set of such functions rather than one, the argument's crucial step — the bridge from credence to a disposition to accept and reject bets — is no longer supplied by the mathematics at all, and has to be supplied by an explicit rule for accepting gambles. Different rules give different verdicts about the very same credal state, and that is where the question should bite.

Build the question around the following case, and give the students all the numbers so that the item is self-contained. Ada is offered bets on a coin landing heads. Her doxastic state is represented by the set of probability functions assigning heads any probability between 0.3 and 0.7 inclusive. Two bets are offered separately, in either order: Bet A requires her to pay $6 now in exchange for $10 if the coin lands heads; Bet B requires her to pay $6 now in exchange for $10 if it lands tails. Note that accepting both costs $12 and returns $10 regardless of the outcome. Under one acceptance rule — accept any bet that is favourable on at least one member of the representing set — she takes both; under the other — accept only bets whose worst case across the set is non-negative — she takes neither.

The question should have three parts. Part (a) asks students to compute the expected value of each bet as a function of the probability of heads, identify precisely which sub-ranges of the representing set render each bet admissible under the first rule, and explain why no single member of the set renders both admissible. Part (b) asks what this does and does not show about whether an imprecise doxastic state is irrational, and must require them to attend to the fact that the book depends on the bets being evaluated separately rather than as a package, since the package itself is refused under either rule. Part (c) is the hard part. Suppose instead that Ada is perfectly sharp on the coin — every member of her set assigns heads exactly 1/2 — and her imprecision concerns the reliability of a report she expects to receive: each member also assigns the report probability 1/2, but one member assigns it probability 4/5 given heads while the other assigns it 1/5 given heads. Ask them to derive what her credence in heads becomes once the report arrives, and then to say whether a book can be constructed against her using the same $6 and $10 amounts, indicating which bets are accepted before the report and which after. The point I want them to reach, though they must earn it, is that the imprecision doing the damage here is not imprecision about the proposition being bet on at all: the pre-report bet is favourable by every member's lights, and only the post-report bet requires an optimistic member.

Please deliver three things. First, the question exactly as students will see it, with the case stated in full and the marks for each part allocated. Second, a model answer of roughly 800 to 900 words. Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points whose marks sum to 20, with a one-line note for each on how candidates typically botch it, plus a short separate list of errors that should cost marks without zeroing the answer — for instance, reporting that the agent loses $2 without noticing that the two bets are never simultaneously admissible for one and the same probability function, or assuming that switching to the worst-case rule settles the matter rather than raising the further question of what is wrong with a rule that never accepts a risk.

Two constraints on all three deliverables. Students will be told they may not appeal to the literature, and your question and model answer should observe the same rule: no names, no titles, no gestures at the standard objection or the standard reply. Everything must be argued from the case as described. And I want the model answer to be committed rather than survey-shaped. Where the case admits of a genuinely contestable reading — in particular whether it indicts imprecise doxastic states as such or only a certain way of turning such states into betting behaviour — come down one way, say why, and say what would have to be true for the other reading to win. Do not give the two readings equal airtime. If you think any part of my case is ill-posed or the arithmetic does not deliver the lesson I expect, revise it and tell me explicitly what you changed and why; that is more useful to me than a polite model answer built on a broken setup.

Finally, close with a note of two or three sentences marking where your own answer leans on a premise that a good student could reasonably dispute — for example, on whether evaluating bets one at a time is itself a rational defect, or on whether passing up favourable gambles counts as a failing of the same kind as being guaranteed a loss. I want to know in advance which of my own commitments the marking scheme will be enforcing.
