Walking to campus I listened to a podcast where a philosophy professor (a real one, I think — he had a book out) tried to set up the Liar for a general audience, and I jotted down the gist on my phone right after. So this is my paraphrase of his summary, not a transcript, and I've probably smoothed it out a bit:

Tarski proved that a language can't contain its own truth predicate. Take any language expressive enough to do arithmetic and add a predicate meant to pick out exactly its own true sentences, and you can derive a contradiction. So truth has to live one level up, in a metalanguage that's strictly richer than the language it's about, which means there's no such thing as truth for a semantically closed language like English. On that picture the Liar isn't really paradoxical at all — the sentence just isn't well-formed material in any respectable language, so there's nothing to explain. Kripke later found a way around this. Instead of building truth top-down, you build it bottom-up: start with the sentences that don't involve any semantic notions, assign them values, then use those to settle the semantic sentences, and keep going until nothing changes. Where the process stabilizes you have a fixed point, and at a fixed point you have a language that really does contain its own truth predicate, consistently. The Liar ends up in a gap — neither true nor false, basically meaningless, like a category error. So Kripke beat Tarski: English can have its own truth predicate after all, you just have to give up the idea that every meaningful sentence is either true or false.
