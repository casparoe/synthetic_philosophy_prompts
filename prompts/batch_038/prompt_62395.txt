You are an experienced instructor of mathematical logic and the philosophy of mathematics who has designed and run hands-on workshops on the semantics of proof for undergraduates. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics for the session in a sentence or two each, saying which you discarded and why; then develop the one you have chosen in full.

The setting: a 75-minute block in an upper-level philosophy of mathematics seminar, 16 students. All have completed a first course in logic — natural deduction, first-order syntax, soundness and completeness for classical propositional logic — and none has encountered intuitionistic logic beyond a paragraph in the textbook. The room has a whiteboard, index cards, and a printer; no laptops and no real money may be involved. I want the students to feel the force of the constructivist reconstrual of the connectives, and specifically to leave having seen three things, in this order: (a) that the disagreement is not merely epistemic — not "we do not yet know whether P" — but about what counts as establishing a disjunction or an existential at all, so that a proof of A ∨ B has to tell you which disjunct and a proof of ∃x φ(x) has to hand you a witness; (b) that the intuitionist is not asserting that excluded middle is false, since ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P) is derivable and ¬(P ∨ ¬P) is refutable, so the position is not the mirror image of classical logic but something finer-grained, and it still licenses excluded middle wherever an actual decision procedure exists; and (c) that abandoning excluded middle is paid for in information rather than merely lost — where the intuitionist systems enjoy the disjunction and existence properties, a derivable existential comes with an extractable witness that the classical derivation of the same sentence need not supply. All three should surface from what happens at the tables, not from anything I say beforehand; the words "intuitionism," "constructivism," and any proper names should be withheld until the debrief.

Mechanics I would like you to build on, though you may improve or replace them: a token economy in which the only spendable resource is a witness or construction rather than a truth value; a challenge protocol under which any team may call on a team that has asserted a disjunction or an existential to name the disjunct or produce the witness within a fixed time, on pain of forfeiting the points; an unlimited or purchasable "excluded middle" token that permits a team to assert P ∨ ¬P for the target sentence without saying which, with a payoff scheme engineered so that the token is genuinely profitable in some rounds and ruinous in others, so that students discover through play that it is a wager rather than a gift; and a target predicate that is opaque within the session — its value fixed by me in secret, or fixed by a rule no student can determine, with a search space far larger than the time permits — paired with at least one genuinely decidable predicate, so that the asymmetry between the two is felt rather than explained.

Please deliver: complete rules, team sizes, and a minute-by-minute timeline; a printable one-page player handout and a separate facilitator sheet; the scoring scheme in exact numbers with worked examples of a profitable and a ruinous use of the token; a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — teams hoarding witnesses, the challenge protocol degenerating into side deals, one student monopolizing a table, the classical side winning so decisively that the intuitionist stance looks like mere obstinacy — together with a pre-emptive fix for each; two or three debrief questions that force the students to articulate (a), (b), and (c) themselves before I name any of it; and a frank account of which feature of the constructivist position the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by hand. I already suspect two defects: that fixing the value of the opaque predicate in secret smuggles in precisely the verification-transcendent notion of truth a Brouwerian wants to deny, and that "whatever this room can manage in seventy-five minutes" is a poor stand-in for "there exists a construction in principle." Tell me whether those suspicions are correct, and how to word the caveat so that it qualifies the lesson without dissolving it. Finally, if a short add-on can let the group feel why some constructive schools accept Markov's principle and others reject it — that is, why moving from "it cannot be ruled out that there is such an x" to "here is such an x" is contentious even among people who agree on everything above — include it, provided it costs no more than five minutes; if it would muddy the water, say so and explain what it would muddy.
