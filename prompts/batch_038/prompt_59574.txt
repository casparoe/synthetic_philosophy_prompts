So — situation. I volunteer at our county nature centre and I run their monthly "big ideas over coffee" night: twenty-odd people, mostly retired, a couple of homeschooling mums, and one extremely confident ex-quarryman who will argue with anything. Nobody's done science since high school. I've got forty minutes, a flipchart, and no projector. November's topic got away from me: I said yes to "what age of the Earth are we actually living in?" and now I have to handle the argument about when the Anthropocene started and who gets to say.

And it's personal, because for years I've been the one in my family who confidently announces that the Anthropocene started in the 1950s. I read that around 2019, repeated it at every Thanksgiving, and then found out that in March 2024 the committee of stratigraphers voted the proposal down — something like 12 to 4 — and the bodies above it ratified that a couple of weeks later. So the fact I've been handing out is either wrong or was never the kind of fact I thought it was, and I don't want to do that to a room full of trusting people again.

What I've gathered from reading, which you should assume is all I know and all they know: the proposal was to put the boundary at a layer of mud in Crawford Lake in Ontario with a spike in plutonium from bomb testing, dated to about 1952; there's a rival date of 1610, tied to the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas after contact and a measurable dip in atmospheric CO2; some people want it pushed back to the Industrial Revolution or even to the invention of farming; and some of the same researchers now say it shouldn't be an epoch at all but an "event" with no start date.

Don't explain the Anthropocene to me. Plan how I explain it to them. Specifically:

- What has to be in place before any date means anything to them. My hunch is that 1952-versus-1610 is noise until they get that an epoch boundary isn't "when the trouble started" but a cut in rock that has to be findable at the same moment all over the planet — is that the right first brick, what's the smallest version of it I can draw on a flipchart, and what does that version itself depend on?
- What single example should carry the whole thing. I'm torn between the lake core (accurate, but nobody in that room has ever seen a core and I can't bring one) and something local and layered — the quarry the ex-quarryman worked, or the town's own buried history. Tell me which, and what the example has to have and what it must not have in order to do this job.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually turn up rather than in order of importance. I'm expecting: "obviously it starts when humans started wrecking things, so farming, or Columbus, or steam"; "it got voted down so the whole thing was nonsense"; "golden spike means an actual spike"; and the big one, "these people just took a vote, so it's politics not science." For each, say whether I head it off before it forms or let it happen and use it, and where in the sequence that happens.
- The hard part: how do I make "who decides" land without sneering at the committee and without feeding the conspiracy reading? I want them to leave able to say why the people who draw the line in the rock are not the same people as the ones who study the causes, and why that isn't a scandal.
- One question I can put to the room in the last two minutes that would tell me whether they've genuinely got the difference between "when did this start" and "where do you cut it," rather than just politely agreeing with me.
- And what I should flatly refuse to go into at this level, said out loud to them, with the reason I give.

Assume I can't fact-check anything live and that if I get a date wrong in front of that quarryman I'll never hear the end of it.
