I work in software and have no formal philosophy training, so I'd like a careful accuracy check on a summary I read. A blog post about the Chinese Room frames the Searle–Dennett dispute over original and derived intentionality like this:

"Searle's argument is often described as showing that computers can't think, but his actual claim is narrower: syntax by itself is never sufficient for semantics, and only a system with the right causal powers — which he takes biological brains to have — could produce understanding. The man in the room follows rules that generate well-formed Chinese without any idea what the symbols mean. Dennett's counter is his 'two-bitser': a device with just two internal states whose states can be read as being about any number of things — which of two doors a mouse went through, whether a lamp is on — depending entirely on how it is wired up and what the interpreter cares about, since nothing inside the device fixes which of these it represents. The two-bitser therefore defeats the Chinese Room, because it shows that the man in the room is genuinely about things in the world in exactly the way a person's beliefs are: in both cases content is fixed by causal history rather than by some extra inner ingredient. Because a program's states are about things in just the way ours are, Dennett concludes that beliefs and desires are not real states of a system at all but merely convenient devices for predicting behavior, fictions we project onto the machinery as we project meaning onto the two-bitser. The upshot is that the quarrel is largely verbal: Searle reserves 'understanding' for whatever brains do, Dennett applies it to any sufficiently well-connected information processing, and there is no further fact left for them to disagree about."

Could you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what someone working on this debate would accept, what they'd regard as a fair simplification, and what they'd object to? Three specific things I'd like settled: whether the two-bitser really is Dennett's reply to the room itself or something aimed at a different target; whether Dennett is committed to the claim that beliefs and desires are unreal, since that is the sentence that made me uneasy; and whether the closing claim that the disagreement is merely verbal survives once the earlier sentences are corrected, or whether a substantive question remains that I couldn't see.
