I TA a seminar called something like "How Do We Cut Up the Past?" and we're two weeks into the periodization unit — whether "Middle Ages," "Renaissance," and "Scientific Revolution" pick out real breaks in how people lived, or are labels imposed backwards by generations who needed a foil to define themselves against. The readings are fine, but the section discussion keeps collapsing into the same mush within ten minutes: "well, there really were discontinuities, but which ones you notice depends on what you look at, so it's sort of both, right?" and then everyone nods and we go home. I want to break that stalemate by handing the students a concrete case they actually have to take a side on. So I need you to build me one.

Here's the spec. Design a research situation — realistic, present-day historian, no time travel, no magic archive, no omniscient narrator — in which three positions on periodization give three different verdicts about the same stretch of the past, and where the verdicts fall out of the positions rather than being asserted. The three positions: (1) a realist, who thinks periods track genuine discontinuities in ordinary life and that if the inherited labels miss one, so much the worse for the labels; (2) a constructivist/nominalist, who thinks a period boundary is an artifact of which variable the historian chose to sort on, and that there's no fact of the matter about which sorting is the period-relevant one; (3) a pragmatist or instrumentalist, who thinks whether there's a break is relative to the question being asked — and this one has to come out as a substantive answer, not a shrug dressed up as sophistication. Hard constraints, please respect all of them: the historian has essentially complete evidence for the stretch in question, meaning every surviving document from one mid-sized European town across roughly a century has been digitized and read, so "we'd just need more sources" is off the table as an escape hatch. The shift she finds has to be sharp, datable to within about fifteen years, and confined to a single domain of mundane practice — no war, no plague, no new technology, no famous individual, no religious upheaval, and no change of ruler can be doing the causal work. And it has to be invisible in everything our standard period labels actually key on: no change in art style, theology, dynasty, or learned culture, so nobody can just say "oh, that's the Renaissance arriving late in the provinces." Invent whatever concrete variable you like — I was vaguely thinking something about how wills or labor contracts are phrased, or a shift in how people dated thier documents, but if you've got a better mundane one, use yours instead.

Then the deliverable. Two paragraphs of scenario, no more. After that, walk me through it: state precisely the question the historian is asking, then give each of the three positions' verdict and the reasoning that gets them there *from the case* rather than from their general philosophy — I want to see the machinery, not a slogan. Then be honest with me about which single feature of the case is doing the work, the one thing that makes the three positions peel apart instead of converging. And show me the case collapsing when you remove it: suppose the same sharp break shows up at the same date in a dozen independent variables, or suppose there's a dated town statute that obviously caused the whole thing — does the disagreement evaporate, and if it does, whose position wins? Finally, tell me how the realist would attack the constructivist's verdict here and whether that attack actually lands. I'm going to photocopy this for eighteen undergrads, so it needs to stand alone without me narrating anything over it, and please write the whole thing in American English — I keep getting "periodisation" and "behaviour" out of these models and it looks bizarre on a handout.
