I'm a second-year PhD student in history of science, and about a third of what I teach in my first-year seminar is claims that people call pseudohistorical — Fomenko, ancient astronauts, literal Atlantis, lost advanced civilisations. Every single year a student asks why one of these gets the label and some other thing that looks just as bad doesn't, and this year I realised I have no answer at all. I have intuitions. I'm not sure they hang together. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here's what I actually think, case by case:

1. Fomenko's New Chronology — pseudohistory, obviously. He has statistical machinery, he's a professional mathematician, he has credentialed followers, he cites primary sources. None of it saves him.
2. Von Däniken's ancient astronauts — pseudohistory.
3. Atlantis read as a literal account of a real bronze-age island empire — pseudohistory. The same text read as a philosophical device — fine, uncontroversial, that's just reading Plato.
4. Graham Hancock's lost civilisation — pseudohistory, even though he travels, dives on sites, and gets mainstream archaeologists to argue with him on camera.
5. Heyerdahl: the Kon-Tiki crossing itself I think is a genuine experiment and a genuine achievement. The diffusionist thesis he built on top of it is refuted and I'd half-call it pseudohistory. One body of work, two different verdicts from me.
6. The Vinland Map — I refuse to call this pseudohistory. It's a forgery. That's fraud, not bad theory, different category entirely.
7. Piltdown Man — same, the hoaxer is a forger. But the professionals who accepted it for decades were credulous experts, and I don't want to call them pseudoscientists either.
8. The Younger Dryas impact stuff: when it was proposed in journals by actual scientists with actual samples and got picked apart in the literature, my verdict is "controversial science that's mostly losing," not pseudoscience. When roughly the same claims get recycled in a streaming docuseries as proof that a comet wiped out an advanced ice-age civilisation that archaeologists are hiding, my verdict is pseudohistory. Same content! Different verdict! I can't tell if that's incoherent or if it's tracking something real.
9. "Clovis first" in American archaeology — people who insisted nothing predated Clovis, wouldn't look at Monte Verde, made life miserable for the people excavating it. They behaved exactly like the cranks they were accusing and they were wrong. Still not pseudoscience in my book. That's dogmatic mainstream science, which feels like it should be a different failure mode.
10. Bernal's Black Athena — loads of specific claims rejected, brutal response from professional classicists, huge public profile, politically charged. My verdict: not pseudohistory, just overreaching scholarship.
11. My own dissertation. I'm arguing a charter in my period is misdated by a couple of generations, on the basis of a scribal hand, a formula I think shows up later than people assume, and one dendro sequence that doesn't line up. I describe it as source criticism. A friend described it as "Fomenko-lite." I want the principle to say why he's wrong, or to tell me he isn't.

My tentative rule, which I suspect is too crude: something is pseudohistorical when its proponents have made it unfalsifiable in practice — every counter-finding gets absorbed as evidence of establishment bias — plus they invert the burden of proof, the "well, prove Atlantis didn't exist" move. Cases 5, 8, 9, 10 and 11 all strain that rule.

What I'm after:

- The principle, or a small set of genuinely competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a first-year and have them apply it without me in the room, with the case above that motivates each clause. If it has to be a cluster concept rather than one necessary-and-sufficient rule, say so and give me the cluster.
- Which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I'd rather lose one of them cleanly than end up with a rule that's secretly just "the ones I dislike."
- What it says about cases I didn't list: the nineteenth-century Mound Builder theory, Velikovsky, the Black Sea deluge hypothesis used as evidence for the Noah flood, and the Kensington Runestone.
- Whether "pseudohistory" is doing any descriptive work at all or is mostly a term of abuse, and whether case 8 (same claims, different packaging) is a bug in my intuitions or a feature the principle should be built around.

One thing I need you to be careful about: I know there are philosophers of science who think this entire project was shown to be a pseudo-problem decades ago — Laudan's line — and others who think it's very much alive, especially where there are real-world stakes like teaching and heritage claims. I want you to seperate out for me what's settled in that literature from what's genuinely contested among people who work on it, and not to slide a contested position past me as though it were the consensus. Same for my own framing: tell me where the question as I've asked it already assumes something people disagree about.
