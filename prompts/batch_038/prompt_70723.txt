My thesis: large-scale social sanctioning — deplatforming, viral shaming campaigns, coordinated pressure on employers, boycott targeting of an individual — is literally punishment, not merely expression and not merely the natural operation of reputation. So the norms we already accept for punishment (proportionality, a chance to be heard, limits on harm to uninvolved third parties, and a bar on sanctions that permanently destroy the offender's ability to rejoin the community) attach directly, and the usual "it's just speech" and "it's just people updating on information" defenses don't get you out of them.

Sketch of the argument, so you have a target:

1. I start from the familiar necessary-conditions list for punishment — burdensome, imposed for a supposed offense, on the supposed offender for that offense, deliberately administered by other people — and note that the last condition, the one requiring that the sanction be imposed and administered by an authority constituted by the legal system against which the offense was committed, is the only one social sanctioning clearly fails.
2. My move is that this last condition is doing two separable jobs: it marks off punishment from private revenge, and it guarantees the publicity that makes a sanction assessable for proportionality. Neither job strictly needs a state.
3. So I replace it with what I'm calling a coordination condition: a sanctioning practice is penal when participants' impositions are organized by publicly available criteria — who counts as the offender, what the offense is, what the sanction consists in, and, importantly, when it stops — even with no constituted authority anywhere in the picture. A big organized pile-on can meet this; a stranger insulting you once on the street cannot.
4. Payoff: once the practice is penal rather than expressive, proportionality bites, and permanently total deplatforming looks like a sentence with no end date and no reentry path, which is why I think it can't be defended the way a fine for a parking violation can.
5. There's a second strand I only gesture at in the paper: when universities, employers or platforms impose these sanctions partly in response to state interest or state pressure, that raises a legitimacy question rather than dissolving my account.
