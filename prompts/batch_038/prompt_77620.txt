Last month I attended a public lecture on the Browning–Goldhagen dispute over Reserve Police Battalion 101 — Browning's account of middle-aged Hamburg reservists under Browning's situational mechanisms, against Goldhagen's argument that a specifically German, eliminationist strand of antisemitism is what does the explaining. On the walk to the station two other attendees continued arguing, and because I could not work out which of them was actually landing blows, I wrote the exchange down afterward as faithfully as I could. Neither is a specialist in the period. Nadia teaches history at a secondary school; Paul designs safety-culture training for a utility company. I am reporting it rather than taking a side in it.

The exchange:

1. Nadia opened by saying the demographic facts settle the matter. The battalion was made up of middle-aged reservists from Hamburg's working class — dockworkers, clerks, drivers — men too old to have been formed by the Hitler Youth and belonging to no ideological elite. If a distinctively German antisemitic culture were doing the explanatory work, that is precisely the sample in which you would expect its grip to slacken, and instead the behavior came out near-uniform. So the cultural variable is inert here and the situational one is not.

2. Paul said she had constructed her control group out of the wrong material. Goldhagen's claim is not that the Nazis indoctrinated these men; it is that a particular way of thinking about Jews was already ambient in German culture well before 1933, so the absence of intensive Nazi socialization is beside the point — the cultural variable was loaded before the battalion was ever assembled. On that reading her control is simply a sample drawn from the very population the cultural thesis is about, which makes it no control at all.

3. Nadia replied that this is exactly the difficulty: the thesis cannot lose. A German policeman who shoots confirms it. A German policeman who refuses gets described as insufficiently exposed, or as an exception, or as evidence of how total the surrounding pressure must have been. A hypothesis that accommodates every possible outcome is not an explanation but a redescription.

4. Paul granted that for argument's sake and then reversed it. The situational account, he said, enjoys the same immunity — if the men had all refused, you would say the situation proved too weak; since they did not, you say it was strong enough — and it pays an additional price. Browning's own list of mechanisms, deference to authority, careerism, fear of standing out, the numbing effect of repetition, the shifting of responsibility onto whoever issued the order, is a list of dispositions. He is a character theorist who is uneasy about the word. And if the mechanisms are traits, then the quarrel was never between situation and culture to begin with.

5. Nadia said a trait functions as a character explanation only if you treat it as something that travels with the person and surfaces across settings. Browning's point is that these traits are elicited, and that what explains is the eliciting arrangement rather than the trait: the same men, unelicited, are clerks in Hamburg. She then put what she called the decisive datum on the table. Before the first massacre the commander told his men that anyone who did not feel up to the work could step out and would not be punished, and fewer than a dozen of roughly five hundred did. The option was real and nearly costless, so whatever separates the dozen from the rest cannot be a difference in their situation, which was identical for all of them. It has to be a difference in the men.

6. Paul said this was the strongest thing she had and still could not bear the weight she was putting on it. Twelve out of five hundred is a variance of about two percent. If you must explain a near-universal outcome, you explain it by reference to the factor present in nearly all the cases; a factor that discriminates only the residual two percent may be worth studying, but it is not the explanation of the other ninety-eight. The costless opt-out establishes only that the killing was not compelled — a point nobody disputes — and leaves the near-unanimity itself to be accounted for by exactly the group-level pressures Browning enumerates.

7. Nadia's next move was about what a theory is for. Her proposed test is portability: can you carry the explanation into a case you have not examined? Browning's travels — you name the structural features and then look for them in a guard unit, a police force, a contractor's site. Goldhagen's does not, because it requires the specific cultural pool, and so it can tell you nothing about the next atrocity anywhere else. Paul answered that portability is a criterion belonging to a training designer's purposes rather than a historian's, and that insisting on it quietly converts a question about the past into a question about what will be useful to him on Monday morning.

8. Nadia then said something that changed the shape of the argument. Her suspicion, she said, is that the situational account became popular partly because it is comfortable: if every perpetrator is an ordinary person caught in an extraordinary arrangement, then the description of any given perpetrator shades into a description of us, and the account acquires an exculpatory drift whether or not its author intends one. Paul said she was fusing two things that must be kept apart, causal explanation and moral address, and that an explanation can be wholly general and portable while leaving the agents fully responsible, since responsibility does not require that the behavior have been improbable. Nadia answered that if that is correct, his objection in move 7 loses its force too, because portability was never doing explanatory work in her argument either — only reassuring work, of the kind she had just accused his position of.

Three things I would like from you:

(a) Take the eight moves in order and give a verdict on each: which side is right, or whether the move is a draw, and — separately — whether the move advances the argument or merely restates the disagreement in fresh vocabulary. Where a move fails, say what its author would have had to add in order to answer the objection actually facing it.

(b) Say who is more right overall, and in particular whether Nadia's charge in move 3 and Paul's counter-charge in move 4 genuinely cancel each other out, or whether one lands harder than the other. I want to know if symmetric-unfalsifiability objections ever defeat themselves, or whether they simply leave both positions standing.

(c) Assess whether the two of them were having one disagreement at all. My hunch, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that from move 4 onward they were using situation and character at different levels of description and therefore talking past each other — but I can see that this diagnosis may be too charitable to both. Then identify what neither of them raised that a serious adjudication of the underlying question would have to address, including whether they were even arguing about the same explanandum.

Please do not recap the historiography, the public reception of either book, or the verdict of the profession; I know the outline and it will not help me judge these eight moves. Do not recommend further reading. Work from the moves as I have set them out.
