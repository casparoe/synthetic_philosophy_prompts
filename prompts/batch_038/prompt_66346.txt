I am working through Aristotle's account of incontinence in *Nicomachean Ethics* VII.3, in H. Rackham's Loeb translation (Rackham renders *akrasia* as "failure of self-restraint" or "unrestraint," and the akratic agent as "the unrestrained man"). Below is the stretch of the chapter that contains the practical-syllogism solution, from 1147a31 to 1147b14, with Rackham's own paragraph numeration removed and one elision marked. I would like help with a series of quite specific interpretive problems in it, and then with a question about how far its machinery can be stretched over a phenomenon Aristotle had no name for.

> (2) Again, reasoning on matters of conduct employs premises of two forms. Now it is quite possible for a man to act against knowledge when he knows both premises but is only exercising his knowledge of the universal premise and not of the particular; for action has to do with particular things. Moreover, there is a distinction as regards the universal term: one universal is predicated of the man himself, the other of the thing; for example, he may know and be conscious of the knowledge that dry food is good for every man and that he himself is a man, or even that food of a certain kind is dry, but either not possess or not be actualizing the knowledge whether the particular food before him is food of that kind. Now clearly the distinction between these two ways of knowing will make all the difference in the world. It will not seem at all strange that the unrestrained man should "know" in one way, but it would be astonishing if he knew in another way.
>
> (3) Again, it is possible for men to "have knowledge" in yet another way besides those just discussed; for even in the state of having knowledge without exercising it we can observe a distinction: a man may in a sense both have it and not have it; for instance, when he is asleep, or mad, or drunk. But persons under the influence of passion are in the same condition; for it is evident that anger, sexual desire, and certain other passions, actually alter the state of the body, and in some cases even cause madness. It is clear therefore that we must pronounce the unrestrained to "have knowledge" only in the same way as men who are asleep or mad or drunk. Their using the language of knowledge is no proof that they possess it. Persons in the states mentioned repeat propositions of geometry and verses of Empedocles; students who have just begun a subject reel off its formulae, though they do not yet know their meaning, for knowledge has to become part of the tissue of the mind, and this takes time. Hence we must conceive that men who fail in self-restraint talk in the same way as actors speaking a part.
>
> (4) Again, one may also study the cause of Unrestraint scientifically, thus: In a practical syllogism, the major premise is an opinion, while the minor premise deals with particular things, which are the province of perception. Now when the two premises are combined, just as in theoretic reasoning the mind is compelled to affirm the resulting conclusion, so in the case of practical premises you are forced at once to do it. For example, given the premises "All sweet things ought to be tasted" and "Yonder thing is sweet" — a particular instance of the general class —, you are bound, if able and not prevented, immediately to taste the thing.
>
> When therefore there is present in the mind on the one hand a universal judgement forbidding you to taste and on the other hand a universal judgement saying "All sweet things are pleasant," and a minor premise "Yonder thing is sweet" (and it is this minor premise that is active), and when desire is present at the same time, then, though the former universal judgement says "Avoid that thing," the desire leads you to it (since desire can put the various parts of the body in motion).
>
> Thus it comes about that when men fail in self-restraint, they act in a sense under the influence of a principle or opinion, but an opinion not in itself but only accidentally opposed to the right principle (for it is the desire, and not the opinion, that is really opposed). Hence the lower animals cannot be called unrestrained, if only for the reason that they have no power of forming universal concepts, but only mental images and memories of particular things. […]
>
> But inasmuch as the last premise, which originates action, is an opinion as to some object of sense, and it is this opinion which the unrestrained man when under the influence of passion either does not possess, or only possesses in a way which as we saw does not amount to knowing it but only makes him repeat it as the drunken man repeats the maxims of Empedocles, and since the ultimate term is not a universal, and is not deemed to be an object of Scientific Knowledge in the same way as a universal term is, we do seem to be led to the conclusion which Socrates sought to establish. For the knowledge which is present when failure of self-restraint occurs is not what is held to be Knowledge in the true sense, nor is it true Knowledge which is dragged about by passion, but knowledge derived from sense-perception. So much for the question whether failure of self-restraint can go with knowledge or not, and with knowledge in what sense.

**Part A. The internal mechanics of the passage.**

1. The dry-food example appears to involve more than two premises: a universal rule about dry food, a universal "predicated of the man himself" (that he is a man), and a particular premise about the food in front of him. What work is the self-predicated universal doing here — is it a self-ascription under a kind, and does it generate a distinct third locus at which the agent's knowledge can fail to be exercised? Note that in this example the failure Aristotle describes is *not* at the level of perception of the sensible particular but at the level of whether the particular food is of the relevant dry kind, and tell me whether that undercuts the tidy "the passion knocks out the perceptual minor premise" reading of the rest of the chapter.

2. In the sweet-things case, Aristotle says of the minor premise "Yonder thing is sweet" that *it is this minor premise that is active*, and yet at 1147b13 he says the last premise is precisely what the unrestrained man "either does not possess, or only possesses in a way which … does not amount to knowing it." Is the standard resolution — that two syllogisms are simultaneously in play, one issuing in avoidance and one in tasting, and that the minor premise is active in the second and inert in the first — actually supported by the text as written, or is it imported? Please say explicitly where the passage licenses it and where it does not.

3. Paragraph (3) offers the asleep/mad/drunk analogy and then, separately, the analogy of the novice student who "reels off" formulae and of the actor speaking a part. Are these one way of "having knowledge without exercising it" or two different ways? In particular, does the actor case imply that the akratic's universal premise is merely vocalized without being grasped — which would locate the failure in the universal, not the particular, and seem to conflict with paragraph (4)?

4. At 1147b11 Aristotle says the akratic acts under an opinion "not in itself but only accidentally opposed to the right principle," because "it is the desire, and not the opinion, that is really opposed." Unpack "accidentally" here. Then tell me whether the resulting picture is ultimately non-cognitive — a motivational override with cognition intact — and how that squares with the Socratic conclusion he draws two paragraphs later, namely that what is present is not knowledge in the true sense but "knowledge derived from sense-perception." Does the chapter end by vindicating Socrates, accommodating him, or merely relocating the problem?

5. Paragraph (4) says the major premise of a practical syllogism "is an opinion," while the closing lines say the ultimate term "is not deemed to be an object of Scientific Knowledge in the same way as a universal term is." Does anything in the argument depend on the major premise's having a lower epistemic status than the universal terms of theoretical science, or is that remark doing no argumentative work? Flag anywhere in the excerpt where you think the interpretive question is genuinely underdetermined by Rackham's English rather than settled by it.

**Part B. Extending the machinery to a case Aristotle does not describe.**

Consider a modern counterpart. On Monday an agent considers two outcomes, a larger reward available in three months and a smaller one available immediately, sincerely judges the larger-later better, and forms a plan accordingly. On Wednesday he takes the smaller-sooner. Nothing in the Wednesday episode resembles the passions Aristotle enumerates: no anger, no sexual desire, no bodily upheaval of the kind paragraph (3) invokes, and no new information. The only thing that has changed is the temporal position of the agent relative to the two outcomes; the sooner reward is now perceptually present and imminent, and his ranking has inverted as a result. In contemporary terms this is the pattern that quasi-hyperbolic discounting is designed to capture: preferences that are dynamically inconsistent, so that a ranking held at a distance is reversed at the moment of action, and so that a plan made by the agent on Monday is defeated by the same agent on Wednesday.

6. Does Aristotle's account have the resources to describe this agent as akratic? His diagnostic criterion, stated earlier in Book VII, is that the incontinent man acts against what he thinks best and that, before entering the state, he did not think he ought to act so. If on Wednesday the agent *now* judges the sooner reward better, he acts in accordance with what then seems best, and the Wednesday choice looks non-akratic even though the Monday-to-Wednesday sequence is exactly what we mean by weakness of will. Tell me whether this is a real problem for the Aristotelian description or a merely verbal one, and whether Aristotle can say that the standing judgment survives the local inversion.

7. Suppose we try to bring the case inside the passage anyway, by treating temporal immediacy as a feature of the perceptible particular: the minor premise on Wednesday is not "yonder thing is sweet" but "yonder thing is sweet *and available now*," while the prohibiting universal concerns the same thing under a description that does not carry the indexical. Is "now" a perceptual particular on a par with sweetness for Aristotle's purposes — that is, can the last premise be corrupted by temporal proximity rather than by appetite? Or does this strain his mechanism, given that the engine of the failure in paragraphs (3) and (4) is desire putting "the various parts of the body in motion," not a distortion in how the particular is presented?

8. A related asymmetry: the agent in the passage is, so far as the text goes, surprised by his own failure; the failure is local and occurs at the moment of action. Modern treatments distinguish agents who correctly anticipate their own future reversals from those who do not, and this distinction determines whether precommitment is sought, effective, or self-defeating. Is there any room in VII.3 for an agent who correctly predicts that he will invert his ranking when the reward becomes imminent — and if there is, could such an agent still be akratic on Aristotle's definition, or would his foresight convert his eventual failure into something else (a settled vice, say, or a deliberate surrender)? Put differently: does Aristotle's model have a place for the anticipatory dimension at all, or is his akratic necessarily unsophisticated about himself?

9. Finally, and briefly: at 1147b14 Aristotle concludes that it is not true knowledge which is "dragged about by passion" but perceptual knowledge. If in the Wednesday case there is no passion to do the dragging, does the conclusion survive with the conative term deleted — that is, can proximity alone degrade the status of the last premise from knowledge to mere utterance, the way drunkenness does in Rackham's rendering? Or does removing appetite leave Aristotle with nothing to explain the inversion, so that the modern phenomenon is not a species of his *akrasia* but a different kind of failure that his vocabulary cannot name?
