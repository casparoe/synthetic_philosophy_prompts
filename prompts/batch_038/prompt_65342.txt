I chair a hospital committee that adjudicates disputes over the determination of death, and I want to know whether our six-year record already commits us on a case coming before us next week.

Our rulings to date:

1. Massive intracranial hemorrhage; no brainstem reflexes, positive apnea test, angiography showing no cerebral flow, diabetes insipidus controlled with vasopressin. Declared dead; support withdrawn that evening.
2. Controlled donation after circulatory death; asystole, five minutes of observation, no autoresuscitation. Declared dead; procurement proceeded.
3. Eight years after anoxic injury; sleep–wake cycles, spontaneous respirations, intact corneal and gag reflexes, no evidence of awareness. Ruled not dead, the minutes recording that his body still governs itself and that our standard has never been awareness.
4. Anencephalic newborn with brainstem reflexes and spontaneous breathing. Ruled not dead, and not a candidate for donation before circulatory arrest.
5. Transected cervical cord, ventilator-dependent, fully awake. Ruled not dead.
6. Absent brainstem reflexes and fixed pupils during barbiturate coma and hypothermia after a near-drowning. Determination deferred pending clearance of confounders; the patient recovered.
7. A patient declared dead on findings like those in case 1 whose body was supported for nine further days at the family's request. The committee reaffirmed the original determination and continued support, describing it as accommodation rather than treatment.

Next week: a man declared dead three months ago on the same findings as cases 1 and 7 — no cerebral flow, no reflexes, apnea confirmed, no endogenous neuroendocrine function, on complete hormone replacement. His body has since healed a laparotomy wound by primary intention, cleared a resistant pneumonia, gained eleven kilograms, and gone on growing. The family asks whether he is still dead, and whether continued support is the somatic maintenance of a corpse or the care of a living patient.

Which of our precedents is the closest analog? More than one rule seems compatible with all seven rulings, so lay out the candidate rules, say where they coincide and where they part company on the new case, give your best prediction of our verdict with reasons, and identify the further case we would have to decide in order to tell the candidates apart — along with what standard we would in effect be adopting with either answer.
