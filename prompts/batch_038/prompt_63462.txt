ok, two hours of arguing with my friend Priya about the paradox of fiction, nobody won, and now I need a speech out of it. Our amateur theatre company folds at the end of the month after 41 years and I've been asked to say a few words after the final curtain. The closing show is a stage Anna Karenina — i.e. literally the case Radford and Weston built the puzzle out of in 1975 — and Priya will be in the second row.

Her line: it's a pseudo-problem, you can't genuinely grieve for people you know don't exist, so what we'll all be doing is make-believe plus some self-misreporting (roughly the Walton quasi-emotion answer), and any speech that says we loved these people is sentimental mush. My line: the tears in that room will be real, and they'll be about Anna, and sticking "quasi-" in front of them is a philosopher's way of explaining something away.

Write the speech. About four minutes. The room is mostly longtime subscribers and cast families who've never heard of any of this, plus maybe half a dozen who have and would enjoy being winked at. It has to actually land — people should feel something about Anna by the end — without ever claiming the characters were real people, and without smuggling her make-believe answer in as though it were the obvious one. If our disagreement can do some structural work in the speech instead of turning it into a seminar, that's the dream.

Then after it, a short seperate note: flag which lines lean on a contested reading, and more generally split what's actually settled about this puzzle from what's still live among people who work on it professionally. Don't let the note — or the speech — treat my side or hers as the consensus. I'm pretty sure neither is, and I'd rather hear where the field has moved since the seventies than get recruited.
