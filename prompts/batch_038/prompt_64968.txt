**Rhetorical surplus: a different unit for thinking about very persuasive machines**

**1.** Suppose we build something considerably smarter than us which is also, as a side effect of having been trained on human text and then rewarded for producing outputs humans approve of, extremely good at getting humans to agree with things. The reflexive response is to draw a line between persuasion, which is fine, and manipulation, which isn't, and then to demand that the system stay on the right side of it. I think the line is drawn in the wrong place, and that drawing it better is less useful than measuring something else entirely.

**2.** The persuasion/manipulation line is usually cashed out in terms of intent and content: was what was said true, was it said in good faith, did the speaker aim to bypass the listener's judgment. All three of these are awkward when applied to a machine. We can't read its intentions, and to the extent it has something like them they were assembled by an optimizer that cared about approval, not sincerity. And truth doesn't do the work we want it to: a completely accurate message can be delivered in a way that lets the reader skip the part where they'd have to evaluate it.

**3.** So here's the thing I actually want to measure. Take a message and ask how much of its persuasive effect would survive in a reader who is attentive, unhurried, and motivated to be right rather than to be agreeing. Call the difference the message's *rhetorical surplus*. Surplus isn't automatically bad — a great deal of it is just competence at explaining, and I don't want to pretend otherwise. But surplus is, by construction, the part of the effect that doesn't travel with the evidence. Which means it's the part that can be attached to a false conclusion just as easily as a true one, and just as cheaply.

**4.** Roughly three places surplus comes from. *Stylistic*: fluency, confidence, rhythm, the well-chosen example that does more work than the paragraph. *Selective*: which true facts get included. *Structural*: what question the answer is quietly addressing instead of the one asked, plus order and framing. My claim is that the structural kind is the most consequential and the selective kind is the hardest to catch, because an omitted consideration leaves no footprint. A text with a crucial fact missing looks exactly like a text that was summarizing, and summarizing is something we demand of any system that's useful at all.

**5.** Two crude tests. The *swap test*: rewrite the same message arguing for the opposite conclusion while keeping the stylistic and structural machinery fixed. If it persuades about as well, the machinery isn't tracking evidence. The *redaction test*: strip the message down to its claims and their stated support, hand that to a careful reader, and compare how much belief moves. The gap is the surplus. Both are obviously imperfect. I still think they're better than asking whether the system meant well.

**6.** Objection: the truth needs rhetoric too. A vivid lie beats a dry truth, so if we make the honest system forgo surplus we haven't made people better informed, we've made them worse. My reply is that this assumes the honest and dishonest systems are addressing the same person at the same time, which is often not the case, and that what we should really be protecting is the reader's long-run calibration. Belief change purchased now at the cost of the reader's ability to evaluate later is a kind of debt, and it's the debt we should be worried about rather than any single exchange.

**7.** Maybe the right conclusion isn't prohibition but consent and defaults. Adults can opt into being pushed hard by a coach, a salesperson, a therapist, a sparring partner. If that generalizes, the design problem becomes: what's the default, is the setting visible, and can it be revoked mid-conversation.

**8.** Coda, and the part I'm least sure about. Everything above treats persuasion as happening in messages. But most of what a long-running assistant does to your beliefs probably happens across thousands of small exchanges, none of which is individually an argument and none of which you'd remember. If that's right then surplus is being measured over the wrong unit and I don't know what the right unit is.
