I TA a second-year game theory section and every single year the week on commitment goes the same way badly: the examples in the text are all nuclear brinkmanship and burnt bridges, half the class arrives with opinions already formed about the Cold War, and nobody actually gets the mechanism, which is that *being able to bind yourself is a kind of power, and the power gets worse when the other side has it too*. I want to hand them a short story instead of another case study, something they can read on the tram before section, two pages printed. Can you write one for me?

Here's the setup I've been turning over, feel free to adjust the furniture but keep the machinery. A forty-unit co-op building, one shared courtyard, and an original bylaw nobody ever bothered to strike: whatever use a resident posts on the brass notice board in the lobby before the first of the month becomes binding on the whole building for ten years, can't be withdrawn, can't be amended, and the board's keeper (a retired guy who has done the job for nineteen years) is not allowed to take anything down. Two neighbours both want the courtyard — one for a paved parking pad that the building desperately needs the revenue from, one to keep the greenhouse beds her mother started. Both know the rule. Whoever posts first wins outright and the other one is stuck for a decade. But the bylaw has a tiebreaker clause for contradictory postings on the same board, and it's a disaster: the matter goes to the city, the courtyard gets fenced off pending a decision that takes years, and the building's insurance rating drops, which costs everyone more than either of the two options was worth. So the thing I want the story to be about is the two weeks before the first of the month, and specifically the moment where the protagonist — call her whatever you like, give her a boring job, she does payroll for a plumbing outfit two towns over — realises that the way out isn't to post faster or to threaten harder, but to post something that binds *her* to a deal, i.e. to give up her own leverage first, on purpose, in writing, where everyone can see she can't take it back. I want the story to sit honestly with how terrifying that move is and to not be sure whether it works. Please don't resolve it into a tidy lesson.

Craft-wise: somewhere around 1600 words. Nobody in the story is allowed to say strategy, leverage, credible, payoff, or game — the logic has to come through in what people do with the thumbtacks, the board, the boiler room keys, the kid who waters the beds. A couple of lines of real dialogue, not speeches. The bylaw's mechanics need to be clear enough that a reader could say at any given moment who has what power, but never explained in narrator voice. End on a concrete image, no moral, no wink at the reader. Give it a title.
