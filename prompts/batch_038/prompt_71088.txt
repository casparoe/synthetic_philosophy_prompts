So I write a column about science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine — mostly AI, with occasional detours into whatever philosophers are fighting about. I'm working on a piece around the "could a trained policy suffer" question, specifically the version where someone points at a reinforcement learning loop and says: look, there's a signed scalar that goes up when good things happen and down when they don't, there's a value function that is basically anticipation, there's a prediction error that fires when an expected reward fails to arrive, which is functionally disappointment — isn't that the skeleton of valence, minus the meat? And the other side says it's a term in a loss function, nothing is felt, you're reading a face into a thermostat.

I don't want to write another both-sides explainer, I've written that piece four times. What I want is a satirical sidebar to run next to it, and I think the truest and funniest angle is the audit genre: what happens when a sincere, competent inspector applies farm-animal welfare inspection procedure to a training run, strictly by the book.

Can you write me that report? Roughly 850 words. It's a completed welfare inspection from an invented certification body — make up the body, the scheme, the document number, the lab, the product name, all of it, and don't name any real people or companies or cite anything, I don't need a bibliography and neither does the joke. Format it the way those reports actually look: a header block with the holding's details and the inspection date, scoring against the usual headings transposed over (nutrition, environment, health, behaviour, mental state), a numbered list of non-compliances with codes and severity ratings, a corrective-action table with deadlines, and a signature block at the end.

The humour has to come from the RL being exactly right, not from the inspector being stupid. He's good at his job. He's applying the framework correctly and the framework is what breaks. So the technical beats I want in there, more or less:

- The scheme scores welfare using net signed reward prediction error per thousand steps, and the inspector has to record that a well-converged agent's average prediction error tends toward zero, which means the metric cannot tell him the difference between an occupant that is content and an occupant that has been trained to expect nothing. He flags this as a limitation of the instrument, not of the occupant, and moves on.
- The replay buffer is treated as retained memory: the holding keeps millions of past transitions, including aversive ones, indefinitely, and the occupant is made to revisit a random sample of them on every update. He isn't sure whether that's rumination or just storage and says so.
- The discount factor is recorded as the occupant's temporal horizon, and the inspector recommends raising it as a welfare improvement, then has to note in the corrective-action column that the licensee says this will destabilise training and cost task performance. A welfare gain purchased with capability, and nobody knows how to price it.
- The exploration bonus is logged as environmental enrichment. The sparse terminal reward is logged as a deprivation issue. Reward shaping gets flagged as a prohibited intervention, on the reasoning that you may not modify the occupant's experience of the environment in order to make the environment tolerable.
- Preference elicitation returns only an argmax, and the inspector records that the occupant's preferences were installed by a contractor and can be reinstalled at will, so any non-compliance can in principle be closed by editing the occupant rather than the pen. He finds this unsatisfying and cannot say why within the form.
- Early stopping gets handled as humane killing and checkpoint retention as suspended holding, and there's an awkward line about whether deleting a checkpoint destroys a subject or merely an outcome.
- And somewhere near the end, the inspector has to record that he considered the cheapest possible compliance route — hold the reward constant at maximum forever, which scores perfectly on every heading at zero cost — and that the scheme contains no provision for rejecting it. That's the one that should make a reader put the page down.

Target the practice and the discourse, not a camp. I want an RL engineer to read it and find every technical claim accurate, and an animal-welfare person to read it and find the audit conventions accurate, and neither of them to feel like the thing they do is being sneered at. Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking, no aside to the reader, and leave the actual question — is anything in there feeling anything — formally out of scope, which the report should state as such in its own administrative language. That refusal is part of the point. Maybe one short free-text observation field near the signature where the inspector drops the register for two sentences and then reclaims it.
