I'm in a seminar on cultural evolution this term, and one of my classmates has been pressing a version of the pathogen analogy. On her account a widely held belief is like an infectious agent: the mind is the host, communication is the transmission route, the item's own replicative interests can diverge from the host's welfare, virulence is optional rather than intrinsic, and immunity, vaccination and quarantine all have cultural counterparts. She draws two conclusions from this in seminar, and it is those I want audited. First, that the spread of a belief carries no information about its truth, so a widely held view may be set aside without being argued against. Second, that the appropriate response to beliefs we take to be harmful is epidemiological rather than discursive — inoculating audiences in advance, restricting transmission channels, containing carriers — instead of persuading them.

Could you work through this carefully? I would like an explicit correspondence table stating what in the cultural case plays the role of host, pathogen, vector, transmission route, virulence, immune memory, vaccine and reproductive rate, with a verdict for each row on whether the correspondence is substantive, strained or merely verbal. Then the disanalogies, sorted into those that actually bear on her two conclusions and those that are interesting but idle; I suspect that the absence of a germ line and of lineage splitting belongs in the second pile, but tell me if I am wrong. I am particularly interested in what the analogy quietly imports: that the host is passive, that acquisition is non-evaluative, that harm to the host and success of the item can be measured independently of one another, and that there is a standpoint from which infected and uninfected can be told apart, given that from inside a belief one apparently cannot.

One further wrinkle. Another student objected that the right picture of transmission is not contagion but reconstruction, with hearers rebuilding an idea out of shared cognitive attractors rather than copying it. Is that objection fatal to the analogy, fatal only to the first conclusion, or beside the point? And if the analogy were swapped for one drawn from mutualism rather than parasitism — most transmitted cultural items, from a language to a cooking technique, are ones hosts actively seek and benefit from — do her conclusions survive, reverse, or merely lose their support? If you think a better analogy is available that does the explanatory work she wants without smuggling in the verdict, say what it is and what it licenses instead.
