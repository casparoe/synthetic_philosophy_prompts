I am a third-year undergraduate double-majoring in philosophy and neuroscience, and I also serve as the student member of my university's Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Committee. This term I am taking a course on the metaphysics of mind whose middle unit is functionalism and multiple realizability, and the two halves of my life have started colliding in an unhelpful way. Our committee reviews roughly a dozen research protocols a term and allows about thirty-five minutes each; the members are a veterinarian, two faculty (a behavioural ecologist and a systems neuroscientist), a senior animal technician, a lay member from the local community, and me. Nobody else on it has read any philosophy of mind, and nobody wants to. Our remit mirrors the national one it is modelled on: vertebrates and cephalopods fall inside scope by default, and for everything else we are supposed to decide protocol by protocol whether the animals in question are owed the same harm-benefit justification. Three groups at this university work with insects, crustaceans, and nematodes, and at the moment we have no coherent way of deciding anything.

The standard criteria that circulate in welfare science look to me like a functional-role analysis in all but name: learned avoidance that persists and generalises, motivational trade-off in which the animal pays a cost in some valued resource to escape or avoid a noxious one, self-administration of an analgesic at a dose the animal does not take when uninjured, protective and maintenance behaviour directed at the affected part, and responsiveness graded across a range of stimulus intensities rather than a fixed reflex. I think the committee has been right to lean on criteria of that shape, precisely because of multiple realizability: insisting on mammalian hardware (an opioid system, a layered cortex, C-fibres) is chauvinism, and it would rule out animals whose organisation is genuinely different but whose functional profile is not obviously thinner. But my course has sharpened two worries that our current form cannot express. The first is the liberal worry in its strong form: taken one at a time, each of these criteria can be satisfied by systems nobody would credit with an inner life at all, since a homeostatic controller trades off resources against damage and a protective routine can be written in twenty lines. So the list looks like a disjunction of separately realisable tricks, and I cannot see what entitles us to assume the disjunction picks out one phenomenon rather than a gerrymandered catalogue of superficially similar behaviours. The second worry runs in the opposite direction, and it is the one that has me most troubled: our form asks only whether the animal exhibits the relevant behaviour, which collapses a functional state that is presently doing its causal work with a standing capacity to do so that is temporarily blocked from expressing itself. Those come apart in exactly the cases where our scrutiny matters most.

So please write the procedure our committee could actually run on a submitted protocol within the time we have, in language a technician and a lay member can follow without any theory behind it. I want the questions in the order we should ask them; a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what merely looks like one; the point at which the procedure terminates in a finding of 'not established' and what the default is at that point; and an explicit marking of which steps are value judgments wearing the clothing of fact-finding, including how much weight uncertainty should carry and which findings require the veterinarian's signature rather than a majority vote. Tell me which of the distinctions above must survive the simplification and which can be dropped for practical purposes, and be candid about where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three live protocols. The first uses shore crabs that learn to abandon a preferred dark shelter after receiving small shocks inside it, with a control group shocked in a neutral area, and the applicant argues that the persistent, shelter-specific avoidance shows more than reflex. The second trains honeybees to discriminate two odours, one paired with sucrose and one not, agitates a subgroup, and tests all animals on intermediate blends; the applicant claims the shifted responses to ambiguity indicate a negative affective state, and one committee member objects that the whole design is an anthropomorphic reading of a foraging heuristic. The third is the one where I expect your procedure to hand me an answer I will not like: an applicant has noticed that every behavioural criterion is by design unmeasurable during anaesthesia and neuromuscular blockade, and proposes that our procedure therefore finds nothing established intraoperatively and imposes no additional burden, with the perverse result that scrutiny is lightest precisely where the invasive work is done. Say whether a properly constructed functionalist procedure really must concede that, or whether it can be written to distinguish an animal whose relevant state is currently causing behaviour from one whose disposition to behave is merely suppressed; and if it can, tell me what evidence a committee is supposed to examine when there is, by construction, no behaviour to examine.

Finally, close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do. I want it to say plainly that establishing the functional role is not the same as establishing that anything is experienced, and I want the strongest version of the objection from someone who holds that the two can come apart: on that view our entire exercise is beside the point, since we would be licensing harm on the basis of evidence that is in principle silent about the only thing that matters. I do not want that objection waved away with a reminder that it is unverifiable; I want to know whether the committee should be told about it before it votes, and in what words. Keep the whole thing to something I could table at Thursday's meeting without anyone needing to read it twice.
