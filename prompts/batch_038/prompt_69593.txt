I teach a first-year graduate seminar in classical Indian epistemology. The students arrive from analytic backgrounds and will have read nothing in the tradition when they get my week-one handout. I want that handout to be a placement instrument: a short case-based battery that sorts them by what they actually believe about how knowledge is acquired, so that I can seat committed opponents opposite one another before anyone has learned the vocabulary in which their disagreement is usually stated. Please write it.

The deliverable is a quiz of nine diagnostic questions plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the classical positions about how many means of knowledge (*pramāṇas*) there are and which candidates are reducible to which. Design requirements:

1. Build from the logical space, not from a list of schools. Open with a compact statement of the space you are working in: the candidate sources under dispute (perception, inference, the word/testimony, comparison, postulation, non-apprehension), and the reduction moves that generate the rival positions — testimony absorbed into inference, non-apprehension absorbed into inference, postulation absorbed into inference, comparison dissolved into a combination of testimony, perception and memory, and any other move you think belongs there. Then justify the order in which you ask your questions: state which items are only meaningful once an earlier item has been answered, and why.

2. Every question must be a concrete case with two or three options that a student can answer from their own epistemic reactions. No question may name a *pramāṇa*, a school, or a thinker; none may invite self-report about a general principle; and the options must be rival answers to the case rather than paraphrases of each other. Sanskrit terminology may appear only in the key, in parentheses after the English gloss.

3. The following cases must appear, in an order of your choosing: a brief unrecognised seeing of an unfamiliar animal before you have classified it as anything, and whether that episode already constitutes knowing; smoke on a distant hill; a stranger at a station who tells you which platform your train leaves from, where you have no independent means of checking them; a Vedic injunction prescribing a rite said to yield an unseen result, kept deliberately separate from the mundane case so the instrument can detect students whose attitude to testimony is source-relative rather than uniform; being told by a forester what a certain word for a wild ox means, then later seeing an animal resembling a cow and thereby coming to know what the word picks out; a man who is alive and unaccountably fat, never eats by day and is never at home at night; knowing that a pot is absent from a bare, well-lit floor you are looking straight at; what changes for you when you discover that a source you trusted has deceived you before — specifically, whether a cognition begins presumed reliable until defeated or begins neutral and requires a further check; and one item about how the number of sources should be settled at all, by counting distinct kinds of object knowable, by counting distinct ways cognitions arise, or by parsimony among the candidates.

4. The key should be a table with four columns: the answer pattern, the position it indicates, the *pramāṇa*-list that position yields, and one sentence on the reduction argument the position is thereby committed to resisting. These must appear as leaves: the Cārvāka single-source view; the Buddhist two-source view, including its principle that the number of sources must match the number of kinds of object; Vaiśeṣika; Sāṃkhya and Yoga; Nyāya with four; the two Mīmāṃsā subschools, which are standardly reported as differing over non-apprehension; and Advaita Vedānta. Do not cite texts and do not append a reading list. Where you are uncertain about a specific attribution or about which subschool is reported as accepting how many, say so in a bracketed note rather than guessing.

5. Close with three pieces of diagnosis. (a) At least three combinations of answers your battery makes possible that no classical position occupies, each with a verdict on why the cell is empty: incoherent, unstable given commitments the school makes elsewhere, unattractive, or merely unexplored. (b) Which pairs of leaves your instrument cannot separate, and for each pair either an additional question that would separate them or an explanation of why the difference is not reachable by case-based questioning at all. (c) A rule for students whose answers are jointly inconsistent. The items are not independent — someone who reduces testimony to inference while refusing to reduce non-apprehension may fall in no position whatsoever. Decide whether the key should assign them to a nearest neighbour with the residual flagged, or leave them unclassified, and defend the choice in two or three sentences.

The handout proper should run to roughly two pages; the key and the diagnosis may run longer. Prose and tables only, no code blocks.
