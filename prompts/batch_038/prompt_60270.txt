I teach a second-year course called "Theories of Truth," and every year, without fail, a handful of students discover Yann Martel's *Life of Pi* and write the same paper about it: the boy on the lifeboat with the tiger, then the hospital transcript in which he offers the two Japanese shipping officials an alternative version — the cook, the wounded sailor, his mother, and himself in place of the hyena, the zebra, the orangutan, and Richard Parker — and the officials, asked which story they prefer, choose the one with the animals. My students then announce that the novel shows faith triumphing over dry fact. This is not wrong exactly, but it is thin, and I want to hand them something better before they start drafting. What I want to try instead is a reading of the book through the Peirce–James dispute over what pragmatism says truth *is*, and I would like you to write that reading for me as a piece of literary-philosophical criticism — you are, for this purpose, a philosopher of the pragmatist tradition who also does serious close work on fiction, and I would ask you to think carefully rather than produce the standard "the better story is the true story" homily that the book's reception history has already supplied a thousand times.

The reason I think the fit is unusually good, and the reason I want your judgment rather than a summary, is that the book's central construction seems to me to generate a genuine dilemma between the two pragmatists rather than merely illustrating one of them. Call it the Tsimtsum problem, which is what I now call it with my students: two candidate descriptions of one course of events, structured as near-isomorphisms of each other, with the same ship lost, the same family dead, the same boy washed up on a Mexican beach, the same officials filing the same inconclusive report. Read through Peirce's pragmatic maxim — the whole meaning of a conception lies in the conceivable practical consequences of accepting it — the two stories threaten to collapse into one claim, since it is hard to name a consequence of acting on one that would not follow from acting on the other; on that reading Peirce is not the evidentialist at the table but the person who says the officials' demand for a difference has no content, and Pi's question to them is a question without an answer to get wrong. A colleague of mine insists this has Peirce exactly backwards: his point, she says, is that inquiry has a destination, that the truth is what investigation would settle on if pursued far enough, and that the novel deliberately forecloses inquiry — no wreck located, no trace of the tiger, no survivors to cross-examine, the file closed — so that Peirce's convergence account is not vindicated by the book but *starved* by it. Meanwhile the officials' objections are all empirically respectable ones (bananas will not bear a man's weight, the carnivorous island is impossible, the tiger leaves no trace, two blind castaways cannot plausibly meet in the open Pacific), and Pi's second story is engineered to satisfy them, which puts the reader in the position of preferring a description because of what it would do to believe it. That, obviously, is James's terrain: a genuine option — living, forced, momentous — where the evidence underdetermines, and where a belief may help bring about the fact it describes. But I am not convinced the novel hands James the victory either, because Peirce also supplies a taxonomy of the ways belief gets fixed, in which choosing the account that is most agreeable to one's taste is a distinctly non-scientific method, and Pi appears to be doing precisely that while presenting it as the more truthful choice. I also want you to take seriously the Peircean point that belief is a rule for action, and to ask whether the book's long middle section — Pi's counting, his journaling, his systematic taming of the tiger, his careful management of rations and water — is doing philosophical work that the hospital transcript alone cannot do: whether Pi's survival habits are the real content of his belief, and whether the two stories differ in the habits they would instill in the person who accepts them.

Please write this as criticism, not as a treatise: faithful in both directions, so that Martel is not reduced to an illustration of pragmatism and pragmatism is not distorted to suit Martel, and so that you are willing to say where the novel resists the reading — including where it seems to be about storytelling and survival rather than about truth at all, and including whether Peirce and James are even the right vocabulary for what Part Three is doing. Do not put words in anyone's mouth: no verbatim quotation from the novel, from Peirce, or from James, since you cannot check the texts and I would much rather have accurate paraphrase; and no bibliography, reading list, or works-cited apparatus, since those are exactly what an unverified model invents. If you name a specific work or date, name it only where you are confident, and keep such naming incidental to the argument. Where the Ang Lee film differs from the novel, follow the novel and mention the difference in at most one line, and only if it bears on the argument.

Structure the response as follows, with these headings in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. **Materials considered** — a short unnumbered preface, about 120 words, in which you list the moments, scenes, and structural features of the book you weighed as candidates for doing the argumentative work, marking in a clause each whether you kept or dropped it and why. This is scaffolding; I want to see it.
2. **Bottom line** — about 120 words: your answer, stated up front, in four sentences or fewer, including which pragmatist the novel's construction favors and whether that favoritism is stable.
3. **The transcript, neutrally** — about 200 words: what actually happens in Part Three and in the framing narrator's material, stripped of interpretation, so that I can check we are working from the same book before you start arguing.
4. **James's Pi** — about 350 words: the will-to-believe reading at its strongest, the genuine-option analysis applied to the officials' choice and to the reader's, and the point at which this reading makes Pi look like something less admirable than the book intends.
5. **Peirce's Pi** — about 350 words: the maxim, the convergence account, belief as habit of action, and the taxonomy of methods for fixing belief; say whether the Tsimtsum problem collapses the two stories as I suspect, and where my colleague's objection about starved inquiry has force against that.
6. **Where the novel resists** — about 300 words: at least two specific places where the reading has to be given up or heavily qualified, and one concession about what the book is doing that is not about truth at all.
7. **What Martel gives the debate** — about 250 words: what the novel's construction supplies to the Peirce–James dispute that argument alone does not, and, in the last two or three sentences, how you would put the result to a second-year student who has already drafted the faith-versus-fact paper.

Aim for roughly 1,800 words in total, and do not pad: I would rather each section be tight and arguable than comprehensive.
