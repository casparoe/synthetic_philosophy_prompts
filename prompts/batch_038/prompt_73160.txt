I'm a second-year student in a public-policy program, and this term I'm in a seminar on institutional design where we've been reading about when, if ever, it is legitimate to abolish a long-standing rule that nobody currently understands. A classmate pressed on me a trade-press book by a journalist arguing for what he calls institutional humility, and though I've only read the one chapter that treats Chesterton's fence and its supposed lineage through Burke and Hayek, it is the chapter my seminar paper would have to engage. I found it rhetorically convincing on a first pass and then increasingly uneasy on a second, without being able to say which sentences are doing the work I object to. My classmate, who is far more sympathetic to the book, says my unease is just the reflex of someone who has been trained to think that any argument for the status quo must be a rationalization. Since I'd rather not cite a distorted version of a real tradition in a paper due in two weeks, I want an accuracy audit of the chapter's central passage rather than a defense or a refutation of conservatism in general.

Here is that passage, condensed as faithfully as I can manage into the book's own order of argument: "Chesterton's fence says that when you come across a fence built across a road and you cannot see any reason for it, you have no business tearing it down; you must first work out what it is for, and only then decide whether it should go. Burke made the same point about constitutions in his Reflections: reformers ought to be required to state the purpose of an inherited institution before they are permitted to alter it, and since they almost never can, the presumption always rests with the established order. Hayek later gave the argument its modern economic form — the knowledge embodied in a long-standing practice is dispersed across many people and largely tacit, so no planner can know what he is destroying when he abolishes it. Note what none of this claims: it is not an assertion that traditions are good. The fence principle is purely epistemic, saying only that it is irrational to destroy what you cannot explain, which is why a progressive can accept it without embarrassment. It amounts to a claim about base rates. Most fences that someone took the trouble to build turn out to have a purpose, so the prior probability that a long-unexplained rule is serving a function you have failed to spot is high, and it rises with the age of the rule. Applied to the current fight over occupational licensure for electricians: because nobody in that debate can say what the old licensing regime was for, the burden falls entirely on those who would abolish it, and since they have not discharged it, the requirement should stand."

Please work through that passage claim by claim and tell me what a careful Burkean would accept, what is a fair simplification, and what is simply wrong. Three things in particular I can't settle on my own. First, does it matter that the book runs Chesterton, Burke, and Hayek together as one continuous doctrine — and can you give me a concrete institution on which the three would return different verdicts, say a rule whose origin we know perfectly well and find disgraceful, or a practice that was never designed by anyone at all? Second, is the move to call the principle "purely epistemic, no claim about goodness" stable? If it governs only what you cannot explain, why does it not equally forbid keeping what you cannot explain, and where exactly does the asymmetry that loads the burden onto the reformer come from — is it smuggled in, or is there a version of the principle that earns it? Third, does the base-rate and age gloss preserve the original argument or quietly replace it, and is that the sort of claim one could check at all? Then tell me whether the licensure conclusion survives the corrections, paying attention to whether the fence principle needs an answer to "what was this rule originally built for" or to "what is it doing now," since I suspect the book slides between them. Finally, I'd like your honest verdict on the disagreement with my classmate: I'm inclined to say the fence principle is a status quo bias wearing a nice story, and he says that is precisely the arrogance Chesterton was diagnosing. Which of us is closer, and what would have to be true for the principle to have content that could actually lose a case?
