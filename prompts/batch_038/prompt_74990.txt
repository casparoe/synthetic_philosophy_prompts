I retired four years ago after thirty-six years as a research chemist, the last two decades of them directing a small group at a national laboratory, and I have since taken up the philosophy of science with something approaching the seriousness I once reserved for grant writing. My reading has led me to the literature on multiple discovery — Merton's contrast between multiples and singletons, and the familiar parade of cases: Newton and Leibniz on the calculus; Scheele, Priestley, and Lavoisier on oxygen; Darwin and Wallace on natural selection; Mendeleev and Lothar Meyer on the periodic system. I confess the subject feels personal rather than academic to me. I lived through the months after the first oxide superconductors were reported, when my group and several others on three continents were converging on the same measurements from the same starting materials, and twice in my career I beat a competitor to publication by a fortnight on results I remain convinced were already, in some sense I have never managed to articulate, in the air. A friend of mine from my departmental days, now a retired historian and temperamentally my opposite, insists the contrary lesson is the right one: that we remember the multiples because multiples make tidy stories, and that the real engine of a field is the handful of results no one else was remotely positioned to produce.

To stop the two of us talking past each other, I have tried to state the disputed claim so that it has a truth value. Let me stipulate a quantity I shall call the redundancy index of a result: the number of distinct research groups anywhere in the world that, at the moment the result was first made public, possessed the instrumentation, the precursor data, and the technical skill to have produced a functionally equivalent result within a year had they turned their attention to it — where functionally equivalent means that it would have licensed the same downstream inferences for contemporaries, not that it would have been worded or motivated in the same way. The claim at issue is then this: for a clear majority of the results customarily ranked as major advances in the physical and life sciences since 1750, had the person or persons who actually produced them died in infancy, a functionally equivalent result would nevertheless have appeared in print within ten years of the date it actually appeared. Note that I am asking about arrival and not about architecture: I am not asking whether the conceptual scaffolding would have been assembled in the same order, only whether the result itself would have surfaced inside the decade.

Give me your credence in that claim, though I care considerably more about the derivation than about the figure. Please decompose it into sub-questions; I would expect at least one term for the probability that the documented multiples are representative rather than a selection artifact of which cases get retold, one for the probability that redundancy indices really were high for the results in question, one for the probability that tacit skill and unrecorded instrumental know-how — the things that never appear in a paper — would have been recoverable by another group inside the window, and one for the probability that counterfactuals of this kind have truth conditions determinate enough for the exercise to amount to more than expression; substitute or add terms freely if you think mine are the wrong ones. Then state how the terms combine into your final number, identify the term to which that number is most sensitive, say what evidence or argument would move it most, and tell me where you suspect my friend's 0.25 and my 0.80 actually diverge — which single term, were we forced to compare notes, would account for most of the gap. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction and no concluding remarks: items one through four should each give one sub-question, your probability for it, and two or three sentences of justification, and item five, which may run to four sentences, should give the combination rule, your final credence, the term it is most sensitive to, the evidence that would shift it, and your verdict on where my friend and I part company.
