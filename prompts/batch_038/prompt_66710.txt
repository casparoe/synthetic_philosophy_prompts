I co-host a small podcast out of a cognitive science department — long-form interviews with people at the overlap of affective neuroscience and machine learning, roughly forty regular listeners, recorded in a seminar room with a bad microphone. Our next guest has become the standard-bearer for a view I'll call teaching-signal valence: roughly, that whether a system has valenced experience at all is settled not by how much reward it receives but by the signed scalar that actually drives its learning — the prediction-error-shaped teaching signal — and that the sign and magnitude of that signal is what pleasure and pain amount to, functionally speaking. She adds a clause that strikes me as both the most interesting and the most exposed part of the package: the system has to represent that signal as its own, inside some self-model, for there to be anything it is like to be on the receiving end of it. She has a slogan to the effect that a lookup table can't be sad, and she's lately been applying the whole thing to language models trained against a learned reward model, which is where I suspect the wheels come off. I have forty-five minutes and I do not want to spend it on her restating the view or on me lobbing consciousness-is-mysterious softballs, so draft me a set of questions that turn on the machinery she's actually committed to.

I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each of them a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give, not a generic and-what-would-you-say-to-that:

- the dissociation in affective neuroscience between wanting and liking — the line of work arguing that the dopaminergic, prediction-error-flavored signal is incentive salience rather than pleasure, with hedonic impact living in seperate small hotspots elsewhere. If that's right, has she located valence in the wrong signal, or can she argue her teaching signal is the liking one?
- agents trained on intrinsic motivation where the reward literally is forward-model prediction error, so unresolved error is attractive. The sign of her valence map looks backwards there. What does her theory say about a system for whom error is the treat?
- replay buffers and off-policy learning: gradient updates computed from transitions the agent hit thousands of steps ago, shuffled and resampled. Does it suffer over old news, and does she need some present-tense condition she hasn't stated?
- the discount factor, and whether a heavily discounted agent has a genuinely different kind of badness available to it — morally relevant, or just a modeling choice?
- reward-model overoptimization: the scalar the policy climbs is a hackable proxy. Which signal is valenced there, the proxy or the true objective that's never available? And what about the fact that during training a language model may never receive that scalar in anything like a first-person way — its a number inside a loss term, computed by someone else's network?
- testability: a behavioral twin with all the same dispositions and the same verbal reports of distress, but whose learning is done by pure imitation while a δ term is merely logged for the experimenters. What does she say, and does the self-model clause buy her anything here or just relocate the problem?

Also give me one question that lets her say what she thinks is most wrong with how this debate gets run: she told me beforehand that most objections she hears come from people who treat reward as something an agent passively receives rather than something its own value function generates, and I'd rather she make that point properly than have me mangle it.

Format, please. Start with The view as I understand it — about five sentences, so I can check I haven't already mischaracterized her before we record. Then Warm-up: one non-softball opener, with two or three sentences on why it works. Then The ladder, which is the main event: the six areas above plus the meta-question, ordered as they'd actually work in an interview, each written out as the question I'd say aloud (conversational, no paragraph of jargon), followed by Most likely reply in a sentence or two, followed by Follow-up. Then In reserve: two questions for if she answers fast and we have time. Then Where you might have her wrong — bullets naming anything in my framing above that a defender of this view would say I've misunderstood, and for the two most serious of those, give me her best actual answer, so I'm not walking in armed with a strawman. Keep the whole thing under about 1,400 words.
