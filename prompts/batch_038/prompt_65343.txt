I run an 80-minute workshop once a term for about 20 people — mostly third-year philosophy students, plus two or three econ majors who drift in for the free pizza — and this term I want to build the whole session around the absent-minded driver. You know the shape of it: two exits off a highway that look identical, he has to take the second one to get home, and once he's on the road he can't tell which exit he's at or remember how many he's already passed. So the plan he rationally adopts before setting off isn't something he can just carry out decision by decision, and the standard sequential-decision advice seems to tell him to sabotage his own plan the moment he arrives at a ramp.

I want them to run into this before anybody names it. Concretely, I want them to come out able to say two things: that the right policy is a mixed one and the mixing probability isn't arbitrary — it's precisely the one that leaves the guy standing at the ramp genuinely indifferent between taking it and not — and that the indifference is a fact about his self-locating belief, about what "I'm at an exit right now" should do to his estimate of which exit he's at. If they leave saying "so sometimes you should just flip a coin, lol," the session has failed and I'll know it.

Use this variant rather than the usual numbers, partly so nobody can look up the answer on thier phone mid-session and partly because I want the arithmetic to have some resistance to it. Take the first exit: 2 (motel, car abandoned on the highway). Take the second: 6 (home). Drive past both: 0 (sleeps in the car at the end of the highway). Before you design anything, actually do the math and show it to me: the optimal exit probability and the value of that policy, and then the interim calculation at a ramp as a function of the exit probability — the credence split between "this is the first exit" and "this is the second exit" given that he's at one, and then exit-value versus continue-value on each side of the fixed point. I want to see that the fixed point is stable, i.e. that a policy above it creates pressure to come down and a policy below it creates pressure to go up, because if it isn't the whole activity collapses and I'd rather find out from you than in front of twenty people.

Then the design itself. What I need:

- The amnesia mechanism. This is the hard part and I don't have a good answer. Real people in a room remember how many decisions they've made, and they'll count off the seconds between cards, and their neighbours will leak information with their faces. Constraints: index cards, one whiteboard, their phones, the room itself. No blindfolds, nobody leaves the room, no elaborate props I have to build the night before. Tell me how you get a genuine "I have no idea which exit this is" out of that, and how you stop the class from colluding.
- The round structure and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, including how many drives you run and how the class converges on the policy rather than being told it. I'd like them to propose and revise the exit probability themselves across rounds.
- A payoff scheme with no real money that still makes the decisions bite. Points are fine but they have to be points people care about, and the scoreboard has to work when different students are playing David in different rounds.
- Whether the driver is allowed to deviate from the announced plan once he's at the ramp. I think he has to be, and the deviations have to be recorded and paid for honestly, because the disagreement between the planner and the guy at the ramp is the whole point — but tell me how to keep it from turning into "you broke the rules."
- A private confession slip at every ramp decision: he writes down which exit he thinks this is and how confident, folds it, hands it in, and we tally it at the end against what was actually true. Tell me what to do with that tally and what pattern I should expect to see if the self-locating-belief point is landing.
- If there's ten minutes left, an extension where the second exit has a landmark that he correctly recognises some fraction of the time — pick a number that makes the point rather than just adding noise — and say what it does to the optimal policy.

Then the debrief: map the specific moments from the session onto the ex ante versus at-the-node distinction, onto what his evidence at the ramp actually is, and onto why randomizing isn't just a way of refusing to answer. Tell me which words to withhold until when — I assume "imperfect recall," "time inconsistency," and "centered belief" all stay in my pocket until the last ten minutes, but tell me if you'd release one of them earlier. And be straight with me about what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this: who derails it, which student type announces the answer in round one, what happens when the coin flips come out lopsided in a sample of twenty, and how I recover each of those without just lecturing.

Finally, an honest paragraph on what this activity can't reproduce and I'm going to have to supply by talking. My guess is the big one is that my David knows the entire setup, knows he's being tested, and knows there is a right answer somebody in the room has already computed — so his credence at the ramp is not the credence of a genuinely absent-minded agent, and the felt tension is partly an artifact of that. If that's right, say what I can say about it in two minutes that doesn't deflate the room.
