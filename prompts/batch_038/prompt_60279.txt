I am in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy, and the second chapter of my dissertation concerns what the dynamic arguments for the structural axioms of rational choice can and cannot establish. Before I circulate a draft to my reading group, I would like a consistency audit of the position I have actually ended up endorsing, because I suspect it is assembled out of materials that do not belong together. Here are the commitments as I would state them in the chapter.

1. *Internalism about rational choice.* The only normative inputs are the agent's own attitudes. An option is irrational for an agent only if some alternative available to her is better by her own lights. No independent metric of well-being enters the assessment, and no critic's ranking substitutes for the agent's.

2. *Diachronic dominance.* If a policy of choice is such that, however things turn out, it leaves the agent with an outcome she strictly disprefers to the outcome some available alternative policy would have produced, that policy is irrational. I regard this as the diachronic analogue of the sure-thing principle, and I regard it as the principle that does the real work in pump-style arguments.

3. *Cyclical preferences are rationally possible.* I am not persuaded that transitivity is constitutive of preference. In multi-attribute choice — trading a small amount of sweetness against a small amount of safety, for instance — I think an agent can genuinely rank a above b, b above c, and c above a, and I do not want to describe such an agent as conceptually confused.

4. *Money pumps mark a normative failure, not merely a cost.* Concretely: an agent ranks a above b, b above c, and c above a; the sums involved are small enough not to disturb any of those rankings; and she prefers more money to less, other things equal. She begins holding a. She is offered c in exchange for a and one dollar, and accepts, since c is above a. She is then offered b in exchange for c and one dollar, and accepts. She is then offered a in exchange for b and one dollar, and accepts. She is back where she began, three dollars poorer. I want to say this is a criticism of her, not a neutral report about an expensive preference structure.

5. *Resolute choice is the right reply to the pump, and backward induction is not to be trusted as a general normative principle.* An agent who has adopted a plan should carry it out even when, at a later node, the continuation of the plan looks to her worse than the alternative available there; and a cyclically-prefering agent can adopt the plan "hold a, refuse every trade" and execute it. So the pump does not show that cyclical preferences are unmanageable. I also want to stress that plan-space here looks well behaved even though outcome-space is not: refusing every trade dominates every alternative plan, and plan-level dominance seems to give the agent everything she needs in order to act.

6. *Local permissibility, in the announced and the unannounced case alike.* In the sequence described in (4), I think the agent is permitted to accept each of the three trades at the moment it is offered, judged by her preference at that moment. I think the same holds in a variant in which she is not told what further trades will follow. I do not think the difference between these two cases is normally normatively relevant: foresight changes what she can predict she will end up with, but not what she may do now. Learning that further trades are coming cannot render impermissible an exchange she prefers to her current holding.

7. *Followability.* A norm of rational choice must be one an agent can apply at the point of decision without surveying and evaluating entire trees of plans. I am wary of norms satisfiable only by agents with unbounded foresight, and this is part of why I am drawn to (6).

8. *An asymmetry among the dynamic arguments.* I accept the synchronic Dutch book argument against incoherent credences as decisive. I am much less confident about its diachronic relatives, because a diachronic argument seems to require that I treat my own future choices as though they were states of nature — exogenous facts about how things will turn out — and I do not think an agent can coherently deliberate on that assumption about her own acts.

What I want from you is an audit rather than an exposition. Please do not survey the literature, and do not give me references or a reading list; work with the commitments exactly as stated above and with the case in (4) together with its unannounced variant.

(a) Identify the genuine tensions and order them by severity. For each, say precisely which two or three commitments cannot all stand, and show why — by working through the case where that is the clearest way to do it. Please do not flag everything: some of what looks bad on this list is not.

(b) Tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what the dissolution consists in. One colleague's immediate objection was that the dollars in (4) smuggle an external metric of value into (1); I suspect that objection fails, but I want it assessed rather than assumed away. Identify any other pairs that merely look incompatible, and say what makes them look that way.

(c) For each genuine tension, give the cheapest revision — the smallest change to the set that removes it — and say what that revision costs me dialectically, including whether it leaves me without an argument I wanted or without a distinction I was relying on. I would rather be told that a fix is available but expensive than be told the package is coherent.

(d) Finally, say which single revision would repair the most, and describe briefly the position that results from making it. I do not want to prejudge which commitment should go, and please do not assume I will sacrifice whichever one turns out to be the least central to my argument; if the repair requires giving up something I have framed as a methodological constraint rather than a thesis, say so explicitly and explain what is left of the constraint afterwards.
