I write historical novels, and, like most who do, I close them with a note distinguishing what the record supports from what I have invented. Reading such notes across the genre, I have come to think they confess one sort of fabrication and conceal another. A novelist will explain at length that a certain housemaid, a certain intercepted letter, a certain meeting never took place, and will say nothing whatever about having supplied a documented queen with her private thoughts, her motives, the sentences she spoke alone in a room — inventions far more presumptuous than any invented servant, since they attach a mind to a real person. And where the record goes quiet, as it does about the enslaved, the illiterate, the colonised, the note presents that quiet as vacant ground the novelist may occupy, rather than as a silence with a cause.

What conception of the past must this convention presuppose? I should like the implicit theory reconstructed rather than merely labelled: why adding a person to the past counts as a departure requiring disclosure, while supplying an interior to someone already on the roster does not; which rival accounts of the historical record the convention quietly rules out; how much of this the practice strictly requires, as against how much it merely sits comfortably with; whether its two halves can be rendered consistent, or whether the inconsistency is load-bearing; and what such a note would have to say if it were written under a conception on which the surviving record is above all an index of who was permitted to write.
