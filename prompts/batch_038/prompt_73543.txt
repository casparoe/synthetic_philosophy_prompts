ok so, my town is turning 175 this year and there's a whole weekend thing going on — parade Saturday morning, historical society tent, a bean supper, and a program on the lawn at like two in the afternoon with folding chairs. There's one slot on that program for a student, four or five minutes, and I got it because I won the essay contest my history teacher runs. He's the only reason I have anything to say, so I want the speech to actually do the thing he taught us instead of just being normal small town graduation speech #47.

Here's the thing he drilled into us in AP Euro. Braudel says there's basically three speeds of history. The slowest is the almost-motionless one — people and their environment, the river, the soil, what grows, how long winter is. Then there's the middle one, the rhythm of economies and societies and generations, stuff that takes decades and turns over slowly. And then there's the top layer, events and individual people, which he treats as froth — the part that sparkles and looks like it's the whole ocean but is mostly just what the slow stuff threw up. My teacher's whole point was that almost every speech and every textbook and every local newspaper lives entirely in the froth.

I want to build my speech on those three speeds, in that order, as the actual structure — not as something I mention and then ignore. Like three movements: the valley and the river first, then the mill and the railroad and the road that went around us, then finally the named people and this specific weekend and this specific year. And it has to end on the third one because that's where the audience is, but I don't want the ending to cancel out the first two.

The town facts you can use, don't make up new ones: river valley, thin rocky soil so the farms were never big, population peaked somewhere over four thousand in the fifties and is about eleven hundred now. Grist mill first, then sawmill, then the building made wooden bobbins for thread for like sixty years, closed in the eighties, now it's self-storage with one apartment upstairs. The rail line came through but the express stop got moved to the bigger town twenty miles away, passenger service ended in the fifties, they pulled the tracks up in the nineties and it's a walking trail now. The state highway got rerouted around us instead of through us in the seventies. Floods roughly every generation — the big ones everybody still dates things by. And the last two summers were the driest anybody remembers, you could walk across the river at the ford in August.

The audience is the problem. Front rows are going to be people in their seventies and eighties who worked at the mill or whose parents did, and for them their entire life is the froth layer, the third speed — the year the plant shut, the summer of the flood, who was mayor when. If I stand up there and say individuals and events are the shallow part of history, what they'll hear is that their lives were shallow. I need the speech to be honest about the three speeds and still land on them as respect, not as a demolition. Also: in March the state rep announced our high school is consolidating with the district two towns over starting next year, so this is genuinely the last 175th of anything, and he'll be sitting right there because he's giving the ten minutes before me. I can't make it about the school closing and I can't go after him, but if I never touch it at all everyone will be sitting there thinking about it and I'll look like I dodged. And my mom now drives forty minutes each way to a job, so I don't want it to be one of those small town big heart things either.

Other stuff: no famous quotes from anybody, not even Braudel — my teacher says quoting the guy whose idea you borrowed is not a subtitute for using it. Don't use the word resilience. No "the future is bright" and no pretending we're coming back. Don't rhyme anything and don't do the three-parallel-sentences crescendo thing speeches always do. Keep it under 700 words. Write it as words I'd actually say out loud to a lawn full of people in folding chairs, in my voice, start to finish — not an outline, not notes with brackets telling me where to fill stuff in.
