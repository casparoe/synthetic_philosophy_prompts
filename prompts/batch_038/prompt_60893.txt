I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and once a year I run a weekend workshop for about ten early-career reporters — mostly people a year or two into local news who want to move onto this beat. The last session is a take-home that I mark, and the top one or two get a paid commission out of it. This year I want the take-home to be about markets-as-knowers, becuase a depressing amount of copy I commission and read treats a prediction market or a betting price like a truth machine, and I want to find out whether these people can actually think about the thing rather than just mutter "efficient markets" and move on.

So: can you write me the whole take-home — the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme?

Build the question around a case I've invented, so nobody can just hand back a lecture they've heard. Use this more or less as written; add to it if you think it needs something, but keep the five facts numbered so students can point at specific ones in their answers.

A regional paper starts an internal prediction market for staff. Contracts pay out on things like whether the mayor gets indicted, whether two hospital trusts merge, whether a named exec has left by the end of March.

1. Everyone trading is a staffer at the same paper, with the same document access, the same contacts list and the same freedom to call people.
2. The paper subsidises it — staff trade with a budget the paper gives them and don't lose their own money if they're wrong.
3. Two of the reporters are both careful, both well-calibrated, both know the other is, and they have swapped every document either of them holds. They still bet against each other on the indictment. Repeatedly. Cheerfully.
4. Volume is high, and prices reliably move before the public announcements do.
5. The editors read the prices every morning and use them to decide what to assign that week.

Then ask three things, roughly in this shape:

(a) What the no-trade theorem and the agreeing-to-disagree result say shouldn't be happening at fact 3, and — given that facts 1 to 3 look like they close off the usual exits — which assumption in the setup must be the one giving way. I want them to be precise about which assumption, not to wave at "common knowledge is unrealistic."

(b) Why fact 4 doesn't license the inference the editors want to draw from it. I specifically want them to handle two things: that the trading could be pure risk transfer or noise and still produce high volume and prices that look prescient, and that a price is not a probability once there's a risk premium and a favourite-longshot tilt sitting in it.

(c) The reflexive wrinkle when you put facts 2 and 5 together — the argument that if prices fully revealed everything nobody would bother paying the cost of digging, so the prices would stop being informative; and that if the paper does pay the cost of digging, the price is partly measuring the subsidy and the assignment habits of the editors rather than the world. What does that do to the editors' claim, and does fact 5 rescue it or wreck it?

For the marking side. It's out of 25, they get 45 minutes, model answer around 700 words. The marking scheme is the part I actually need, so make it the meat: five or six moves a good answer has to make, what each is worth, and the specific wrong turns that should cost marks. I want the wrong turns to be the ones I see in real copy — treating the no-trade theorem as though it were a claim that markets are always right, saying "well, they must have different information" when fact 1 rules that out, reaching for overconfidence bias as a universal solvent, thinking a big trading volume is itself evidence of information, and confusing the efficient market hypothesis with any of these theorems. And then tell me what separates an A from a B by naming the moves, not by adjectives — B answers in my experience get all the labels right and still miss why fact 5 changes anything.

One more thing: keep the prose at a level where a smart reporter who has never taken a decision theory course can follow it, but don't sand down the argument to get there. Define any term the first time it appears.

Start straight in with the take-home question exactly as it would appear on the page, then the model answer, then the marking scheme. No preamble, no commentary, and don't restate any of this back to me.
