My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about David Lewis's short paper on penal substitution — the one that opens not with theology but with a secular case: an offender justly sentenced, and a devoted, innocent friend who volunteers to take the sentence in his place. Lewis asks whether that gives anyone the slightest reason to spare the offender, and the answer we mostly give in criminal law is no. I want to settle the argument by other means, so I would like a poem.

Here is the shape of the disagreement, so that you have the material without needing the paper. Lewis's diagnosis, as I read him, is that whether substitution is even conceivable depends on what function you assign to punishment. If punishment were compensatory — a benefit owed to the victim, or a debt of the kind a friend can discharge for you — then who bears it would be incidental and the substitution would go through. But we do not think of criminal punishment that way: it is essentially the offender's own suffering, which is why a fine can be paid by a friend and a flogging cannot. That is what makes the Christian doctrine look incoherent, since the guilty cannot be punished in the person of the innocent. Lewis's way out is to deny that the substitute is a stranger. In the religious case the offender is not a separate individual standing alongside Christ but a member of a body whose head suffers, and it is the offender's own acceptance — his taking that suffering as his — that makes it his. My friend's reply, which I could not answer then and cannot answer now, is that this rescues the doctrine only by spending something expensive: either persons are not the separate beings we take them to be, in which case what happens is a shared cost rather than a punishment; or, if the acceptance is what does the work, then the acceptance is doing the atoning and the suffering is merely its occasion, which is not penal substitution at all.

Now the poem. I want a sonnet — fourteen lines, loosely iambic pentameter, your choice of rhyme scheme, though the octave should be closed enough to sound like a verdict. The argument must be carried by the grammar rather than by exposition, and here is the constraint that does the work. Throughout the octave, the offender and the friend must remain distinct third-person singulars, two separate men in a room, and the octave should end on the asymmetry between the penalty that can be transferred and the one that cannot. The turn at line nine should not announce a change of scene; it should enact Lewis's move by dissolving the singular, so that from line nine through line twelve the offender is referred to only as part of a plural — a member of something whose head is suffering — with his acceptance appearing as the thing that makes the suffering his. The couplet is then permitted exactly one return to the singular third person, and that return should land like a bill slid across a table: the price of the rescue, stated but not adjudicated. The couplet must let both of my friend's objections be heard at once — that the plural has swallowed the person, and that the acceptance has swallowed the punishment — without choosing between them.

Two prohibitions. Do not use the words atonement, substitution, salvation, or grace; someone who knows the paper should recognize it from what the poem does, and someone who has never heard of it should still find the poem coherent. And do not let the octave drift into courtroom abstraction — I want the physical weight of the scene, the sentence read aloud, the friend standing up, and the plain difference between money and a body.
