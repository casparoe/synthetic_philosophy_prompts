I have spent about fifteen years construing patent claims: office-action practice, claim construction briefs, the person of ordinary skill in the art, intrinsic versus extrinsic evidence, prosecution history estoppel, the specification as its own lexicographer, and the doctrine of equivalents. I am completely fluent in that machinery. I am, by contrast, an amateur reader of criticism: I know the names of the positions in the debate over authorial intention, but I do not hold the arguments in view the way I hold my own doctrine in view.

The positions as I understand them: the intentional-fallacy line associated with Wimsatt and Beardsley, on which consulting the author's stated designs is a mistake because the work is public once it exists; Hirsch's rejoinder, on which a text means what its author meant by it, that this is fixed once and for all, and that what the work comes to mean for later audiences is a different thing altogether; and a middle view, roughly Nehamas's, on which interpretation proceeds by constructing a plausible author out of the work rather than by consulting biography.

My working hunch is that my field already ran this debate to ground and settled it institutionally, and that all three positions can be stated in the vocabulary of claim construction. Restate them in that vocabulary, one by one, as faithfully as you can — using the filing date as the moment meaning is fixed, the hypothetical skilled reader, the admissibility rules, and equivalents reaching technology the inventor never contemplated.

Then work through three pressure points I cannot resolve myself. First: the specification is essentially the inventor's own contemporaneous account of what he meant, and we treat it as the primary evidence; letters and diaries are excluded in criticism as fallacious. What does that difference actually turn on — publicity, reliance, or something else? Second: is the doctrine of equivalents the same move as Hirsch's separation of fixed meaning from growing significance, or is it a different move wearing the same clothes? Third: my claims typically have several named inventors and an assignee who wrote nothing; how does the translation handle that?

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want: what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to make fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Say whether the residue indicts my framework or suggests the literary debate is less substantive than it looks. Do not import my field's premises and present them as results — in particular, claim construction assumes the point of interpretation is to fix a boundary that strangers can rely on, and if the literary debate does not share that assumption, that is a finding, not a bridge.

Commit to a judgment on each item rather than hedging, and where you are unsure whether something genuinely lacks a counterpart or you simply have not found one, say which of the two it is. And if my hunch is confused at the root — if the mapping can only be produced by quietly treating literary interpretation as a way of allocating something, which would make it a distortion rather than a translation — tell me that up front instead of handing me an elegant analogy I would have to distrust.
