For thirty-five years I worked in renal physiology, and since retiring I have been attempting to repair the philosophy I never read. I now convene a monthly discussion group — two former clinicians, a solicitor, a historian, and myself — and this term we have been working through the question of what makes a consent valid. Our method is case-driven: I circulate a short vignette, we argue, and we record a majority verdict with reasons. After nine meetings our record stands as follows.

1. A study described to participants as measuring sleep quality, but in truth measuring compliance with instructions; risks trivial; thorough debriefing afterwards. Verdict: consent invalid.
2. The same design, except that what was withheld was only the investigator's hypothesis; participants were told in plain terms everything that would be done to them and every risk attached to it. Verdict: valid.
3. An operation disclosed truthfully as carrying a four per cent mortality, but put as "ninety-six per cent survive" rather than "four per cent die"; nothing else concealed. Verdict: valid.
4. A volunteer in acute debt, fully informed, accepted a well-paid first-in-human trial whose payment we judged proportionate to its risk. Verdict: valid.
5. A factory owner told his workers that anyone declining to join the firm's dermatology study would be dismissed, though he had no right to dismiss on that ground and the workers knew it. Verdict: invalid.
6. The same owner, in the course of a genuine and independently planned downsizing, with every statutory entitlement honoured, told his workers that volunteers for a study would be retained and the rest would enter the redundancy pool. Verdict: valid.
7. A man with early dementia, whom two clinicians judged able to understand, retain, and weigh what a low-risk study involved, consented; his adult daughter objected strenuously. Verdict: valid.
8. A woman three weeks into a severe depressive episode consented to a high-risk, well-paid trial; she could recite the risks accurately, but the study clinicians reported that she could not weigh them against her own situation. Verdict: invalid.
9. A landlord who had unlawfully neglected a building, leaving it damp and unheated, then offered the tenants a substantial rent reduction if they would enrol in a respiratory study. Everything about the study was disclosed, and the offer improved on what would otherwise have befallen them. Verdict: invalid; our recorded reason was that the choice came from the very party whose wrongdoing had produced it.

Next month's case is one I have drafted myself, and it is the first on which I expect us to deadlock. A company drew down a shared aquifer over three decades. Every permit was in order; on my stipulation nothing it did was wrongful at the time it was done, and the consequence for neighbouring wells was not knowable on the hydrology then available. It is now established that the drawdown caused those wells to fail. No compensation has ever been paid, and the company's holdings are worth a good deal more because it controls what water remains. It offers each household year-round piped water from its own deep borehole in exchange for enrolment in a twenty-year surveillance study — periodic imaging with a contrast agent, carrying a small risk of permanent kidney injury — together with a waiver of any future claims concerning the water. Everything material is disclosed, including the causal finding and including the fact that the study's data will bear directly on the harms for which the company might one day be answerable. The adults are competent and, by the test we applied in cases 7 and 8, able to weigh what is offered. Nothing is threatened. A household that declines is left precisely where events would have left it in any event: dry wells, and water bought in by the tanker load.

As to temperament: the clinicians have sided with the reasoning in case 8 whenever capacity has been in issue; the solicitor presses for a principle keyed to what was disclosed; the historian has proposed that consent fails whenever a transaction leaves a person with fewer acceptable options than they had before the proposer appeared on the scene; and I have generally supported whichever account explains case 9 without thereby making case 4 invalid.

What I should like from you is the following.

(a) Reconstruct at least three principles that account for all nine verdicts without exception. Be explicit about the clause on which each turns — what must be disclosed, what the consenter must be able to weigh, and, most contentiously, against which baseline a proposal is to be measured.
(b) For each principle, state what it implies about the aquifer case, which of the nine verdicts is load-bearing for it, and which verdict each rival must explain away or dismiss.
(c) Name the closest analogue among the nine. I expect cases 6 and 9 to be fought over; if you think either governs, say which disanalogy is doing the work.
(d) Give me the verdict we ought to record if we wish to be consistent with ourselves — and then, with equal candour, the principle we would be adopting if we went the other way, together with the kinds of transaction each choice would license that we have not yet considered.
(e) Assess the historian's option-set principle against our record: which verdict kills it, or how it must be amended to survive, and what it then implies about the aquifer case.
(f) Someone will argue that a household with dry wells can weigh its options no better than the volunteer in case 8 could. Tell me whether that is a live reading of our own precedents or a misuse of them.
(g) Finally, propose the case we should set ourselves the month after next that would discriminate between the two leading principles, and state what each of them would say about it.

Please argue from the structure of the cases rather than from a roll-call of authorities: where a distinction has a recognised name you may use it, but do not build the answer on attributions, and do not offer me a bibliography or a reading list. British English, if you please.
