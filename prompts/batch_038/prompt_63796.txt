I am writing a seminar paper on democratic theory and have got myself into a muddle about the boundary problem — the worry that a democracy cannot settle the composition of its own demos by democratic means, because any vote on who is entitled to vote already presupposes an answer to the very question it was supposed to decide. My tutor has asked us to bring in our own considered verdicts on enfranchisement cases and then try to state the principle lying behind them, which is what I am attempting here. I would like you to do the hard part with me: take the list below, work out whether there is a single principle of inclusion that delivers all or very nearly all of my verdicts, and tell me plainly which verdicts have to go if the principle is to stay simple. I should say at the outset that I do not want references, a reading list, or a survey of who has defended what; I cannot look anything up at present and I am after the reasoning, not the literature.

My verdicts, as concretely as I can put them. A woman who arrived at twenty-two on a temporary work permit, has now lived here thirty-one years, pays tax, raised her children here, and has simply never bothered to apply for citizenship because her old passport is more convenient for travel: she should vote in national elections. A man born abroad to parents who were on secondment, who holds one of our passports, has never set foot in the country, speaks no word of the language and has no intention of ever living here: he should not. A tourist here for ten days, fully subject to our criminal law and paying the same tax on what he buys while he is here: he should not. A nurse on a two-year contract, resident, taxed and subject to the law throughout, who knew from the day she arrived that she was going home: she should not, though I am considerably less sure about her than about the tourist. A five-year resident: she should, I think. A three-year resident: this is the case I keep going back and forth on, and I cannot tell you why. A citizen serving a four-year custodial sentence for fraud: he should keep the vote. A citizen with advanced dementia who has not followed an election in six years and could not tell you what one is: she should keep the vote, largely because I cannot think of any capacity test that would exclude her without also excluding people I very much want included. A citizen who lived here until he was forty, then emigrated and has voted by post ever since: he should keep the vote, though I struggle to say how he differs in principle from the passport-holder who has never visited. A foreign holder of our government bonds who stands to lose heavily if we default: obviously no vote, and this is the case that makes me suspicious of the idea that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in it. A sixteen-year-old: no, even though a constitutional decision taken this year will bind her for sixty years and her grandfather for ten. A person in the seventh year of an asylum procedure, living in a reception centre, not permitted to work, with no realistic prospect of naturalisation: I think yes for municipal elections, and I genuinely do not know about national ones. Nobody not yet born gets a vote, but I do not think it follows that decisions whose only victims are unborn are thereby placed beyond democratic scrutiny, and I would like the principle to acknowledge that asymmetry rather than swallow it. Finally, and importantly: the question of who belongs should not itself be put to a referendum of those already inside, for the circular reason given above — yet leaving it to judges or to treaties makes me uneasy in a way I have not managed to articulate.

My tentative principle, offered for you to attack, is roughly this: the franchise belongs to everyone whose life is comprehensively and enduringly governed by the state's rule, in the sense that they could not realistically withdraw from it without a cost amounting to the ruin of their life. The wording is deliberate — 'comprehensively' to dispose of the tourist and the bondholder, 'enduringly' to dispose of the two-year nurse, 'could not realistically withdraw' to explain why the prisoner and the woman with dementia stay in. It seems to do a fair amount of work, but I can see at least three places where it misfires and I want to know whether they are fatal or merely in need of repair: it appears to enfranchise sixteen-year-olds, whom I want out; it appears to disenfranchise the long-term expatriate citizen, whom I want in; and it appears to enfranchise the asylum-seeker for national elections, precisely where my conviction runs out. So please state the principle, or at most two candidate principles, precisely enough that we could sensibly disagree about particular cases falling under them; say which of my verdicts each clause is there to capture; tell me what they imply for cases I have not listed — a permanent resident who refuses citizenship on political principle because she objects to the oath, the residents of a dependent territory who are subject to our statutes but unrepresented in our legislature, and whether the electorate for a referendum on independence ought to be wider, narrower, or exactly coextensive with the electorate for an ordinary general election — and then be honest about which of my verdicts no acceptable principle can save. Please commit yourself to one of the candidates rather than presenting them as equally respectable options, and finish by telling me what would change your mind: which single verdict of mine, if I surrendered it, would let a considerably simpler principle through, and where you think the weak joint in your own reasoning lies — in particular, whether your preferred principle genuinely escapes the circularity objection or merely relocates it somewhere less visible.
