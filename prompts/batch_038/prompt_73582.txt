I am a seventeen-year-old high school junior, and for the last two years I have worked weekend shifts at a small custom sign and vinyl-lettering shop owned by a former sign painter. Between three and six times a week, a customer brings in lettering that someone drew by hand or generated without any type design training — a monogram, house numbers, a café menu board, a wedding suite, a whole taproom alphabet for a brewery — and before we quote the job I have to answer two questions. The first: is each modified glyph still the letter the customer claims it is, or has the styling taken it out of its category? The second, when a full set is involved: do these letters form one coherent style, or a mixed bag assembled from different hands? The stakes are concrete but modest. If we cut vinyl that turns out illegible from across a parking lot, the shop eats roughly sixty to a hundred and twenty dollars in material plus a Saturday of labor, and any refund or re-cut has to be approved by my boss. So my real job is triage: deciding what can be settled at the counter, what must be refused, and what must be escalated because the question is about money or taste rather than about letterforms. Constraints worth knowing: I have no formal training in typography, my only usual helper is a part-time coworker with no more training than I have, and on slow weekdays a decision arrives as a phone photo and has to be answered within a day. At the counter itself I have about five minutes before the conversation stops being useful.

What I currently run is something I call the two-second test: show the glyph by itself, at final size, to someone who has not seen the design, and if they name the correct letter within two seconds it passes. It fails in three ways I have written down. It passes forms that are only readable because the viewer has already been told the answer — a monogram whose S is a single continuous ribbon that reads to strangers as a five or an integral sign, though the client insists it is obviously an S. It fails forms that are perfectly serviceable inside a word — an a whose bowl has been opened into a straight stem and an arc, which looks like an o alone but is never missed in the middle of a name. And it says nothing at all about the second question: a brewery alphabet in which twenty of the twenty-six letters share a fifteen-degree shear and squared terminals, while the Q and the g were evidently drawn by a different person with round ones.

A librarian gave me a book from the mid-nineties by Hofstadter and his research group, and the chapter that stayed with me is the one on the Letter Spirit project, which tried to get a computer to render the same letter in many different styles on a deliberately minimal grid while keeping each version recognizably that letter. Here is what I took from it, in my own words, and please correct me where I have it wrong: a letter has no fixed checklist of features, because its essence gets realized differently in every style, and membership in the category shades off gradually rather than switching on and off; and style is not decoration laid on top of a neutral form but a consistent set of decisions carried across a whole alphabet. If that is right, my two-second test is measuring the wrong thing entirely, since it treats one naive viewer's reaction at one instant as though it marked the boundary of a category.

What I would like is a procedure I can actually run in a shop. Please give it to me as numbered steps in order, saying for each one what a pass and a fail look like, who is capable of running it, roughly how long it takes, and what to do when it cannot be run at all because there is no second person available or no time. Show me where the procedure terminates in a yes, where it terminates in a no, and where it hands the decision to my boss because what remains is a question of taste or of who absorbs the cost. I want you to tell me which of the Letter Spirit distinctions you decided had to survive the translation into shop practice, which you dropped as unusable, and why — and I want you to flag explicitly which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because my boss will assume that anything appearing on a numbered list is objective. Address the isolation-versus-word problem head on: if the unit of judgment is the single glyph, defend that choice, and if it is the word or the line, explain what I am supposed to do with a monogram that has no word around it.

Then run the procedure on my three cases — the ribbon S monogram, the opened-bowl a in the wedding suite, and the brewery alphabet with its two mismatched letters — and give me a verdict for each along with what I would actually say to the customer. For at least one case, give me the answer the procedure genuinely produces even if it is the one I would rather not deliver: the ribbon S belongs to my boss's own family, we have already quoted it, and half of it is cut. Finally, tell me what this kind of procedure cannot decide under any circumstances, where a shop simply has to fall back on the customer getting what the customer paid for, and give me two or three sentences I could realistically say to a client when the procedure comes back negative.

I am not writing a paper, so please do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I cannot look anything up behind the counter and would not have time to read it if I could.
