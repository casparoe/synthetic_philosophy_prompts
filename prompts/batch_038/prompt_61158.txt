I run one ninety-minute session of a five-day interdisciplinary summer school whose students are graduate researchers in political science, psychology, and public policy. They are comfortable with expected-value arithmetic and most have met a payoff matrix at some point, but only two or three have seen anything called epistemic game theory and none has met a modal operator outside a logic class they have forgotten. My session sits between a morning on expected utility and an afternoon on mechanism design, so I may assume they know what it means to have a preference ordering and to reason about outcomes, and I may assume nothing further. Last year I built the whole thing around a live round of "guess two-thirds of the average of the numbers everyone writes down," and it failed in an instructive way: the room enjoyed it enormously, it was won by a student who had seen it before, and when I asked afterward what they had taken away, three separate people said some version of "so it's about how many steps of thinking ahead you can manage, and the quicker people win." That is not merely imprecise; it is close to the inverse of what I want them to leave holding. I am not asking you to explain common knowledge to me. I am asking you to plan my ninety minutes.

The first thing I need is the dependency structure: which pieces must be in place before the phrase "common knowledge of rationality" can be stated coherently rather than merely echoed, what genuinely depends on what, and where I have mistaken a habitual order for a forced one. My rough guess is that mutual knowledge has to be distinguished from common knowledge before either is applied to anything; that the iterated structure earns its keep only once the students have seen a case in which some finite level of it demonstrably fails to deliver what the full thing delivers; and that the notion of a rational agent has to be fixed before any solution concept is named, since otherwise they will slide silently from "being rational" to "playing the equilibrium" and never notice the slide. But I cannot tell whether that sequence is right or merely comfortable, and I suspect I have been reaching for the centipede far too early, using it as a spectacle before they can see what it is supposed to be a spectacle of. Tell me also which single case should carry the session, and be specific about what that case must have and what it must lack. I am torn between a knowledge-hierarchy puzzle of the muddy-children type, the two-generals exchange of messengers that never terminates, a short centipede, and a coordination problem built around a public announcement with something slightly wrong about its publicity. For each alternative you reject, say what it teaches that my carrying case will then be short of, and whether and where it should still make a cameo. You should assume two students have already met the blue-eyes island puzzle online and will bring it up unprompted, and that one of them is writing a dissertation on polarization and will keep trying to steer the session toward Aumann's result about whether rational agents with a common prior can disagree.

Second, the misconception map, which is the part I care most about. I already expect "common knowledge just means everyone knows, the extra layers are a technicality," "announcing the rules aloud in front of everybody thereby makes them common knowledge," and "if both players are rational and that is common knowledge then they must be playing a Nash equilibrium" — which I take to be simply false in general, and which I have never managed to make vivid without a counterexample too formal for this room. Tell me what else they will bring with them or construct on the spot, and then, for each misconception, the exact step in the sequence at which it first becomes available to them, because a confusion that cannot arise until after step five should be headed off at step five and not defensively immunized against at step two. I also keep a standing policy of saying out loud what I will not explain, since the afternoon instructor picks up whatever I flag, so tell me what to refuse at this level and roughly what to say while refusing it. Please return the plan in six titled sections, in this order: The through-line, in three or four sentences, giving the single thing the session exists to convey so that I can test every later choice against it; Dependency chain, the ordered components with a one-line justification of each placement, about 300 words; The carrying case, what it is, what it must have and lack, and the disposition of each rejected alternative, about 300 words; Misconception map, each misconception with the step at which it becomes available and the intervention that heads it off, about 350 words; The check, one question I can put to the room at the end, with what a right answer sounds like and what a memorably wrong one sounds like, about 150 words; and Refusals, what I will explicitly decline to explain and why, about 100 words. Keep the whole plan under roughly 1400 words, and do not recommend readings, slides, or handouts: I have a whiteboard, ninety minutes, and an audience that has already eaten lunch.
