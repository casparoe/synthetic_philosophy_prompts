Two members of a committee are designing a review panel that will decide, by simple majority, whether a new technology is safe to deploy. One insists that every panellist must be given the same expert briefing pack and the same training in the relevant technical methods: a majority vote is worth nothing unless each voter is individually better than a coin flip, and shared training is the only realistic way to secure that. The other objects that this destroys the very condition that makes majority voting reliable — if everyone reasons from the same evidence by the same route, their errors become correlated, the panel collapses into one mind repeated many times, and a flaw in the briefing pack is not caught but replicated unanimously.

Write a dialogue between them, each trying to win the other over. Both must be fully competent and neither may be permitted cheap points: the advocate of shared training should press the fact that an ill-informed majority is not merely unreliable but can converge on the wrong answer with near-certainty as the panel grows, while the advocate of independent judgement should press the fact that correlated error is invisible from inside the panel, so that unanimity ceases to carry any information at all. Have them work through whether independence is a constraint on how each vote is formed, or only on how the votes relate to the fact at issue once that fact is held fixed — and whether the committee can design around the tension, or must simply choose which failure mode it prefers. Neither character may appeal to empirical studies or to the authority of any named writer, and the theorem's conditions should be argued over rather than recited. Please write in British English.
