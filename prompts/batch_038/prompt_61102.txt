I'm putting together a talk for a small workshop on philosophy of psychiatry and the centerpiece is supposed to be an argument that pushes Hacking's looping-effects idea further than he does — from "classifying people changes them" all the way to "so classification of people can't be validated." Rereading it last night I got the feeling it's a runaway, that it proves a lot more than I want, but I cant locate where it starts running away. Here's the thing as it currently stands:

1. A diagnostic category is scientifically legitimate only if it can be validated — if there are studies that could show it picks out a group that's stable and that predicts stuff: course of illness, response to treatment, family aggregation, whatever you like.
2. Validation studies don't stay private. They get taught in residency, folded into guidelines, summarized in magazines, argued about online; they feed back into who shows up for assessment, how clinicians ask about symptoms and write them down, and how the people who get the label understand and present themselves.
3. So the group you validate isn't the group that exists once the validating is done and public; the extension of the category shifts under the weight of the evidence offered for it.
4. So any validation, by the time it's finished, is evidence about a group that no longer exists. You were aiming at a target that moved before the arrow landed.
5. Something that can't be aimed at a stable target isn't a measurement, it's an intervention that happens to produce numbers.
6. So no diagnostic category can be validated. And since (1) makes validation the price of legitimacy, psychiatric classification isn't badlyvalidated — it's structurally incapable of the thing that would make it scientific.
7. Nothing in (2)–(6) used anything specific to psychiatry, so the same goes for any publicly known classification of people: economics, criminology, education research, public health. Only classifications of indifferent things — rocks, isotopes, quasars — can be validated, because those don't read the literature.

The parts I want to keep are (2) and (3): I really do think the feedback is pervasive and it's the whole reason I got interested in this. I don't want to keep (6) or (7) at all. I think epidemiology is a science, and I think a category can be more or less exposed to this effect, because obviously it matters whether the label is one people are motivated to acquire, whether it's stigmatizing or aspirational, whether the people being classified are the same people reading about the classification, and whether you're classifying by self-report or by something nobody can perform on demand. Also my supervisor read the draft and wrote in the margin: "does this survive being aimed at itself? 'interactive kind' is one." I genuinely can't tell whether that's a devastating objection or just a cute one.

So — which step breaks, and which premise do I have to eat if none of them do? Tell me why the guilty step looked innocent going in, and price the alternatives for me; I can see that giving up (5) and giving up (1) are both on the table and I suspect they aren't equally expensive, and I want to know what each one costs the rest of the argument. If what survives the fix is a weaker but still interesting claim — something like validation is always indexed to a time and a publicity regime rather than being impossible outright — say so, and tell me how much of the original bite is left over. And please deal with the margin comment: is self-application an actual problem for this argument, or does it only feel like one?
