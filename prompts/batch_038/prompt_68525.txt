I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I've got a piece coming up on time's arrow that I don't want to fudge. Write me the essay the piece would be the dumbed-down version of — a real argument that lands somewhere, not a both-sides survey with a shrug at the end.

The question: does the low-entropy past actually explain the thermodynamic arrow, or does it just relocate it? Take the standard package — microscopic laws that run just as well backwards, plus a boundary condition saying the early universe sat in a fantastically special low-entropy macrostate, plus some typicality or indifference assumption spread over the microstates compatible with that macrostate. Is that package an explanation, or a dressed-up restatement of the thing that needed explaining?

Deal with:
- Loschmidt's reversibility objection, and whether it still bites once you've granted the boundary condition;
- how much work the typicality assumption is doing — is it the load-bearing wall or the cracked one;
- Boltzmann brains as an internal reductio of leaning on typicality at all;
- the rival picture where entropy just has a minimum somewhere and rises in both time directions, so nothing is privileged about "the past." Does that buy anything, or just move the specialness into the middle of the universe?

My hunch, which may be wrong, is that the typicality assumption is doing everything and nobody wants to say so out loud. Push back on me if you think that's wrong, but pick a side and defend it. No lit review, no citations, no reading list — just the reasoning and the verdict.
