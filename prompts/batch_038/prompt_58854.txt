Write a dialogue between two philosophers of equal ability: one who denies that genuine moral dilemmas exist — for whom, in any situation, exactly one course of action is what the agent ought to do all things considered — and one who thinks some conflicts are irresolvable in principle and that the denial depends on a trick played with the word ought. I have followed this debate in rationalist-adjacent spaces long enough to know every standard move, so do not reach for the trolley problems or the student in occupied Paris; I would learn nothing from a rerun. Use this case, which is my own:

A physician with a single remaining course of an experimental treatment admits two patients who both need it, having quietly bent an eligibility rule she considers stupid. The violation is discovered too late to reverse. The two claims are otherwise exactly matched — same prognosis, same dependents, same age — and no tiebreaker is available that does not beg the question. She gives the course to one of them. Afterwards she is left with a specific standing duty toward the other: she must tell that patient's family the truth about why the supply ran out, which will end her career, and she must personally fund and arrange the palliative care the hospital will now decline to provide.

Let the whole argument hinge on that leftover duty. The denier's best line is that it is not the ghost of an overridden obligation at all but the ordinary price of her earlier wrongdoing — she created the conflict, so there was never a moment at which she had two live oughts, and the residue tracks her fault rather than any surviving claim. The pluralist must explain why the residue then attaches so precisely to the loser instead of diffusing into a general duty to make amends, and must handle the charge that dissolving dilemmas by prior fault proves too much, since most real conflicts are self-made. Also exploit the wrinkle I built in for this purpose: the very same duty seems to arise in a variant where the manufacturer cut the supply and nobody is at fault. Neither philosopher may appeal to intuitions about the case as raw data; both must argue from the work these duties do and from what a theory of obligation is supposed to explain. Have them press each other on whether agent-regret is evidence about the structure of obligation or merely about the agent's psychology, and on whether the denier's all-things-considered ought performs any function that a consistent deontic logic cannot.

End somewhere definite: either one concedes a particular, nameable point, or the disagreement is exposed as resting on a stipulation — and if so, state which stipulation and what each side gives up by adopting it. Something readable in one sitting, with no scene-setting about the room they are standing in.
