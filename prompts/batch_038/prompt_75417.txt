I am a junior at a public high school, and I serve as the president of our philosophy club. For our meeting two weeks from now I have been assigned to defend a position I do not yet find fully convincing, and I would like to prepare a rigorous case in advance. The topic concerns the hypothesis that thinking is carried out in an inner representational system with a combinatorial syntax and semantics — a language of thought that is distinct from, and explanatorily prior to, any natural language a person happens to speak.

The standard version of that hypothesis treats the inner system as innate and as essentially uniform across our species: one mentalese, shared by every normally developing human being, which supplies the representational medium in which beliefs and desires are first framed and into which the sentences of natural languages are translated. The version I have been asked to defend drops the uniformity while keeping the innateness. It holds that the inner system is innate but comes in a family of mutually unintelligible variants, and that which variant a given child ends up with is settled by the character of her earliest preverbal experience — the rhythm and contour of the speech addressed to her, the statistics of the scenes she witnesses, the kinds of distinction her caretakers reliably track — before she has any public language of her own. On this view, conceptual development is not the translation of a common tongue of thought into a particular natural language, but the growth of an idiosyncratic dialect of thought that then shapes, and is in turn reshaped by, the language the child learns to speak.

Please argue for this dialect version of the language of thought hypothesis as against the standard uniform one. I am especially interested in whether the considerations usually offered in favor of positing an inner language at all — the productivity and systematicity of thought, the apparent fact that we can entertain contents we have no words for, the way reasoning seems to proceed over structured representations rather than over sounds or images — are neutral between the two versions, or whether some of them actually favor the dialect version once they are stated carefully. I would also like the argument to explain what the dialect view can say about the fact that people who share no natural language nonetheless manage to cooperate, deceive one another, and negotiate with considerable success, since I expect that to be the first objection raised from the floor. Finally, please close by identifying what you regard as the single strongest objection to the dialect view that my argument does not manage to answer, and state plainly why it survives.
