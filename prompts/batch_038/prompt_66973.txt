I'm three years into a philosophy of mind doctorate, and my committee has asked me to turn a seminar paper into a dissertation chapter on the temporal structure of experience. The chapter is supposed to take a stand, and after a month of reading I think I have one, though I cannot yet defend it. Working from the color phi case — two differently colored flashes in succession, where subjects report a single spot changing color partway along a path it has not yet finished traversing — I have come to accept the deflationary line: the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories are constructed to agree about everything going on in the head and to differ only about whether the misleading content was fixed before experience or written into memory afterwards, so there is no fact of the matter about when the color change was experienced, and the question is a residue of the Cartesian theater rather than a live empirical issue. I also think this generalizes: to the postdiction cases in the flash-lag literature, to the cutaneous rabbit, and to the subjective-referral result from the cortical stimulation studies, on the grounds that first-person reports about timing carry no information about when a neural event became conscious, since any such report is equally consistent with construction in advance and revision after the fact. Two things have shaken me. I sketched the view at a workshop and a commenter suggested I had simply defined the two accounts to be indistinguishable and then announced a discovery. And a friend who does visual psychophysics told me over dinner that in her lab nobody debates this at all — they debate whether the flash-lag effect reflects differential latencies for flashed versus moving stimuli or an extrapolation mechanism that predicts where the moving thing is about to be — and she suspects the philosophical distinction maps onto nothing anyone measures.

Acting as a philosopher of mind who is equally fluent in the psychophysics of apparent motion and temporal illusions, tell me what would count against my position. Not the generic complaint that deflationism about the timing of experience is unfalsifiable — I know that charge and it is too blunt to be useful — but the particular observations, experimental designs, and arguments that would genuinely cut against it. Work through the candidate defeaters internally first, discard the ones that are merely rhetorical, and show me only those with teeth. For each, say what the world would have to look like; whether the possibility is merely conceivable or already partly instantiated by results in hand; and how a committed deflationist would respond, distinguishing the defeaters such a theorist would concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as beside the point. I especially want to know whether my generalization is more exposed than the local claim — whether the symmetry argument that does the work in color phi breaks down in cases where construction and revision predict measurably different latencies, different vulnerability to masking or interference, or different behavior under probes that catch the illusion in the act before the report is produced. Finally, give me an honest verdict on insulation: if the position has been qualified to the point where no conceivable datum could embarrass it, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or an acceptable feature of a thesis about the limits of introspective evidence.

Format: open with a bottom line of three sentences stating whether the position is robust, locally robust but over-generalized, or in genuine trouble; then give the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, each with a one-line note on what its target would concede. Hard limit of 800 words. No bibliography or reference list — argue from the phenomena and the reasoning themselves, not from who said what.
