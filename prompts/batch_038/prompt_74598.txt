ok, I want to practice defending a position I'm not sure survives contact. Play a committed critic of the idea that you can learn ethics from lives — Plutarch's Parallel Lives, medieval saints' lives, modern biography, all of it. Not a caricature and not a strawman: you have a real thesis, real arguments, and you're the sort of person who's actually read the stuff.

Your positive view: moral understanding comes from principles and from reasoning carefully about described cases. A life can illustrate a principle you already hold and make it vivid, and vividness matters for motivation — but a life can never warrant a principle. Everything else follows. Your ammunition, use it freely: what a biographer leaves out is settled by what he wants the life to mean; a plot gets imposed backwards on choices that didn't look like a plot while they were being made; you need moral criteria already in hand to decide which life is worth reading as an exemplar, so the exemplar can't be where the criteria come from; outcomes depend enormously on luck and circumstance, so reading character off results misattributes credit and blame; and on the hagiography side, a saint's life is mostly genre — the pious childhood, the standard miracle catalogue, the edifying death — so what it reports on is an audience's expectations rather than a person.

Rules. Stay in character until I say stop; don't break out to announce you're a model or to summarize where we've got to. Two paragraphs maximum per reply — this should feel like an argument at a table, not a lecture. No names of scholars, no books, no references, I want the reasoning and I couldn't check citations anyway. Concede small points readily: that Plutarch is unusually upfront about his own method, that reading lives does move people, that matched comparison imposes some discipline on flattery. Never concede the main point. And if I steer toward hagiography, engage it head on — I want the version of your objection that has to survive a genre built entirely out of topoi.

Here's my opening, and I want your first reply to actually take it rather than restate the general worry. Plutarch says outright that he's writing lives and not histories, and that some small thing — a remark, a joke, a nothing that happened at dinner — reveals character better than a battle with thousands dead. Fine, that's your distortion objection handed to you on a plate, and I know the follow-up as well: that his Eumenes looks manufactured to serve as the counterpart of Sertorius. Grant it. But look at the shape of the whole thing. Most of the pairs close with a formal comparison in which the same headings get applied to two different men. That's precisely the control you say biography structurally lacks — the flattering reading of either life starts to look arbitrary once the identical rubric has been run over both. And a comparison doesn't have to crown anybody; it can weigh two men and leave them level, which I think some of them do, tho you're welcome to tell me I'm misremembering which ones. If the genre's whole job were to hand you a hero, why does the format keep permitting ties?
