Seminar thing — I have to lay out where I've landed on lying vs. misleading and I want a consistency check before I do, because I keep flip-flopping. My commitments:

1. There's a principle difference, not just one of degree, between lying and merely misleading: lying involves a wrong that misleading doesn't.
2. That wrong is located in the assertion itself. When you assert you give the hearer your word, and an entitlement to believe you; the liar abuses that. The misleader never vouches for the false content, so there's nothing of that kind to abuse.
3. Blameworthiness tracks the agent's quality of will — not effects, and not the incidental details of how the act happened to get carried out.
4. In the paradigm misleading cases — the evasive non-answer to a reporter's question, telling the person at the door that your friend went to the hospital when she's really in the back room — the deceiver's intention is identical to the liar's: get them to form one particular false belief.
5. Intent to deceive isn't necessary for lying. A bald-faced lie told to an audience you know won't buy it is still a lie, and still wrong.
6. Some misleadings are all-things-considered worse than some lies: a carefully engineered true sentence that lands someone in a much more damaging false belief than a small lie would have.

My own hunch is that 5 and 2 are the problem pair, but I'm not confident. Go through all of them: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each, what's the cheapest fix and what does that fix cost me elsewhere? Then say which single revision would clean up the most. Im not equally attached to all six, and I'm fine being told that one of them is badly stated rather than false.
