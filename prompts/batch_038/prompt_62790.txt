We're doing Children of Men at my reading group next month — the Cuarón film, not P. D. James's novel — and half the group are the sort who used to read the population-ethics threads on Overcoming Bias and LessWrong while the other half just like dystopias, so I want a reading both camps can bite into. The hook I want you to pull on is whether the film actually thinks a smaller population is worse, and worse *for whom*. By 2027 nearly everyone alive is materially fine — the elite have their little walled city with all the pleasures of the past, the state still runs, the trains still go — and yet the whole world is coming apart, and nobody who never gets born is being harmed by their own non-existence, at least not on any person-affecting account. So the film seems to be running on something other than a totalist calculus, and I'd like you to say what.

My own hunch, which I want you to test rather than flatter: every faction in the film wants Kee's baby instrumentally — Luke wants it as a political tool for the Fishes, the Human Project want it to restart fertility, the government would want it as propaganda — and the only people who want it for its own sake are Theo and Miriam, who have no use for it whatsoever. If that's right, the film isn't pronatalist in the "more people means more value" sense at all; it's saying that children are what make the living able to act, which is a claim about expectations and co-ordination rather than about the size of the population. Tell me where that reading lands, where I'm overreading it, and which stretches of film actually force it — obviously the moment the shooting stops in Bexhill when the soldiers see the baby, maybe Jasper's hideaway in the woods, but I'm much less sure about the rowboat and the Tomorrow at the end.

The bit the group will really fight about: two of them think a slow, gentle decline in births with rising living standards is fine or even good, and they say the film cheats by making the decline total and immediate. So run the counterfactual — suppose the world had been told in 2009 that the species would end in 2080, but that everyone alive would live long, comfortable, well looked-after lives. Does the film's collapse still happen? If it does, the badness isn't about aggregate welfare; if it doesnt, then the despair is contingent on a belief rather than a fact, and that's a different lesson entirely. And please dont dodge the Bexhill strand, because it's the sharpest thing in the film: the state is desperate for children while it cages, deports and shoots the people who already exist and are begging to come in, and the one fertile woman on the planet is a refugee the same state is hunting. Does the film's answer to "is a bigger population better?" come out as "yes, but only ours"?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two — a claim about what the film is committed to, plus the scene or stretch that forces it. No introduction, no conclusion, and no plot recap, we've all seen it a dozen times.
