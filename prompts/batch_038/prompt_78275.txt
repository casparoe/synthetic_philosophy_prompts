I am an undergraduate in a senior seminar on the philosophical foundations of game and decision theory, and for our unit on the paradox of backward induction I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute discussion section with eleven other students. Instead of opening with a survey of positions, I want to hand out a single decision tree that sorts the room by what its members actually believe about the long centipede game. I would like your help building it.

The material to be sorted is this. Take the standard finite centipede: two players alternate between taking, which ends the game at once and gives the taker the larger share of a growing pot, and passing; if both pass all the way to the terminal node, they divide a pot larger than either could have secured by taking early. Backward induction implies that the first player should take on the opening move, and the reasoning appears to survive every backward step, yet in laboratory play subjects very rarely take at the first node and games typically continue several moves past the point at which the theory says they should have ended. I want the tree to carry a student from an initial commitment about this game to a named position on what, if anything, has gone wrong.

Requirements for the instrument:

1. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a student can answer honestly without knowing which position the answer leads to. No node may name a view, a solution concept, or a theorist. Answers should be forced choices, two or three per node.

2. The nodes should include, at minimum, questions about: whether the players' rationality is genuinely common knowledge rather than merely true and widely believed; what a player ought to believe about the other's rationality after actually observing a move the theory calls irrational; whether counterfactuals concerning nodes that are never reached are fixed by the game tree alone or partly by the players' beliefs; whether a solution concept must impose sequential rationality at unreached nodes; whether uncertainty about the other player's type or payoffs is a defect in the model or an accurate description of the laboratory setting; whether bounded cognition is best represented as noise in choices, as a limited number of iterated steps of reasoning, or as something that changes what the players have reason to do; and whether the question on the table is normative or explanatory.

3. That last distinction should be structural, not decorative. I want the tree to separate two axes: whether backward induction correctly describes the game as posed, and whether a subject who passes early has thereby made a mistake. Someone who thinks the theory is right and the subjects are wrong should land in a different leaf from someone who thinks the subjects are right and the theory misdescribes the situation, and there should be leaves for the intermediate combinations, including the uncomfortable one on which the theory is right, the subjects are wrong, and nothing of philosophical interest follows.

4. Aim for eight to twelve leaves. Each gets a descriptive name for the position, the ordinary label it goes by if it has one, and a single sentence stating the commitment the position finds hardest to swallow. Use standard terminology rather than attributions to particular papers, and include no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind: the students will be working from the handout alone.

5. Build the logical space before populating it. Lay out the answer-combinations your questions permit, then locate the positions within them, then identify the combinations no recognized position occupies. For each empty cell, say whether it is empty because the view would be incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because no one has thought to defend it. I suspect that the cell corresponding to the view that counterfactual reasoning at unreached nodes is perfectly well behaved but common knowledge of rationality collapses only after the first off-path move is unexplored rather than impossible; tell me if you disagree.

6. Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate, and say which further question would separate them, even if including it would make the tree too long for the handout.

7. The tree must fit on two sides of a letter-size page and be readable as a simple indented outline.

Two further notes. My own working view is that everything turns on the epistemic significance of an unexpected move, and I want the instrument to be capable of telling me I am confused rather than quietly confirming me, so please do not arrange the branches to funnel answerers toward that node. Finally, if you judge any of the questions I have specified to be a poor node, either because it collapses into another or because it begs the question against one of the leaves, say so explicitly and propose a replacement rather than including it in silence.
