**Role and task.** You are a philosopher of mind with two unusual areas of overlap: you work on the epistemology of mind-attribution (what behavior can and cannot license as evidence of mentality), and you are fluent in the tradition of informal logic and the theory of evidence. You are being consulted on a reasoning pattern, not on a doctrine. Please think carefully and at length before answering; the deliverable is a piece of conceptual carving, and I care far more about the boundaries of the thing than about what it ends up being called.

**Who I am and why I am asking.** I am a comparative-cognition researcher, not a philosopher, though I read some. Over roughly fifteen years of sitting in seminars, reviewing papers, and arguing with colleagues across three different subfields, I have kept encountering one specific move. I have never found it named anywhere, and the standard taxonomy of informal fallacies that I looked through does not seem to contain it. I want to know whether it has an established home in the literature, and if it does not, I want it defined properly.

**The pattern, as I see it.** A person holds that some behavior is evidence of a mind. A mechanistic account of that behavior then appears — a learning model, a retrieval account, a cue-based explanation — and the person withdraws the mental attribution immediately and completely. So far this looks like ordinary good inference: a rival explanation does the work, so the target hypothesis loses support. But the withdrawal has three further features that recur together, in the same people, across unrelated cases.

First, it is retroactive. The person does not say they were wrong to attribute; they say the mentality was never there and that the attribution was an illusion. Second, the illusion itself becomes fuel: the earlier attribution is offered as an instance of how readily people project minds onto things that lack them, and is used to lower the baseline for the whole category going forward. Third, and this is the part that puzzles me, the direction reverses wherever no mechanistic account is on offer. For a capacity that nobody has yet modeled, the same person says the performance cannot be explained mechanically and that this is precisely why mentality must be attributed. The evidential weight of behavior is thus set to zero exactly when a rival explanation exists and to something close to decisive exactly when none does. Asked what would count as a rival explanation in the unmodeled case, the person typically has no candidate and says that the absence of one is itself informative.

**Three instances.** (1) A colleague spent years describing a corvid's solution to a novel displacement task as understanding the situation. When a modeling paper reproduced the performance from simple reinforcement over perceptual cues, she said the bird obviously had no understanding at all, and she cited her own earlier description as a textbook case of how easily researchers over-read minds into animals. Two years later, on a task with no such model, she said the performance could not be accounted for by simple processes and that something like reasoning must be involved. (2) A clinician friend says that when a chatbot produces a formulation of a patient's difficulty that is, on the page, indistinguishable from a good one, it is merely rearranging material it has already encountered and involves no clinical insight; when a colleague produces the same formulation, it is intuition built on years of cases. He will not identify any feature of the outputs that distinguishes them. The difference, he says, is in how they were produced — and when I ask whether a production process he could not describe would still count, he says we would recognize insight if we saw it, and this is not it. (3) At fifteen I was sure that a program which beat a grandmaster must understand chess. I am now sure it does not, and I think anyone who says otherwise is being fooled by the moves. I am equally sure that I understand chess, and I would not defend that claim by pointing at my moves.

**Nearby things I have already considered, so you can see where the gap is.** I know the general complaint that appeals to the unexplained are appeals to ignorance, and I know the historical argument that irreducible "life force" kept retreating as biology advanced, which I have seen deployed against people who think consciousness is different in kind. I also know that there is a respectable tradition on which mentality is a posit justified by its explanatory role, and a rival tradition on which the whole inference from behavior to inner states is misconceived. None of these seems to be my pattern: the pattern is not over-attribution, not the retreat of a single posited property, and not a theory of what mind-attribution is. It is a rule about when behavior counts as evidence at all, and the rule appears to be chosen after the fact, case by case, in the direction that keeps the conclusion safe.

**What I want from you.** Please begin with an explicit brainstorming stage, visible in your answer, in which you list the candidate established concepts that might already cover this — from the informal-fallacy tradition, from the philosophy of mind, and from the theory of evidence and reasoning under uncertainty — and for each one state precisely where the description fits and where it does not. Do not skip this stage or compress it into a sentence; I want to see the near misses and why they are near misses. Then give me all of the following.

1. A verdict on naming: is there an established concept, or a small cluster, that covers the pattern as I have described it including all three of its features? If yes, name it and explain which of my three features it already captures and which it leaves out. If no, say so plainly and then coin a term, with a definition tight enough that a reader could apply it to a new case without further guidance.
2. The central question, and the one I most want effort spent on: is this pattern ever a legitimate inference rather than a defect? Lowering confidence in a mental attribution when a mechanistic rival appears is unobjectionable; so is treating unexplained competence as suggestive. Taken one step at a time, nothing looks wrong. Explain what, if anything, makes the whole bad, and state the conditions under which a differential evidential standard of this shape is warranted rather than question-begging. I suspect the answer turns on whether the standards are fixed in advance or adjusted in response to what turns up, and on whether the same person applies them symmetrically to cases they care about and cases they do not — but I want your own account, argued, not my guess echoed back.
3. A set of operational markers: things visible from inside an argument — kinds of question to ask, kinds of response that indicate which side of the line you are on — that would let a referee or a reader distinguish a genuine differential standard from a conclusion-protecting one. These should be usable by someone who is not a philosopher.
4. The edges. Give me at least three cases that superficially resemble the pattern but are not instances of it, and say why. Include, if you can, a version of the anti-reductionist position about consciousness that is honestly not this pattern — that is, a way of holding that some capacities resist mechanistic explanation without making the evidential standard hostage to the current state of the modeling literature.
5. My instance (3), since I am the one exhibiting it: is the asymmetry between what I say about the program and what I say about myself a case of the pattern, or is there an available difference between first-person and third-person attribution that dissolves it? Answer directly even if the answer is unfavorable to me.

**Constraints on your answer.** Write in clear prose with numbered sections matching the above. Do not survey the problem of other minds; I am not asking for background and I already know the general shape of the debate. Do not produce a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I cannot check them and they add nothing here. Where you attach a technical term to a particular author, do so only if you are confident of the attribution; otherwise present the term on its own. Aim for depth over breadth, and where you are uncertain, mark the uncertainty explicitly rather than smoothing it over.
