My brother and I argued until two in the morning last Tuesday about the dark room objection to the free energy principle, and we ended somewhere neither of us had intended. I held that the objection is dissolved by prior preferences: an agent that expects of itself that it will be warm, fed, upright, and among familiar things finds a dark room intolerable not as a matter of taste but because it contradicts what the agent expects itself to be. He held that what actually rescues the account is the epistemic term in expected free energy — that a policy is scored partly by how much uncertainty it resolves, so a sealed, unchanging room is rejected for being informationally barren, and curiosity is load-bearing rather than decorative. We went around this circle perhaps a dozen times, and somewhere near midnight I noticed that we had stopped describing mechanisms and started describing two men.

So I would like you to write the short story our argument should have been, roughly two thousand words, set at two fire lookout stations on neighboring peaks in the final season such stations were still staffed. One lookout is my brother's man: he cannot leave a question unresolved, and he will spend a week walking out to the same corner of his catwalk, changing his angle to parallax a thing he has seen, trading the clarity of his nights for the settling of a doubt. The other is mine: his day is built entirely out of expectations about himself — the bed made before the coffee, the weather entered at the same four hours, the same two shirts — and the world, sampled only in this narrow way, obliges him almost perfectly.

On the same afternoon, at the same bearing, both of them see something. The story is about what each does next, and what each is subsequently capable of seeing.

Four requirements, and they matter more to me than the setting does. First, the thing on the horizon must never be identified — not in the last line, not in a hint, not by the reader's inference from accumulated detail; what is at issue is the handling of the signal, not its content, and the ending still has to feel like an ending rather than a shrug. Second, the story must find room for all four ways of closing the gap between what a man expects and what he receives: revising the expectation, moving so that the world matches it, deciding the mismatch was a smudge on the glass or a floater in the eye and letting it go, and simply declining to look. At least one character must take the third route and at least one the fourth, and the fourth must work — visibly, for a while, with a real comfort to it — before it costs him anything. Third, no jargon: none of the vocabulary of the theory may appear anywhere, and no character may expound, allude to, or be shown reading it; the ideas have to be carried entirely by furniture, weather, procedure, and behavior. Fourth, at least three of the story's turns must be delivered through entries in the official observation log, where the bland required fields — hour, visibility, wind, whether there is smoke to report — do work that dialogue would otherwise be tempted to do.

Above all, take a side. I want the finished story to settle the argument between my position and my brother's rather than merely illustrate it, and I want a reader who has never encountered the debate to be able to say which of the two men the events vindicate and exactly which scene does the vindicating. If it turns out to be him, so be it; I would rather lose to a story than win by omission.
