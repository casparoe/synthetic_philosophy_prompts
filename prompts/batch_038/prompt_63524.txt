I came at this from practice rather than from the literature — years on committees, and later mediating disputes between partners in small firms — and only afterward discovered that what I had formed a view about has a name. What I want now is for the view to be tested, not reported back to me.

The question is what the difference amounts to between several people doing one thing together and several people doing their own things alongside one another. The familiar accounts, as far as I follow them, locate the difference inside the participants: a shared plan whose sub-plans mesh, an intention each holds with a plural subject, a primitive we-mode. My instinct runs the other way.

My tentative principle: what makes an activity joint is not a state of anyone's mind but a standing entitlement to repair. People act together just in case, when the shared course of action begins to come apart, each participant is entitled to require of each of the others that they get it back on track, and each is answerable to the others for their own part in its coming apart. Three refinements I want examined rather than assumed. The entitlement is practical rather than moral: it is exercised in a look, a word, a shrug, an exasperated silence, and it need never actually be exercised at all. It must run in both directions: a party who can only be demanded of is an instrument, and a party who can only make demands is a superior, and neither is a participant. And it is directed at the maintenance of the activity, not at the value of what the activity produces. I am aware this looks like a cousin of the view that grounds collective agency in a joint commitment and in the parties' consequent right to rebuke one another. The difference matters to me: I want the entitlement constituted by the practice of repair itself, not by any act of committing, precisely so that it can arise when nobody has decided anything.

My verdicts:

1. Two strangers in a cafeteria both lunge for a tray a child has knocked off the counter; between them they steady it; the milk survives; they walk off without speaking. Nothing beforehand, nothing afterward, four seconds in all, and no moment at which either could have rebuked the other for anything. I am certain this is two people doing one thing together.

2. A faculty committee of nine issues a report whose central recommendation no member would endorse if asked individually, and several privately think wrong. Every one of them says without strain that we recommended it, and the chair routinely tells drifting members that the committee needs them back at the table. Clearly joint, and the cleanest case I have for my principle.

3. A couple have made Friday dinner together for twenty-two years. It has never been planned, agreed, or discussed; if one is not in the kitchen by six the other is surprised and faintly aggrieved. Joint, obviously, and here the entitlement to repair is real but has essentially never been exercised in words.

4. A stadium crowd runs a wave. When I stay seated my neighbors hiss, elbow me, and gesture at me to stand: the repair entitlement is exercised loudly and publicly. Yet I do not think the wave is an action of the crowd; I think it is sixty thousand separate amusements that happen to interlock. My principle says otherwise and I am not prepared to give up the verdict.

5. A flash mob. Three hundred strangers rehearse separately, by video, over six weeks, and perform once in a station concourse. At the moment of performance nobody present has any standing over anybody else, and afterward they disperse permanently. I want to call the performance joint action, which seems to force me to locate the entitlement in six weeks of rehearsal that were not the action at all.

6. Two climbers roped together on a face. One has decided that if the other slips he will cut the rope. Every outward mark of the entitlement is in place; the commitment is absent. My verdict is disjunctive and I suspect it is unsound: it is joint action, but void, the way a promise made with the fingers crossed is a promise.

7. A mob dragging a man out of a jail. Mutual belief is total, everyone intends that we kill him, and the repair norms are ferocious — the man who hesitates is shoved, jeered, and named a coward. My principle says joint action and I agree. But I am not satisfied, because it seems able to register the jointness only by treating the mob's norms as entitlements, and I do not think they are entitled to anything. I want to know whether 'entitled' in my principle is doing internal work, meaning entitled by the participants' own lights, and whether that is a defect.

8. A person drafting a document with a language model that never refuses, never pushes back, and accepts every correction cheerfully. Not joint action; rebuking it is like rebuking a stuck drawer. But a shepherd and a well-trained dog on a difficult field: the dog can be rebuked and does, in its way, demand. I want to say that one is joint. These two verdicts sit badly together and I cannot see what separates them on my own account.

9. The one I keep circling. A long-married couple each privately believe the other would rather not host the annual dinner, and each therefore hosts it for the other's sake. Everything about the activity is jointly run — the shopping divided, the failures repaired, the reproaches made and taken. But the end they share is false in both directions. Is this joint action, or a well-organized mutual misunderstanding that merely looks like it?

What I want back is an account. State a principle — or two or three rival candidates, if the material genuinely splits that way — precisely enough that its clauses could be argued about, and tag each clause to the case or cases that force it on you. Then give the resulting verdicts on five cases I have not listed: a jury returning a unanimous verdict after a holdout has been worn down; a congregation whose members each take themselves to be doing what God requires rather than what the others expect; a strike line whose members stay out under threat of expulsion from the union; a parent and a four-year-old playing pirates; and two people who have played one game of correspondence chess a week for thirty years and met only once. Say plainly which of my nine verdicts your account cannot keep, and for each whether it must be overturned or merely re-described. Where I have offered a disjunctive verdict (case 6) or a pair that ought to match and does not (case 8), tell me whether the disjunction is sustainable or a symptom of something wrong upstream.

Finally, make a judgment I have been unable to make. There is a simpler principle in this neighborhood — roughly, that joint action requires each participant's contribution to be governed by a standing expectation the others can enforce across a stretch of time during which the activity could degrade — and it costs me the cafeteria tray and probably the flash mob, while disposing of the wave and the chatbot in a single stroke. My own account saves more verdicts at the price of several clauses whose only apparent justification is the verdicts they save. Tell me which is the better account and why, and do not settle the matter by observing that each has something to recommend it.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a register of who holds what. If a familiar position turns out to coincide with one of your candidates, note it in passing and move on; I care about the account.
