Jaspers splits guilt into four kinds — criminal (prosecutable violations), political (liability that falls on you just as a member of a state), moral (your own conduct and attitudes, judged by conscience rather than a court), and metaphysical (a failure of human solidarity; being among the living while others were killed). I want to stress test whether the scheme actually earns all four parts, using three cases instead of the usual one:

(A) A country held a colony for about 90 years, extracting resources and forced labor. Its present-day citizens are on average much better off because of it, but almost none of them endorsed the arrangement or had any say in it, and a large share are descendants of post-independence immigrants.
(B) A country whose citizens overthrew the regime that committed the atrocities — a good number of the overthrowers were imprisoned or killed by it — but the state that emerged inherited the same institutions, treasury, borders, and treaties.
(C) A country that was neither perpetrator nor beneficiary: its people knew little, gained nothing measurable, and had no realistic capacity to intervene.

For each of the four catagories, give a 0-10 rating of how much guilt attaches to the present-day citizens in each case, so twelve numbers total. Then rank the four by how much discriminating work they actually do across (A)–(C): which ones move between the cases, and which just sit at the same value no matter what you feed them. If one turns out to be redundant, or to be doing all the work while the others decorate, say which and why.
