You are a political philosopher specialising in constitutional theory and the philosophy of legitimacy. Please think carefully. Before settling on a structure, spend a short opening section brainstorming the candidate theories each design might presuppose, and only then argue.

Consider two long-settled features of democratic constitutional design. In most parliamentary systems the head of state and the head of government are two different people: the official who actually governs holds office only for as long as the legislature tolerates it, and in several countries (Germany since 1949, Spain, and a number of others since) the legislature may withdraw that tolerance only by electing a replacement in the very same vote; meanwhile a monarch or ceremonial president who embodies the state serves for life or a long term, can be removed only for grave wrongdoing if at all, and bears no responsibility whatever for policy. In presidential systems the same individual occupies both roles, for a fixed term that no political body can shorten, the only early exit being an impeachment trial whose grounds are typically framed as offences rather than as loss of support or plain incompetence.

What account of political legitimacy, and of what representation consists in, does each of these arrangements presuppose? Distinguish what each design strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say which rival accounts each one quietly forecloses — in particular, whether the parliamentary arrangement commits us to the view that governing authority is a standing delegation, revocable at any moment by the body that conferred it, and whether the presidential arrangement commits us to the view that a direct electoral mandate creates a non-revocable status, such that ending it early can only be recast as a finding that the holder forfeited it. Then assess the internal coherence of each: can a single office really be at once the impersonation of the whole polity, which cannot be blamed, and the partisan chief executive, who must be? Finally, suppose a reformer introduced what we might call constructive impeachment — a legislature could remove a sitting president mid-term by supermajority, but only by naming in the same vote a successor to serve out the remainder of the term. Which of the two presuppositions would that preserve and which would it abandon, and would the resulting system still be presidential at all, or would the underlying theory of authority have been swapped out while the label stayed the same?

Write in British English, and keep the analysis tight and argumentative rather than encyclopaedic.
