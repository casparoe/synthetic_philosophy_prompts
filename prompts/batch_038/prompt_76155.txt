My brother and I ran a small design studio for eleven years and we're dissolving it — he keeps the anchor client and the name, I keep the equipment, the freelance book, and most of the cash. Four months of misery. What actually got us unstuck was a spreadsheet, because I went down a rabbit hole on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution and now I won't shut up about it. The rule I've been pushing on him and on the mediator: plot the achievable payoff pairs, mark the walkaway point where we just litigate and both end up worse off, mark each of us's best-possible payoff subject to the other getting at least their walkaway, draw the straight line from the walkaway point to that ideal point, and take where it meets the frontier. The mediator wanted to maximize the product of our gains over the walkaway instead, and I argued them out of it on the strength of one axiom that seems to me just self-evidently right: if my best case gets better and nothing about his situation changes, I shouldn't end up doing worse. Nobody should be penalized because the pie grew on their side.

I want the opposite of evangelism now. Where is this view exposed? Not a comparison of the two solutions — I can recite that.

The ideal point is what my whole argument hangs on, and it also looks like the easiest number for my brother to inflate. Can he actually game it? What would he have to do — turn down a real offer, credibly threaten to torch an asset, walk into a worse outcome for himself — and does the fact that he can move my share by manipulating an option nobody would ever pick count as a knockdown objection, or is that just the price of a rule that looks at the whole feasible set instead of the chosen point? Relatedly, I worked out that when the frontier is a straight line, Kalai–Smorodinsky and the product rule land on exactly the same spot, halfway from the walkaway to the ideal in the proportional sense, whatever the slope. Is that right? And if it is, has my crusade been about nothing, since our frontier is close to straight?

Also: what does dropping independence of irrelevant alternatives commit me to here that I might not be able to live with — give me a case in our actual situation where a dead option changes the split. Does the endogeneity of the disagreement point hurt my rule more than it hurts the Nash one? Is there any bargaining procedure that actually lands on Kalai–Smorodinsky, or is it just asserted?
