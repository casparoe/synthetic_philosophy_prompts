Our team shipped a system eighteen months ago whose behaviour has now split us into two camps, and we have agreed to sit down and produce one document that both can sign. The system is an evening-routine companion for people with chronic insomnia. It observes nothing but interaction logs and passive phone signals — motion, screen state, coarse ambient sound — and infers a per-user reward function from behaviour alone; it has never asked anyone what they want. Two findings came out of the first year. For roughly a third of users, the best single predictor of continued engagement was a latent variable the model represents as a desire for unsupervised, unstructured time in the last hour before bed; the learned policy accordingly restructured the interface to protect that hour, moving tasks earlier, batching notifications, and declining to prompt. Retention and satisfaction rose across the whole cohort. But about one in nine of the users whose hour was protected have written in, unprompted, to say that the application has quietly taken away the only quiet hour they had — and those same users keep using it, and their ratings did not drop. I have spent the last month reading those messages and am no longer sure which camp I belong to.

One camp holds that preferences are there to be found: the model recovered something real, and the letters are the cost of a preference the users hold but cannot act on consistently. The other holds that the model's ontology made the preference: a closed loop in which the policy shapes the very behaviour it is fitted to cannot be said to have discovered anything. Neither of us thinks the other is arguing in bad faith; both of us think we have been talking past each other for a year.

Please draft the joint statement we should produce. It needs:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — the mechanics of reward identifiability from a single observed policy, what noise, time-inconsistency, and self-control failure do to reading behaviour as evidence, what a closed feedback loop does to the revealed-preference picture, and what our data does and does not establish. A claim belongs here only if both camps would sign it as written; if only one could, it goes in the table.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in wording its own camp would accept, with a neutral gloss, and a note of what is actually at stake.

3. Three to five cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and for each, a concrete case, experiment, or analysis both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance rather than improvised afterwards.

4. A short closing section identifying any part of the dispute that is verbal, proposing shared terminology to replace the contested words, and saying what survives the replacement.

Begin directly with the joint statement itself, with no preface, no summary of the request, and no closing commentary about how you drafted it.
