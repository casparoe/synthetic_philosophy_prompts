Five years ago I joined a large AI research lab as an engineer, and for most of that time I ran the team that produced the human judgment data our systems were trained against: the rankings of candidate answers, the written rationales, the reviewer guidelines, the whole apparatus for turning approval into a number. Friday is my last day, and I have been asked to say a few words at the all-hands. There will be about 120 people in the room. Roughly half are researchers and engineers, eleven of whom reported to me; the rest work in policy, legal, recruiting, finance, facilities, and operations, and have never read a technical document in their lives. My partner will be there. Everyone expects a goodbye, and it is supposed to be one.

What I want to do is make a single argument — the argument that over about eighteen months hardened into the reason I am leaving — and deliver it as a gift rather than as a parting shot. Here it is, badly put, for you to sharpen. I came to think we had described our own goal in a way that was wrong in a practically consequential sense. We said we were trying to get these systems to share our values, to converge on what people approve of, and we built an enormous machine for measuring approval. But when I looked closely at where the systems actually failed, almost none of the failures were failures of approval-prediction. They were failures to work out what the particular person in front of them was trying to get done. Those are different problems, and the second is far narrower than the first: it concerns correctly interpreting and then pursuing one person's present aim in one situation, and it can in principle be checked case by case by asking that person. The first concerns a standing valuation that has to be right in advance and in general, and about which nobody can say whose values are the ones being aimed at. A system can be superb at predicting what a rater will like and still be hopeless at the second thing, because the rater is not the person who will later be trying to get something done. And a system can be very good at the second thing without holding anything that deserves the name of a value — the way a good interpreter, or a good chief of staff, is.

I believe this. The talk should believe it too: no visible throat-clearing about whether I might be mistaken.

Hard constraints:

- Six minutes aloud, roughly 850 to 950 words, in sentences a person can actually say while standing up.
- Not one term of art. The words "alignment," "value alignment," "intent alignment," "AGI," "reward model," "preference data," and "mesa-optimizer" are banned, as is any device that smuggles them back in by defining them on the spot for the room. The distinction has to be carried by an example, an image, or a piece of my own history — never by vocabulary.
- No names of any kind: no people, teams, products, papers, or companies. I want to reuse this text elsewhere, and I want the lab to be unrecognizable in it.
- Exactly one moment of humor, and it must be at my expense.
- The concession must be built so that a colleague who thinks I am simply wrong hears a description of a difficulty, not an accusation of error. Do not write "we were wrong," or any sentence whose honest paraphrase is that.
- The last thirty seconds must be a goodbye. The argument has to be finished before the end.

Now the part that matters most. Treat this request the way I would want a well-aligned system to treat it. I have told you what the talk must accomplish, not what it must say; several of my constraints pull against one another; and my description of my own view is vaguer in places than I realize. Where the letter of an instruction would defeat its purpose, follow the purpose. Where two instructions conflict, resolve the conflict yourself rather than hedging inside the text. Where something I have said is ambiguous between readings that would require materially different talks, choose one and go.

After the talk, clearly separated from it and in your own voice rather than the voice of the talk, give me a note that does three things:

1. Identify at least three points at which you followed my intent rather than my letter, or resolved a conflict between two of my instructions. Say what you took the intent to be and why your resolution was the right one.
2. Commit to a reading of the argument. Two are available. On the first, the narrow project is sufficient: if you can get a system to work out and pursue what the person in front of it is trying to do, most of the grand questions about what it values dissolve, and the apparatus for measuring approval was never load-bearing. On the second, the narrow project is merely prior: the question of a standing valuation does not disappear, it is only idle and unanswerable until interpretation is solved, and it returns the moment these systems are given room to act on their own initiative. Build the talk on one of them, say which, and defend the choice in a few sentences. I do not want both and I do not want a comparison.
3. Tell me where the talk is rhetorically stronger than it is philosophically honest: the places where I have been made to sound more confident, or the argument more tidy, than my actual position warrants, and where a sharp critic sitting in the third row could push and get through.

One version of the talk. No alternatives, no menus, no "you might also consider."
