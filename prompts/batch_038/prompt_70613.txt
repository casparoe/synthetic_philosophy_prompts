I'm a phil-of-science PhD student working on falsification and long-run prediction, and last week I sat in on an econ-history seminar where two of the panelists got into it over Malthus and never resolved it. Its exactly the case I want to write up but I honestly can't tell who's winning. Roughly how it went:

(1) Amara said the Essay is just refuted. Malthus argued population presses on food supply faster than food supply can grow, so any rise in wages gets eaten by more mouths and living standards snap back to subsistence. Instead real wages per head multiplied several times over while population multiplied too. Prediction failed, theory dead.

(2) Ben said it's right but early. The core mechanism — population responding to resource constraints — is still true, he just took the constraint as fixed. And he had a preventive check alongside the positive one, so falling fertility as people get richer is inside the model, not bolted on after.

(3) Amara said that's a rescue that cannot fail: population collapses, positive check; it doesn't collapse, preventive check. A theory with an explanation for both outcomes explains nothing.

(4) Ben said double standard — auxiliary hypotheses are legitimate when they're independently testable, and the preventive check makes claims about age at marriage and completed family size that were borne out by data nobody had in 1798. Amara said the preventive check only got real weight in the big rewritten second edition, after he'd been pushed on it, so by his own chronology it was a patch.

(5) Ben said the demographic transition is the preventive check operating at scale. Amara said no: for him restraint is a moral response to the fear of falling into poverty, whereas the transition runs on the cost of children and of women's time, so the sign of the fertility–income relationship is the opposite of what his model needs.

(6) Ben's last move was Duhem–Quine: the wage claim failed only given an auxiliary of fixed agricultural technology, and that auxiliary was perfectly reasonable then. Amara said D–Q licenses adjusting auxiliaries, not armoring the core, and the one auxiliary that would have saved him — treating technical progress as endogenous — is the one he refused, since diminishing returns on land was for him a law of nature.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, and how wrong the other one is? Then tell me whether "right but early" is even a coherent verdict, or whether it's doing something sneaky. And is there something both of them are taking for granted that they shouldn't be?
