I need a thought experiment built to order, for a seminar on the epistemology of tradition. Its purpose is fixed in advance, because the purpose determines what will count as a legitimate case: I want to find out whether Chesterton's fence can function as a criterion that discriminates between practices, or whether it is in the end a principle that always sides with whatever happens already to exist. A case that announces the conservative's verdicts rather than generating them from the principle itself is useless to me, however elegantly it is written.

**The design.** Choose one ordinary institution — a hospital, a firm, a court, a municipal office, a university department, a professional body — and place within it two long-standing practices, A and B. Practice A must be one whose function is structurally invisible from inside the institution: the reason it persists is a reason that the practice itself suppresses, pre-empts, or makes unavailable to anyone positioned to evaluate it. Practice B must be one whose function is fully articulable: the reformer can say exactly why B was adopted, and can show that the reason has lapsed or is now served by other means. The conservative in your case must recommend keeping A and demolishing B, and she must reach both verdicts through Chesterton's reasoning about what one owes to a fence one did not build — not through independent sympathy for A or independent distaste for B.

**Constraints.** Realistic throughout: no science fiction, no exotic technology, no religious taboo carrying its own justification. The reformer must be diligent, competent, well-informed, and in good faith; nothing may turn on her laziness, ignorance, or ideology. Neither practice may be one whose harm is already a matter of settled public record, and neither may be an obvious moral atrocity. A must impose real, identifiable costs on real people, and the reformer's case against it must be genuinely good on its face, so that the conservative who keeps A is keeping something that hurts and is not merely avoiding a costless error. A's opacity must not be the trivial kind — not mere complexity, not a shortage of expertise that a consultant could supply — and the difference between A and B must be confined to that single feature as far as you can manage.

The scenario should run no longer than four hundred words. Then supply the following, compactly:

1. An explicit list of the respects in which A and B have been held fixed — age, cost, who bears the cost, the reformer's diligence, the quality of the institutional records — and a statement of the one respect in which they differ.
2. The Chestertonian reasoning for each verdict. For B, explain why satisfying the demand to understand the fence licenses removal rather than merely permitting it. For A, explain precisely what the conservative takes the reformer's diligent failure to see the function to show, and why she thinks that failure is evidence rather than mere absence of evidence.
3. A falsification test: state what evidence, obtainable from inside the institution by people subject to it, would convince your conservative that her diagnosis of A is wrong. If the honest answer is that nothing inside could, say so plainly and then confront the charge that her principle is unfalsifiable and therefore empty.
4. An identification of which feature of the case does the work. Then run two perturbations: what happens to the verdicts if A's costs fall on the conservative's own group instead of on others, and what happens if B's lapsed function turns out not to have lapsed after all. Say which perturbation threatens the design and which the conservative can absorb.
5. The reformer's strongest attack on the case as constructed, and the conservative's strongest reply. Do not let the reply merely reassert the principle.

Where Burke's distinct emphases — prescription, the standing of inherited arrangements, the claim that a society is a partnership between the dead, the living, and the unborn — pull apart from Chesterton's, say which of the two your conservative is actually relying on at each step, and whether one of them would deliver a different verdict on A.

I want no references and no bibliography, and do not put words in Chesterton's or Burke's mouths inside quotation marks; paraphrase their arguments instead. Finally, if you conclude that some specification cannot be met in combination with the others, do not quietly drop it: state which one fails, explain why the combination is impossible, and then give me the closest case you can build.
