I chair the data-governance committee for a mid-size freight brokerage — five of us: two analysts, an ops manager, a claims lawyer, and me. Every week we get maybe six requests to change a definition in the business glossary (transit time, on-time, active shipment, accessorial, detention), and the meetings go badly in a predictable way. Half the time somebody says it's just a matter of what we decide the word means, so there's nothing to argue about. The other half somebody says no, there's a fact of the matter about what our operations actually are and the definition should track it. We can never agree on which kind of dispute we're in, and we burn fourty minutes on things that should take five.

Write a triage procedure the committee can run on one request in about twenty minutes, grounded in whatever the analytic/synthetic debate really supports — including the argument that there's no clean line between the two, and the point that you can't end a disagreement just by declaring the sentence true by definition. I want the questions in order with a stated pass condition for each, a workable rule for telling "we stipulate this, and here's what makes one stipulation better than another" apart from "there's evidence bearing on it," something about how entrenched a definition is and what a revision cascades into downstream, and an explicit handoff for the ones that are really about who eats a cost rather than about truth. End each run at one of three exits: approve the change, reject it, or send it to whoever owns the P&L because it was never our call.

Then run it on three cases: whether on-time is measured against the appointment window in the tender or against what the customer says they expected; whether a shipment cancelled and rebooked under a new pro number counts as one shipment or two, where the answer moves our published volume by about 4%; and whether a driver waiting 45 minutes at a dock is detained and therefore billable. At least one of them should land somewhere I won't like — I've spent two years defending our current detention definition, so don't soften it if it turns out I've been treating an empirical claim as a stipulation.

Mark every place where the procedure is a judgment call wearing a lab coat, and close with what it can't do.
