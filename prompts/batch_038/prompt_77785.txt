I have spent four years chairing incident-review boards for three unrelated organisations — a hospital network, a regional water authority, and an open-pit mining operator — and I have kept a private log of the verdicts I reach on individual cases, together with how confident I am in them. I would like to know what principle, if any, those verdicts add up to. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, for references, or for reading recommendations; I want a principle stated precisely enough that a non-philosopher member of one of my boards could apply it to a case none of us has seen.

The cases, and what I said about them:

A. A hospital's insulin-infusion algorithm is killed by a vendor firmware push that alters the dosing curve under one rare comorbidity combination. The vendor's release note was generic, the hospital's clinical lead was never told an update had occurred, the vendor's engineers did not foresee the interaction, and neither side departed from professional norms. Verdict: no individual is culpable — and yet I logged this as a genuine responsibility gap, a defect in the arrangement rather than a relief from blame.

B. The identical failure, except that the changelog was in fact emailed to the clinical lead eleven days earlier and he deferred reviewing it until after a long weekend. Verdict: the clinical lead is culpably responsible, the gap closes, and — this is what troubles me — my assessment of the vendor is unchanged from A, even though in A the vendor's opacity is precisely what made the harm possible.

C. A river-delta flood-gate controller runs a learned policy. It optimises its stated objective perfectly and causes a levee failure because the objective omitted a culvert. The engineers who wrote the objective knew the culvert existed and had scoped it out as immaterial. Verdict: the objective-writer is responsible. I would say this to his face and I am entirely confident.

D. The same controller, the same failure, except that the omitted factor was an interaction no one in the field understood until it was described after the event. Verdict: nobody is responsible, and unlike A I logged no gap at all. It felt to me like a landslide.

E. An autonomous haul truck kills a spotter who entered a marked exclusion zone during a shift change. Perception failed in heavy dust. Afterwards we find eleven near-misses of the same kind in the logs, each closed by operations staff as "sensor noise" without investigation. Verdict: the operations manager is responsible — and my verdict would not change even if the truck's perception had performed flawlessly, which tells me something about what my judgments are actually tracking.

F. A trading firm's execution algorithm triggers a violent dislocation that costs thousands of small investors real money. The programmers followed every regulation, the strategy was legal, no one intended or foresaw the outcome, the harm is diffuse, and the firm is fined an amount it treats as a cost of business. Verdict: someone is responsible, and I want to say it is the firm — but I cannot shake the suspicion that "the firm" is doing evasive work in that sentence.

G. A surgical robot in shared-autonomy mode makes a wrong-side incision. The surgeon could have overridden at any moment through a foot pedal, but the override carried a 400 ms latency, and over two hundred prior cases she had acquired a settled habit of deferring to the system's suggestions. Verdict: the surgeon is responsible, but less so than she would have been with zero latency. I find myself treating the machine's response characteristics as a mitigating factor, which is not a move I expected to make.

H. A domestic robot vacuum with no network connection knocks a lit candle into a curtain and burns down a house. The manufacturer met every applicable safety standard. Verdict: nobody's fault, and it feels exactly like D.

The principle I have been operating with, more or less unconsciously, is this: a gap exists where the harm was preventable by some identifiable human at a cost that human could reasonably have been expected to bear, and no such human exists; and the gap amounts to a moral defect rather than mere misfortune where the arrangement that made the harm possible was adopted deliberately for the benefit of an institution. The principle strains hardest exactly where A and D meet. In D the water authority adopted its controller deliberately, for its own benefit, just as the hospital did in A, so the second clause should deliver a defect in both — yet my response to D is that it was weather, and my response to A is that something is wrong with how we built this. H seems to escape only because no institution is involved, but if the absence of an institution is what does the work, then no privately owned autonomous system could ever generate a defect, and I do not believe that. I have tried patching the principle with a comprehension clause — a gap is a defect only if the harm was comprehensible to someone at the time — but "comprehensible in principle to somebody" is true of nearly everything once described after the fact, and if I tighten it to "comprehensible to the field," then C and D come apart correctly while A collapses into D, which I refuse to accept.

What I would like from you:

1. One, or at most three, candidate principles, each stated clause by clause, with each clause tied to the specific case or contrast in my log that motivates it. Vague terms like "reasonable" or "appropriate" should be cashed out into something a board member could check against a case file.

2. For each candidate, an honest accounting of which of my eight verdicts it reproduces and which it must overturn. If one of them saves all eight, tell me what it costs in complexity and whether I should be willing to pay that cost; if the simpler principle requires me to give up either my A-versus-D asymmetry or my insistence in B that the vendor's culpability is unchanged, tell me which surrender you recommend and why.

3. Rulings on four cases I have not managed to decide: (i) a municipal ambulance-dispatch optimiser, properly validated and monitored, which systematically under-serves a low-income district because its target encoded response-time efficiency rather than survival — no individual knew, no dashboard carried a signal, and the pattern surfaced only in an external audit two years later, by which time the attributable excess deaths are in the hundreds; (ii) a greenhouse climate controller sold to thousands of small growers, one batch of which has a drifting sensor, destroying a single grower's crop — the manufacturer's quality assurance was industry-standard and sampled 0.1% of units; (iii) an unmanned surface vessel operating under restrictive rules of engagement which fires on a fishing boat after misreading a spoofed identification signal planted by a third state, where the vessel's developers had documented that exact vulnerability in writing and recommended against deployment in contested waters, and procurement overrode them; (iv) a language-model-driven eligibility assistant that gives a benefits claimant confident, wrong advice about a filing deadline — nobody designed an advice function, nobody reviewed outputs, and the deploying agency's staff understood the tool as merely informational.

4. Your view on two questions my log keeps raising and I cannot settle: whether the number and identifiability of victims should enter the principle at all, since F presses on it and A does not; and whether my distinction between a gap that is a moral defect and a harm that is simply misfortune marks a real feature of these cases or only records my mood.
