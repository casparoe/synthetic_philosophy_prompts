CLUELESSNESS ALL THE WAY DOWN: THREE KINDS, AND A PROBLEM WITH THE ESCAPE ROUTES

1. setup. the standard cluelessness worry, roughly as Lenman presses it: if what makes an act right is the total value of everything that follows from it, and consequences propagate indefinitely forward in time and outward through the population, then the sum you're supposed to be computing is dominated by terms you can't estimate. tiny probabilities multiplied by astronomically large stakes swamp whatever confident local calculation you can do. the usual replies are something like: (i) the remote effects mostly cancel in expectation, so just go on the near ones; (ii) retreat to rules or intuitions that get justified indirectly, at the level of the rule rather than the act; (iii) be a good Bayesian about it — assign wide priors, gather evidence, act on whatever is robust across the range of estimates you can't rule out. my paper argues there's a domain where all three fail, and that the failure is different in kind from the one the cluelessness argument is actually about.

2. three kinds of not knowing.
C1 magnitude: i don't know the sign or size of the far-off effects.
C2 variance: i can't bound how wrong my estimate of C1 might be — no reliable second moment.
C3 reflexive: the act of getting the information changes the thing being measured, so the estimate is destroyed in the making of it.
the claim i want: in C1 and C2, information-gathering is weakly dominant. more evidence can't make you worse off except in opportunity cost, and it can narrow the range. in C3 it can have negative expected value, and there's no ceiling on how negative. that's the difference that breaks (i)-(iii), because all three replies secretly assume the C1/C2 picture where more careful estimation is always at worst neutral.

3. example A. a donor funds a programme that ranks primary schools in a region by exam results and channels money to the top decile. before the ranking, teacher effort was diffuse and largely invisible — some of it went to kids who would never show up in a score. after it, you get teaching to the test, quiet encouragement for weak students not to sit the exam, and the strongest teachers migrating to ranked schools. now the key move: the donor cannot find out what the counterfactual was. the ranking is what reconfigured the system, so there's no measurement available that would recover the unranked baseline. the ignorance here isn't a matter of not having collected enough data. it's that the data-collecting act destroyed the data.

4. example B. an individual choosing a career by reading published impact estimates from an advisory org. those estimates are themselves a social object: they move thousands of people, so the actual impact of the recommended path depends on how many others follow the recommendation, which depends on the recommendation being published and being believed. the advice is self-undermining in proportion to its uptake. and note that a single reader's response to this can't be "go research the methodology more carefully," because the quantity being estimated is a function of the estimate. the more accurate and widely trusted the estimate, the further it moves the thing it was estimating.

5. what i want to draw out. call an intervention epistemically closed when you can't learn whether it worked, not because you were lazy about evaluation but because evaluating it is one of the things that changes it. then a paradoxical policy conclusion follows: in closed domains the right response might be deliberate non-tracking — committing in advance not to measure, and picking interventions whose effects you agree beforehand not to follow. which looks exactly like rule-worship, and maybe it is a kind of self-effacing consequentialism: the theory telling you to stop doing the theory, for the theory's own reasons.

6. is this just Goodhart? my answer so far: Goodhart says a measure stops tracking the target once the target becomes the measure. that's a claim about corruption. C3 is a claim about the detectability of corruption — the corruption is unobservable in principle rather than merely overlooked. with Goodhart you can in principle set up a control arm and discover the target moved. in C3 you can't, because establishing a baseline is itself a targeting act.

7. objections i know about.
(a) RCTs and natural experiments recover baselines. my reply: spillover and anticipation contaminate the control arm once the ranking is public, and the funder of the study is usually the same actor who published the ranking.
(b) proves too much — every social intervention is reflexive to some degree, so C3 is a continuum and not a category, and my taxonomy does no work at all. i think this is the killer and i have no answer.
(c) if C3 only applies to social systems, then it's a sociology point wearing philosophy clothes. the original cluelessness argument was never restricted to social systems — it's about acts generally — so i may have answered a different question than the one i was assigned.
(d) the self-undermining problem in section 5: to know that measurement is harmful, i had to measure something. if closure is detectable by me, it isn't closure.

8. where i want to land. whether an agent in a closed domain can make a non-tracking commitment rationally, and whether she's allowed to revise it later. my suspicion is that if revision is permitted she's back in C2, so the commitment has to be non-revisable — which makes it look less like a smart policy and more like a vow.
