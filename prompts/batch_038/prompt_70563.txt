I am preparing a response for a workshop on quantitative approaches to history. Over the course of a single afternoon, a visiting historian invoked "history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes" twice, in opposite directions: first to concede that long sequences of state breakdown and recovery do recur across unrelated societies, and then, an hour later, to deny that any such recurrence could license a forecast about a particular country in a particular decade. I want to object, but I cannot yet say what the difference between those two uses amounts to.

Writing as a philosopher of social science who is equally at home with the technical side of cliodynamics — secular cycles, demographic-structural models, out-of-sample forecasting — lay out the distinct precise claims that this slogan could be making. Think the candidate readings through carefully before writing, but give me only the finished result: a numbered menu, in which each entry is stated as a claim that could be true or false rather than as a loose paraphrase of the original. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false; where it matters, indicate whether the resemblance it posits is something an observer projects onto events or something the events themselves supply.

Then answer the question I actually care about: which readings support the historian's concessive use, which support his dismissive use, and whether any single reading can carry both jobs at once without collapsing into an equivocation. If the dismissive use is available to him only on a reading that also forbids his own historical work, say so plainly.

Keep the response self-contained — no reading list, no name-checking, no citations. Hard limit: 450 words.
