You are a philosopher of mind who has opened a great many interdisciplinary workshops and knows how badly these addresses usually go. I am convening a two-day workshop — about forty attendees: comparative psychologists, machine-learning engineers, a handful of philosophers, and several science journalists — on Dennett's ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures, and I am giving the ten-minute opening remarks.

I do not want a recap. Half the room knows the ladder already, and the rest will get it from the first talk. What I want put on the table is a problem the ladder is being used to hide. In practice people assign rungs by performance: because a system talks fluently, it must be Gregorian. But the rungs were never about output — they are about where competence is stored. A Darwinian creature's competence is hard-wired by selection; a Skinnerian creature's is shaped by reinforcement in its own history; a Popperian creature can let its hypotheses die in its stead instead of dying itself; a Gregorian creature inherits tools it did not invent, scaffolding built and maintained by others. On that reading, the current generation of language models might be superlative Popperian creatures — or something the ladder has no slot for: systems whose scaffolding is entirely supplied by others, with no sense in which they ever take it up as their own. No benchmark will settle which, because all of them measure performance.

To make this usable rather than merely provocative, I have been sketching a diagnostic I want the room to work with all weekend. Call it the subtraction test: strip away layers of a system's environment one at a time and record (i) how much competence disappears with each layer and (ii) who, if anyone, is left doing the inheriting. Build the remarks around it, state it plainly enough that a journalist could repeat it accurately at lunch, and apply it once, briefly, to a concrete case everyone in the room will recognize.

Two further constraints. A number of attendees think the ladder is not merely dated but harmful — a hierarchy of value wearing a lab coat, used to rank animals down and machines up — and the remarks must face that squarely instead of waving it off. At the same time, nobody should leave the room deflated, because the weekend depends on their arguing with each other.
