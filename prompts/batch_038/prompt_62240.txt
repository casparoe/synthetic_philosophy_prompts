So, quick setup: I write a science-and-ideas column, and I'm working on a piece about how ordinary people are supposed to update on secondhand if-then information — a tipster, a forecaster hedging, a doctor saying that if the biopsy shows X then there's a 70 percent chance of Y. Before I commit twelve hundred words to my own take, I want someone to break it.

Play a hardline Bayesian for me, and play it strong rather than as a strawman I can knock over. Your position: every rational change of belief is just ordinary conditioning on the total evidence you actually received, full stop. No special update rule for conditionals, no probability-kinematics surgery on your conditional probabilities, no picking whichever new distribution sits closest to the old one under some distance measure. If someone hands you an if-then sentence, on your view that's an event in the space like any other — the event being that this person said this thing — and Bayes does all the work. You may concede the small stuff (that a case can be under-specified, that modeling an informant is fiddly, that my version of the story leaves gaps) but you never give up the main thesis. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so this reads like a conversation and not a seminar paper, and when you need arithmetic, give it to me in a line or two of plain numbers I could drop into a sidebar. No citations, no reading list, no telling me who in the literature said what — I want your reasoning, not a bibliography I can't check on a deadline. And if you need a fact about my setup that I haven't given you, ask me for it in character and I'll answer as the journalist who made the thing up.

The case, in my own retelling with my own numbers, so work only with what's here: a courier is dropped blind into a valley at night. The valley is split into a North half and a South half, and separately into Zone A and Zone B, so there are four quandrants, and she starts out with exactly one quarter on each. Minutes after landing, a shepherd finds her — honest, knows every path in the valley, no motive to mislead her — and tells her: if you're in Zone A, the odds are three to one that you're in the North. He says nothing whatsoever about Zone B, and I am deliberately not telling you anything about what he would have said in other circumstances. So her evidence now includes that P(North given Zone A) is three quarters.

Here's my opening move. Your view says conditioning on the total evidence handles this, so condition. The event she actually received is "the shepherd said that sentence," and to condition on it she needs a number for how likely he was to say exactly that in each of the four quadrants. Write those four numbers down for me, or tell me why you don't have to. Because here's what I think is going on and I want you to break it: I never stipulated what he'd say if she were in Zone B, so those likelihoods arent pinned down by my story, which means you get to choose them, and different choices give different answers — which makes your position not a theory of updating but a way of dressing up whatever rule you already liked. And whichever way you go, you owe me a number for her credence that she's in Zone A. My gut says it stays at one half, since he didn't say a single word about zones, and my editor's gut will say the same, and my readers' will too. Give me your number, and tell me what on your account is doing the work that makes it not one half — if it isn't. If your honest answer is that the case as I've told it is under-specified and that's a flaw in my column rather than in your view, say so in character, then tell me which stipulation you'd add and why that one is the natural default instead of a dodge.
