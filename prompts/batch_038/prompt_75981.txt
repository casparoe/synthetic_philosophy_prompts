So I sit on the scientific review panel of a smallish private foundation — crop science, about 40 labs, rolling five-year awards — and I owe them a memo by Friday on the third renewal of the Adeyemi group. I got into a really long argument about it last night with a friend who does grant evaluation for a much bigger funder and is convinced the philosophy of science settles the question. I still can't tell which of us is right. Help me write the thing.

Facts, as neutrally as I can state them:

- Core claim since 2009: drought tolerance in their study crop is driven primarily by one root-architecture trait (deep rooting angle), and everything else is downstream noise. That's the bit they never touch.
- 2009–2016 it went great. It predicted yields at two new field sites before anyone measured them, and both came out right. That's why it got funded twice.
- Since 2017 there have been three serious anomalies and three patches:
  1. A trial in a high-salinity region failed badly. They added a soil-microbiome mediator term; the parameter values were fitted to that same failed trial. Then they used the patched model to predict a second saline site's yield, and it was right.
  2. Two cultivars with the "wrong" root angle did fine. They narrowed the model's domain to cultivars above a certain rooting-angle threshold — i.e. the claim now covers exactly the subset of cases where it was already known to work. Nothing new has come out of that.
  3. A long-term trial showed tolerance declining as CO2 rose, which the framework doesn't permit. They added a stomatal-conductance coupling term that would be testable — but only with a phenotyping facility nobody in the country has, realistically 8–10 years out.
- Rival: a group two states over, microbiome-first, no root-architecture commitment. Started 2019, much cheaper, explains less, has no anomalies, and made one bold prediction in 2023 that hasn't been checked because the measurements are still pending.

Nadia's position: its a degenerating problemshift in the textbook Lakatosian sense — protective belt expanding, content redefined to save the core, one patch already refuted — and the framework hands the panel its verdict for free. Cut it, fund the rival seperately, done, and the memo takes an afternoon.

Mine: Lakatos is explicitly against instant rational appraisal. You can only call a programme degenerating with hindsight; his go-to case is Copernicanism sitting around empirically worse than Ptolemy for the better part of a century before it caught up (and if I'm mangling that example, don't build anything on it — the point is just that the verdict comes late). Also, patch #1 produced a real prediction that came true, and patch #3 isn't refuted, it's untestable right now. And the rival has zero corroborated excess content, which on the sophisticated-falsificationist line means there's nothing to rationally move to. So on Nadia's own framework we might be obliged to keep paying for a programme she calls degenerate.

The killer problem: our charter says the decision must come with reasons that would justify it to a competent outsider at the time it's made. "History will tell" is not a fundable reason. So whatever you land on, it has to be actionable.

Write the memo. What I need in it:

1. Bottom line up front: renew, decline, or renew-with-conditions — with a term and a dollar shape (invent plausible numbers) and what the money specifically buys.
2. Classify the three patches. Are they the ad hoc1 / ad hoc2 / ad hoc3 kind of adjustment, worst to least bad? And be honest about whether those labels do work here or just relabel facts I already gave you.
3. Take patch #1 head-on: the parameter was tuned to the anomaly it's supposed to explain, then a second prediction from the patched model came true. Is that novel content or is it circular? This is the part Nadia and I actually shouted about.
4. Say what a panel that can't appeal to hindsight does instead — and be candid about whether the reasons you end up using are still Lakatosian, or whether you've quietly switched frameworks in order to get a decision out.
5. The rival: does the absence of a corroborated rival block cutting Adeyemi, or is that just the framework immunising itself against ever being replaced? Pick a side.
6. Our five-year clock as its own distortion: the theory says no verdict is available at decision time and the charter says we must decide anyway. Is the defect in the charter, in the theory, or in the pairing?
7. A short section making the strongest case for whichever side you reject, argued by someone who isn't stupid.

No citations, no reading list, don't invent references — I just want the document. 1200–1500 words, headings are fine, and it should read like something a real panel would circulate, not like a philosophy paper.
