I have written social-realist fiction for some twenty years, and a difficulty keeps recurring in my drafts that I now suspect belongs to moral philosophy rather than to craft. I would like to know whether the pattern below has an established name, and if so what exactly it names.

The pattern: a character spends a book positioning himself to receive something — a prize, a title, a patron's favour, the gratitude of a man he has served — and when he finally receives it, the reader's sense that he deserves it has quietly evaporated. Nothing has gone wrong. He does not gloat, he is not exposed as a fraud, his work is genuinely excellent and the body that awards it has judged correctly. Yet the thing arrives in the reader's mind as a payment rather than as a recognition, and there is a small deflation. Set beside him a second character who sought nothing, who is embarrassed when it comes, and who is felt to have earned it completely — often more completely, we sense, than the first man did, despite having done less.

I want to be precise, because the crude version of this observation — that readers dislike strivers — is not what I mean, and a term for that would be useless to me. Two further instances from life rather than fiction. A mother does a great deal for her children; while she does it without reference, we are moved and grateful; the day she produces an itemised account of everything she has given up, our gratitude collapses, not because the account is false or the sacrifices smaller than she says, but because the sacrifices have been invoiced, and one does not feel gratitude toward an invoice. A man who has been kind to a friend for years without once mentioning it, and then in a quarrel says that after all he has done, this is what he gets: from that sentence onward he is, in our eyes, no longer owed anything.

What seems to happen is that the deliberate pursuit or production of the grounds of a reward converts that reward, for the person conferring it, from an acknowledgement into a settlement — and once it is a settlement it cannot be deserved in the way we wanted. The desert does not merely shrink; it changes category. And it changes category even when the pursuit wholly succeeds, which is what separates this from the familiar paradoxes about aims that defeat themselves: my character gets exactly what he went after.

So. Is there an established concept for this? I know the paradox of hedonism, but that concerns a goal that cannot be reached by aiming at it, and here the goal is reached and only the deserving of it is lost. I know that social scientists use the phrase 'the paradox of meritocracy' for something quite different — that organisations which advertise themselves as meritocratic end up rewarding men over equally performing women — so I would want any borrowing of that name flagged as a collision. And Aristotle's great-souled man claims honours openly, and is supposed to be the finest of men for doing so, which suggests that whatever I have hold of, it is not simply a prohibition on claiming one's due. What are the nearest established concepts, how does the thing I am describing differ from each, and which of them fits best?

I also want the edges tested rather than assumed. Why does none of this happen to a wage, which everyone pursues openly and no one is thought less entitled to for pursuing it? Does the effect run out at the far end, so that a person utterly indifferent to any reward also becomes impossible to honour? Does it depend on the reward being conferred by someone who could have withheld it — would a committee, or an algorithm, or a rule of the game be immune? And does it matter whether the striving is concealed and merely suspected, or announced?

A bare label will not serve. I need the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and the conditions that switch it on and off. If there is no established term, say so plainly, then coin one, define it tightly enough that I could tell a colleague when it does and does not apply, and name the existing concept it would most easily be confused with. Finally, tell me whether the phenomenon has any consequence for a society that makes all of its honours competed for in the open, since on my account such a society would be manufacturing winners whom nobody, including the winners themselves, can experience as deserving.
