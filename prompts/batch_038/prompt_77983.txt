My brother-in-law works in risk at a regional electric utility — substations, distribution lines, about forty field crews — and because I once nearly finished a philosophy degree they put me on the safety committee, where my actual job is taking the minutes. Two years ago a consultant built them a spreadsheet for deciding where the annual safety budget goes, and it works like this: every candidate project (conductor insulation upgrades, lockout-tagout interlocks, replacing a fleet of bucket trucks) gets a probability per year that the bad thing happens absent the project, multiplied by the number of people exposed, multiplied by a dollar figure per harm — they use something like eleven million dollars per fatality and smaller numbers per lost-time injury — and then the total gets divided by the project's cost. You sort by dollars-saved-per-dollar-spent and fund straight down the list until the money runs out. There are two extra columns, though. One flags any project that removes a hazard entirely rather than merely reducing its likelihood, and those jump the queue no matter where they sort. And there's an informal rule that they won't fund anything whose whole justification rests on a probability estimate smaller than one in a million, because "we can't number that and we're not going to pretend we can."

At the quarterly review last month the safety director ran a calibration exercise to see whether the room's gut matched the spreadsheet. He put up two pairs. First pair: Program A, certain to prevent 100 line-contact injuries over a decade, versus Program B, 10% chance of preventing 500, 89% chance of preventing 100, 1% chance of preventing nothing. Nearly everyone picked A. Second pair: Program C, 11% chance of preventing 100 and 89% chance of preventing none, versus Program D, 10% chance of preventing 500 and 90% chance of preventing none. Nearly everyone picked D. One of the lineworkers then worked out that A is just C with the same 89% bonus bolted onto it, and B is just D with that same bonus bolted on, so the two pairs should sort the same way if the spreadsheet is right — and the director waved him off with "they're different questions and you know it." The spreadsheet would have ranked B over A and D over C in both pairs, which is basically just the arithmetic.

So here's what I want, and please don't explain the famous paradox back at me — I know roughly what it is and I know the name, that's not the hard part. I want to know what the spreadsheet practice itself presupposes. Reconstruct the view of risk that multiplying-and-sorting only makes sense under: what has to be true about how a rational chooser stands to probabilities for that ranking rule to be the right way to allocate safety money? Does it commit the committee to the idea that the ranking between two programs is untouched by adding the same third possibility to both of them, and which rival picture of risk does that quietly rule out — is it a claim that certainty is worth something no probability-weighting can capture, that risk is a property of the whole portfolio rather than a sum of independent parts, that the one-in-a-million floor is an epistemic limit rather than an evaluative one? Then tell me whether those two extra columns are consistent with the ranking column, or whether the commitee has two incompatible theories of risk living in one document — and if the latter, which of the three columns is the odd one out and why. Be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, and point out where it's underdetermined, i.e. where three or four quite different theories would generate the exact same spreadsheet and the exact same meetings. And finally: if they swapped the implicit view for one that lets certainty carry the extra weight the room clearly thinks it carries, what concretely has to change — the columns, the ranking rule, what counts as an acceptable justification in the minutes?
