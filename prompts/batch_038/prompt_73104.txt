hey — second year phil phd here, TAing the induction week of intro philosophy of science, and our department does a little end-of-term reading where the TAs are basically contractually obligated to read something aloud. I want to do a sestina on Goodman's new riddle of induction, and I've been staring at it for a week. Can you write it for me?

The form, strictly:

- six sestets plus the three-line envoi, unrhymed, loose pentameter (don't sweat perfect meter, I just want the lines to have a spine)
- end-words, first stanza in this order: emerald / green / grue / bleen / before / blue
- standard rotation, i.e. 123456, then 615243, 364125, 532614, 451362, 246531, and the envoi taking them (6,2) (1,4) (5,3) with the second word of each pair mid-line
- American English spelling throughout — and yes, I'm aware how that lands given the subject

The situation I want: the speaker is cataloguing a bequest of cut stones, and part of the collection is going into a vault that isn't scheduled to be unsealed until some fixed date far off. Give me a date, a bequest, a sealed case, whatever — I just need "before" to have a real referent in the poem and not be a free-floating variable. The curator is writing the inventory of what's already been examined, and there's a whole drawer nobody has looked at.

What the form has to do, and this is the part I actually care about:

1. No new evidence, ever. Everything the poem knows is on the table in stanza one. Every later stanza is the same stones re-described in a re-sorted vocabulary. If a new fact shows up in stanza four, the poem has cheated, because the whole point is that the rotation never adds material — it only permutes a fixed inventory.

2. Somewhere around stanza five I want the flip. That's the stanza where bleen leads and green comes last, and for a few lines green has to sound like the newcomer, the disjunctive one, the word doing something gerrymandered — because from inside the other vocabulary it is exactly that, and most people who talk about this argument never actually inhabit the other side of it. Make it feel like the curator has, briefly, learned the other language.

3. Leave both readings live and refuse to adjudicate: the stones change after the date, or the stones stay as they are and it's only our predicate that was going to shift. Don't let the speaker conclude that of course they're green. The ledger can't say.

4. The envoi carries all six words and lands the entrenchment thought without using the word, or "projectible," or naming Goodman or his book. Image, not argument — a groove worn into the ruling of the ledger, a pencil stub, whatever. Somebody who's read the argument should feel the doctrine arrive; somebody who hasn't should just feel a poem end.

Also please don't make it cute about how silly "grue" sounds. That's the failure mode I'm most afraid of, along with it turning into a philosophy essay in line breaks. It should work as a poem for a room of undergrads who've never heard of any of this, and be exact enough that the Goodman person in our department doesn't wince.

After the poem, add two or three sentences telling me where you think the rotation is doing the philosophical work — which stanza carries what — so I can check you actually built the thing rather than filling in a template.
