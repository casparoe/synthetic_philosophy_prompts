I'm the token philosopher on the Ashcroft Prize committee. Every few years we hand a chunk of money to a forecast made at least twenty-five years back that events have since, in our charter's word, been vindicated. Every cycle we spend most of the meeting arguing about what that word commits us to. Thursday is the meeting and I've said I'd circulate something beforehand.

Here's our record. Six rulings, all of them mine to summarize fairly badly:

- The very first thing we took up was Malthus's Essay. Denied. The reason in the minutes is that nothing could have counted against it: whatever happened there was always a check available to explain it, and we noted the later editions had grown an additional one to answer critics.
- Arrhenius, 1896, on carbon dioxide and warming: awarded, despite his thinking the doubling would play out over millennia rather than inside a century, because the physical mechanism was his and his sensitivity figure landed inside today's range.
- The 1968 bestseller promising mass famine within a couple of decades: denied, on the ground that the thing which actually fed everybody, yield growth, was already underway and had no place in the model.
- Molina and Rowland, 1974, on CFCs destroying stratospheric ozone: awarded. The catalytic chlorine cycle is exactly what did the damage, even though nobody, them included, had imagined the polar chemistry that turned a gradual thinning into a hole.
- The 1972 Club of Rome world model: denied, 4-3, and the stated reason is basically that we couldn't locate a claim specific enough to check against anything.
- A 1969 forecast that a named European port's tonnage would halve by 1990 for want of containerisation. It halved in 1988. Denied, because the causal story turned on a state freight monopoly that never existed; the real driver was something else.

Now the mess. Finalist A is a 1997 actuarial report predicting that a particular country's public pension scheme would be unable to pay scheduled benefits by 2025 and that replacement rates would be cut by roughly a fifth. The date came and went with no insolvency. But in 2011 the legislature raised the retirement age and cut the replacement rate by about a fifth — fourteen years early, and by statute rather than by collapse. Our economist says the report's underlying arithmetic was right. Two complications: there's a buried footnote in the original conceding that political response might move dates without moving the arithmetic, which nobody cited at the time and a graduate student found last month; and the sponsoring institute published a paper in 2026 arguing the report was correct all along, which reads, frankly, like a rescue. Finalist B is the chair's favourite, a 1993 forecast that a certain country's fertility would stay below replacement through 2020. It did. Our demographer's objection is that it couldn't really have come out otherwise, given the momentum already in the numbers.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Give me the candidate principles that jointly fit all six past rulings — I suspect at least two quite different ones do — and say what each of them implies about A and about B. Tell me which past ruling is the closest analog to A and where the analogy strains. Then be blunt about what principle we'd be adopting under each live option: award A, award B, split it, or award nothing. And there's an objection I want handled properly rather than waved at: someone will say that a forecast whose stated trigger never fires *because* people acted on it is exactly the Malthus problem in reverse, creditable no matter what happens. Is that right, or does the footnote change it? Finish with the one hypothetical case, describable in a sentence or two, that would best force our principles apart for future cycles.
