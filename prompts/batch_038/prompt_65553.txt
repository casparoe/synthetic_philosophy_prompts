The question I set was:

State Scanlon's contractualist criterion of wrongness, explain the aggregation problem it is said to face, and assess his response to it. Your answer must explain how his view can require saving the greater number when the harms are unequal without ever summing complaints across persons.

The submitted answer:

Scanlon's contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it would be forbidden by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. Because the standard is reasonableness rather than advantage, the test is impartial: we consider a principle from the point of view of everyone affected together, weighing all of their interests against one another, and we accept it if that joint weighing would not be rejected.

The aggregation problem arises from the individualist restriction, the rule that only individual persons' reasons count and that these may not be summed across persons. Suppose one person will die unless I rescue him, while a billion others will each suffer a mild headache. Intuitively the billion headaches together matter more than the one death, but contractualism has no way of adding them up, so it appears to imply that we should let the billion suffer and save the one.

Scanlon's response is the balancing argument. We compare complaints pairwise, one individual at a time. In the unequal case the dying person's complaint is far stronger than any single headache-sufferer's, so a principle directing us to rescue him cannot reasonably be rejected, while the opposite principle can be rejected by him. Because pairwise comparison does this work, Scanlon holds that no case ever requires adding complaints together: the same machinery handles the headaches case, and the objection loses its force.

Where harms are equal, however, pairwise comparison yields a tie, since each of the five has a complaint exactly as strong as the one's. Here Scanlon follows Taurek in holding that the numbers do not count: we should flip a fair coin, because giving each person an equal chance of being saved is the only way to give positive weight to every life. His theory is therefore non-aggregative all the way down, saving the greater number when the harms differ in size and declining to when they do not.
