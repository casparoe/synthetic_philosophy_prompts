I'm in a public policy MA and my tech-and-society seminar is on Winner this week — the essay where he says artifacts can be political in two different registers, one where a particular design or arrangement settles some issue in the affairs of a community, and a stronger one where a technology seems to require, or to be strongly compatible only with, certain kinds of political relationships. I mostly buy the first and I'm suspicious of the second, partly because the examples everyone leans on (the parkway bridges built to a height that keeps buses off them, the machines brought into a plant to break a union) all look like the same story to me: someone with power wanted something and the hardware delivered it. That's just politics with a longer fuse. The strong sense is supposed to be doing something more, and I want to find out whether it actually can.

So here's a case from my own city that I've been weirdly obsessed with, because my partner works on the transit agency's data team. Mid-sized place, roughly 40 million boardings a year, almost all bus plus two short light-rail lines. Over three years they phased out cash on board entirely; as of last month there are no fareboxes left on any vehicle. You pay by tapping either a registered app account or an open-loop contactless bank card. The registered tier gets a daily cap and free transfers inside a 90-minute window; the unregistered card tap gets neither, so a poor rider without an account pays more for the same trip. They did keep a fallback: a reloadable prepaid card sold at corner stores and libraries, cash top-up in ten dollar increments at a machine, but it's unregistered by design, so no cap, no free transfer, and the balance is gone if you lose it. The agency's public rationale has been entirely operational — cash handling costs, farebox maintenance, dwell time at the front door, evasion. An agency-funded study found no ridership drop among low-income riders 18 months out; a riders' coalition put out a 60-page rebuttal arguing the study was measuring the wrong thing, since the unbanked share of registered riders fell from about 12 percent to about 4 percent and nobody tracked where the missing trips went (their guess: walking, and the informal van services that run out of two shopping plazas). Then two details my partner keeps bringing up. One: the validators and the rebuilt tap-only gates at both rail stations are capital assets now, so restoring cash anywhere means a fresh procurement, ballpark eighteen million. Two: the whole account and fare-logic system is run by a vendor on a nine-year contract, and the agency no longer has staff who can touch fare rules internally — changing a cap, a transfer window, or a discount eligibility rule means filing a change request with a sixty-day lead time. Which produced a quiet institutional fact that no one voted on: the council can still set the fare *level*, but the fare *rules* are now effectively administered through a vendor's queue.

Argue that this is political in the strong sense — that the system as actually built requires, or is strongly compatible only with, a particular kind of political relationship between the agency, its riders, and the firms sitting between them — and not merely in the weak sense of a design choice that happens to have winners and losers. Take the best rebuttal seriously rather than strawmanning it: the politics here live in the procurement and the contract, not in the artifact, because you could keep the exact same taps, validators, and gates but run the fare logic in-house, make the prepaid card full-featured and cash-toppable at fare price, and cap it properly, and then you'd have the identical technology with none of the politics — which would show the hardware is doing no work at all and Winner's second sense is really just an observation about who signs things. Make the case anyway if you can, and be explicit about which feature in my story is carrying the weight (the sixty-day queue? the two-tier account structure? the capital lock-in?) and what happens to the argument if I delete that feature. Also tell me what would have to be true of that phrase "requires or is strongly compatible with" for the claim to come out non-trivially true rather than true of every large technical system, and give me a counterfactual test I could actually apply — is there any real arrangement of this same technology that would be politically inert, and if there is, does that sink the strong reading or just locate it? Finally, separate what's settled from what's genuinely live among people who work on this literature; my sense is that plenty of them think the strong sense collapses into the weak one plus some speculative claims about necessity, and if that's right I want you to say so plainly instead of treating my framing as the standard view or hedging both ways at once. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give me the argument — no preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
