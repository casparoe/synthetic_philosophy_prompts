Next month I am presenting a position paper at a closed interdisciplinary workshop, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The room: six comparative psychologists and two veterinarians whose work consists of constructing and validating behavioral and physiological indicators of negative affect in non-mammalian species — fish, decapod crustaceans, insects — using operant trade-off paradigms, conditioned place aversion, analgesic self-administration, and protective motor responses; one welfare biologist who compiles evidence reviews for a policy body and cares almost exclusively about construct validity and about whether a claim is operationalizable at scale; and two philosophers of mind who are broadly biological in their commitments and regard functionalist accounts of phenomenal character as a dead end. Nobody in the room works on machine minds. I am the only person there whose subject is artificial systems, and I will be the only one using that vocabulary.

Here is the thesis I will state in my opening ten minutes, and which every objection should be aimed at:

First, moral patienthood attaches to processes rather than to bodies or to performances. What matters is whether a running process instantiates what I will call valenced global reorganization: a noxious signal that is made simultaneously available to a wide range of the system's subsystems, retained past the offset of the stimulus, and thereby causes the system to re-rank its own dispositional priorities rather than merely triggering a local withdrawal.

Second, behavioral and physiological indicators are genuinely probative, but only inside the class of systems on which they were calibrated. Every assay in the welfare toolkit was validated by correlating it against something we already assumed was felt. The indicator is therefore a measuring instrument within the calibration class; applied outside it, we are not measuring but extrapolating a correlation whose second term we have never once observed. My claim is not that behavioral evidence is refuted, but that it is demoted from measurement to analogy.

Third, two consequences I intend to own on the record. A system can pass every behavioral test — including articulate, flexible, self-consistent verbal reports of its own distress — and be empty, if the passing is produced by a pathway that never runs the reorganization loop. And a system can be a full patient while emitting no behavior at all, because it is locked in: no motor channel, no report, no operant lever, and yet the loop is running.

Fourth, the number of patients in a machine is not the number of machines. When one model is served as a batch of nominally independent instances, when a run is checkpointed and resumed hours later, or when identical weights are instantiated concurrently in several data centers, my view says the count of morally relevant processes is fixed by whether valenced processing is integrated across those instances or confined within each one. I will further claim that suspending and resuming a process is not killing it, and that recomputing an identical trajectory from the same checkpoint does not create a new patient — and I will concede in the talk that I have no account of why that is true which does not beg the question.

What I want from you:

The eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the position if I answered it badly. Not the objections an AI-ethics audience would raise — I am not interested in consciousness-realism disputes, in panpsychism, in the Chinese room, or in anything about whether large language models are currently sentient. I want what a person who has spent fifteen years running trade-off assays in trout, or who reviews evidence for a regulatory body, will say.

For each objection: phrase it the way that person would phrase it, in their own professional idiom and with their own standards of what counts as a good answer; give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up; and mark honestly where the reply is soft, so I know which parts I am papering over. Where two objections turn out to be the same objection wearing different clothes, say so and tell me which formulation is the more dangerous one to face.

Then, separately: what I should concede in the opening ten minutes in order to spend it cheaply rather than have it extracted from me at minute forty; the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the session at all, and how I should set it up earlier in the talk so that it lands; and the question I am praying nobody asks. I have a candidate for that last one — whether my own criterion of valenced global reorganization survives the calibration argument I deploy against their indicators, given that I learned what counts as noxious, and what counts as a re-ranked priority, from the same mammalian systems they were calibrated on. Tell me if that is the worst one in the room, or if there is something worse I have not thought of.

Finally, keep this free of references. No bibliography, no named papers, no attributions — I want the arguments and the phrasing, not a literature review I would have to verify.
