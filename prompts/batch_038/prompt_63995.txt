**Unstatable deflationism**

1. Set-up. The deflationist says truth has no underlying nature to discover. Everything there is to say about it is already said by the equivalence schema — the proposition that snow is white is true iff snow is white, and likewise for every other proposition. On this picture the point of the truth predicate isn't to describe some property of propositions; it's a bit of logical machinery that lets us generalise over claims we can't list one by one ("everything he said was true").

2. The complaint everyone makes. The generalisation problem: deflationism supposedly can't explain why believing what's true tends to get you the milk, because each instance of the schema explains only one case, and an infinite pile of instances doesn't add up to a general explanation unless something substantive binds them together. I don't think this is the sharpest objection, and my draft concedes it. The deflationist's whole point is that the generalisation is licensed by the predicate itself and needs no further nature behind it. So I want to set this aside rather than fight it.

3. My worry instead: stating the thesis. Deflationism looks like it wants to say something like — for every proposition, its being true consists in nothing over and above things being as that proposition says they are. But then either (a) that is a general characterisation of a whole class of propositions, i.e. a substantive claim about what truth consists in, which contradicts the deflationist's own prohibition on such claims; or (b) it collapses back into the schema, in which case it isn't a claim about truth at all but an endless list of claims about snow, electrons, cats on mats, and the doctrine has no content beyond those first-order matters. There's a third escape — treat deflationism as a doctrine about the *concept* rather than the property — but then the doctrine can't be expressed in the object language at all, only gestured at by using the concept in a way that outruns the schema, which is just horn (b) with extra steps. So deflationism is false, trivial, or unstatable.

4. An asymmetry I want to develop. This bites harder on Horwich-style minimalism than on Quine-style disquotationalism. Minimalism needs truth to be a genuine, if very thin, property, precisely because it wants the generalisations to do explanatory work; so it can't happily accept horn (b). Disquotationalism can retreat to a claim about a language and a speaker rather than about the world, and shrug off the charge of triviality — but that retreat costs it its grip on propositions, and on the truth-aptness of stuff that isn't obviously sentences of a language, like moral or modal claims. I'd also mention Field's line that deflating truth drags you towards deflating meaning, which seems to me to be a cousin of horn (a), though I'm not sure how close the cousinship is.

5. Where I want to land. Not that deflationism is refuted, but that its only stable home is a kind of quietism: you can show what the truth predicate does, you can't say what truth is. And even that quietism is unstable, because the deflationist's polemical use of the doctrine — as a stick to beat correspondence, coherence and pragmatist theories with — is itself a saying, not a showing.
