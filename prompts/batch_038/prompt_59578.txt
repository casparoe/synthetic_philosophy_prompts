And here's one student's answer, pasted as submitted:

> The formulation in the question looks like one paradox but is really two, and keeping them apart does most of the work. The first is static: if S deceives herself into believing p, she ends up believing p while also believing not-p, and it is unclear what it is to hold an outright contradiction. The second is dynamic, and it is about the project rather than the outcome: getting herself to believe p seems to require that S intend to do so, and to intend it she has to keep in view the truth she is trying to dislodge. The dynamic problem is the nastier one, because it does not go away even if you grant that contradictory beliefs are possible — a plan to make yourself not-know something looks self-defeating in the way a plan to fall asleep quickly does.
>
> Sartre's discussion of bad faith starts from the dynamic problem in its Freudian version. If repression is done by a censor, the censor has to know what it is repressing in order to pick it out and screen it off, so the apparatus has to contain both the dupe and a very well-informed liar, and Freud has simply relocated the paradox into the head. Sartre's own move is to deny the relocation: consciousness is translucent to itself, so in bad faith there is one consciousness apprehending the truth non-thetically while attending to it in some other mode. There is no second self doing the hiding. Davidson basically takes this over and turns it into partitioning: the mind is divided into semi-autonomous structures, so the belief that p and the belief that not-p can coexist without the agent holding a contradiction, and the intention to deceive sits on the other side of the wall from the belief it produces. That handles the static problem cleanly and the dynamic one too, since at no point does anyone both know the truth and believe the lie. The cost is that the walls have to be leaky in a controlled way — the deceiving structure has to guide the deceived one's evidence-gathering — and once you admit that, the unity of the agent starts to look like a fiction, which was Sartre's complaint about Freud in the first place. Davidson is paying it, not avoiding it.
>
> Mele's deflationary account attacks the other end: he denies that the paradigm cases have the structure that generates the paradox. The mechanisms are ordinary and well documented — biased evidence gathering, dwelling on what confirms, ease of recall, the general tendency of desire to shape belief — and they need no partitioning and no homunculus. He does keep the project, though: on his view the self-deceiver is still doing something, aiming at a belief. What he gives up is the requirement that she hold not-p as an outright belief at the same time, since the true belief can be brief or merely dispositional. So Mele escapes the static paradox and still owes an answer to the dynamic one, which he tries to pay by saying the project is usually only half-formed, closer to a drift toward a belief than a plan. That feels like the weak spot, and it is where the "you have changed the subject" objection bites: if there is no intention and no contradiction, what is left is motivated irrationality, and calling it self-deception is just a way of dressing up wishful thinking.
>
> My conclusion is that the deflationary kind is possible and probably common, and that strict self-deception — an intention, a liar and a dupe in one skull — is not, or is at best a limiting case we never actually meet. Which is uncomfortable for a course that has just spent six weeks on novels full of people who obviously do it.
