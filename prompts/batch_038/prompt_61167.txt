I am a high school senior doing an independent study on early Chinese philosophy, and my teacher has had me reading the Chuang-tzu in James Legge's old translation — the second of his two volumes of Taoist texts, published in 1891. I have hit a wall in Book XXII of Legge's numbering, "Knowledge Rambling in the North," which I gather corresponds to chapter 22 of the standard edition. I have read the speech below perhaps fifteen times and I still cannot tell what it is doing, so I would be grateful for some help.

Brief context: a personified figure called Knowledge asks three others how the Tâo is to be known. The first, Dumb Inaction, gives no reply at all; the second, Heedless Blurter, starts to answer and then forgets what he was going to say; the third, Hwang-Tî, answers fluently and at length. At the end of the book Hwang-Tî sums up: Dumb Inaction was truly right because he did not know the thing, Heedless Blurter was nearly right because he forgot it, and "I and you are not nearly right, because we know it." The speech I am stuck on comes from the middle of Hwang-Tî's long answer. Here it is, with the sentence immediately preceding it:

> Here now there is something, a regularly fashioned utensil;--if you wanted to make it return to the original condition of its materials, would it not be difficult to do so? Could any but the Great Man accomplish this easily?
>
> Life is the follower of death, and death is the predecessor of life; but who knows the Arranger (of this connexion between them)? The life is due to the collecting of the breath. When that is collected, there is life; when it is dispersed, there is death. Since death and life thus attend on each other, why should I account (either of) them an evil?
>
> Therefore all things go through one and the same experience. (Life) is accounted beautiful because it is spirit-like and wonderful, and death is accounted ugly because of its foetor and putridity. But the foetid and putrid is transformed again into the spirit-like and wonderful, and the spirit-like and wonderful is transformed again into the foetid and putrid. Hence it is said, "All under the sky there is one breath of life, and therefore the sages prized that unity."

Four things puzzle me, and I suspect they are connected.

1. Who is the "I" in "why should I account (either of) them an evil?" The surrounding speech seems to be addressed to Knowledge in the second person — "if you wanted to make it return," and so on — and then a first person appears with no warning. Is this Hwang-Tî speaking of himself as one who has achieved the composure he is describing, or a generic "I" meaning any of us, or is the whole clause a quotation of some stock saying that the text is repeating rather than asserting? The answer seems to matter for whether the sentence expresses a conclusion the speaker has reached or merely a rhetorical question posed to the listener.

2. "The Arranger." Legge capitalizes the word and then adds a parenthetical gloss of his own, which makes me suspect he is supplying an agent the sentence does not actually name. Does the text mean that someone arranges the succession of life and death, or that there is an ordering or pattern to the succession that nobody — not even a sage — can know? These give very different pictures. If there is an Arranger, then "who knows" is rhetorical and someone does know the connexion; if there is not, then the question looks genuinely open, and I cannot see how the next sentences, which explain life and death entirely by the gathering and dispersing of breath, answer it. Also, does the breath account quietly replace the Arranger, so that the question in the first sentence is withdrawn by the explanation in the second and third?

3. The valuation problem. The speech first says that neither life nor death should be accounted an evil, and then a few lines later appears to grant exactly the ordinary ranking it has just dismissed: life is spirit-like and wonderful, death is foetid and putrid. Is that a flat contradiction, or is there something in the wording that defuses it? I notice that Legge puts "(Life)" in parentheses, as if he has had to supply the subject, and that the verb is passive — "is accounted beautiful" — which might mean the point is about how people reckon these things rather than about what they are. But if the ranking is only reported as popular opinion, why state it so precisely and so symmetrically, and then say that the two terms transform into each other? Does the reciprocity cancel the ranking, or does it preserve the ranking while making it unstable?

4. The inference in the last sentence: all under the sky is one breath, and therefore the sages prized unity. How does a claim about what everything is made of support a claim about what one ought to prize? And is that "therefore" a real inference at all, given that the whole sentence is introduced as a quotation ("Hence it is said"), which may mean the text is citing an authority rather than reasoning?

One further question, if it is not too much: does the breath account make death a dissolution back into stuff — which would tie it to the utensil sentence just before it, where a fashioned thing returns to the original condition of its materials — and if so, should death here be described as a return rather than as an ending? If it is a return, I am not sure the passage needs the "why should I account either of them an evil" line to do any work at all.

Two requests about how you answer. First, please commit rather than survey: where the text is genuinely open, say so, but where you have a view about what the passage is doing, give it and say why. Second, and more important to me, tell me what would change your mind on each of the four points — which sentence or distinction in the passage would settle it, or what would have to be true of the text behind the translation for your reading to collapse. I especially want you to flag every place where your reasoning is leaning on a feature of Legge's English (his capitalization, his parentheses, his punctuation, his choice of "Arranger" or "breath") rather than on something you can be confident about in the original, since I cannot read Chinese and cannot tell whether these puzzles are puzzles in the text or artifacts of a Victorian translator.
