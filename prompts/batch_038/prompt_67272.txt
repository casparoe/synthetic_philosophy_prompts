The question the candidate was answering was this:

*Question 3 (40 minutes). "A forgery of a Vermeer that is perceptually indistinguishable from the original must have the same aesthetic value as the original." Set out the argument for this claim and identify the premise on which it turns. Then evaluate two responses to it, saying in each case whether the response shows that the forgery is aesthetically worse, or only that it is aesthetically different.*

Here is the script, transcribed as written, with paragraph numbers added by me for reference:

(1) The claim in the question is a version of what can be called the no-aesthetic-harm thesis: a forgery that cannot be told from its original by looking at it cannot be aesthetically worse than that original. The argument is short. Aesthetic value is value a work has in respect of what can be experienced in it. If two objects are perceptually indistinguishable, nothing that can be experienced in the one is missing from the other. So whatever aesthetic value the original has, the forgery has.

(2) The premise the argument turns on is the middle one: aesthetic properties supervene on the properties available to perception, so that no difference in aesthetic value is possible without a difference in what can be seen. Note that the thesis is not that forgery is harmless all things considered. The forger lies, is usually paid for it, distorts the market, and corrupts the historical record on which scholars depend. None of that is aesthetic. The thesis says only that none of it makes the object on the wall any less worth looking at.

(3) The best-known response is Goodman's. He refuses to treat original and forgery as two simply indistinguishable things. Where a connoisseur can tell them apart, the difference is one that an untrained viewer could in principle learn to see; and once I know that there is something there to be seen, I am no longer in the position of someone faced with two merely identical objects but of someone who has to look at them differently. So there is an aesthetic difference now, before I have acquired the trained eye. Goodman's claim is in effect that the perfect forgery is impossible: there is always some way of telling, and knowing that a difference can be found is enough to make a difference. Part of his apparatus here is the division of the arts into autographic ones, where authenticity depends on a particular history of making and forgery is possible, and allographic ones, where a notation fixes what counts as a genuine instance of the work.

(4) His point is not confined to the first class, and that is where it does its most work. Take a violin finished last month in a workshop in Cremona that sounds, to the best ears in the best room, exactly like a Stradivari. Take a performance of a Bach cantata given from a score that turns out to have been fabricated in the last century. Take a poem word for word identical to one in a printed collection that someone has passed off as a newly surfaced manuscript. In each case the audience cannot tell the difference; in each case there is a difference nevertheless, and it is an aesthetic one. Anyone who denies this owes an account of what the audience is failing to hear or read.

(5) The stronger response, to my mind, is that a work's value is bound up with the achievement it represents. The Vermeer is a solution to problems set by a tradition and by a particular career; the forgery is a solution to a different problem, namely how to make something that will pass for that. The forgery is therefore inferior not because it looks any different but because it embodies a lesser achievement. This is why exposure changes people's experience of the work: those who admired van Meegeren's pieces before the trial found afterwards that they could not look at them in the same way. Their experience of the object changed, so the aesthetic value of the object changed, so aesthetic value cannot be a matter of the perceptible surface alone.

(6) The achievement response has a cost. It concedes to the defender of the thesis that nothing in the appearance of the object is worse, and it locates the difference in the making rather than in the thing made. If aesthetic value is value in respect of what can be experienced, the response has changed the subject: it establishes a lesser artistic value, which nobody denied, rather than a lesser aesthetic value. Unless the two notions come apart, the response is either trivial or beside the point; and if they do come apart, the question set has not been answered.

(7) Something similar goes for Goodman. His argument establishes at most that original and forgery differ in a respect a trained viewer can detect. Difference is not inferiority. Grant him everything and he still has not shown that the forged Vermeer is worse to look at, only that it is a different thing to look at, and that a competent viewer's experience of the two will not be the same. The question asks specifically whether the responses establish inferiority or only difference, and on that Goodman gives at best half an answer.

(8) My conclusion is that the thesis is false, because provenance is aesthetically relevant. But it is false for a reason its opponents rarely state cleanly: not that the forgery looks worse, but that the aesthetic value of a work includes the value of the achievement it embodies, and that an audience's experience is properly responsive to that. A forgery is aesthetically inferior in the way a brilliant pastiche is inferior to the manner it imitates, even when nobody can tell them apart.
