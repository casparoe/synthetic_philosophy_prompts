so I'm in my third year of phil of bio and I'm prepping my quals answer on the units of selection debate, and I've got myself wedged on something that I suspect should be easy.

here's my reasoning. for something to be a genuine unit of selection it has to be a replicator, and replicators have to make copies of themselves with high enough fidelity that the copies inherit whatever made the originals successful. genes do that. organisms arguably do it, at least in the developmental sense. but groups? groups don't churn out daughter groups the way organisms make offspring — in basically all the models people actually cite, the trait-group ones included, groups form, everyone interacts, and then the group dissolves and the individuals go back into the pool. there's no group-level reproduction to inherit anything through.

which makes me think that when people say "multilevel selection" they're just describing the same gene-level process with the covariance term sliced up differently. that would also explain why you can always drag Hamilton's rule back out of a group-structured model — if the structure is doing no real causal work at the group level, the two formalisms have to be interderivable, and my experience reading the Price equation derivations is that they always come out even.

so what I can't figure out: on that picture, what is Dawkins actually disagreeing with someone like Sober or Wilson *about*? Is it purely verbal — a fight over who gets the word "unit" — or is there an empirical question in there that the two sides would answer differently if you could run the experiment? And if it's purely verbal, how come people who should know better keep treating it as one of the big live questions in the field instead of a notational dispute?

I keep feeling like I'm missing a step, since it seems weird that a whole literature would be about nothing. can you tell me where the step is?
