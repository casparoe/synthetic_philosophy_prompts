I am a second-year master's student in a seminar on international criminal law, and I also work roughly ten hours a week on the research desk of a small documentation NGO. We publish two situation briefs a week on ongoing mass-atrocity situations, and the question the desk has never been able to answer is when we are permitted to write that genocide is occurring, has occurred, or is at serious risk. Our briefs are quoted by wire services and read into the record by a parliamentary committee, so both errors are costly in ways that pull against each other: over-labeling destroys our credibility and lets the governments we cover cast us as advocates, while under-labeling, as our director puts it, is one way of making an atrocity disappear. We also have a consistency problem. A brief last year used the word; this year, in a case that looks comparable to me, the desk declined to, and the only explanation anyone could give is that the composition of the room had changed. I would like a procedure that removes that variable.

The constraints are these. The analysts are students with no legal training. Evidence is open-source only: satellite imagery, humanitarian reporting, survivor testimony, officials' public statements, broadcast and social media. There is no subpoena power, no forensics, no access to closed intelligence. A staff lawyer reads each brief before publication but has perhaps fifteen minutes and will only approve or veto, so the reasoning has to be the analyst's. Some briefs concern ongoing situations and others are retrospective assessments of completed episodes, and I suspect the two should not be held to the same threshold.

Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions an analyst runs down the page. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer from open sources and what that answer licenses: a flat assertion, an assertion with an explicit qualifier, a formulation confined to risk, a formulation that describes the conduct without naming the crime, or no publication at all. Mark clearly where the procedure stops and hands the decision to the director because what remains is a question about our mission rather than about the facts. Be candid about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume — above all, how one infers an intent to destroy a group from pattern and rhetoric when no official says so aloud, and how one decides whether the part of a group being targeted is a substantial part.

It should also tell us what to do where the legal category and the moral or sociological one come apart, since the concept Lemkin was working with was considerably wider than the treaty definition we are bound to describe: it took in the destruction of a group's language, institutions and cultural life, and it was not limited to groups defined nationally, ethnically, racially or religiously. Specifically: may a brief say that something is genocide in the wider sense while stating that the legal elements are not made out, and if so, how do we stop readers from hearing only the first clause?

Then run the procedure on three cases and give me the operative sentence each brief would carry. I expect the second to come out against us, and I would rather see that now than discover it in print.

1. A religious minority in a besieged region. Documented killings of its members by security forces; systematic sexual violence against its women; a state broadcaster running months of dehumanizing messaging about the group; a cabinet minister recorded calling for the region to be emptied of that population; humanitarian access blocked for five months with credible reporting of starvation in the sealed towns. The government maintains it is fighting an armed insurgency embedded in the civilian population.

2. A government targets a region defined by open support for an armed opposition movement, using deliberate starvation, destruction of markets, wells and clinics, and mass forced displacement; the death toll is in the hundreds of thousands. Most of the affected population shares an ethnicity that would fall within the protected categories, but the sources we rely on describe the targeting as political — allegiance to the movement rather than ancestry — and there is no official rhetoric invoking ethnic identity at all.

3. Over a decade, a state has sterilized women of an ethnic minority without informed consent and removed a large share of its children to state boarding schools where their language is banned and their given names replaced. Deaths are few. The state describes the program as development and integration.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do even when followed correctly, and which of its steps would break first under our actual deadline and staffing. I am not looking for authorities or citations — our lawyer handles those — I need reasoning an analyst can run from the documents in front of them.
