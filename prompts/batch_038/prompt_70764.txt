I'm putting together a problem set for an intermediate game/decision theory seminar and I want one case that makes a single point land: "just eliminate the dominated strategies" isn't a determinate instruction until you say whether mixtures are allowed to do the dominating. So: design a two-player 3x3 game, wrapped in an ordinary competitive situation — two food trucks picking lunch spots, two candidates picking a message, your call, just not an abstract grid with X and Y. Small whole-number payoffs, negatives only if the story makes sense of them. The constraints I care about:

1. Nothing is strictly dominated by any *single* other strategy, for either player. A student who only ever compares pure options against pure options gets nowhere at all.
2. Each player nonetheless has exactly one option that falls, and in both cases what kills it is a mixture of their other two. Give me the weights and write out the comparison against every one of the opponent's options — don't just assert the domination.
3. Everything strict. No weak dominance anywhere, so I don't have to get into order-dependance with the class.
4. The option that dies for player 1 should be the sensible-looking middle one, the hedge. The lesson I'm after is that a built-in compromise can be strictly worse than genuinely randomizing between the two extremes.
5. Once both dead options are gone, the leftover 2x2 should have no dominance at all and a unique Nash equilibrium, in mixed strategies. Give me the probabilities, and confirm the full 3x3 has no pure equilibrium.

Then the write-up: what each player can rule out and why, the equilibrium, and — separately — a demonstration that each knocked-out option is never a best response to any belief about the opponent's play, including mixed beliefs. That's the same fact from the other direction and I want them to see it twice.
