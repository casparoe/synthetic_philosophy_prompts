student answer:

Foucault begins Discipline and Punish with the observation that within roughly a century the public execution disappeared and was replaced by the prison. The usual story is that this was moral progress: punishment became humane once we stopped taking pleasure in cruelty and started treating the offender as a person who could be improved. Foucault thinks this story is backwards. What changed was not how much suffering was involved but the target and the technique of power. On the scaffold the sovereign displayed his force on one body in front of a crowd, and that display was risky, because the crowd might side with the condemned and turn the ceremony against the authority it was supposed to celebrate. Discipline instead spreads power thinly across many bodies and many small moments: timetables, drills, ranks, exercises, examinations. Nobody has to be spectacularly violent, and yet the control is more continuous and more complete.

Power/knowledge is Foucault's name for the fact that knowledge is never neutral. The expert sciences of the individual — criminology, psychiatry, clinical medicine — develop because they serve the interests of those who hold power, and their claims about the criminal mind function as ideological cover for domination. The critic's task is therefore to look behind these sciences, show whose interests they mask, and recover the truth about punishment that they distort.

The Panopticon is Bentham's prison design: a ring of cells around a central tower, with the cells lit from behind so that the guard can see into each one while the prisoner cannot tell whether anyone is watching. Foucault's point is that modern prisons were built on this plan, and that this is what explains their success: the architecture does the work that chains and armed guards used to do, because the inmate is under continuous observation and knows it.

What discipline produces is docile bodies — bodies that are at once more useful and more obedient. The examination is the central instrument here, because it turns each person into a case that can be written down, compared and ranked against a norm, and so the individual becomes knowable and manageable in a way he had never been before. This is why Foucault says that under modern power it is the soul that imprisons the body rather than the other way round.
