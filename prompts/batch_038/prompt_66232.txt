Distinguish that account from a first-order representationalist rival, and identify the objection you regard as the most serious one against it. **A student's answer:**

> Rosenthal's transitivity principle says that a mental state is conscious just in case the subject is, in some suitable way, aware of being in that state. The principle is meant to capture the datum that what separates a conscious mental state from an unconscious one is not anything intrinsic to the state itself but the subject's awareness of it; consciousness on this picture is a relational property. This also shows why the account is a theory of mental-state rather than creature consciousness: it asks not what makes an organism awake or aware of its surroundings, but what makes one of the organism's states a conscious one. It differs from first-order representationalism in that the latter makes consciousness depend on how the state represents the world.
>
> The account is dispositionalist. On Rosenthal's view a state is conscious if the subject is disposed to think that she is in it, whether or not she actually does. This keeps consciousness cheap enough to cover the ordinary run of cases in which we are not introspecting, though it has the awkward consequence that any state a subject would report if asked is thereby a conscious state. The higher-order state is itself a kind of inner sense, a quasi-perceptual inward attention that monitors first-order states, which explains why introspection feels like a kind of looking within.
>
> The most serious objection concerns misrepresentation. A higher-order thought can occur without its target, so the theory permits a conscious experience with no underlying first-order state at all. Rosenthal can reply that the phenomenal character of an experience is fixed by what the higher-order state represents one as being in, but that reply concedes the objector's point rather than answering it.
>
> Finally, because the higher-order state must be occurrent, the theory is committed to the prefrontal cortex as the seat of consciousness, and patients whose introspective capacities are abolished by lesions there refute it.
