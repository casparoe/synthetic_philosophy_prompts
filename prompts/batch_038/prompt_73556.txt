Ok so I read an essay by a guy who did physics PhD work and now writes a philosophy-ish newsletter, about objective chance and how it's supposed to constrain what you believe. He's clearly smart and mostly sounds right to me, but I have no way of checking him and a couple of bits made me uneasy. Here's his argument compressed into one block (my condensing, but I kept his wording and his order):

"David Lewis's famous 1980 paper tried to connect objective chance — the probability that's out there in the world, in a radioactive atom or a fair coin — with the degrees of belief of a rational agent. His bridge is the Principal Principle: if you know the objective chance of some event, your credence in that event should simply equal it. That sounds like a constraint on rationality up there with 'don't believe contradictions.' But Lewis himself noticed it can't be stated that baldly. I know the chance that this coin came up heads was 50%, and I also watched it land heads; the principle as stated tells me to believe it's 50% likely that it landed heads, which is absurd. So it needs a proviso — only 'admissible' evidence counts — and once you're in the business of adding provisos, it stops looking like a law of rational belief and starts looking like a rule of thumb.

The deeper trouble is that Lewis's chances aren't primitive. They're read off the total pattern of events, past and future, by whichever system of laws is simplest and strongest. So a chance hypothesis can assign a small but genuine chance to a future that would, if it came about, make that very hypothesis false: a long enough run of heads that would make the best system say the coin isn't fair after all. Chance undermines itself. Lewis never found a fix and I don't think one exists. If you want objective chance you have to give up the idea that it's nothing over and above the pattern of events, and if you want to keep that you should drop objective chance and admit probability is just credence plus symmetry arguments. Worth adding: the Principal Principle is really van Fraassen's Reflection Principle pointed at physics instead of at your future self — defer to the expert, whoever the expert turns out to be. Lewis conceded he couldn't derive it, so in the end the whole apparatus rests on an unargued assumption."
