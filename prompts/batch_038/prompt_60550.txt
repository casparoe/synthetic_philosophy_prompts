I'm in a joint cog-sci/philosophy reading group that's spent the last six weeks on cognitive penetration of perception and we have gotten nowhere, which is weird because usually the two halves of the room at least learn something from each other. On one side you've got the modularity people, going back to Fodor's claim that early vision is informationally encapsulated from belief, plus their contemporary heirs who argue that basically every top-down effect in the literature evaporates once you control for attention, demand characteristics, low-level cueing and what subjects are doing with their eyes. On the other side you've got the theory-ladenness tradition out of Hanson and the philosophers who care about the epistemic stakes — if what you believe can change what you see, then a perceptual belief isn't an independent check on that belief, and the Siegel-style worry is that penetrated experiences get epistemically downgraded. And then there's the empirical middle: the label studies where hearing a color word changes discrimination between equally spaced hues, the face-lightness adjustment studies where faces categorized as Black get reproduced as darker than their actual luminance, the memory-color effects, all of which the first camp says is just perceptual learning or attention wearing a cognitive costume.

Here's the thing that made me want to ask you rather than just re-read the same arguements. I've been designing a version of the label paradigm for my own project and it produced a result that I can't tell whether it helps either side. Participants learn two novel nonsense labels for two regions of a hue continuum over two days, with feedback, and then do a four-alternative odd-one-out with target and distractors held at constant perceptual distance in a color space. We get a boundary-shaped discrimination advantage at the trained cut, which is the standard finding. But we also ran a control where the label pairing is scrambled in the cover story — a third of subjects are told the wrong pairing and genuinely report believing it — and the advantage shows up exactly the same in those subjects. So the effect tracks the training history, not the current belief. Our psych person says that settles it, it's just perceptual learning, nothing cognitive penetrated anything, and our philosopher says that's a cheap win because the whole point of penetration was never supposed to require a belief you can report on demand at the moment of viewing, and anyway learning-driven changes are precisely the diachronic case the encapsulation thesis was built to swallow. Nobody moved. I should also mention a smaller detail that I can't decide is important: in the online cueing version, where a label is flashed before the array instead of being trained in, we get nothing — and I remember one of those color-word papers reporting the same asymmetry, that a visual preview of the target color did less work than the word did, which strikes me as either a big deal or an artifact.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? My working hypothesis — treat it as a hypothesis and push on it if it's wrong — is that the room is running two different debates under one label, and that the word "cognitive" is doing double duty, meaning something like "belief-like, semantically evaluable, potentially irrational" for the philosophers and "not driven by the retinal input plus hardwired early filters" for the vision scientists, so that the same experiment can be a refutation for one camp and irrelevant for the other. If that's right I want to know where the seam is exactly, and whether there's a residual substantive disagreement left over once you pull the two apart — something that would still be a genuine fight about how visual processing is organized, rather than about whether an experience can be epistemically downgraded. And if you think I'm wrong, tell me what the real fault line is: competing standards about which effects count as data at all, a hidden empirical question nobody has actually tested (like whether any effect survives when the learning history and the current belief are pulled apart in both directions), or bedrock intuitions about what it would take for a mental state to be "the right kind" of state to see with. Finally, and this is the part I care most about for the group: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress in the next six sessions — a stipulated definition everybody signs up front, a paradigm that separates the diachronic from the online case, a formal result, a change of question, or should we just accept that this one is structurally unresolvable and say why? Please don't give me a survey of the positions, I've read the survey versions twice already.
