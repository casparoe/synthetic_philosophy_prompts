Retired three years ago from fisheries stock assessment — 35 years of trawl surveys, length-frequency tables, the whole dismal bussiness of estimating how many fish are in a patch of ocean you can never actually see. Only recently got into philosophy properly, mostly self-taught with a reading group that meets in a pub. We're on Peirce's 1878 "Illustrations of the Logic of Science" series and I'm stuck hard on a bit of the sixth paper, "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis" (Popular Science Monthly, 1878) — the bean bag business.

One housekeeping note: the free transcription I'm reading eats the fractions (it prints "23" where the original had two-thirds), so I've put those back in square brackets. Everything else below is verbatim.

Peirce first writes out an ordinary deduction about a bag of beans:

"Rule.—[Two-thirds of] the beans in this bag are white.
Case.—This bean has been drawn in such a way that in the long run the relative number of white beans so drawn would be equal to the relative number in the bag.
Result.—This bean has been drawn in such a way that in the long run it would turn out white [two-thirds] of the time."

Then he turns it around:

"If, however, not knowing what proportion of white beans there are in the bag, we draw a handful at random and, finding [two-thirds] of the beans in the handful white, conclude that about [two-thirds] of those in the bag are white, we are rowing up the current of deductive sequence, and are concluding a rule from the observation of a result in a certain case."

and: "So that induction is the inference of the rule from the case and result."

Later he gives the general gloss: "Induction is where we generalize from a number of cases of which something is true, and infer that the same thing is true of a whole class. Or, where we find a certain thing to be true of a certain proportion of cases and infer that it is true of the same proportion of the whole class."

What I need help with:

1. That "Case" line is doing something I don't understand. It isn't "this bean came out of the bag." It's a claim about the long-run behaviour of the drawing procedure. Why would he state the premise that way? Is "drawn in such a way that..." a fact about my hand, about the bag, or about a protocol I've adopted — and how could anybody know it? In my working life we never got that premise for free. A survey vessel is a machine for making a stubbornly non-random sample behave *as if* it were random: we towed where the fish were, we fished by daylight, our nets let the small ones out, and then we spent years arguing about corrections. So when Peirce writes the case as a long-run equality, is he smuggling in the uniformity of nature, or is he assuming something much narrower and more local — a design property of the sampling rather than a metaphysics of the world?

2. "Rowing up the current of deductive sequence" — what exactly licenses the inversion? He also inverts a different way to get hypothesis (inferring which bag the handful came from). Why is the rule-inversion the one we call knowledge and the case-inversion the one we call a guess, when structurally they look like the same move?

3. The thing I actually want settled. My niece does machine learning, and when I showed her this she said Peirce is simply dead in the water because of the no-free-lunch results: averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected error, so no rule for going from sample to whole is better than any other, and "drawn at random" isn't a property you can appeal to because the averaging already includes worlds that punish it. Her line was that the Case premise is the conclusion wearing a hat.

So: is she reading the passage right? Does the no-free-lunch result contradict what Peirce is doing here, or does it just make explicit that his Case line is a substantive assumption rather than a freebie — with the two of them then talking past each other? I'd like a concrete anti-inductive bag if you can build one: a bag where the Case line fails, e.g. one whose composition quietly shifts to make my sample misleading. Then tell me whether Peirce has excluded that bag by argument or only by stipulation, and whether a real stock assessment ever faces one.

4. Related, and I suspect this is where my niece slides: "in the long run." Peirce's long run is over repeated draws from one bag; the no-free-lunch average is over bags (or target functions) at a single shot. Are those even the same kind of averaging, and does anything follow about one from the other?

Please do this as a plain-language walk through the passage first — I'm new at this, so define any term you need rather than assuming I know it — then a short list of exactly which claims the passage is committed to, and finally, for each of those claims, whether the no-free-lunch business touches it, misses it, or only looks like it touches it.
