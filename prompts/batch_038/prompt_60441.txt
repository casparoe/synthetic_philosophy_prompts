I serve as counsel to a nine-member statute revision panel in a mid-sized common-law jurisdiction; our standing remit is to sweep the criminal code for offenses that no longer earn their place. One of them is a residual catch-all, in substance: whoever does an act tending to outrage public decency, knowing it to have that tendency, is liable on indictment. For sixty years it has functioned as the code's home for conduct whose wrongness is thought to lie in what it expresses rather than in what it does to anyone in particular. Last autumn it was tested in a way nobody anticipated. A municipal gallery hosted a touring exhibition of plastinated human cadavers — every donor had consented in writing — and, in an adjoining room, a digital installation of generated video loops depicting acts that would amount to the desecration of a body if performed on an actual corpse. There was no corpse, no identifiable living person, and no non-consensual likeness of anyone real. The prosecutor declined to charge, reasoning that the offense presupposes something real; a coalition of residents' associations then petitioned the panel to recommend that the offense be clarified so as to reach the simulated material.

The coalition's argument is not that anyone was harmed. It is that this community holds the dead to be inviolable, that the holding-together of a society consists in convictions of that kind, and that a simulated desecration attacks the conviction itself; as evidence they point out that a substantial share of gallery visitors reported distress of precisely the kind they say they would have felt on encountering the real thing. The gallery's counsel replies that no one was harmed and no one was wronged, and that the visitors' distress is a fact about the visitors rather than about the work. Two colleagues on the panel want to split the difference and treat the material as a regulated nuisance — restricted hours, screening, an age limit — instead of criminalizing it; a third insists that if the distress is real and foreseeable, the case is already inside a harm-based framework and no new principle is being invoked at all. The enabling instrument complicates things further. The panel may recommend retention only where the conduct reached offends the settled moral convictions of the community and there is no other adequate means of addressing the mischief, and nobody has ever defined settled. One commissioner says a conviction is settled if a durable majority holds it now; another says it is settled only if it has run continuously across generations and still survives being stated plainly and defended out loud; a third wants an intensity test, under which what matters is whether ordinary people react with indignation rather than mere queasiness. Those tests will not always agree, and the installation is exactly a case where they come apart: the conviction about the dead looks old and continuous, but I doubt a majority would report indignation rather than revulsion at a video loop.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the panel before its March sitting. It must come down on one of four outcomes — retain the offense unchanged; retain it with a defined test (in which case give me the elements, in the form drafting instructions could carry); repeal it; or repeal and replace it with a regulatory scheme (say what the scheme actually does and what it leaves unprotected) — and it must give reasons the petitioning coalition could genuinely answer rather than reasons that merely restate the prosecutor's position. Take their strongest form seriously: the claim that a society's morality is not a detachable list of rules that could be revised without the society ceasing to be the same society, so that conduct can damage it without damaging any individual, and so that a convincing simulation of the conduct can damage it too. Then be explicit about how you are reading that claim — whether, pressed, it collapses into a claim about harm after all, or whether it is a claim that the harm-based framework is simply incomplete, since the two readings license different recommendations and the panel should know which one it is being asked to adopt. Say which of the three readings of settled you would write into the instructions, and what the installation case shows about the other two. Close with a short transitional paragraph covering what the panel should tell prosecutors and the gallery in the interval before any amendment could take effect. Roughly 1,300 words. No citations, footnotes, or reading list: two lay members sit on the panel and have never studied jurisprudence, so the reasoning has to carry itself. You may name the mid-century exchange between these two positions if it helps orient the reader, but nothing in the memo should turn on knowing who the participants were.
