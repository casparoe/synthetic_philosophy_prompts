Next month I am giving a forty-minute paper, with twenty minutes for discussion, at an interdisciplinary workshop on testimony and trust. What I need is a preparation sheet for the question period — not a defence of the view, and not the objections it would draw from a general philosophy audience.

The thesis. I argue that the standard data for the knowledge norm of assertion — the oddity of asserting something while disavowing knowledge of it, the standing availability of the challenge "how do you know?", the impropriety of asserting what a held lottery ticket will do — are genuine but misdescribed. On my account, in asserting that p to a particular hearer one warrants that the hearer may feed p into practical deliberation without checking it independently, and the strength of warrant required is fixed by two features of the hearer rather than by anything in the speaker's mind alone: what the hearer stands to lose if p turns out false, and how costly independent verification would be for that hearer. Consequences I accept and will state openly: propriety is relative to the audience, so one and the same token utterance can be proper to one hearer and improper to another; knowledge is neither necessary nor sufficient, since one may properly assert what one does not know to a hearer who will verify anyway, and improperly assert what one does know to a hearer who cannot check and would act irreversibly; the challenge "how do you know?" is a demand to display the grounds that make unchecked reliance safe, not a demand to display knowledge as such; and where the audience is indeterminate — a broadcast, a public-health notice, a sentence that can be lifted out of context — the norm defaults to the most exposed member of the intended audience, which I take to explain why official communication is systematically more guarded than any individual official's evidence requires.

The room. Roughly twenty-five people. The core are analytic epistemologists who treat the norms of assertion as constitutive rules of a practice and who are invariantists about knowledge; several are weary of the stakes-sensitivity literature and will hear my view as pragmatic encroachment arriving by another route. Two are knowledge-first, and hold that the knowledge norm is a starting point rather than a hypothesis to be tested against case intuitions. Two linguists work on evidential marking and speech-act semantics; one has published on assertions that openly disclose an imperfect source. Two legal scholars work on negligent misstatement, professional advice, and reasonable reliance; they will find my account obvious, which is itself a danger, because if it is merely the law of advice restated then it is not a theory of assertion at all. The remainder are graduate students in science communication, interested chiefly in the broadcast case.

What I want. Eight to ten objections in the order they will actually arrive, ranked by the damage each would do in that room rather than by philosophical interest. For each: put it in the vocabulary of the sub-audience that will raise it; give a reply I can deliver aloud in about ninety seconds; mark where that reply is soft or quietly gives up ground; and say whether I should concede the point during the talk in order to defuse it. Flag any objections that turn out to be the same objection in two vocabularies, since the legal and the epistemological versions may converge and I would rather meet them once. Note anywhere my own framing above is likely to be heard as a claim I am not making. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, which objection I am hoping nobody raises together with the least bad answer to it, and what I should cut from the talk to buy time for the discussion.
