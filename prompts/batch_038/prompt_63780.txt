I'm not an academic. I help run a small mutual-aid fund in my town — five of us, a few thousand dollars a quarter, decisions made mostly by arguing — and a while back I fell down an online rabbit hole about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and came out the other side holding a position I've been cheerfully defending ever since. Roughly: if a group's own ranking of prospects is rational in the expected-utility sense, if every member's ranking is rational in that same sense, and if the group can't break a tie that every single member is indifferent about, then the group's ranking has to be a positive weighted sum of the members' utilities. So summing welfare isn't one moral theory competing with others — it's what you get forced into by arithmetic plus a few conditions that sound almost too weak to argue with. On this picture, being a utilitarian isn't a substantive ethical commitment, and refusing it is basically refusing to be coherent. I want you to treat that as my actual position and tell me where it's exposed.

So: what would count against it? Concretely, not "these are idealizations." For each condition, what would a real decision problem have to look like for it to fail, and which of those failures are live options rather than just logically possible — because a group like ours is small, we disagree about probabilities constantly, and I half-remember someone saying something about everyone having to share the same beliefs over states for the "ex ante" version of the argument to go through, and trouble when they don't. Is that right, and how much damage does it do? Then there's the weighting thing I can't shake: the theorem gives positive weights, but does it give equal ones, or does equality need an extra symmetry condition? If it needs one, is that condition carrying all the moral weight I thought the theorem was carrying, and how defensible is it? Related and more troubling to me personally — each person's utility numbers are only pinned down up to some rescaling and shifting, so where each person's zero sits seems to matter enormously to the sum. One of our members is clinically depressed and rates everything low; another is relentlessly upbeat and rates everything high. Does the theorem have anything at all to say about whose scale counts, or is it presupposing that interpersonal comparisons have already been handed to it? If the latter, isn't that the "it assumes what it proves" objection, and how do people who like the theorem respond?

I also want to know what accepting it costs me that rivals don't pay — prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, rights-based views, Rawls's maximin choice from behind a veil, which as far as I can tell is the same kind of setup as Harsanyi's impartial observer but reaches a different answer, and I'd like to understand what small difference between them does all that work. Are there cases where the weighted-sum verdict is flatly unacceptable and a defender can't just shrug? And please give me an honest read on whether the position has been qualified into safety: people seem to retreat to "it's about rationality, not morality" or "the weights are whatever the social preference happens to be," and I can't tell if that rescues the view or empties it. Rank the defeaters — fatal, serious, merely awkward — and tell me which ones a defender of the theorem would themselves concede as counting against them and which they'd swat away. Last thing, and the one I care about most practically: forty grant applications on the table and no way to put numbers on anyone's welfare — does the theorem have any operational content for a group actually deciding, or is the honest answer that it constrains the shape of a social ranking without helping you produce one? No bibliography or reading list, I'm not going to chase papers down. And please don't just tell me it depends which axioms you pick — tell me which axioms are genuinely up for grabs and what the world would have to look like.
