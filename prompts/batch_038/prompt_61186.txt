I am in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD; my dissertation concerns first-person authority and the metaphysics of phenomenal character, and one chapter has gradually mutated from a defense of what I take to be the standard reading of Dennett's 1988 paper "Quining Qualia" into something considerably more ambivalent. My reading group has been going over the paper this term, and a disagreement has surfaced that I have not been able to resolve to my own satisfaction: not about whether the paper's arguments succeed, but about what kind of conclusion they are entitled to, and consequently what their target actually is. I would like you to write an essay on that controversy, and to come down firmly on one side of it.

The controversy can be framed as a choice between two readings. On the first, which I will call the error-theoretic reading, the paper identifies a reasonably determinate folk posit — experiences characterized as intrinsic, private, ineffable, and directly or immediately apprehensible — and argues that nothing satisfies that characterization, so that a substantial body of ordinary and philosophical claims about experience come out false or at least systematically misleading. On the second, the diagnostic reading, no such determinate posit is available to be refuted: the term never fixed a unified kind in the first place, and the paper's thought experiments are best seen as showing that the concept loses its grip precisely where it was designed to do work, so that the upshot is not that a claim is false but that there was no well-formed claim to assess. The material that seems to me to drive the choice is the case of the two coffee tasters — as I reconstruct it, one reports that the coffee tastes exactly as it always did but that he has come to dislike it, while the other reports that he likes it just as much as ever but that it no longer tastes the way it used to — together with the inverted-spectrum-style scenarios in which a subject wakes to find colors apparently transposed and cannot determine whether the transposition lies in present experience or in the memories and comparative dispositions used to detect it. In both sorts of case the interesting claim is not that we are ignorant of a fact about which component shifted, but that it is hard to see what would constitute such a fact. My worry is that this cuts both ways: it looks like support for the diagnostic reading, since the concept is failing to deliver determinate verdicts, but it also looks like an implicit substantive premise — roughly, that there is no phenomenal difference without a difference in discriminative and reactive dispositions — and if that premise is doing the work, then the paper is not analyzing our concept but replacing it with a functionalist explication in something like the Carnapian sense, which would make the eliminativist conclusion a piece of revisionary metaphysics rather than a discovery about what we meant all along.

So: please write an essay of roughly 2,500 words, pitched at a specialist audience, that weighs these two readings against each other and reaches a verdict. I would like you to say explicitly which reading the argumentative strategy of the coffee-taster and spectrum cases better supports, and to press on at least three points: whether the "no phenomenal difference without a functional difference" premise is a neutral conceptual observation or a loaded metaphysical thesis, and if loaded, whether importing it makes the conclusion question-begging against a realist who is happy to grant that undetectable phenomenal differences are not differences in experience while insisting that such differences could not obtain anyway; whether the error-theoretic reading is even coherent given that it seems to require the very phenomenal vocabulary it disowns in order to state what has gone wrong — a recoil problem that I suspect also threatens the diagnostic reading, though perhaps less severely, since the latter need not claim that anything is false; and whether the distinction between the two readings actually matters downstream, for instance for how one should respond to the knowledge argument, the zombie argument, or the explanatory gap, or whether it is an intra-mural nicety with no dialectical consequences. A conditional verdict is perfectly acceptable — something along the lines of holding that the diagnostic reading is correct with respect to some of the arguments and the error-theoretic reading with respect to others, provided you say which is which and why — but please do not end by shrugging and declaring the debate merely verbal in a way that leaves the substantive question untouched. Where the exegesis is uncertain, work with the arguments as I have characterized them rather than hewing closely to the paper's exact phrasing, and feel free to construct additional cases of your own if they sharpen the issue. I am not looking for citations, a bibliography, or a survey of the secondary literature; I want the argument itself, and a defended conclusion.
