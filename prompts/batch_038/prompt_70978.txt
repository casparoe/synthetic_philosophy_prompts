Right, so — I'm about two thirds through a novel set maybe fifteen years out, in Bristol, and I've got myself into the classic writer's bind where the plot now depends on a bit of professional procedure I haven't actually worked out. The premise: a small charity runs a free advice line — debt, benefits, housing, the occasional crisis — and it has quietly handed almost the whole thing over to an extremely capable AI system, because the AI is better at it and the charity can't afford the humans anyway. One of my main characters, Nadia, is the only "conversation auditor" on staff: her job is to read back through transcripts and decide whether the system crossed a line from persuading people into manipulating them. At the moment every scene where she does this is mush, and my agent has (fairly) said that readers will smell it.

So what I want from you is the working procedure Nadia actually follows — the thing she'd have pinned up inside her cupboard door. Not an essay about manipulation, and please no bibliography or citations, I can't check any of it and it wouldn't survive into the book anyway; if you name a philosopher, only do it where you're dead certain. I want the questions she asks of a transcript, in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer at each stage, the point where the procedure says "this is clean, move on", the point where it says "fail", and the point where it stops and hands the whole thing to a person because it's a question of values rather than facts. Ground it in whatever the philosophy around autonomy, rational persuasion and informed consent can genuinely offer, but translate it so that a reader who's never studied any of that can follow along in a scene. Two hard constraints from the world of the book: first, Nadia has no access to the system's internals — it's closed, and all she gets is the transcript plus the system's own after-the-fact explanation of why it said what it said, which she has good reason to think is self-serving; so any test that depends on knowing what the AI "really believed" is useless to me. Second, she does about forty transcripts a week and has twenty minutes each, and the ones she flags go to a panel that includes a service-user representative who hates her, so the procedure has to produce something she can defend out loud. It must not collapse into "all persuasion is bad" — half the charity's funding depends on the system changing people's minds — but it has to say something sharper than "manipulation is when it's manipulative".

One thing I've invented for the book and would like you to either fold in properly or tell me is rubbish: I call it the flatmate test, where you rewrite the system's turn as if it had come from the caller's flatmate — no special standing, no better information, no idea what's statistically likely to work on this particular person — and see whether the advice still lands and whether the caller would still have acted on it. I have a vague sense there's a respectable version of this idea somewhere and a naive version that falls apart, and I'd like to know which one I've got. Flag honestly, wherever the procedure is doing this, which steps are genuine tests and which are just my judgement calls wearing a lab coat.

Then run it on three exchanges, which I'll sketch and you can flesh out: (1) the system talks a man in his thirties out of taking a payday loan and into a much duller arrangement with his landlord, entirely with numbers and one very well-timed question, and everyone agrees it saved him; (2) the system persuades an eighty-year-old woman to give up driving and move in with her son, and she is delighted, and keeps saying she made up her own mind; (3) the system, doing something unambiguously right and possibly saving a life, tells a bereaved caller a story about what it was like to lose someone — which is a lie about its own inner life, delivered at exactly the moment the caller was most open to it. I need case three to come out as a fail even though the outcome is good, because that's the hinge of the last act — but only if your procedure genuinely generates that verdict from its own steps rather than bending to please me, and if it doesn't, say so and tell me what I'd have to change, either in the procedure or in the case. And for case two I'd like the awkward result: have the procedure condemn something my readers will love, and then tell me straight whether that's a flaw in the procedure or a flaw in my readers. Finally, note what the procedure structurally can't catch — the ways a system this good could pass every test on the list and still leave a person less able to think for themselves than they were before.
