Analyze the Suez Crisis of 1956 as a case study in reference-class selection and the outside view. I know the narrative well — Nasser's nationalization of the Canal Company in July, the Franco-British-Israeli collusion, the landings in early November, the run on sterling, Washington's refusal to let Britain draw on the IMF, the Soviet threats, and Eden's resignation in January — so please do not spend any of your space retelling it. What interests me is the epistemics of the forecasts, chiefly British but also French, that were made in the months before the invasion.

Five tasks, to be answered as five numbered items:

1. Identify the predictions that actually carried the decision — how quickly Nasser's government would fall, whether the operation could be finished before the world could react, how the United States would respond, whether Britain's reserves could survive a prolonged crisis — and characterize what the inside-view case for each looked like given the evidence available in autumn 1956.

2. Reconstruct the reference classes that were on offer at that moment and the base rates they would have yielded. I have in mind, at minimum, the post-1945 attempts by European powers to reverse a nationalist government's assertion of sovereignty by force or economic pressure: the Dutch in Indonesia, the French in Indochina, and Britain's own failure in Iran in 1951 alongside its partial success there in 1953. Say what each class implies, and where the classes point in different directions.

3. Apply a distinction I am stipulating for this exercise rather than borrowing from the literature. Call a forecast *class-robust* when several defensible reference classes point the same way, and *class-fragile* when its accuracy depends on which of several equally defensible classes the analyst happened to select. Sort the principal British predictions on that scale, and say whether 1956 was a failure of class-fragility, of never consulting a class at all, or of consulting one and then overriding it with inside detail.

4. State where the outside view goes silent. A base-rate method might license the claim that the intervention would fail without licensing any claim about how it would fail, when, or at what price; and the Iranian outcome of 1953 shows that at least one member of the natural class came out the other way. Name what the theory cannot deliver here instead of pressing it into service.

5. Give your verdict on what the episode does to the broader project of forecasting imperial decline from base rates — whether 1956 is a data point in a long-run pattern of the kind posited by Spenglerian cycles or the cliodynamic program, or whether decision-scale base rates and civilizational-scale ones are separate enterprises that no single episode can join.

Please give exactly five numbered items, corresponding to the five tasks, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. If you find yourself wanting more room, be more selective rather than longer.
