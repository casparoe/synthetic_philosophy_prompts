I teach an upper-level philosophy of physics seminar — fourteen students, a single hundred-minute session. They will already have worked through the Everettian literature on probability: the incoherence and quantitative problems, the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic program and the standing charge that it is circular, the Saunders–Wallace appeal to subjective uncertainty after branching, Vaidman's talk of the measure of existence of a world, and Greaves's suggestion that what a rational Everettian agent requires is not a credence function but a caring measure. They can recite all of this fluently. What they cannot yet do is feel where the pressure actually lies.

I want an in-class game that makes the architecture of the problem tangible — above all, one that drives them into discovering for themselves that the Born rule is not forced on them by the branching structure, but sits downstream of a prior normative commitment about how much an agent's future successors ought to matter to her now.

The device I have in mind, which I want you to build out in full working detail: before any measurement round is played, the class must collectively adopt a rule for distributing a player's payoff across her post-measurement successors — equal shares among all branches irrespective of weight, shares proportional to squared amplitude, winner-take-all in the highest-weight branch, or some rule they devise themselves. Only then are they permitted to bet. Subsequent rounds should demonstrate that their earlier choices would have come out differently under a different distribution rule, and that they possess no argument for preferring one rule over another which does not already assume the conclusion.

Please supply the following.

- A minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes.
- The exact branch structure for each round — how many branches, what weights — together with a method for resolving each round in the room using nothing more elaborate than a die or a coin.
- The full text of the betting slips or cards the students complete, written so that a student who has missed the reading can still participate.
- At least one round engineered so that branch count and branch weight come apart dramatically, exposing first the intuitive pull of counting and then its collapse.
- One round conducted after the split but before the outcome is revealed, so that students can interrogate whether their own post-branching hesitation behaves like ordinary ignorance about a fact of the matter.
- A credit system carrying real but trivially small stakes — no money, nothing that could reasonably be called coercive — with a settlement procedure the students can audit without my intervention.

Then the debrief. It must map specific moments in the game onto specific moves in the literature: where the quantitative problem surfaced, where the circularity charge actually bit, and where the subjective-uncertainty and caring-measure readings genuinely diverge in what a player would do with her credits. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect at least one student to accuse the setup of smuggling in collapse, and at least one to find an arbitrage between distribution rules — and how I should handle each failure rather than talk past it.

Two further points I want stated plainly rather than hedged. First: which side of the debate do your rules quietly favour, and precisely what would I have to alter to render the game neutral between the caring-measure and the self-locating-uncertainty readings? Second: identify the feature of the Everettian situation that the game cannot reproduce — in particular, that a squared-amplitude weight is not a probability over mutually exclusive outcomes, since no branch fails to occur — and tell me what I must supply in speech that the mechanics will not deliver on their own.
