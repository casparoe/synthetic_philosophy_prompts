I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on reasons and motivation, and I need help drafting one essay question on the internal/external reasons debate. The course spends three weeks on Williams's 1979 account — the claim that an agent has a reason to act only if he could reach that action by a sound deliberative route from his actual subjective motivational set, together with his diagnosis of external reason statements as failed attempts to motivate — and then two weeks on the standard replies: the charge that any internalism worth having must idealize the agent's psychology, that the idealization either collapses back into externalism or is arbitrarily calibrated, and that the thesis cannot accommodate cases in which an agent recognises a reason that has no hook whatsoever in what he currently cares about. My problem is that the questions I have set in previous years (roughly, "explain and assess the internal reasons thesis") were answered almost entirely by competent recitation: students reproduced the labels, quoted the S-notation back at me, and scored well without ever having to decide anything. I want a question whose crux is a decision they cannot look up.

The question should be anchored in a case I want you to write up tightly, and the case should have a wrinkle that is not in the standard literature. Here is the seed: a hospital runs a disclosed, consented-to "resilience programme" for nurses, built out of operant conditioning, scripted reflection exercises, and small monetary rewards; a mid-career nurse completes it and emerges genuinely wanting to take the double shifts she previously refused, and she can describe the programme's techniques in detail, remembers exactly what she was like before it, and would have declined the shifts on the old psychology. I want the question to force candidates to rule on whether she now has a reason to take the double shifts, and — the part that should separate the answers — whether it makes any difference to the existence of that reason that she knows how the desire got there and retains the memory of her earlier self. Candidates should have to say what work the sound-deliberative-route clause is doing in a case like this: whether sound deliberation *from* an installed desire can confer a reason, whether the notion of soundness imports evaluative standards the agent need not endorse, and whether the fact of her prior consent does any philosophical work at all on Williams's analysis, given that consent leaves S untouched. If the honest answer is that the genealogical knowledge is irrelevant to the internalist, I want the model answer to say so and then explain what that costs the view; if it is not irrelevant, I want the answer to identify precisely which clause of the thesis does the work. Please do not let the question be answerable by simply siding with the internalist or the externalist and repeating that side's best-known objection.

Deliverables: first the exam question itself, which must be self-contained and no longer than 150 words, since candidates sit it under closed-book conditions with no texts in front of them (so it must contain every premise it needs, and must not ask them to reproduce or cite any published argument); second, a model answer of at most 600 words, written as a candidate would write it, without a bibliography and without leaning on any source a student could not reconstruct from understanding alone; third, a marking scheme as a table, out of 25 marks, listing five must-hit moves with the marks each carries and a one-line note on how candidates characteristically get each one wrong — the notes should name real errors, such as glossing internalism as Humeanism about motivation, reading S as a list of desires rather than a broader set of commitments and projects, taking "external reason" to mean a reason independent of the agent's beliefs rather than of his motivations, or treating the idealization reply as though it defeated internalism rather than relocating the problem. Finally, add three short bullets identifying answers that should be capped at a C or below and why. Keep the entire response under 1,200 words.
