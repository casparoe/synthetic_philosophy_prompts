I'm three years into a novel set in a commodity trading house in a second-tier port city, and the whole thing turns on one scene in the last third: a trader named Hallam takes the other side of a very large position from someone he respects, both of them convinced they're the one getting the better deal, and neither of them needing the money or trying to unload risk. To get the mechanics right I've been paying two readers. One is Ravi, an economist who works on information and markets; the other is Dana, who ran a small proprietary desk for about fifteen years and now consults. I had them both over for dinner in November to go through the chapter, we got through maybe two-thirds of a bottle each of something too young, and instead of notes on my prose they spent ninety minutes trying to destroy each other. I've written the argument down as best I can from what I scribbled and what I remember — some of the phrasing is mine, not theirs, so please don't get hung up on exact wording, just the claims.

Here's how it went. (1) Ravi said the scene isn't merely implausible, it's impossible: if Hallam and his counterparty started from the same beliefs about the world and both update honestly, then the bare fact that the other guy is willing to take the other side is itself evidence that ought to move him, and once that's common knowledge between them their views have to converge, so a trade motivated purely by private information unravels before it's struck — he said it's a theorem, not a hunch, and that any trade which does go through must be somebody hedging or raising cash, not betting on what they know. (2) Dana said that's the least trade-shaped sentence she's ever heard, that for two decades she made money on deals where both sides left happy, and that if the theorem were true the entire industry would be a rounding error; she added that most orders reach a desk for reasons that have nothing to do with anybody's opinion — a pension fund reballancing, a fund meeting redemptions, a shipping company locking in fuel costs — so a big order on your screen tells you close to nothing. (3) Ravi said fine, that's precisely what the result allows, those are non-speculative trades, and his claim is only about information-driven trade between people who know each other to be rational; he then volunteered that the assumption of common knowledge of rationality is very strong and probably false of actual humans, and that for a novelist this might even be useful, since you can write characters who lack it. (4) Dana said that concession empties the thing out: if it only bites when an assumption no real person meets, it's a statement about Ravi's model rather than about markets, and invoking it to veto her chapter is like refusing to describe a bridge because the steel is assumed massless. (5) Ravi said there's a version that survives without common knowledge because prices do the work — if the order flow already moved the price, then the price contains a chunk of what the informed party knows, so a rational counterparty facing someone who insists on trading should widen his quotes or walk away, which is the adverse-selection story behind the bid-ask spread and is measurable rather than philosophical. (6) Dana said so it's the winner's curse, the oldest rule on any desk, wearing a lab coat; and since the market maker gets paid for it in the spread, the "impossible" trade happens continuously at a price, which she took to settle it. (7) Ravi said not quite: if you could keep widening spreads until trading felt safe, then prices would end up reflecting everything anyone had bothered to find out, and then nobody would bother finding anything out, because digging up information is expensive and this would leave it unrewarded — so markets have to rest somewhere in between, where being better informed pays just enough to cover the cost of getting informed; and he said my chapter, in which Hallam reads everything he needs off the tape in a single afternoon, breaks that.

They wouldn't put any of it in writing, so you're stuck with me as the court reporter. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step — not who wins on points overall, but for each of the seven numbered claims, whether it's flat wrong, right for the wrong reasons, or actually correct, and which assumption is doing the work in it? I'd specifically like to know whether the theorem Ravi is invoking really does forbid my scene as I've described it, given that Hallam and the counterparty are not hedging and not short of cash but do know each other well and each thinks the other is sharp; whether Dana's objection in (4) is a real limitation or just a philosopher's complaint about models that Ravi is entitled to shrug off; whether (5) and (6) are actually the same claim in different clothes, since that's the point where I stopped being able to follow; and whether (7) is a genuine constraint on how much a character can learn from watching prices or a slide into something vaguer. Then tell me what both of them missed entirely — I suspect at least one of them is fighting a straw version of the other — and finish with a plain answer about whether the chapter can stay as written or what the smallest change would be that makes it defensible. I have no economics training beyond what's in the argument above, so define a term the first time you use it, and please don't send me a reading list or references, I can't chase citations and would rather you just reason it out in your own words.
