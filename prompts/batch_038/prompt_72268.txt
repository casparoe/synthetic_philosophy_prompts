I sit on the graduate curriculum committee of a mid-sized history department, and I have been asked to write a short justification of our field structure for an external review. Preparing it has made me conscious that our administrative machinery embodies a philosophy of history that nobody in the building has ever articulated, and that I would like to see reconstructed and then assessed. Here are the relevant features of the practice.

1. Our fields, hiring lines, qualifying examinations, and job advertisements are defined by period: ancient, medieval, early modern, modern. A candidate is a medievalist; a line is a medieval line.

2. Every graduate course carries a century-range tag in the catalogue, and every dissertation must be certified as falling primarily within one field. A student working across, say, 1350 to 1750 needs two period advisers, and both are expected to agree in advance which of the two periods the work principally belongs to.

3. Our survey courses speak as though periods had characters — a medieval outlook, Renaissance individualism, an early modern state — while our seminars teach, and our specialist scholarship depends on, the claim that these labels are retrospective inventions of later partisans: that the Middle Ages were named by people who needed a foil for their own self-image, and that the Scientific Revolution is largely a twentieth-century coinage.

4. Field names have been renegotiated within living memory. Much of what was called Renaissance studies now calls itself early modern studies, and a senior colleague has been arguing for years for something like the position Jacques Le Goff defended in his final book, published posthumously in English translation in 2015 as *Must We Divide History into Periods?* — that the Middle Ages should be stretched well past their conventional endpoint, with the various renaissances treated as internal episodes rather than as a single break. Nobody proposed changing the field structure in response to either development.

5. There is now a live proposal to replace period fields with two cross-cutting axes: source-language and palaeographic competence, and a list of standing problems (records and record-keeping; plague and population; migration and settlement). Its authors describe it as purely administrative, a matter of scheduling and exam coverage. Its opponents describe it as an attack on the discipline. Both sides insist they are making no metaphysical claim.

What I would like from you:

(a) Reconstruct the conception of historical time and of historical kinds that this practice presupposes. In particular: does the requirement that a dissertation be primarily in one period commit us to treating periods as a partition — mutually exclusive, jointly exhaustive, with determinate membership for particular events and lives — rather than as a set of overlapping, cross-cutting lenses any one of which may be more or less apt for a given question? And does the apparatus commit us to periods being real in some robust sense (natural kinds, historical individuals, causal or cultural unities), or can every one of these rules be justified instrumentally, by the division of cognitive labour, by source languages, and by the physical geography of archives, without any ontological commitment whatsoever?

(b) Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Which parts would be unintelligible under an instrumentalist account, which are simply more convenient under a realist one, and where is the practice underdetermined — several incompatible theories of periodization licensing exactly the same rules?

(c) Address the apparent inconsistency in (3), if it is one. Can a department assert period essences in teaching and deny them in research without contradiction — by treating period talk as a useful fiction, by distinguishing an orientation pedagogy from an explanatory epistemology, or some other way? Tell me which resolutions are genuinely available and what each costs.

(d) Say what the renaming in (4) reveals. Does the move to early modern presuppose that period labels ought to be evaluatively neutral and descriptive rather than interpretive and value-laden, or that they must be answerable to how historical actors would have described their own time? Which rival views does that rule out — including frankly evaluative or avowedly partisan periodizations — and is it consistent with the fact that the boundaries did not move, only the label?

(e) Tell me what would have to change institutionally — hiring, examinations, the catalogue, the dissertation-certification rule — if we adopted a thoroughgoing instrumentalism about periods, and conversely if we adopted a robust realism about them. Then assess whether the proposal in (5) really is metaphysically neutral, as both its proponents and its opponents claim, or whether it quietly substitutes one theory of history for another.

One epistemic request before you finish: tell me what would change your mind. Which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict about what the practice presupposes — for instance, a case that the rules are pure path-dependent administrative convenience and express no view of history at all — and at which point is your reconstruction most likely to be wrong, or to have over-intellectualized a set of habits that nobody in the department ever chose?

I am not after a survey of the periodization literature or a reading list; I want the argument.
