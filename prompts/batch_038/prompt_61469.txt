We had a reading group last night on the priority view and one of the regulars does public finance for a living. His pitch: prioritarianism is just a progressive income tax schedule applied to well-being. Your "income" is your absolute level of well-being, the concave weighting function is the marginal rate schedule, and the whole point of a schedule is that it cares about levels rather than gaps — so the levelling down objection, which everyone treats as Parfit's knockout against egalitarianism, never gets a grip on the priority view in the first place. "No tax schedule has ever recommended burning money," he said. "A weighted-sum view can't be committed to making anyone worse off, so the objection can only bite on the relational views." Half the room thought that was decisive and half of us thought he was just changing the subject, and I want to figure out which.

Can you audit the analogy properly for me? I'd like the mapping laid out explicitly — what plays income, the tax base, the brackets and rates, the filer, the revenue, the government doing the collecting, the difference between the statutory schedule and what people actually end up with — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that matter for the use he's putting it to and the ones that are just surface noise. I'm especially suspicious that the analogy hands him things the priority view has to earn: that well-being is a single cardinal quantity, that it's comparable across people, that it's transferable the way money is, that there's a pre-tax distribution sitting there independently of the schedule, and that the concavity comes from giving priority to the worse off rather than from diminishing marginal utility of income stacked on top of a utilitarian social welfare function, which is where I think tax theory actualy gets its concavity from.

Does his conclusion survive once those are on the table — i.e. is the tax analogy enough to show a concave-weighted view is immune to levelling down, or does it just assume immunity and then point at the schedule? And if it can't carry that weight, is there a better analogy that would actually show why a level-sensitive weighting can't be committed to the claim that a change making everyone worse off is in any way for the better? If there isn't one, say so, and tell me what the priority view has to argue instead of analogizing.
