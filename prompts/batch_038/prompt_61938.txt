Second-year philosophy student here, on a module that's basically metaethics with some value theory bolted on. I help run our department's weekly undergrad workshop — about 18 people, mixed years, two of whom aren't even philosophy students — and I've got the 75-minute slot next Tuesday. I want to do something on the wrong kind of reasons problem: the thing where someone offers you a reward for admiring or valuing or being amused by something, and the reward is obviously a reason to want to have the attitude but somehow doesn't count in favour of the attitude itself.

But I dont want to explain any of that up front. I want the group to walk into it. So design me a workshop where the incentive structure does the philosophical work and people only get the label at the very end.

What I want built in:

1. Real stakes. I can genuinely put up about £6 (chocolate, or cash to a couple of people) and I can promise other real things — a cushy task assignment, first pick of the snacks, whatever. Tell me what the reward should be and how big it needs to be for the problem to bite rather than feel like a joke.

2. A payout-rule problem. If you pay on self-report everyone just says they like it; if you pay on being spotted liking it, it turns into a performance and nobody's attitude is genuine at all. I want them to hit that themselves. Give me the rule you'd actually use.

3. Two kinds of reward. For some people the incentive should be sterile — it's seperate from whether the thing is any good. For others it should be leaky: the reward is itself evidence the object is worth admiring, because whoever's paying only pays when a panel has rated it highly. Then get the two halves comparing notes. I want them to notice that the reason that looked obviously wrong-kind in one case looks like a perfectly ordinary reason to admire in the other, and argue about whether wrong-kind-ness lives in the source of the reason or in what the reason bears on.

4. A cultivation round. Now the reward attaches not to having the attitude but to having done things likely to produce it — rereading, steelmanning, dwelling on the good bits. They can do that one easily, for the money. Then ask whether that makes it a reason to admire after all, indirectly.

5. Swap in belief. Same setup, but the target is now believing something. I suspect they'll find they simply can't, and I want that contrast to make them ask whether the whole problem is really about voluntariness — and whether that's an explanation or just a relocation, since round 4 seemed to show attitudes are cultivable too.

Constraints: the object of admiration needs careful choosing. Obviously worthless and nobody can do it; obviously great and everybody can; either way the exercise collapses. Tell me what to use and why. No lecture at the start, no name-dropping until the debrief. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, the debrief script mapping each moment onto the distinction, a triage list of what to cut when I overrun (I will), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and an honest note on the part of the idea the activity flat out can't reproduce so I have to supply it by hand.

British English please. And at the end, tell me how confident you are that this genuinely generates the effect rather than just being an elaborate way of handing out chocolate, and single out the bit of the design you're least sure about.
