I run a grad seminar on non-human causes in history — disease, climate, volcanoes — and there's a move that keeps coming up that I want to pressure test before I say something dumb in print.

Half my colleagues in environmental history are forever insisting they aren't determinists, and the way they say it is: the climate deals the cards, societies play the hand. Same people will also say that smallpox dealt Cortés a hand no army of his own could have dealt him, or that the year without a summer after Tambora was a hand nobody could play well. The metaphor is doing a lot of work for them: the deck constrains, the play decides, so you get to grant the non-human causes real weight while denying the outcome was fixed. And they cash it out in a fairly strong normative claim — you can still hold Europeans responsible for what happened in the Americas even though the demographic collapse was mostly done by pathogens, because the pathogens were just the cards, not players.

I'm now suspicious that the anti-determinist work is being done by the card table rather than by any argument. So: audit it. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the dealer, the deck, the deal, the players, the rules, the stakes, the other tables — then the places it breaks, and separate the breaks that actually threaten their conclusion from the ones that are just decorative. My own short list of suspects: a dealer sits outside the game and has intentions; the deck is fixed before play and doesn't reshuffle in response to how you play; you get dealt once, whereas climate keeps dealing continuously; the players can't see their own hands; and a card game has a discrete end. Which of those are fatal, which aren't, and what have I missed? Then tell me what the image quietly assumes about what counts as an agent — I have a feeling that calling something a card rather than a player already concedes it's a mere condition, which is the opposite of what these same people want when they call a volcano a historical actor.

Relatedly, the rival camp in my seminar reaches for a different image: the climate as protagonist rather than setting. Compare the two. Can a single account hold both, or are they pulling in opposite directions? Does the responsibility claim survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if it collapses, is there a better image — one that lets a cause be genuinely historical without having to be an agent — or is the absence of such an image itself the finding?
