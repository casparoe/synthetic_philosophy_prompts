My wife and I have eight cryopreserved embryos from a cycle completed last spring, and our clinic has begun offering, as an optional extra, a commercial service that ranks embryos by polygenic scores for a set of common conditions and a handful of non-medical traits. I am a structural engineer rather than a philosopher, but the decision has pushed me into the literature, and out of that reading I built what I took to be a reductio of the standard defence of selection. I now think the reductio proves considerably more than I intended, and I cannot locate the step at which it goes wrong.

The target is the principle most associated with Julian Savulescu, which I will paraphrase rather than quote: prospective parents should select, from among the children they could have, the one whose life can be expected to be best, or at least as good as that of any of the alternatives, on the basis of the relevant information available to them. Call it PB. My argument runs as follows.

1. The reason PB invokes is impersonal and comparative. It registers only how much wellbeing the resulting child can be expected to have, and it is not derived from anyone's rights, claims, or existing commitments.
2. A reason of that form is indifferent to the instrument by which expected wellbeing is raised. Selecting one embryo rather than another, using donor gametes, choosing a different reproductive partner, conceiving later, conceiving elsewhere, or purchasing prenatal and postnatal intervention are all simply ways of bringing it about that the child who exists has a better expected life.
3. Nothing in PB privileges the embryological instrument over the others; if PB's reason carries weight in the laboratory, it carries the same weight per unit of expected wellbeing everywhere else.
4. As a matter of fact, the environmental and timing variables listed in (2) account for far more of the variance in a child's expected prospects than the differences a polygenic score can detect among embryos created by the same two parents.
5. Hence, if PB's reason has any weight at all, prospective parents are required to delay conception, relocate, accumulate resources, and where necessary change partners or gamete sources until no further improvement is affordable; and since for every feasible arrangement there remains a better one they might still bring about, the requirement has no natural stopping point.
6. No credible morality has that shape. It does not instruct a couple to forgo having a child together for fifteen years in order to move an expected-wellbeing estimate by a few percent, and it does not instruct one spouse to seek gametes from a third party on such grounds.
7. Therefore PB is false; and if it is false, nothing morally distinguishes transferring the higher-scoring embryo from transferring the lower-scoring one, so the choice I face is one of indifference.

Two features of this trouble me. First, (5) and (7) are conclusions I do not believe for a moment, yet I cannot see which inference licenses me to refuse them while keeping the rest. Second, the reasoning appears to run in the opposite direction as well: if the instrumental indifference asserted in (2) and (3) is correct, then moving to a better school district, paying for tuition, or funding music lessons would seem to lack the moral grounding that ordinary parents assume those choices have, which is surely not a consequence anyone wants.

I should state where my sympathies lie, so that the diagnosis is not distorted by my preferences. I am attached to (4), which I take to be an empirical claim about effect sizes rather than a philosophical one. I would also like, if it can be had, some reason — even a weak and defeasible one — to prefer an embryo with no predicted serious condition over one carrying such a prediction; I do not want the upshot to be that the two transfers are exactly on a par.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Identify the first step that fails and say what kind of failure it is: an equivocation on the scope of the children one could have, a suppressed premise about whether PB's reason is decisive or merely contributory, a hidden assumption that the reason must be acted on regardless of cost to the parents, or a false empirical claim. Then tell me whether PB can be confined to a fixed set of alternatives — the embryos already in the tank — without that confinement looking arbitrary, or whether the line between selecting among existing options and improving the circumstances of conception simply tracks what fertility clinics happen to sell. Finally, address the retreat I keep encountering in the literature, on which PB is said to identify a defeasible moral reason rather than a requirement, such that a couple who ignores it is not thereby blameworthy: does that retreat actually block (5) and (6), or does it merely concede that the reason is too weak to bear on the decision in front of me? For each restriction you propose, say what its defenders are giving up.
