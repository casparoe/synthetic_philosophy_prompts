I'm an ICU nurse, eleven years in, mostly transplant and neuro critical care, and our hospital ethics committee asked me to put together a twenty minute talk on how death actually gets called in practice versus how it gets argued about in print. I keep coming back to one case and I think it might be the spine of the whole talk, but I can't tell whether I'm overreading it. I want you to treat it as a test case for the competing accounts of death rather than as a legal or emotional story.

The case, so you don't have to guess at it: Marlise Muñoz, a paramedic in Texas, was found unconscious at home by her husband in late November 2013 and taken to John Peter Smith Hospital in Fort Worth. She was 14 weeks pregnant. She was declared brain dead a couple of days later, and her husband — also a paramedic, and someone she'd told explicitly that she would not want to be maintained artificially if that happened — asked the hospital to withdraw support. The hospital refused, pointing at a Texas statute that bars withdrawing or withholding life-sustaining treatment from a pregnant patient. The family sued. In January 2014 Judge R. H. Wallace Jr. ruled that the statute simply didn't apply, because Muñoz was legally dead and the law governs patients — he expressly declined to rule on the statute's constitutionality, and no one re-examined the death determination. She was disconnected on January 26; spontaneous respiration never returned and cardiac activity stopped within minutes. By then the fetus was around 22 weeks and the family's attorneys were describing severe abnormalities, including fluid in the skull, attributed to the oxygen-deprivation event.

So here's what I want. Run the case through three accounts and be disciplined about the mapping each time — what counts as the criterion, what counts as the evidence, who gets to say. First, the whole-brain / organism-as-a-whole account: on that view the hospital was maintaining a corpse, and the pregnancy is a grotesque coincidence. But does it stay that easy? For two months that body was regulating temperature, blood pressure, and waste, and gestating — which looks like exactly the somatic integration the account says a living organism does, and there are those reported cases of bodies going on for years after a brain-death declaration, growing and healing and even carrying pregnancies. Say plainly whether Muñoz pressures the formulation or whether the formulation has a ready answer. Second, the higher-brain / personhood account, where she was dead from roughly the first day and the debate was about a body that had lost the capacity for consciousness: what does that view make of a legal system that agreed with it and still couldn't say so? Third, the Truog and Miller line — their 2008 NEJM piece arguing that brain-dead donors aren't really dead, that the dead donor rule is a fiction we maintain, and that we'd be better off honestly permitting vital organ donation from living patients with devastating brain injury under proper consent. Is Muñoz a confirmation of their diagnosis, or does the case cut against their proposed fix, given that what everyone in the courtroom wanted was for the machines to stop?

Then the part I most want and that I can't do myself: what did the judge's move reveal? A contested question about whether a human being was alive got resolved by construing the word "patient" in a statute, with nobody disputing the underlying determination — and the hospital, which insisted she was dead enough to be past the reach of her own advance directive, was simultaneously treating the body as a patient for the purposes of the pregnancy provision. I have a hunch that what was actually operating was a purpose-relative notion of death, a status assigned separately for each legal question rather than a biological event anyone measured, and I don't know if that's a real position or just me being cynical after a decade of watching these calls get made. Tell me whether the hunch holds up, and tell me what the circulatory-death side of the house makes of all this — under DCD logic she was a controlled donor waiting to happen, declared by one standard while another standard was being applied to the same body in the same bed, and I'd like to know whether that's a genuine tension or two criteria that simply never touch. Finally: which features of the episode does each account explain, which does it misdescribe, and which account, if any, is the one this case was never designed to test? Where a view is silent, say it's silent — don't recruit it.

Format: lead with a three-sentence bottom line giving me your verdict on whether the case supports, undermines, or just fails to engage the whole-organism account, then the analysis. Hard cap of 800 words total, no bullet points, no headers longer than a few words.
