**Notes: "The Luring Lottery as a broken instrument"**

Setup. Hofstadter's two superrationality devices. Platonia: 20 people each independently decide whether to send a telegram; if exactly one does, that person gets a billion; if none or more than one do, nobody gets anything. The Luring Lottery from the June 1983 column: readers mail in an entry, one winner is drawn at random from the N entries, and the winner takes home a million divided by N, so every extra entry shrinks the prize for everone.

Standard line: in Platonia superrational players randomise at p = 1/20; in the lottery the dilution is supposed to make staying out the superrational move, so the column tests whether readers can reason their way to the cooperative answer.

My claim is that these aren't the same kind of case, and that the lottery one is broken *as a test*.

1. In Platonia, a symmetric player's expected payoff goes as p(1−p)^19, which has a genuine interior max at p = 1/20. Fine. But that only delivers P(exactly one sender) = (19/20)^19 ≈ 0.37. So even in the clean case superrationality is not a coordination device, it's roughly a 1-in-3 ticket on coordination happening.

2. In the lottery, the per-player expected payoff at common entry probability p is roughly 10^6/(n²p) — strictly decreasing in p all the way down. p = 0 isn't available (payoff exactly zero; the supremum is never attained) and every p > 0 is beaten by a smaller one. So there is no superrational recommendation at all: the optimisation is degenerate rather than merely hard. Whatever entry count came back would be equally compatible with superrationality, with ordinary egoism, and with plain confusion. An instrument with no scale isn't a measurement.

3. Fix: attach a small real cost to entering (in 1983 terms, requiring a stamp does it). Then payoff(p) = p·(10^6·E[1/N²] − c) is maximised at exactly p = 0, the supremum is attained, and the prediction "nobody enters" becomes falsifiable and non-vacuous, since it separates superrationalists from optimists who figure N will be small. Costless entry is what kills the experiment.

What I'd like comments on:

- Is the monotonicity argument in 2 actually right, or am I botching the conditional-on-entry expectation (I'm conditioning on being in the pool and then multiplying by p — is that double counting)?
- Is "superrationality has no determinate answer here" too strong? Is there a standard move — a limit profile, maximising the aggregate payout, breaking symmetry with a focal ordering like alphabetically-first-entrant — that rescues a recommendation without smuggling in anything ad hoc?
- Is the 0.37 remark in 1 a real limitation or a category error, given that no symmetric randomising scheme could do better?
- Does the redesign in 3 really make it a test, or does readership heterogeneity swamp the symmetry assumption anyway, so that a Harsanyi-style estimate of others' entry propensities is the only honest analysis?
- Tone: does this read as a "well actually" paper? How do I make the criticism land without seeming like I'm dunking on Hofstadter, who was transparently running a game rather than doing decision theory?
