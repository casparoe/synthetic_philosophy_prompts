I serve on a municipal commission in a mid-sized port city, and I need help with an argument that keeps winning the room. Roughly sixty years ago the city cleared a dockside quarter — let's call it the Marlow Flats — to make way for a ring road and a civic plaza. Something over four thousand households were displaced, most of them recent arrivals from two neighbouring countries; the demolitions were entirely legal, ordered by a council in which nobody from the quarter had a seat, and the compensation paid out was a small fraction of assessed value. Since the late 1990s the city has marked the anniversary of the demolition order with a public reading of the names of the displaced households in the plaza that replaced them, and the photographs, ledgers and recorded testimony live in a funded collection at the central library. The proposal now before us is not erasure: keep the archive, keep the plaque, add a small interpretive wall — but fold the reading of names into a new, forward-looking civic holiday in the spring. No names read aloud, no anniversary, one day devoted to the city as it now stands.

The proposal's chief advocate is a clinical psychologist who consults for heritage bodies, and he is completely open that his argument is imported from the consulting room. Societies, he says, are like patients. A person who has to recount the worst thing that ever happened to them once a year, in public, in front of an audience, is not recovering but re-enacting; a competent therapist would move that patient out of compulsive retelling and toward a life no longer organised around the event. The reading of names, on his account, is the city picking at a scab. Worse, the third generation — people whose grandparents lost their homes but who themselves never lost anything — is being conscripted into grief it does not have, which he calls a manufactured symptom, and he points out that our own survey data show the highest attendance among residents with no family connection to the quarter at all. He cites David Rieff's case for forgetting approvingly: remembrance has never reliably inoculated anyone against repeating anything, and ritualised memory is often the fuel for the next grievance rather than the brake on it. A polity, he concludes, is entitled to health, and health includes the ability to stop. I am on the other side, closer to Margalit — memory is what makes thick relations possible, and some obligations run to the particular people you share a past with rather than to humanity at large, so a duty isn't dissolved by noticing that keeping it makes everyone feel worse — but I suspect that is a different vocabulary for refusing his argument rather than an answer to it, and I want to know whether I'm wrong about that.

So: audit the analogy rather than the politics. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — who or what is the patient, who is the therapist, what is the trauma, what counts as a symptom, what is the treatment, what is cure, and what does the annual reading correspond to inside the clinic. Then separate the disanalogies that are merely decorative from the ones that are load-bearing for the use he is making of it. I want particular pressure on three points. First, whether there is a patient at all once the people doing the remembering were never the ones harmed, and what it means that in a polity the person who declares the treatment finished is not the person being treated. Second, what happens to the mapping when the goal is not feeling better but owing something to people who are dead or scattered and cannot consent to being let go. Third, whether the clinical picture can even register the difference between the two halves of the proposal — it keeps the record and abolishes the ritual, a distinction that seems to matter enormously to memory and nothing whatever to therapy, where there is no such thing as an archived symptom. Also tell me whether he is doing evidential work or only rhetorical work: strip the clinic out and say what empirical claim, if any, is left standing, and what would count as testing it. If the therapeutic frame is simply the wrong one, don't just report that — give me your best alternative analogy, with its own mapping and its own weak points, and say what it licenses that his does not.

Two housekeeping requests. I am not after a reading list, a bibliography, or named sources; argue it from the concepts and from the case as I've described it. And please don't be even-handed. End by telling me how you would vote if you sat on this commission, name the two or three considerations that actually decide it for you, and say what would have to be true — about the city, about the descendants, about the evidence on grievance — to move your vote the other way. If you think some piece of the therapeutic vocabulary deserves to survive into the deliberation, say which piece and keep it in your own account, rather than hedging around it.
