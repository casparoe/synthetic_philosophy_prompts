I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and next month we are running a half-day in-service for its members: two ICU attendings, three floor nurses, two chaplains, a risk-management attorney, and one faculty member with a bioethics background. The agenda item is the authority of a written advance directive over a patient who now lacks decision-making capacity, is not in distress, and appears contented to go on living. When we last discussed this, the meeting collapsed within ten minutes: half the committee said that ignoring the directive disrespects the woman who wrote it, the other half said that following it harms the person in the bed, and everyone reached immediately for the question of whether the patient is still the same person — a question that, in my experience, nobody in that room can make progress on and that settles nothing about what to do at the bedside.

I want a decision-tree handout that sorts each member onto a position by what they say about cases, never by what they call themselves. Specifications:

**Form.** Ten to fourteen questions, each a short stipulated vignette or a claim to accept or reject, with two or three answer options. Every question must be answerable in under thirty seconds by someone with no philosophy training, using no term that is not glossed in the question itself. No question may ask what view a person holds, name a position, or invite the reader to guess which leaf an answer leads to; a question that most people can answer correctly by noticing which option sounds more like the position they already favor is a failed question.

**Trunk.** The first three questions must concern, in this order: whether following the directive in the case at hand makes anyone worse off; whether not following it wrongs anyone, and if so whom; and whether the directive functions as evidence of what the patient wanted, as the exercise of a standing power she once held, or as something whose force comes from the practice it belongs to. The question of identity or continuity across the dementia may appear only below these three, and only if phrased as a case rather than as a metaphysical question.

**Vignettes.** Build the branches from these, and do not add cases beyond them: (a) the directive was written at sixty, competently and repeatedly; at eighty-two the patient has advanced dementia, recognizes no one, but smiles, hums, accepts food, and shows no signs of suffering; (b) identical, except that the patient is in evident untreated pain and cannot report it, so the directive and her present condition point the same way; (c) the directive rests on a belief she held at sixty — that advanced dementia is invariably agonizing and humiliating — which, in this patient, has turned out to be false; (d) the directive was written before the existence of an intervention that could partially restore her cognition; (e) the directive's stated reason is not her own good but something expressive — she refused to be maintained as a spectacle for her grandchildren; (f) the directive is written in a jurisdiction that permits a physician to act on a pre-dementia directive even if the patient physically resists at the time; (g) at sixty she expressly addressed the possibility of her own later dissent, asking that the directive stand whatever her demented self then seems to want.

**Leaves.** Six to eight, each carrying: the name of the position, or the word unnamed if you believe no one defends it; the name of a well-known defender in parentheses only where you are confident of the attribution, otherwise a description instead; one line on the leaf's most awkward commitment; and the leaf's verdict on our draft policy, which reads: *a directive is void where, at the time of the decision, the patient expresses contentment and a wish to continue living and shows no signs of distress, notwithstanding her lack of capacity.* The leaves must include, somewhere in the tree: a precedent-autonomy view on which the directive protects the integrity of a life rather than the welfare of its subject; a present-interests view on which only the current subject's experience counts; a view keyed to the patient's retained capacity to value or to have concerns of her own, however rudimentary; a substituted-judgment view on which the directive has evidential rather than binding force; a practice-based view on which the force of directives is an artifact of the institution that makes long-range planning possible; and a view on which such directives express a devaluing judgment about lives lived with cognitive disability. Also include a node at which a reductionist account of what matters in survival can be read as either strengthening or weakening the directive's authority, and let those two readings lead to different leaves.

**Appendices.** First, map the logical space the questions open up — the combinations of answers the tree permits — and identify at least two combinations no named position occupies, each with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to work it out. Second, list any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, saying which of the vignettes above would separate them if it arose and which realistic case would not.

No bibliography, no reference list, and no citations beyond the parenthetical defender names already specified. It must fit on a double-sided sheet.
