I'm prepping a seminar session on precommitment — Ulysses contracts, constitutional entrenchment, the whole Elster-and-Holmes stretch of territory — and I have a case-design problem I haven't been able to solve. The line I want my students to push against is the credibility requirement: a constraint that the constrained agent can lift whenever she feels like it isn't a constraint at all, it's a note to self, so genuine self-binding has to be irreversible or at minimum expensive to reverse — and then you get the regress, since the decision to bind itself needs binding, and so on down. The countervailing line, roughly Holmes's, is that a constraint nobody can revoke isn't a commitment but an imposition, the dead-hand objection. My students spotted the tension immediately and I tried to hand them a case where the two come apart in an instructive way: a state legislature bound by a fiscal rule it can repeal by simple majority at any moment. It flopped. They said fine, then it's a slogan, not a constraint; I said but repeal requires reasons stated on the floor and a recorded vote; they said that's just delay plus embarrassment, delay isn't binding, and embarrassment is a cost, so I'd smuggled the credibility story back in through the side door. Which is a fair hit, and it's exactly the reply I want the new case to make unavailable.

So here's what I'd like you to build. A thought experiment in which a precommitment device is formally revocable at will — a single act, no waiting period, no supermajority, no third-party veto, no forfeiture or penalty of any kind attached to revoking, and no reputational or audience cost either — and which nonetheless reliably binds the agent in precisely the circumstance it was designed for; and in which making the device irreversible would break it. Constraints: realistic institutional or individual setting (a hospital, a firm, a legislature, a person managing a condition she knows cycles — your pick), one paragraph for the scenario, no exotic technology and no predictors better than a shrewd observer. Crucially, the case may not turn on the cold self being a better judge of what the later self will want: stipulate that her ex ante forecast of her own future values is accurate and that the siren-state agent isn't simply better informed. If the mechanism is "she'll be glad she did it," the design has failed.

Deliverable, and please start with the scenario itself — just the paragraph, no title, no preface, no restating my question — then the demonstration that it meets spec. I want: what the credibility theorist predicts about this device and why the prediction misfires; what the no-dead-hand theorist predicts, and whether she comes out right for the right reason or right by luck; the irreversibility ablation, where you change exactly one feature and show the device breaking, with the reason for the breakage stated; the single feature of the case doing the work, plus a second ablation removing it and naming which verdict flips; and finally how a committed Elster-type would attack the case — my guess is he'd insist that whatever blocks revocation in the crisis moment is a cost wearing a disguise, so tell me whether he's right and what I say back. The test I'd apply, and the one my students will apply: I should be able to state the mechanism in one sentence and not get the reply "so it's a price after all." Finish with a short paragraph on whether the same mechanism scales up to constitutional entrenchment or whether something has to be added at the collective level — I suspect the answer is that it does scale but only if the polity has something an individual doesn't, and I want to know what you think that is.
