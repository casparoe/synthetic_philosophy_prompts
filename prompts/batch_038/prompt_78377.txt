So, background: I write novels. The one I'm working on has a Jesuit astronomer at the center of it — not a saint, a guy who is genuinely convinced that God knows what every free creature would do in any set of circumstances, and who runs his whole life off that conviction. To get him right I'm moderating a public conversation at an independent bookshop next month with a philosopher-theologian who's spent his career defending middle knowledge. Tickets are mostly laypeople, but I'll be taking notes like a spy the whole time.

His version of the view is a bit idiosyncratic and I want the questions to bite on that, not on the textbook one. He holds that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are primitively true — nothing in the creature, no disposition, no inner structure, makes them true; they're just true, and he thinks demanding a truthmaker for them is a category mistake. He also allows that for a decent minority of choices there's no truth of the matter at all about what the creature would freely do — gaps — and he thinks that's a feature, because it gives God room to improvise rather than execute a script.

Give me about nine questions in a running order I could actually read aloud live, and for each one: the answer you'd bet money he reaches for, plus a follow-up short enough to say out loud that makes that answer cost him something.

Coverage I need:

- the truthmaker problem aimed straight at his "they're just true" move — I want a question that makes him feel the distance between that and admitting he has no account at all
- the Báñezian rival with divine premotion: not "how do you differ," but something that makes the difference look like a distinction without a live difference, given that on his picture God already sees the whole shape of every life before deciding whether to create it
- the congruist refinement — God governing by selecting the circumstances in which He knows the creature will freely cooperate — and whether on his account that's anything other than manipulation with a nicer vocabulary. This one matters personally: my astronomer uses exactly this reasoning to justify arranging other people's lives for their own good.
- the gaps. What does providence amount to when a chunk of the future has no truth value yet? Is God gambling with my character's soul, and can He even be said to have chosen this world rather than another one when whole stretches of it aren't fixed?
- one question about whether the free-will defence really needs the full machinery or only a fragment of it — what does he buy with the extra, and what would he lose by trimming it?
- one that asks him for the best picture or analogy he's got for how God can know what I'd do without causing me to do it. I'm a novelist, I will absolutely steal it, so shape the question to invite an image rather than a technical answer.

One more thing. A scene turns on the astronomer realising God actualized a world in which He knew his daughter would do something unforgivable, and did nothing to close off the circumstances that made it happen. Give me a question that puts that to the guest as a live moral question rather than as a puzzle — and in your note, be honest about whether thats the sort of question he's fielded a hundred times and has a canned response sitting behind it.

No generic warm-up, nothing like what drew him to the topic, and don't send me hunting: I want questions, not a reading list. Keep the wording something a bookshop audience can follow, and if a question needs a technical term, spend one plain sentence making the term do work before you use it.
