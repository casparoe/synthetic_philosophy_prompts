I am a novelist at work on a linked collection about people whose occupations require them to attach numbers to things that cannot be numbered, and I need one further story for the sequence. I want the story to be driven by a distinction that fiction almost always gets wrong, so I will state the distinction plainly rather than trust you to recall it.

It is Keynes's, from his early work on probability. He held that probability is an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, not a degree of confidence inside anyone's skull; that a great many such relations are not numerically measurable at all, and that some pairs of hypotheses are not even comparable, so there is simply no fact of the matter as to which is likelier; and that distinct from the probability of an argument is its weight — how much relevant evidence stands behind it. Two people may hold the identical probability and occupy utterly different epistemic positions, because one has a great deal to go on and the other has next to nothing. Decades later he drew the practical consequence: for genuinely novel contingencies there is no calculable basis, and yet decisions are taken regardless, by something other than a weighted average of prospective outcomes.

Write a short story of 1,500 to 2,000 words in which this is the machinery, not the message. The situation: a specialist underwriter in a small reinsurance market has until Thursday to decide whether to write the first operating season of a technology that has never operated commercially anywhere — you may choose what it is, provided it is a single physical installation in one specific place. There is no loss history. What exists instead is one imperfect analogue from another century, a manufacturer's own projections, and the opinions of two men who have never seen the thing run. A colleague wants the business, and points out that everyone in the market will settle on roughly the same rate; the protagonist does not dispute the rate. The scene I most need is the one in which those two agree on the number and disagree about whether there is enough to go on, and the reader feels that the second disagreement is the only real one.

Constraints of craft: single consciousness throughout, in first person or close third; no scene organised as a debate; no character may articulate the philosophy, and the vocabulary of it — weight, ambiguity, animal spirits, Keynes himself — must not appear anywhere in the story. Give the reader the texture of the work: how a rate is assembled, what the paperwork looks like, what these people do between decisions. And withhold the two endings this material usually reaches for. The cautious one is not vindicated by catastrophe, nor is the calculator punished. The story should close in such a way that a careful reader cannot say which of the two was behaving rationally, or whether that was ever the operative question.
