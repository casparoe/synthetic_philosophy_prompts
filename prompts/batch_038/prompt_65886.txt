For the past year I have been sitting in on a Sunday-evening online reading group about measurement, incentives, and public policy. I am seventeen and the rest of the group is mostly graduate students and two people who work in city government performance management, so I mainly listen and take notes, but our central argument has now run for months without anyone moving, and I would like an outside diagnosis. The subject is Goodhart's law. One camp treats it as a real limit on quantified governance: any number you turn into a target is only a proxy for what you actually care about, and sustained pressure on a proxy eventually severs the connection between the proxy and the goal, so the responsible conclusion is to distrust single metrics, hold several imperfect indicators in view at once, and protect professional judgment from audit. Their examples are school league tables, hospital waiting-time targets, police crime statistics, university rankings, and GDP growth targets, and they insist these are not unrelated accidents but one failure repeating itself; they also point out that a nearly identical claim about social indicators was being made before Goodhart wrote anything, which they take as evidence that the point is general rather than local. The other camp thinks this inflates a narrow claim into a slogan. They note that what Goodhart actually wrote, in a 1975 article about United Kingdom monetary policy, was that "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes" — a claim about a system of agents who notice the target and reorganize their behavior around it, which is the same intuition behind the Lucas critique and rational expectations from that period. On their reading, the sentence everyone quotes, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," is a later and much broader restatement, and what gets lumped together under the heading of Goodharting is really a bundle of separable problems with separable remedies: measurement error plus regression to the mean, which makes the top-ranked school partly lucky rather than partly excellent; confounding, where the proxy and the goal are both driven by some third thing; relationships that hold through the middle of a range but not in the tails where the metric gets pushed; and deliberate gaming by people who know exactly what is being counted. A thermometer, they say, does not stop working when you use it to run a furnace.

My own hypothesis — which the first camp calls naive and the second camp says is not really a hypothesis, since it just restates their side — is that most of the argument is about what the word "law" is doing, and that the two camps are answering different questions under one name: one asking whether a target can ever be trusted, the other asking whether targeting destroys the thing it measures. I suspect they also disagree, without noticing, about which cases count as instances of the phenomenon at all, since the thermometer case looks to one camp like a decisive counterexample and to the other like a change of subject. But I may be wrong, and I have noticed that something like a hard question survives my deflation: if an optimizer pushes a proxy far into a region where the proxy was never checked against the goal, it is not obvious that any amount of better statistics or better causal modeling guarantees the proxy still ranks outcomes correctly there, and that sounds less like a verbal issue than a formal one that might in principle be answered.

What I want from you is the diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about "law" or "collapse" or "target," or two groups working on different questions under a shared heading? Where exactly do the sides part company — on facts, on which cases count as data, on what an explanation of a target's failure has to show, or on starting intuitions about whether institutions can be trusted with quantification in the first place? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work, and if so, state it precisely enough that I could tell whether it has an answer? Please engage the positions as I have described them rather than surveying the literature, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that splits the cases, a theorem bounding how far optimizing a proxy can carry you from the goal given the proxy's accuracy, a change of question, or an example that would force one camp to concede something — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. One practical note: please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, since I cannot check any of them and would rather have your reasoning than your references. I would also like you to say explicitly whether the gap between Goodhart's original sentence and the popular slogan is itself part of the reason this will not close, and whether either camp has been arguing against a claim the other never made.
