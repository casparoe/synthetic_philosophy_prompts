Our department's spring lecture series closes with a staged dialogue: a colleague will appear in character as Leibniz — an old Leibniz, in Hanover in the last years of his life — and I will question him for forty-five minutes before an audience of advanced undergraduates and faculty. I need the questions, and I need them to be the questions a serious opponent of his moral philosophy would ask rather than the questions an introductory course would.

Please draft eight to ten. For each, give me (a) the answer the character is most likely to give, reconstructed from what Leibniz actually maintained and wrote, not from a generic rationalist position, and (b) the counter-question that answer provokes, framed so that he cannot meet it by restating the doctrine at a still higher level of abstraction. A question whose only possible outcome is another recital of the best of all possible worlds is worthless to me. Where I indicate the reply I expect below, treat my indication as a sample of the specificity I want, not as a substitute for your own reconstruction.

Two standing constraints. Every question must be one that a critic of his own century could have put to him — Bayle, Arnauld, Pufendorf, a hostile reader of the *Théodicée* in one of the learned journals, or one of his own correspondents — or a direct extension of one. And nothing on the calculus quarrel, nothing on his reception or influence, nothing that requires him to speak of philosophy after his death; biographical detail may enter only where the biography is itself the difficulty.

At least one question must fall under each of the following heads.

**Justice as the charity of the wise.** His definition of justice as the benevolence of the wise invites the charge that it is enlightened egoism in moral dress: if to love is to take pleasure in another's good, and the just man acts for the sake of that pleasure, then his motive is his own delight and the other person's good is merely its occasion. Expect him to insist that love is not a means to a pleasure obtainable otherwise, and that a parent's delight in a child's flourishing shows a motive whose object genuinely is another. The follow-up should test whether that reply distinguishes disinterested love from any strong attachment whatsoever.

**Whether the metaphysics carries the moral weight placed on it.** If created substances do not act upon one another and every change unfolds from within the subject, in what sense is there a moral community rather than a set of perfectly synchronized monologues — and in his conception of the moral world as a city of God, what is doing the work: governance, or merely the fact of the harmony? Ask him what an obligation is, and to whom it runs, in a system in which no agent can affect any other.

**The criterion by which one possible world is best.** He analyzes perfection as harmony, unity in variety, and in a letter to Christian Wolff in 1715 glosses it as "the harmony of things, or the state where everything is worthy of being observed," adding that one may even call it "the degree of contemplatibility." Ask whether a criterion of that shape selects for a world best worth looking at rather than best worth living in, and whether the optimum it yields is a moral achievement or an aesthetic and combinatorial one. He will reply that greater perfection in the whole is inseparable from greater happiness in the parts; ask what he says about the case in which the two come apart, and whether he is entitled to assume that it never arises.

**The ground of moral truths and his quarrel with voluntarism.** He denies that obligation can rest on the will or the power of a superior, on the ground that this erases the difference between a sovereign and a bandit, and he locates moral truths in the divine understanding rather than the divine will. Press him on where the standard of justice then comes from if not from any will, and whether his God is subject to it, identical with it, or only its most reliable knower. Bring in the distinction between antecedent and consequent will and ask what that distinction rules out: is there anything God might have done that he refrained from doing *for a reason*, and if so, is that reason prior to him?

**Necessity, contingency, and blame.** If this world is the best, and the complete concept of an individual contains everything that will ever be true of him, the sinner's sin was going to occur once God chose this world. He will say that moral necessity inclines without necessitating, and that contingency survives because the inclusion of the predicate in the subject can be exhibited only by an analysis no finite mind could complete. Ask whether "inclining without necessitating" states a distinction or merely names the place where one is needed, and whether a truth whose analysis can never be finished is contingent in any sense a defendant could appeal to.

**Optimism and moral effort.** If every evil that occurs is a component of the best possible arrangement, what is the moral status of someone who successfully prevents one — has he improved the world, or has he simply executed what the best arrangement already required? Ask whether the doctrine can accommodate moral progress as anything more than the unfolding of what was optimal from the outset.

**Animals and the boundaries of charity.** He grants them sensation and memory and denies them the apperception and reason that make a mind a person; he also holds that no soul is ever annihilated but only transformed. Ask whether animals fall inside or outside the reach of universal benevolence, what follows about cruelty to them if they fall outside, and whether the preservation of their souls is anything *to them* if they cannot reflect on their own persistence.

**Statecraft against universal benevolence.** In the service of the Elector of Mainz he drafted a memorandum urging Louis XIV to invade Egypt, in part as a way of turning the French king away from war with the Dutch. Ask how a moral theory whose measure of the good is God and whose first directive is benevolence toward all minds generates advice of that kind: whether the inhabitants of Egypt enter the calculation at all, whether the calculation is one of diverted harm rather than conferred good, and whether a theory that treats one war as an instrument for preventing another can distinguish statecraft from the self-regarding love he repudiates.

Three further things I need from you. First, order the set so that it builds: begin where he is strongest and most assured, and end on the question he can least escape. Second, for two of the questions, identify the point at which the character will stop reasoning and merely reassert, and tell me what to say at that moment which returns him to the difficulty without letting the room watch the exchange collapse. Third, name the single question of the set on which you judge that he has no adequate answer, and say in two or three sentences why the resources available to him in Hanover in his last years do not reach it.
