For my dissertation in a metaethics module I am examining the claim that Quine's epistemological holism leaves no room for a difference in kind between moral and scientific statements — that moral principles merely sit further out along the continuum of remoteness from sensory evidence, and are revisable in principle for much the same sorts of reasons as any physical law. My own credence that this Quinean picture is sustainable is about 0.6, though I cannot presently say where that confidence rests.

Please give me your own number for the claim that a consistent Quinean has no principled way of marking moral belief as epistemically second-class, together with the decomposition that produces it. I should like a probability and a line of justification for each of at least the following: whether confirmational holism is itself true, given the standing objection that Quine never argues for it and that, taken seriously, it would render every statement equally open to revision; whether moral principles are answerable to sensory evidence in anything more than a notional sense; whether the centre and the periphery of the web of belief map onto the scientific and the moral at all, or whether that metaphor is doing more work than Quine can license; and whether treating moral claims as cultural posits, on a par with posited physical objects, is compatible with also treating them as truth-apt. Then show me how these terms combine into your overall figure, identify the term to which that figure is most sensitive, and say which single argument or piece of evidence would move you furthest.
