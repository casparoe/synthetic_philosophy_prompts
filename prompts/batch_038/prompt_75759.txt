I am presenting a 30-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on institutions and long-run development, and I need a question-period prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis. The received account says a process is path dependent when small, contingent early events are amplified by increasing returns and lock in an outcome that would have been different — and different in a durable way — had the start been slightly otherwise. My paper argues that this cannot be a property of a process alone. Path dependence is a property of a process indexed to two things the standard account leaves implicit: a level of description, and a class of admissible counterfactual histories. Absent both, "lock-in" is not a claim about the world but a way of narrating it.

To make this checkable rather than merely deflationary, I propose a criterion. For a given process, compare two spreads of outcomes: the spread you get across a family of admissible early perturbations, and the spread you get across repeated runs of the unperturbed process with independently realized noise. Call the ratio the perturbation-to-noise ratio. If it is near one, the process is simply noisy; every "small event changed everything" story about it is a description of variance that was already there, and there is no distinctive lock-in phenomenon. If it is large, and if the perturbed outcome still persists after you switch off the feedback mechanism that supposedly produced it, you have genuine path dependence rather than amplification in name only. On this account increasing returns alone never imply path dependence: feedback amplifies whatever perturbation arrives, but the substantive question is whether the perturbation in view is large relative to the ambient disturbance the system routinely absorbs and recovers from. I then argue that the large empirical literature regressing present-day outcomes on historical variables measures something adjacent but distinct — durability of initial conditions under slow adjustment — and that a robust result of that kind is entirely compatible with a perturbation-to-noise ratio near one.

The audience, in the detail that matters. Roughly thirty people. A plurality are quantitative economic historians who run persistence regressions and think in terms of identification, instrumental variables for historical shocks, and whether a counterfactual history is inside the support of the data; they will hear my criterion as unfalsifiable and will want to know what estimate would refute it. Two formal modelers of technology adoption and network externalities, who work with multiple equilibria, basin-of-attraction language, and coordination dynamics; they will say I have rediscovered equilibrium selection and dressed it up. Three or four interpretive historians who regard counterfactual history as illegitimate in principle and will ask why I should care about a perturbed past that did not happen, and who will suspect that my level-of-description move makes the whole vocabulary an imposition on the record rather than a finding about it. One philosopher of social science, attending for the conceptual cleanup, who will press on whether I have explained the phenomenon or dissolved it, and on whether my criterion smuggles in a privileged description of outcomes. One development economist with policy commitments who will ask what survives for institutional design if much of the alleged lock-in is noise: whether early intervention loses its urgency, or whether my view in fact strengthens the case for acting early.

What I want from you. Draft the prep sheet: the eight or nine objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by the damage each would do to the paper if I answered it badly. Phrase each the way that sub-audience would phrase it and in their own vocabulary — the modeler should not sound like the historian, and neither should sound like the cliometrician — so that I can recognize the question when it comes. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes at a podium, an explicit statement of where that reply is soft or where it depends on a stipulation I chose rather than discovered, and a note on what I should concede immediately instead of defending. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session, and identify the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one whose force I cannot answer in the time available and should think hard about in advance.

Two further requirements. First, for each objection, mark whether the underlying disagreement is something people in these fields treat as settled or whether it is genuinely live among those who work on it; I want to know exactly where I would be claiming a consensus that does not exist, and where I am one party to an unresolved debate. Do not present a contested position as the settled view in either direction — including my own. Second, where your suggested replies rely on a contested reading of the literature or on a substantive assumption about how such processes are modeled that experts dispute, flag that reliance rather than smoothing it over; I would rather know that a reply is rhetorically strong and dialectically fragile than have it fail in front of the two modelers. No bibliography, no citation list, no suggestions for further reading — I want the objections and the answers.
