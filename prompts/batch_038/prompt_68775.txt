**The question (25 marks):**

Consider the following case, which we shall call the Compliance Auditor. Dr. Vant, a neuroscientist, wants Emery, a fund manager, to transfer a client's savings into an account that Vant controls, and he wants this done without Emery ever learning that he was assisted. Vant therefore implants a device — the Auditor — which continuously monitors Emery's brain for a marker M. M is a neural signature that occurs at the onset of any decision by Emery to refrain from making the transfer. The device is programmed as follows: if M occurs at any point before three in the afternoon, the device stimulates Emery's brain in such a way as to be causally sufficient for his deciding, at three o'clock, to make the transfer; if M never occurs, the device remains permanently inert and does nothing at all.

In the actual course of events, M never occurs. Emery deliberates, weighs the chance of detection against the sum involved, decides at three o'clock on his own to make the transfer, and makes it. He acts on reasons he endorses, he experiences no sense of pressure or alien intrusion, he is uncoerced, and he never discovers the device.

(a) State the principle of alternative possibilities, and argue whether Emery satisfies it. [8]

(b) Explain the flicker-of-freedom response to cases of this kind. What alternative possibilities, if any, remain open to Emery, and does their availability undermine the conclusion you reached in (a)? [9]

(c) Does the Compliance Auditor case show that causal determinism is compatible with moral responsibility? Justify your answer by distinguishing carefully what the case does establish from what it does not. [8]

**The submitted answer:**

*(a)* The principle of alternative possibilities, or PAP, holds that an agent is morally responsible for what he does only if he could have done otherwise. Emery plainly could not have done otherwise. Suppose he had started to decide to refrain: M would have occurred, the Auditor would have fired, and the transfer would have happened anyway. The one branch of the case in which Emery refrains is a branch in which the device has already guaranteed that he does not refrain. So PAP is not satisfied. And yet this looks like a textbook responsible agent. Nothing intervened. He weighed reasons and settled on one of them, he endorses the reason he acted on, nobody forced him, and he would have explained his decision to anyone who asked. If we hold on to PAP we are forced to say that Emery is not blameworthy for the transfer, which is the wrong result. So the case defeats PAP: being able to do otherwise is not necessary for moral responsibility. What does the the work is the actual history of the decision — whether it issued from the agent's own reasoning — and not the shape of the alternatives around it.

*(b)* Critics object that cases like this one never really remove every alternative possibility; they remove only the alternative possibility of the action itself, and various residual flickers survive. Here the surviving flicker is Emery's ability to have begun to decide to refrain, that is, to have produced M. The usual reply on the other side is that these flickers are not robust alternatives, in the sense that the agent would not be credited or blamed for them, and so they cannot carry responsibility. I think that reply misfires. The distinction between robust and non-robust alternatives is supposed to do real work, but robustness has to be cashed out in terms of whether the alternative was up to the agent. Producing M would have been entirely up to Emery, since M is nothing other than the onset of his own deciding to refrain; it is not something the device manufactures out of nothing. So the flicker that survives in the Auditor case is a robust alternative by any non-question-begging standard. Emery therefore had a robust alternative possibility after all, and the case does not deliver the conclusion that PAP is false. It only shows that the alternative Emery had is not the one we would naturally describe as refraining.

*(c)* Frankfurt-style cases are usually presented as neutral with respect to determinism, but the Compliance Auditor is not neutral, and it is worth seeing why. In the actual sequence the Auditor never acts, so nothing in the case is determined by Vant. Whether Emery decides is left open up to the moment he decides. Yet the case is constructed so that the alternative branch closes deterministically: if M occurs, the stimulation is causally sufficient for the decision. Since Emery is responsible in the actual sequence and the actual sequence contains no external determination, the case shows that what responsibility requires is not indeterminism but the right kind of causal history. That is a positive result for compatibilism. More than that, it disposes of incompatibilism altogether, because the incompatibilist's argument is an argument from the impossibility of doing otherwise to the absence of responsibility, and (a) has shown that the first premise does not entail the second. Once PAP goes, the incompatibilist has no route left from determinism to irresponsibility.
