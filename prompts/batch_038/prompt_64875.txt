I'm a second year in an aesthetics seminar that's jointly run with the Slavic department, and in three weeks I'm giving a 20 minute paper at our end-of-term work-in-progress night on Tolstoy's *What Is Art?*. My thesis is deliberately provocative and my advisor thinks its either the best thing I've done or a career-ending misreading, so I'd like to know what's coming. Here's the reading I'm defending: the infection theory isn't an aesthetic theory at all, it's a piece of social epistemology. Tolstoy says outright that "Art, like speech, is a means of communication, and therefore of progress" — speech carries knowledge across generations, art carries feelings the same way — so I want to treat art on his account as the testimony channel for feeling, and read his three conditions as reliability conditions on that channel rather than as artistic virtues. Sincerity is the sincerity norm on the source (and he himself collapses all three conditions into it), clearness is signal fidelity, individuality is an anti-stereotype condition that stops the receiver from substituting a generic emotion for the specific one the sender actually had. Then comes the move I expect to get hammered for: the religious-perception clause is not the theocratic add-on that ruins the theory, which is how the analytic people always tell it. It's the shared background of accepted truths that any testimony channel needs in order to be creditable at all — the analogue of a doxastic practice's own internal standards. Payoff: Tolstoy turns out to be an epistemic rather than a moral critic of art. When he goes after counterfeits, the professional artist class "actuated by personal aims of covetousness or vanity," criticism, and the decadents he names — Baudelaire, Verlaine, Ibsen, Maeterlinck, Wagner — those are unreliability charges about the channel, not content charges about nasty feelings being transmitted. And his claim that "the degree of infectiousness is also the sole measure of excellence in art" becomes a claim about transmission success rather than about beauty.

The room will be about fifteen people and very mixed. Two Russian lit faculty who insist the post-conversion religious writings are the non-negotiable context and who will say I'm sanitizing Tolstoy to make him palatable to a philosophy department; one analytic aesthetician, a value pluralist, hostile to any moralism about art and unimpressed by my insistence that I've read the moralism out; a visiting epistemologist who works on testimony, probably friendly to the frame but merciless about the uptake end (you can believe what a testifier tells you, but you don't recieve a feeling as true, so where's the truth-aptness that's supposed to make the analogy work?); and a pile of grad students who know Tolstoy's aesthetics only from the anthology excerpt and from the reputation as the guy who hated Shakespeare.
