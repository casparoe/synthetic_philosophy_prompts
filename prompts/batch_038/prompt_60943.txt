I spent seven years treating the wrong kind of reasons problem as a technical puzzle, and four months ago it became something else. I began cognitive-behavioral treatment for a health anxiety that had started to cost me work and sleep, and the protocol asks me to do precisely what the literature I know says cannot be done: to adopt and hold a belief — that a given symptom has a benign explanation — on the strength of what holding it will do for me, rather than on any bearing it has on whether it is true. My clinician considers the instruction unremarkable. I cannot tell whether it is perfectly legitimate or strictly unintelligible, and I have found that I cannot settle the matter by going back to the debate, because the debate has not moved in twenty years.

The same paralysis shows up in the graduate seminar I have taught on this material. My students agree completely about the data: the subordinates offered a reward for admiring a petty tyrant, the prize for believing something on command, the toxin, the demon who punishes disbelief. Everyone agrees that such considerations are somehow beside the point of whether the attitude is correct or justified. No two of them agree about why. Roughly, the room contains: those who say the pragmatic considerations are genuine reasons that bear on a different question — not whether p, or whether the object is admirable, but whether to have the attitude at all; those who say that a consideration is a reason for belief only if it could function as a premise in deliberation about whether p, and that because such deliberation is transparent to the truth, prize considerations are not reasons for belief at all but at most reasons to want, or to bring it about, that one believes; those who say that 'is a reason for' names a single relation that prize considerations do stand in, and that the object-given/state-given gloss on the distinction is broken anyway, since there are right-kind reasons against belief and against intention and state-given considerations turn up everywhere rather than marking a special puzzle; those who say the whole impasse is an artifact of taking reasons as fundamental, and that once fittingness is made basic the right-kind reasons simply are the fittingness-makers, leaving prize considerations as reasons in some other, non-fittingness-conferring sense; and those who think the mark of the wrong kind is structural rather than metaphysical — that such reasons fail to transmit, so that a wrong-kind reason to believe p gives no reason to believe p's obvious consequences. All of this began, of course, as a difficulty for reasons-first and buck-passing accounts of value, and nobody in the seminar thinks that difficulty has been discharged.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that this is not a disagreement about extension at all — nobody disputes which cases are which — but a disagreement about the theoretical job 'reason' is being asked to do: to be a weight in a scale, to be a premise in deliberation, to explain fittingness, to explain transmission. On that reading each camp is answering a different question under one word, and the arguments fail to engage because the crucial term shifts roles between them. The rival hypothesis I cannot rule out is that a hidden substantive question is doing all the work — namely whether belief formation can be under practical control — and that the surface dispute about reasons is downstream of it.

Three constructions of my own, which I would like you to run the diagnosis against rather than ignore. First: you are offered a large sum if you come to trust that a new colleague will keep a confidence of yours; your trusting her reliably raises the probability that she will, because she responds to being trusted and you behave differently toward her when you do. The consideration is plainly about your state, yet it also bears, indirectly and causally, on the question whether the proposition is true. Which test classifies it, and does the case embarrass the tests or my description? Second: the same prize is paid only if you also believe every obvious consequence of what you are paid to believe, so that the pragmatic consideration is engineered to transmit. If transmission failure is the mark of the wrong kind, has the mark been removed, or has my stipulation simply changed the subject? Third: the evidence for and against p is exactly balanced and the prize is enormous. Does the agent have most reason, all things considered, to believe p? I want to know whether the camps must answer this differently, or whether they can all say the same thing here — because if they can, the weighing question is not what divides them.

What I want from you is the diagnosis, not a verdict and not a tour of the positions. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal or terminological, a difference about which theoretical work a term must do, a hidden factual or psychological question about the control we have over our attitudes, a dispute about which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for. Ground the diagnosis in what each camp is committed to and where those commitments fail to make contact, and tell me explicitly whether the appearance of a single debate is itself the illusion. Then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a decisive case, a change of question, or a formal result such as a weighing semantics or a representation theorem for 'counts in favor of' that would settle whether wrong-kind considerations enter the same scale — or argue that progress should not be expected and explain why. State what evidence would falsify your own diagnosis, and end by telling me what the diagnosis implies about my clinician's instruction: whether it is intelligible, and if so in which of the senses of 'reason' the camps have been fighting over. Argue from the content of the positions; I am not asking for citations or a map of who holds what.
