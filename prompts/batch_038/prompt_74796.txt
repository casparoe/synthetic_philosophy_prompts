I serve as rapporteur to a nine-member national board that certifies software used in implanted medical devices. Members are appointed in three blocs of three, by the health ministry, the professional engineering body, and a patient advocacy federation; they sit for staggered five-year terms, so roughly a third of the board turns over each cycle, and we meet monthly. The enabling statute frames certification as three findings — that the system meets the published performance specification, that residual risks have been reduced to an acceptable level, and that the manufacturer's post-market surveillance plan is adequate — and provides that certification issues if and only if all three findings are made. Our decisions bind, and they are appealable to an administrative court that reviews whether the board acted on the findings it actually recorded, so we must publish a written justification naming each finding and the reasons supporting it. Two cycles ago we hit a case in which a majority of five accepted each of the three findings while a different majority of five rejected certification, the coalitions not overlapping in the obvious way; the vote on the conclusion and the votes on the findings pointed in opposite directions, and the losing applicant's counsel asked, in writing, which of them was the board's decision. The chair has now asked me to draft a standing decision rule, and I would like the specification before I read another paper on the subject.

I know the general shape of the literature only from a social choice course a decade ago: there is a theorem to the effect that once the propositions on the agenda are logically connected, no aggregation rule can simultaneously keep the collective set consistent and closed under what the agenda implies, respect unanimity, avoid dictatorship, and treat propositions and voters in some uniform way — the last condition being the one I remember people disputing as a technical convenience rather than a normative demand. I also have a vaguer memory of a result about the long run: that a body can be perfectly coherent meeting by meeting and still, through turnover and through treating earlier decisions as settling later ones, end up holding a pattern of judgments that no individual member ever held and that sits badly with what it decided years earlier. Since our board is exactly that kind of continuing body, with precedential habits and a court reading its reasons, I do not know whether that second worry is a genuinely additional requirement on a rule or just the first requirement applied at several times.

So: write out what an adequate decision rule for a body like ours has to deliver. Give me the requirements — the phenomena it must handle, the cases it must come out right on, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must be consistent with — and attach to each one the concrete situation that makes it a requirement rather than a nice-to-have: the paradoxical vote, the appeal, the member who is asked to record a finding she disbelieves so that the conclusion she wants will follow, the applicant who learns the rule and shops for the framing that suits him, the month when the board must produce an answer and cannot be allowed to hang. For each requirement, say in the same breath whether you take it to be non-negotiable for our purposes, negotiable, or an artifact of how the theorems are stated that a practitioner should feel free to drop; and tell me which of the things people demand here are in fact prejudices of one camp, anonymity and uniform treatment of propositions being my prime suspects. Then identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, say precisely where the slack is and what has to give if we keep the court-reviewable written justification, and tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates. I would also like your view on whether expertise-weighting is a coherent way out, given that our three blocs are appointed for different competences and our counsel thinks differential weights could be defended. Finally, in a paragraph at most, say how the obvious contenders — voting on each finding, voting on the conclusion, some qualified or weighted majority, and any sequential or agenda-driven procedure — do against your list. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and since I cannot scan a list, let each requirement be introduced and its status marked within its own sentences. No bibliography and no citations; I want the reasoning, not the references.
