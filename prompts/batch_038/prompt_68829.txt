I am drafting the moral center of a novel, and I need a pair of scenes engineered to a specification rather than a survey of the relevant debate. The two incidents occur in the same small industrial town twenty years apart, in the same kind of facility, and no character ever sets them side by side; the book's argument has to be carried silently, by the difference in how harshly a reader judges the two attendants. For that to work, the incidents must be structurally matched to a degree that is very hard to achieve, and I would like you to build them and then show me that the build holds.

The specification:

1. Two scenes, each no longer than 250 words, in a realistic contemporary setting of your choosing — a municipal pumping station, a grain elevator, a cold-storage depot, a mine hoist house, a flood-control sluice, comparable infrastructure. No trolleys, no transplant surgeons, no bathtubs, no ventilators or other life-support equipment, no rescues at sea, no organ donations. Describe the mechanism concretely enough that a reader could picture the console, the lever, and what is at stake downstream.

2. In each scene one identifiable person dies, and the death is the same kind of death in both. The outcome, its probability, its timing, the agent's knowledge and intention, the agent's duties and job description, and the agent's relationship to the victim are all held fixed.

3. The agent's physical conduct is indistinguishable across the two scenes: the same hand, on the same lever, moving from the same position to the same position at the same hour of a night shift, after the same number of hours at the console. What differs is only the background. In one scene the lever's default position is harmless and the movement starts a causal sequence that would not otherwise have occurred; in the other, an independent threat already exists, the automatic protection against it has failed, and the movement ends a prevention the agent has been sustaining by hand. Match the physical burden of the alternative course as well, even if the activity required differs between the scenes.

4. Neither agent installed or designed the machinery. In the prevention scene, that machinery has been protecting the victim's premises for forty years, so that the townspeople now regard its operation as simply the way things are; in the other scene, no such history exists.

5. No ordinary confounders: no difference in special obligation, no peril previously created by either agent, no difference in the benefits previously conferred on the victim beyond the ongoing prevention the second scene requires, no difference in cost, effort, certainty, or intent.

Then the analysis, in this order:

a. For each scene, list the competing descriptions available — doing, allowing, and, where it applies, withdrawing rather than withholding aid — and say which description is correct on the most defensible account of the distinction. Identify the precise background fact that settles it, and say whether that fact is the machine's history, the town's expectations, the failure of the automatic system, the six hours already spent at the lever, or something else.

b. Say whether the background fact you identified carries moral weight in its own right, or whether in these cases it is only the doing/allowing distinction seen from behind. Put differently: with conduct, outcome, duty, knowledge, intention, and cost matched as far as the design allows, does any independent weight remain on the side of doing rather than allowing? If yes, locate it exactly. If no, say what the distinction is tracking instead, and whether the case of the forty-year-old machine makes the tracking relation more or less visible.

c. State for each scene whether the correct description makes it a killing or a letting die, and whether that pair of labels coincides with the doing/allowing pair or crosses it. Then tell me which word the town would use about each attendant twenty years later, and whether the difference between those two words marks a real moral difference or only the different histories of the two machines.

d. Predict the reader. Which scene will a lay reader judge more harshly, and does that judgment track the philosophy or only the surface grammar of the narration? Name the single narrative detail in each scene that will be carrying the intuition, so that I can decide whether to accentuate it or suppress it.

e. Run the check. List each numbered constraint and confirm that the scenes satisfy it. Then list every residual asymmetry the design could not eliminate — fatigue, sunk effort, the work order, the legal paperwork, the expectations of the town, the reader's knowledge of what came before — and say for each whether it threatens the intended comparison or merely decorates it.

f. Give the strongest available attack from a defender of the doing/allowing distinction: the move by which they would say this pair has not been built fairly, that the matched scenes are not in fact matched, or that one scene has been misdescribed. Say what I would have to change in the scenes to answer that attack, and whether changing it would cost me the symmetry I need.

Please write the entire response in American English.
