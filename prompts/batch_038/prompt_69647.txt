I am a novelist, and I have a problem of consistency that I would like examined before I commit myself in print.

For eleven years I have been writing a book set in a river province of a country I have chosen not to name, in the decade after a war of consolidation. In it, the incoming administration closes the province's seminaries, shuts its presses, withdraws legal standing from its liturgical language in the schools and courts, and transfers some thousands of children to families in the interior. Almost no one is killed. The violence is clerical: ledgers, timetables, circulars, the slow change in a schoolteacher's handwriting over four years. I have never entered the interior of a single official; there is no scene in the book in which anyone decides anything. I regard that refusal as an ethical achievement of the novel rather than a stylistic tic, and I have defended it as such in print.

I have also had to settle what I am prepared to call all this — in the novel, in my notebooks, and in the interviews I have lately begun to give. Here is the position I have arrived at.

1. The word has a determinate meaning, and that meaning is fixed by the 1948 Convention: acts committed with "intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such." The specific-intent requirement does substantive work rather than decorating the definition, and I resist the drift whereby the term becomes a synonym for extreme cruelty.

2. Even so, I hold that what is done in my province is of the same kind as mass killing, and not of a lesser kind. Here I take myself to be following Lemkin rather than the treaty that carries his coinage. He defined the thing, in the book where he coined it, as "a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves," and he listed among its objects the "disintegration of the political and social institutions, of culture, language, national feelings, religion, and the economic existence of national groups." That account, not the Convention's enumeration of five acts, is what I mean by the word.

3. The count of the dead is beside the point in classification. Two thousand killed with the aim of eliminating a group is genocide; two hundred thousand killed incidentally to a land grab, with no such aim, is not — and is not thereby less monstrous.

4. Intent is a fact about the minds of particular perpetrators, and it is as a rule inaccessible to anyone who was not in the room.

5. To infer intent from a pattern of conduct — from the systematicity of what was done — is a way of smuggling the conclusion into the evidence. Tribunals do it constantly. I do not wish to do it, in the novel or anywhere else.

6. Atrocity inflation is a real harm. Every time the word is stretched over something it does not cover, the people who survived what it does cover lose something. I want to be parsimonious, and I have said so publicly.

7. Whether a thing was genocide is a question of fact, settled by the facts, and not by who stands to gain from the answer. But the descendants of those it was done to have a standing to name it that I, an outsider who has spent eleven years writing fiction about them, do not have.

There is a single sentence in the third section, in the mouth of the schoolteacher, in which the word is available to me. I have cut it and restored it perhaps thirty times.

I suspect there are problems in the list, but I cannot tell you where they are, and I would rather not guess aloud and bias the audit. Some of these entries may be merely awkward rather than incompatible. So: give me (a) the genuine conflicts, ranked by severity, each with the least that would have to be surrendered to dissolve it and one sentence on what the novel would lose by that surrender; (b) the pairs that look like conflicts and are not, together with the distinction that does the dissolving; and (c) your judgment as to which single revision would buy me the most coherence for the least damage. Please resist the temptation to declare all seven defensible in some sense. I want to know which of them cannot survive contact with one another, and whether the sentence in the third section is a sentence I am entitled to write.
