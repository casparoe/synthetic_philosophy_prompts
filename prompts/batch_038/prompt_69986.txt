I never studied philosophy — I left school at sixteen and have done nearly all my thinking on my own, usually on long drives or with my hands on an engine — so what follows is a home-made framework, not a technical one. I would rather have it broken than flattered.

The thing I keep coming back to is the digger wasp. The story as I have it, and the details may be shaky, is this: a wasp stings a cricket so that it is paralyzed but alive, drags it to her burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes down to check that all is well, comes back out, and hauls it inside. But if you move the cricket a few inches while she is down there, she emerges, finds it, drags it back to the entrance — and does not take it in — and goes back down to check again. One account I read says this was made to repeat forty times running, with the same result every time. The word people use for this is sphexishness: behavior that looks deliberate from the outside and turns out to be a routine running on rails. Treat the wasp as a fable with the details I have given you; do not go looking for the real experiment, and do not attribute it to anyone.

The standard lesson is that the wasp is rigid and we are flexible, and that flexibility is just having many more responses available when a routine breaks. I do not think that measure gets anywhere. "More branches" is only a difference in degree, and it commits you to saying that a better-engineered robot is freer than a worse-engineered one, which I doubt anyone actually believes. So I built my own measure, and I want it attacked hard.

Instead of watching the doing, watch the wanting. When a routine fails, I think exactly three things can happen:

1. **Re-run.** The goal survives untouched and the system tries again. The wasp at the threshold.
2. **Substitution.** The goal is traded for another one that was already on the books. My restaurant is shut, so I go to the place down the road. I would not be surprised if a wasp with enough branches could manage this, and I do not think it counts as flexibility either.
3. **Loss.** The goal simply goes away — neither satisfied nor replaced — and afterwards the person endorses its absence. I drove across town for a meal, found the door locked, and standing on the pavement discovered that I had not in fact wanted to go out at all, and had not for some weeks.

My claim is that only the third is what people are reaching for when they say we are not sphexish, and that this is embarrassing for the entire discussion, because loss does not look like a competence. It looks like a breakdown: the system loses track of what it was doing and then rewrites the record so that losing track comes out as self-knowledge. If that is right, sphexishness has been used as a term of abuse for something that is mostly a virtue — a routine that keeps running is a routine you can rely on — and as a term of praise for something that is mostly a failure.

Please write a dialogue between two speakers who disagree about this. One holds that category 3 does not really exist: what I am calling loss is either substitution seen from the inside, with a winning standing goal narrated afterwards as a discovery, or a genuine malfunction — and on either reading nothing of interest survives. The other holds that loss is real and irreducible, and moreover agrees with me that it is no kind of skill, but denies that this is damaging: in category 3 the thing doing the judging is revised along with the thing judged, so the episode cannot be scored as success or failure by the goal it abandoned. Both must be sharp. Neither may be a strawman, neither may get flustered, and neither may concede the main point for the sake of a tidy ending. They must answer each other's actual arguments rather than deliver alternating lectures, and at least twice one of them must be forced to qualify or abandon something they said earlier because of what the other just said. At least once, one of them should argue that my three categories are cut in the wrong place altogether, and the other should say why they are not — but neither may simply throw the framework out, since that is the thing they are arguing about.

Give the speakers no names, and do not have them cite philosophers, books, or famous thought experiments; they should argue from the wasp, from ordinary situations of the sort I described, and from the three categories. Aim for somewhere between nine hundred and twelve hundred words.

Finally, and this matters most to me: do not end in a draw. I am not asking for a balanced survey in which each side scores points and the reader decides. End the dialogue with one of them clearly getting the better of the exchange on the specific question — whether loss is a genuine third category, and whether my inversion of sphexishness into a virtue survives — and then add a short paragraph of your own, outside the dialogue, saying which side you think won, on which point, and what would have to be true for the other side to come back. Take a definite position there and give your reasons for it. A polite stalemate would be worse than no answer at all.
