I am the instructor for a third-year philosophy of mind course, and I need a second reader's judgment on one submitted essay before I finalize the grade. I have spent the better part of an evening arguing with a colleague about whether taxonomies of this kind — ladders of increasingly sophisticated minds — do any real explanatory work or merely redescribe a continuum in a flattering vocabulary. That argument has no bearing on your task, and I have deliberately kept my own view out of these materials. What I need from you is an assessment of this particular essay on its own terms.

**The assignment.** Students were asked to respond, in no more than 1,500 words, to the following question:

> Daniel Dennett sorts minded creatures into four kinds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian — arranged as a "tower of generate-and-test." Set out the taxonomy and the distinction between each rung. Then answer this: does the taxonomy have genuine explanatory content, or is it a post-hoc redescription of a continuum of cognitive sophistication? Your answer should say what would count as evidence that the rungs are real rather than imposed.

Grading conventions for this course, so that your number is comparable to mine: 70 is a correct and organized exposition of the material with no analysis to speak of; 80 is a correct exposition plus a defensible argument of the student's own; 90 and above is reserved for essays that are both accurate and genuinely critical, where the criticism is developed rather than gestured at. Points come off for philosophical errors and for inferences that do not hold, not for taking an unfashionable line.

**The submission.** Paragraph numbers are mine, added for grading convenience; the student did not include them.

(1) The taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures is often dismissed as a metaphor — a ladder we impose on a continuum of cleverness. I want to argue that it earns its place for a more modest reason. Each rung names a distinct way of moving the work of trial and error out of the world and into the organism, and so each rung carries predictions about which competences a creature can and cannot have. Dennett's tower of generate-and-test is not a story about how much mind a creature possesses. It is a story about where the selection happens.

(2) The rungs are named for the thinkers whose work best describes the selection process operating at each level: Darwin for design by natural selection, Skinner for design by reinforcement, Popper for design by the elimination of bad hypotheses before they are acted on, and Richard Gregory for the work on intelligent tool use and on how children acquire cultural artifacts that supplies the top rung. Darwinian creatures get their competences from selection on their design. What they can do is fixed at birth, and the species improves only through the death of individuals whose design was worse. Bacteria, plants, and the fixed action patterns of insects sit here: the Venus flytrap closes on contact and needs no view of the world to do it.

(3) Skinnerian creatures add a second selector inside the skin. Reinforcement picks out, from among the behaviors the organism emits, which will be retained, so the organism can adjust to conditions its ancestors never encountered. The cost is that the adjustment is made by doing: the creature has to perform the behavior and be rewarded or punished for it before anything changes. In both of these cases the testing is carried out in the world, on the body of the creature. That is the general shape of the bottom two rungs, and it is what the Popperian innovation reverses.

(4) It is worth pausing on what the intentional stance licenses at each rung, because this is where the taxonomy is most often misunderstood. On Dennett's account, to call something a believer is not to make a claim about its inner machinery but to say that the best way to predict its behavior is to ascribe beliefs and desires. That instrumental reading has a consequence the tower makes vivid. A system whose behavior is best explicable by ascribing beliefs has crossed into belief; a system better predicted mechanically has not. Darwinian creatures are precisely the systems we predict mechanically, so the flytrap falls below the intentional level. Skinnerian creatures largely fall below it as well: what a pigeon acquires is a disposition to peck, and the best prediction of the pigeon is given by its reinforcement history rather than by anything it takes the world to be. Belief proper therefore arrives at the Popperian rung, and not before.

(5) What arrives there is the capacity to let one's guesses die instead of oneself. A Popperian creature has, over and above its behavioral repertoire, an internal model of its environment, and it can run candidate actions off against that model, eliminating the ones that would come to grief before it commits to any of them. Dennett's borrowing from Popper is deeper than the slogan suggests, however. For Popper the eliminating is done by criticism, and criticism is a public, linguistic activity: a conjecture can be refuted only if it can be stated, objected to, and argued against. So the internal model that makes a creature Popperian must itself have something like the structure of a language. It follows that the Popperian rung cannot be occupied by anything lacking language, and it also explains why becoming a language user is not a fifth step: we do not acquire a new kind of competence when we learn to speak, we merely do at full speed what any Popperian creature is already doing.

(6) Gregorian creatures are those whose external environment has been rebuilt by their predecessors into a standing supply of thinking aids — words, pictures, maps, number systems, and above all writing. What is distinctive is not that they communicate, since bees communicate, but that they can install in themselves competences no selection process ever designed, by taking up artifacts whose own design was the product of other Gregorian creatures' intelligence. This is the rung where the tower does its most interesting work, and also where it is most often undersold. Going up the tower is not merely going up in competence; it is going up in the kind of inner life a creature has. A Darwinian creature has no perspective on its world, a Skinnerian creature has something like one, a Popperian creature has a genuine one, and only a Gregorian creature can make itself an object of its own consideration. The tower therefore answers the question it is usually accused of dodging: it tells us where consciousness begins, and it tells us that the differences among a flytrap, a pigeon, a chimpanzee, and a person are differences in kind rather than differences in how much experience there is.

(7) The strongest objection to all this is that the tower is mammal-centric. Octopuses open jars, solve novel problems, and use tools, while having a nervous system in which most neurons are distributed through the arms rather than gathered into anything like a brain. Bees perform route integration and social learning that looks, from the outside, exactly like the competences the taxonomy reserves for the higher rungs. If the rungs can be occupied by animals whose architecture is nothing like ours, the taxonomy may be a ladder we impose on a continuum rather than a set of joints we find in it. Dennett's reply would presumably be that this is no problem, since the taxonomy is about function and not implementation. But if it is about function alone, it is hard to see why the rungs should come in a strict order at all, and I suspect the honest conclusion is that the tower is a useful heuristic which has not yet been shown to pick out natural kinds. One thing it does settle is a live practical question: if belief, and with it any perspective a creature has on its own condition, first appears at the Popperian rung, then insects cannot be subjects of suffering in any sense that would confer moral standing on them, and the recent interest in insect welfare rests on a confusion between complicated behavior and a mind.

(8) So the taxonomy explains rather than redescribes — but not for the reason usually given. Its content lies in the claim that each rung relocates selection further inside the organism, and in the predictions that follow about which competences must be absent below each rung. Whether those predictions have been tested is another matter, and the essay above has not tested them.
