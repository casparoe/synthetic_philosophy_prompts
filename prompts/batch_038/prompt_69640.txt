I am a graduate student in political theory preparing a commentary for a departmental policy seminar on whether my country — a small, militarily non-aligned European state that currently fields an all-volunteer professional force — should reintroduce compulsory service. During the discussion a senior participant defended conscription with the slogan "the army is a school of the nation," and then used it twice in ways that struck me as quite different, though he clearly took them to be the same point.

The first time, he deployed it against a cost-effectiveness objection: granting that a professional force delivers more capability per euro spent, conscription supposedly repays the difference in civic cohesion, cross-class solidarity, and a population that understands what its own defence demands of it. The second time, when I objected that any realistic draft in a country of our size would be highly selective — Sweden paused its draft in 2010 and, after reactivating a gender-neutral system in 2017, has called up on the order of four thousand people a year from an age cohort of roughly one hundred thousand — he replied that selectivity was beside the point, because the school works on those who never put on the uniform as well.

I want to be able to say precisely why those two uses cannot rest on a single claim. Please lay out, as a menu rather than as one favoured interpretation, the distinct precise theses the slogan might be expressing. For each reading, give me:

1. A one-sentence formulation that fixes its content: who the pupils are (only those who serve, the whole age cohort including the exempt, the officer corps, the political class, the nation's institutions); what the curriculum consists in (skills, dispositions, a shared experience, habits of obedience, knowledge of the state's coercive apparatus); and what the claimed output is (better citizens, a more cohesive society, a more legitimate state, a mobilizable reserve).

2. A classification of the kind of claim it is — conceptual or constitutive (about what citizenship amounts to), empirical (about the causal effects of service), expressive (about the symbolism of universal liability), or normative (about when a state may coerce and impose lethal risk). Much of the confusion in that seminar seemed to come from treating a claim of one kind as though it answered an objection framed in another, and I would like that made explicit.

3. A verdict: near-platitude that nobody disputes, substantive but defensible, empirically untestable given how modern drafts are actually administered, or simply false.

4. What sort of finding would confirm or undermine it, described in terms of kinds of evidence rather than named studies or authors, since I want to reason about it myself.

Please attend in particular to the following distinctions, which I suspect are where the interesting work lies:

- The difference between a treatment effect of serving and a selection effect of being selected, and what happens to each civic reading once service covers a few percent of a cohort rather than most of it. If the conscripted are screened for aptitude and motivation, what remains of the claim that service produces civic dispositions?
- The difference between the army teaching the nation and the nation teaching itself through the army — that is, between an institution that transmits dispositions to individuals who pass through it and a standing structure of universal liability whose mere existence alters how those who never serve relate to the state and to one another.
- The militia reading, on which the schooling is indirect: the army's real civic product is a cadre of trained civilians who carry military norms back into workplaces, municipal councils, and voluntary associations, so that the civic effect scales with the size of the reserve rather than with annual intake.
- Whether any reading survives the shift from the mass conscript armies of the twentieth century to today's small, technically specialized, often expeditionary forces, or whether the slogan is now doing retrospective work.
- Whether there is a reading on which the slogan is not about civic improvement at all but about legitimacy: that a state may impose lethal risk on its population only if the population bearing that risk is, in the relevant sense, the same body as the one authorizing it.

Finally, tell me which of the readings you have listed would actually have supported each of my interlocutor's two moves, and which would not; and whether his second move — that selectivity is irrelevant — rescues the first move or quietly abandons it. I would like the result as an ordered list I can bring to the seminar, with the verdicts stated plainly rather than hedged.
