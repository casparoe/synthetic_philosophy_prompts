I'm on a city naming commission — twelve of us, brand new intake, and I've somehow been volunteered to run the 90-minute orientation before our first real case comes up in the spring. Nobody has any philosophy. There's a retired postal carrier, two realtors, a high school history teacher, and several people the council appointed for reasons nobody can name. I dont want to brief them on the case. I want to teach them one distinction they can carry into every case we'll ever see: that a commemorative name isn't a piece of historical record — that naming a boulevard after someone is doing something different from documenting them, and that most of what people yell about at these hearings is really a fight over which of those two things they think is happening.

Don't explain the distinction to me, I have it. Plan how I explain it to them, in 90 minutes, with a whiteboard. Specifically: what has to be in place before the distinction means anything at all (I suspect "an honor is a present-tense thing you're doing, not a claim about the past" is load-bearing, but I don't know where in the sequence it goes or what it needs underneath it first). Whether to carry the whole thing on our own live case — the boulevard is named for the engineer who built the levee project that also cut a neighborhood in half, and local historians genuinely disagree about whether he pushed for that route or just drew what the council picked — or whether that's a trap, because it'll collapse into an argument about facts I can't settle in the room. If it's a trap, tell me what a clean stand-in case would have to have and what it would have to leave out (I'm guessing a case where the person's record is beyond dispute, but explain why). Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive: "removing a name erases history," "you can't judge people by today's standards," "he did far more good than harm so it nets out," "just put up a plaque explaining it," and the one I dread most, "the name doesn't mean anything anymore, it's just an address people type into a GPS." For each, give me the moment in the sequence where I head it off before it forms, not the moment I'd have to argue it down afterward — and say which of the five is actually a reasonable position that deserves an answer rather than a correction. Give me one question I can put to them at the end that shows whether the distinction landed, ideally one where somebody who memorized my phrasing answers differently from somebody who's got it. And tell me what I should refuse to get into at this level, and say out loud in the room that I'm refusing it.

Last thing, and it matters: as you lay out what I should tell them, keep straight for me which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on memory, monuments and public naming, and which parts are live arguments. I don't want to hand twelve laypeople a contested normative view with a consensus price tag stuck on it — especially anywhere the answer might turn on how the unresolved history of our own engineer comes out. Flag those spots explicitly.
