An episode I keep turning over: the 2018 Uber self-driving fatality in Tempe. The facts I'm working from — flag it if I've got one wrong — are that the car picked up Elaine Herzberg roughly six seconds out as she pushed her bike across a dark road, but its classfier had no pedestrian category away from a crosswalk, so it flip-flopped between vehicle, bicycle, and unknown object for about five seconds. Each label came with a different prediction (vehicle/bike = moving with traffic in the next lane; unknown = standing still), and every flip made her a brand-new object with the track history dropped, so it never built up a collision trajectory and never slowed. At about one second out, action suppression held off braking for a full second while control got handed to the safety driver, who was looking at her phone.

I think this is a frame problem case, but backwards from the usual invocation: Uber wasn't crushed by having to write down everything that stays the same — it had no persistence assumptions at all, and that's what killed her. So which frame problem is it actually? McCarthy and Hayes' bookkeeping version, the qualification/ramification stuff, or the Dreyfus–Fodor relevance version where you can't say locally what matters? And name the specific inference the system needed plus what kind of default would license it.

The part I care about most: every NTSB fix is a small enumeration — add a jaywalker class, keep track IDs, re-enable the Volvo AEB, two drivers not one. Does that show the frame problem was never deep, or is it exactly the pessimists' point that the list of abnormal cases never ends?
