I've spent fifteen years working on body ownership and interoceptive inference — rubber-hand and full-body illusions, temporo-parietal junction stimulation, predictive accounts on which the sense of being a self is a standing model kept updated by visceral and proprioceptive signal rather than anything given. I've been asked to co-teach a session on Avicenna's flying man, and I want to know how much of that argument survives being run through my machinery.

The argument as I understand it: consider someone brought into existence all at once, fully formed, in a void — sight screened off, limbs arranged so they touch neither one another nor anything else, no sound, smell, or taste, and no sensation of heart, bowels, or brain either. Such a person, Avicenna says, would still affirm his own existence while affirming no limb, no organ, and nothing external, and would not take his self to have length, breadth, or depth. Two further claims matter: this self-awareness is immediate, produced by no prior operation, and continuous — there is never a moment when the mind is unaware of itself. The conclusion drawn is that the self is a substance other than the body and separable from it.

Restate all of that in my vocabulary, step by step: the setup, the intuition it is meant to elicit, the move from not being aware of the limbs to not being the limbs, the denial of extension, the immediacy and continuity claims, the separability conclusion. For each, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all.

Then the residue report, strictly done. Don't smuggle my framework's presuppositions in and call them translation; if the honest rendering of some step simply begs the question against Avicenna, say so rather than dressing it up as a mapping. My own hunch, which I want tested rather than indulged, is that the deprivation condition is doing two incompatible jobs: the argument needs total sensory silence to establish that the awareness is non-sensory, but the intuition feels compelling only because the reader silently keeps interoception and a body schema intact — and in my framework a subject with genuinely zero interoceptive signal has no self-model with which to affirm anything. Tell me whether that is right, whether it is merely one reading among several, or whether it misses what the argument is actually doing. Finally, which parts retain force after the translation, and is what falls away a limit of my framework (no room for a first-person givenness that is not itself a representation) or a defect in the argument? No citations or scholarship, please; I'll handle the medieval side myself.
