So I keep circling back to Hofstadter's MU-puzzle and I want an essay that actually picks a side rather than summarizing the puzzle. Quick setup so we're on the same page: start with MI, four string-rewriting rules, target MU, and the answer is no — the number of I's you can ever reach is never divisible by 3, and MU has zero I's. The point GEB wants you to take away is that no amount of grinding inside the system gets you there, but a person can see it by jumping out of the system, i.e. by reasoning about the rules instead of applying them, and Hofstadter treats that as basically the same move as seeing that a Gödel sentence is true.

Here's the twist I want taken seriously, becuase I don't think the standard telling survives it. Hand the same puzzle to a plain old program: breadth-first search over derivations up to some depth, never finds MU, notices the I-count pattern in the data it collected, conjectures the mod-3 invariant, and then proves it by induction. Operationally that program did exactly what Hofstadter says the human does — it stopped pushing symbols around and started proving theorems about the symbol-pushing. So either the program jumped out of the system too, in which case jumping out isn't a mark of anything non-mechanical at all, or it didn't, in which case somebody owes an account of what's different, and I don't think anyone gives one that isn't just restating the intuition.

Write the essay around that dilemma. I want both sides genuinely argued: the Lucas/Penrose line that any particular program can be stepped outside of by someone who can see it as a program while humans can't be pinned to any one system; and the opposing worry that the system in jumping out of the system is never actually well-defined, since every formal system sits inside larger ones and the whole thing looks like an infinite regress of levels rather than a boundary you cross once. Also worth getting into: whether the relevant contrast is really mechanism versus non-mechanism, or whether it's a contrast between local search and generalization that machines plainly have too, and whether the Gödel analogy even holds given that the human here never had to establish their own consistency the way the Gödelian argument supposedly requires.

Then commit. Is JOOTSing a real difference in kind of reasoning, or just a difference in level of description that a machine can occupy as easily as a person? Don't end up fence-sitting, and don't wiggle out by saying it all depends what you mean by mechanical — unless you then say precisely what you do mean and defend that.
