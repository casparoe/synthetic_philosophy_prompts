So I'm stuck in the middle of a fight at work and I want to see what the document that ends it would look like.

I do the philosophy side at a small research org, and we've been asked to help a state ag agency decide whether farmed insects get covered under its welfare regs — the immediate target is black soldier fly larvae being raised by the ton for feed, and commercial honeybees. Two of the people advising on it have been going back and forth in meetings for about six months, and I honestly can't tell how much of the gap between them is real.

- One is a gradationist: experience and moral status come in degrees, there's no bright line, a bee or a fly larva has some nonzero moral weight, and the only honest question is how much rather than whether.
- The other holds what I'll call the threshold view: sentience is on/off, either there's a subject of experience there or there isn't, and what actually differs between a pig and a beetle is the richness of that subject's life, not the presence of experience. On this view "degrees of sentience" is a muddle that either collapses into degrees of *evidence* or into a claim about welfare content that doesn't require consciousness itself to be graded at all.

Write the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between them should have produced. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of claims both would sign. This should include stuff about what the evidence for insect sentience actually consists of (nociception and its limits, associative learning, motivation and trade-off behavior, how their nervous systems are organized), about what would count as evidence going the other way, and about the practical stakes for the regs. Also: a wrinkle both of them have to handle, which is that the draft regs already list decapod crustaceans, and each side thinks that fact supports *them*.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the disputed claim / the gradationist's formulation, in words they'd accept / the threshold person's formulation, in words they'd accept / which crux it turns on / the concrete case or piece of evidence both agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. The predictions have to be actual predictions — what each says will happen in the specific case — not "more work is needed here."

3. The cruxes, called out separately. I have guesses: (a) whether a graded account needs a cardinal scale to be usable at all, or whether ordinal ranking is enough — and if ordinal is enough, what the regs are supposed to *do* with it when the question on the table is literally how many larvae you trade for one hen; (b) whether "is experience graded?" and "is moral status graded?" can come apart in both directions, which would mean part of the fight is over which of the two questions they've been arguing about for six months; (c) the thermostat problem — if the gradationist's own indicators get satisfied by systems neither of them wants to include, does the gradationist bite the bullet or add a cutoff, and if they add a cutoff, how is that not the threshold view with extra steps. Stress-test those, drop the ones that don't hold up, and add the cruxes I've missed.

4. A short section saying which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and what's genuinely left over once those are stripped out. If your answer is that none of it is verbal, argue for that.

No bibliography and no reading lists, I dont want that, just the arguments and the formulations. And please don't referee the thing — the deliverable is a text both of them could put their names to, so each position has to be stated at full strength rather than strawmanned and then gently corrected.
