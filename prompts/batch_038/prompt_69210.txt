I am drafting a chapter on invertebrate welfare for an edited volume, and before I submit it I would like a consistency check on the position I actually defend. Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can manage.

(a) Moral considerability rests on the capacity for valenced experience and on nothing else. Cognitive sophistication, phylogenetic proximity to us, sociality and behavioural flexibility neither add nor subtract anything in themselves; they matter only insofar as they are evidence about experience.

(b) Sentience comes in degrees, and the moral weight of an individual scales with the intensity and duration of what it feels. I put an individual honeybee's whole life at no more than a thousandth of a human life, and I would be comfortable going lower.

(c) Where I am uncertain whether a creature is sentient, the correct response is to weight the possible states by my credence in them and act on the expectation, rather than to suspend judgement until the question is settled.

(d) My credence that any given insect species is sentient is low: roughly one in ten for a bee, and lower for many other arthropods. I resist arguments that it should be much higher on the present evidence. What moves me here is largely the sparseness of the insect nervous system — I see no obvious candidate for the kind of integrative architecture that experience would seem to require.

(e) I also hold that my credences across insect taxa are strongly correlated rather than independent: the evidence bearing on bees bears in much the same direction on ants, wasps and beetles. So although my per-species credence is low, my credence that some insects are sentient is high — well above four in five.

(f) Taking the standing global population of individual insects to be of the order of 10^18, and applying (b), (c) and (d), I conclude that insect welfare is among the largest moral problems of our time, larger in aggregate than the whole of vertebrate animal agriculture. I have done the multiplication: even after the thousandfold per-life discount and ninety per cent uncertainty, insects come out several orders of magnitude ahead, and I regard that result as robust rather than as an artefact.

(g) I am nevertheless no expected-value fanatic. I reject Pascal's mugging, I am sceptical of any reasoning in which a small probability attached to an enormous quantity is permitted to dominate a decision, and I deny that the value of a population is a straightforward linear sum of its members' welfare — I reject the repugnant conclusion, and I do not think a teeming world of lives barely worth living is better than a smaller world of flourishing people. Relatedly, I hold that marginal changes in my credence about insect sentience would not alter my practical priorities, since on my view those priorities are already saturated.

(h) I hold a laissez-faire view about wild ecosystems. Even if wild insect suffering is enormous, I do not think we should intervene to reduce it: our ignorance of trophic cascades makes intervention reckless, and the presumptive default is to leave the system alone. My practical priorities are therefore insect farming and insecticide use, where the consequences of what we do are at least tractable. The suffering in the wild is real but not ours to fix.

The case I use to test the whole package: a locust control programme will kill on the order of 10^13 individuals in a season. I say this is a greater moral loss than a programme that would kill 10^6 farmed chickens, and I say that anyone who does not feel the comparison that way is exhibiting a defect of sentiment rather than a defect of arithmetic.

Please audit this set rather than survey the wider debate for me. Specifically: identify which pairs and clusters genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do; rank the genuine tensions by severity, saying for each what would have to be given up and what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere; check whether the quantities I multiply together in (f) are the quantities my other commitments tell me to use; tell me whether my locust verdict is one my own principles can actually deliver; and finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most.

Begin your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence — the most serious inconsistency in the set — and then give the reasoning supporting the ranking. No preamble, and no restatement of my question. Please write in British English.
