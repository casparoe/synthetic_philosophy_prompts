I run a 12-person philosophy-and-politics reading group, mostly second-years, 90 minutes on a Thursday evening, and I want a session on judgment aggregation where they walk into the discursive dilemma themselves instead of me explaining it up front. No names or theory dropped until the end.

The shape I'm after: they act as a small committee making a decision that's real for us — either which of four candidate topics we spend next term on, or a mock panel that actually hands over a £10 book token to somebody — and the decision has to decompose into a few yes/no premises plus a conclusion, set up so that voting premise-by-premise and voting conclusion-first come apart. So give me the exact question, the premises, the private view cards for all 12 people, and the full profile written out as a table so I can check the arithmatic myself (I've been handed too many of these where the numbers dont in fact produce the split). Plus the procedure, who chairs it, how votes are taken, and a reward scheme that doesn't need real money if you can avoid it.

Then two wrinkles I want actually built into the mechanics rather than gestured at in the debrief. First: run the committee a second time on a slightly different proposal with one member swapped out, so the group ends up contradicting something it decided ten minutes earlier and has to sit with it — I want them to feel the diachronic version, where a panel that's perfectly coherent at every single meeting still manages to make its own record incoherent over time. Second: make somebody put their name to the outcome. One of them has to draft and sign the short justification letter that goes out to the "applicants", and the design should guarantee that they personally disagree with at least part of what they're signing — I want the group-agent point to land as a stomach sensation rather than a slogan, i.e. the decision is genuinely ours but nobody in the room actually holds it.

Also fold in a third thing if it fits cleanly: does the order the premises get voted in change the result? If it can, engineer the cards so a sharp student discovers that live, mid-game, without me pointing at it.

Then the debrief. Map each moment of the session onto the concepts — why the split arises structurally rather than from anybody being stupid, and what each of the fixes costs (premise-first, conclusion-first, or giving up on the group having one coherent view at all). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it; my guesses are that they smell the trick halfway through and start voting strategically, or that one confident person steamrolls the room, but tell me what actually happens and how to steer it back. And be straight with me about which feature of the idea a paper-and-pencil committee game just can't reproduce, that I'm going to have to supply by hand.
