Right, so I run a fairly tiny podcast — a few hundred regulars, mostly people who've spent as many years on the alignment forum as I have — and next month's guest is a safety researcher who's been pushing one specific proposal that I think is fascinating and also possibly bonkers. I want to grill him properly rather than let him talk past me for an hour, so I need help with the questions.

His proposal, as I understand it: suppose a very capable AI ends up bargaining with other advanced agents it can never causally influence — agents far outside its light cone that might threaten it, for instance by announcing they'll do something ghastly somewhere in the past or in a simulation unless it complies. Rather than let those threats land on whatever the system actually values, we deliberately install an extra goal alongside the real ones. Something it cares about for its own sake but which is basically a throwaway — the illustration I've seen is making the agent care intensely about money being burnt, so a threatener threatens to burn money rather than to hurt the things we care about. The catch is that the agent has to care about protecting the surrogate at least as much as it cares about avoiding the real harm, otherwise the whole thing falls over.

Give me eight to ten questions in the order I should actually ask them, so the pressure builds. No warm-up filler about what got him into the field, and nothing he can bat away with a shrug. For each one, a line saying what answer you expect and the follow-up keyed to it.

The things I specifically want him cornered on:

- Calibration. How do you get a system to weight the surrogate goal precisely against real harm, and how would you ever check you'd got it right? Slightly underweighted and threats just get redirected back onto what matters; overweighted and the agent starts sacrificing real value to protect the decoy.
- Signalling. In an acausal deal nobody can read your source code. How does a distant threatener come to believe your agent has a surrogate goal at all — and doesn't publicising the plan amount to advertising a soft spot to every would-be extortionist out there?
- Spillover. Doesn't this just make the system cheap to buy off in ordinary, causal negotiations with humans, and what stops surrogate-goal logic leaking into those deals?
- Threats the decoy can't absorb: ones aimed at information about its dispositions, ones that require it to modify itself, ones against agents it genuinely cares about.
- Whether the threat model earns its keep. Would any sophisticated agent bother with acausal threats when it can't punish non-compliance afterwards? And if most such threats are empty, what is the proposal buying us?
- His go-to deflection is that we don't know the threat model yet and this is cheap insurance. Build a run of questions that makes that progressively harder to use, ending with one about what it would cost if he's wrong and what evidence available right now would tell us the whole approach is a mistake.
- Why this rather than just making the system refuse every acausal threat outright — and what we'd be giving up on the trade side by being tough.
- Who the principal even is, given humanity isn't a single one, and whose values end up as collateral. Plus the awkward fact that we'd have to install this now, in a system nobody can fully interpret.

Finish with one question about the smallest version of this that could be tested on current models, and what result he'd count as a failure.
