I am a novelist — four published books, and I teach craft seminars, so my working life is conducted inside a fairly definite vocabulary: what a character wants as against what she needs, the object of desire, stakes, what a person will and will not trade, the scene conceived as a negotiation of leverage and appetite, the symbolic weight of an object that is worth something quite other than what it fetches, inheritance and debt as the two engines of the family plot, and the standing workshop admonition that every transaction on the page is also a transaction of power or affection. I have read Aristotle in translation but never studied him formally, and I would far rather be corrected than flattered.

Please take his account of money and exchange and restate it in that vocabulary of mine, claim by claim. The claims I have in mind are these: the argument in the Nicomachean Ethics that need is what actually holds exchange together, and that money exists by convention rather than by nature as a stand-in for need — called nomisma precisely because it is set by nomos and is in our power to alter or render useless; the accompanying remark that money serves as a pledge against future need, though it is not itself always of the same worth; the concession that things so greatly different cannot in truth be made commensurate, though with reference to need they become commensurate enough for practice; the requirement that exchange between unequal persons be proportionate rather than equal, with the farmer, the shoemaker and the builder set against one another in a cross-ratio that money exists to equalize, and with the city held together by proportionate requital; and the argument in the Politics that household management must be distinguished from money-making, that retail trade takes wealth from other men rather than from nature, and that lending at interest is the most unnatural form of wealth-getting, since there the offspring is money made from money itself.

Render each of these as precisely as you can in the terms of my craft, and resist the temptation to smuggle my assumptions in under the cover of translation: telling me that Aristotle is really describing motivation, or that money is really only a symbol, is exactly the move I want you either to avoid or to label as an imposition when you make it. Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the point of the exercise. Which of these claims translate cleanly, which survive only with visible distortion, and which have no expression at all in a vocabulary built out of appetite, leverage, and what a thing is worth to the person holding it? For each piece of residue, tell me whether it indicts my vocabulary — whether craft can render desire and advantage with total precision and has no term whatever for whether the terms of a bargain between unequals were fair — or whether it suggests that Aristotle's account is less load-bearing than it appears, for instance that need is doing all the work and money merely decorates it, or that the cross-ratio of builder to shoemaker is not a theory of price and cannot be pressed into one.

I ask because I am drafting a novel in which a bequest and an old debt drive everything, and I have noticed that I can describe every exchange in it as a matter of who wants what and who has the leverage to obtain it, while I cannot find a single place where the question of whether the terms were fair would do any narrative work at all. I want to know whether that is a poverty in my toolkit or an accurate report about the material. Plain prose, please, and no bibliography, reading list, or citations: I want the translation and the residue, not the scholarship around them.
