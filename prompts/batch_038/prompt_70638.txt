I'm prepping a talk for our grad reading group on procreative ethics and I've argued myself into a corner I can't climb out of. You're a philosopher who works on population ethics and person-affecting views — you know Benatar's asymmetry cold but you're not in the fan club. Think carefully before you commit; I'd rather get one right answer than a survey.

Here's the argument. I built it to show the asymmetry has real teeth, and now I think it has too many.

(1) Any sentient life contains pains that are bad for the one whose pains they are, and none of those bads would have been there had that being never existed.
(2) The absence of those pains is a good even when there's no one around to enjoy their absence — that's exactly what the asymmetry claims.
(3) The pleasures that being would have had are not a missing bad when it never exists, since there's nobody for whom their absence is a deprivation.
(4) So for any possible being, never existing is better than existing, and there's therefore a reason against bringing it into existence that doesn't belong to anybody in particular.
(5) Nothing can outweigh a reason of that kind. The only candidates are goods the being would get — pleasure, love, meaning — and by (3) those are precisely the goods that carry no weight when the alternative is nonexistence. So the reason against procreation is never outweighed, however good the life would have been.
(6) Hence procreation is always impermissible: not risky, not regrettable, wrong — and still wrong for a child who would have had a wonderful life.
(7) Now generalize. What made the reason in (4) subjectless was that the pains get avoided and nobody is thereby deprived. But that same description fits death: killing or letting die also removes pains, and once the being is gone there's likewise nobody for whom missing pleasures counts as a deprivation. So the reason in (4) applies to ceasing to exist just as much as to never existing.
(8) And it applies to any sentient beings we can affect, not just humans. So a gene-drive sterility program that phases out a wild rabbit population painlessly over three or four generations would be a moral improvement rather than a tragedy, and the same reasoning says don't resuscitate the guy whose remaining life is mostly suffering.
(9) But (8) is something I flatly refuse to accept, and Benatar himself insists none of this licenses killing people who already exist — he thinks death is a harm. So either the asymmetry can't be generalized the way (7) generalizes it, or the asymmetry is false and antinatalism goes down with it.

I'd much rather keep (2) and (3), because the asymmetry is the whole point, and I'd like to keep some version of (6) too. My instinct is that (7) is the troublemaker but I can't say what's wrong with it — the two cases really do look alike to me when I write them out. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost, in commitments I'd have to give up rather than in slogans?
