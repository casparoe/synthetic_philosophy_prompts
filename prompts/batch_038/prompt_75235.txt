Not an academic — I read philosophy of mind for fun and I keep running into an inference I can't find a handle for. Here's the shape of it. Take a case where things go right and a case where they go wrong, and suppose the two are exactly alike from the inside: actually seeing a red apple on the table, and hallucinating one so well that the person couldn't tell the difference. Then the claim is that because the two feel identical, what's really going on in both can only be what they have in common — so the person who genuinely sees the apple is, strictly speaking, just having a seeming-to-see, and the apple itself is a further hypothesis on top. The bad case sets the terms, and the good case gets demoted to the bad case plus luck.

The thing that strikes me is how little the actual examples matter. Nobody ever points at a real illusion or a real hallucination; the mere possibility of a matching bad case seems to do all the work. And I notice the same move in places that have nothing to do with perception. A vivid false memory that feels just like a true one, so what I have when I'm really remembering turns out to be only a memory-like seeming, and whether the event happened is an extra inference. Or a confident liar who sounds exactly like an honest witness, so hearing someone speak is never really hearing the truth, only hearing truth-sounding noises.

So does this have a name? I've seen people gesture at the veil of perception and at the argument from illusion, but those sound like the conclusion, or like one particular application of it, rather than the underlying principle that indistinguishability forces you down to the shared element. I want the principle.

And I'm not going to be satisfied with a bare label — I want the carving. Specifically: is it one principle or two? I suspect that "both episodes share a common mental element" is a different claim from "the subject in the bad case has all the same evidence and justification", and that people slide between them. Which of the two do the resisters actually resist, and can you deny one while granting the other? What does the denial amount to — is the objector making a claim about the nature of experience, about what can be known from the inside, or about something else entirely, and do they owe an explanation of why the subject can't tell the cases apart? Also: does the principle need actual indistinguishability, or does a merely possible bad case that nobody is ever in do the same job? And is my extension to memory and testimony a recognised move or am I on my own — is it stable to reject the principle for perception while accepting it for memory? If the thing genuinely has no name, say so plainly and coin one, with a definition and edges: what counts as the same move, what doesn't, and how it differs from ordinary external-world scepticism, which looks like a conclusion you could reach without this step at all.

Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on it. Don't hand me one side's reading as the standard view — in particular, tell me if the answers about one-principle-or-two and about what the resisters are resisting are things people actually disagree on rather than things you can just tell me.
