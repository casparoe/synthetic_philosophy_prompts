So I'm in a political philosophy seminar on historical injustice and I want to diagnose something that's been driving me nuts for weeks, because the reparations argument in that room never actually moves even though almost nobody in it disagrees about the facts. Everyone agrees slavery was a monstrous wrong, everyone agrees its effects ran straight through segregation into present-day wealth and health and imprisonment gaps, and nobody in the seminar thinks a living person is *guilty* of what their ancestors did. We still spent two whole sessions going in circles.

Here's how the camps look to me:

Camp 1 says the claim is basically a debt. Somebody took something that had an owner — labour, land, inheritance — and the value of it is still sitting somewhere traceable: estates, endowments, institutions, generational wealth. On this view reparations is rectification and the only real intellectual work is tracing who holds what.

Camp 2 says it's about harm to living people. Present-day Black Americans are worse off on a bunch of measures, that disadvantage is caused by the historical wrong, so it's a causal harm claim with identifiable victims and a diffuse set of defendants.

Camp 3 says both of those get the shape wrong and the point is political: a state whose institutions were built and maintained by the wrong owes repair to the people whose standing in it was damaged, and this doesn't need named victims or named payers at all, it's about the conditions under which the polity can claim to treat everyone as equals now.

Then there's the objection one guy in the seminar keeps pressing and nobody seems to have a clean answer to, which is: for camp 2 at least, if the harm is what produced you, in what sense are you worse off? Every actual descendant exists only because of that specific chain of events — different history, different people, different you. So the counterfactual baseline for harm looks like non-existence, and camp 2 gets squeezed into camp 1 or camp 3 by default.

My hypothesis, and I want you to attack it rather than just spell it out: this isn't one disagreement. I think the word is doing two seperate jobs — a backward-looking quasi-legal one (liability, causation, damages, a defendant) and a forward-looking political one (acknowledgement, standing, legitimacy of shared institutions) — and camps 1 and 2 are answering the first question while camp 3 is answering the second, and they keep talking past each other while insisting they're on the same topic. I also suspect part of the stalemate is that people disagree about what would even count as a satisfactory answer to 'who exactly pays whom': one side treats the specification problem as fatal, the other treats it as beside the point.

So, is that right? Is this an equivocation problem, a genuinely substantive disagreement that just happens to be hard, or two different questions wearing one name? Which camps are actually disagreeing with each other rather than with a caricature? Does the non-existence objection really collapse camp 2, or is there a version of the harm claim that survives it — something that doesn't need a counterfactual comparison with a world where you don't exist? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here: a distinction everyone's missing, a change of question, an empirical finding about how far the tracing actually gets you, something else? Or is the honest answer that no progress should be expected — and if so, is that because the question is ill-posed or just intractable? I'd rather be told I'm wrong in an interesting way than have my hypothesis tidied up.
