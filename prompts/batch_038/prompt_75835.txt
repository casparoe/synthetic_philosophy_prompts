I am drafting a chapter of my dissertation on the epistemology of exemplary narrative, and I have hit an interpretive wall at the methodological preface that opens Plutarch's Life of Alexander — the paragraph, before the account of his parentage, in which Plutarch announces that what he is composing are lives rather than histories. He argues there that the most celebrated achievements do not necessarily display a man's virtue or vice most plainly; often something of no consequence, a remark or a jest, discloses character more revealingly than engagements in which thousands fall, than the greatest marshalling of armies, or than sieges of cities. He then likens his own procedure to that of portrait painters, who draw their likenesses from the face and from the expression of the eyes and pay scant attention to the rest of the body, and asks to be permitted likewise to dwell on the indications of the soul in shaping the image of each life, leaving the narration of mighty contests to others. My problem is not philological: I want to know what claim is actually being made.

I can see two arguments here, and the painter analogy appears to license neither. On an evidential reading, the trivial incident is privileged because it is unguarded — the agent has had neither time nor motive to compose himself, so the act transmits information about a disposition that grand, heavily observed, and heavily strategic performances filter out. On an expressive reading, the trivial incident is privileged not as a better sign but as a more complete one: the whole person is present in it, undivided by office or role, whereas generalship and legislation are always jointly authored with circumstance, subordinates, and luck. But painting the eyes is neither a matter of catching a sitter off guard nor a matter of the whole sitter being contained in the eyes. It looks instead like a claim about where the likeness is concentrated, which suggests a third reading I cannot name.

Which of these does the analogy support, and if the third is the right one, what is the principle that makes likeness concentrated rather than distributed? Does that principle give Plutarch any grounds for disparaging great deeds at all, given that the Lives are overwhelmingly composed of battles, sieges, and laws? Please identify the step at which you think my reconstruction goes wrong.
