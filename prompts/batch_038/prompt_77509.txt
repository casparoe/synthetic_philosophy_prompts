I dont have any philosophy training, I just think about this stuff on my own. I drive a forklift in a distribution center and I've been on the same shift for nine years. I can do basically the whole thing without thinking — I know a pallet is going to be short before the scanner tells me. Then about two years ago they moved the racking around, and for a month I was worse than the the guy they hired the week before. I'd reach for an aisle that wasn't there.

Around then I read about this wasp somewhere. The digger wasp that stings a bug and drags it to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to check everything's okay, then comes out and drags it in. If you move the bug while it's checking, the wasp finds it, drags it back to the entrance, and goes in to check again. Forever. It never gets bored, never asks what's happening. I think a philosopher had a name for this, something sphex-y. It's supposed to be the opposite of free will.

So I rewatched Groundhog Day with that in my head and I think everybody has the movie backwards. The standard line is that Bill Murray is trapped because he's a rigid, selfish guy and he gets out by loosening up and caring about people. But look at what he actually does. By the end he has a script. He knows where everybody in town will be at what time, what they'll say, when the kid falls out of the tree, when the mayor chokes. He catches that kid without looking. He's got the piano, the ice carving, the French poem. He is more wasp than the wasp at that point — he just has a much better burrow. And that's the day the loop ends. The movie rewards the script.

So here's what I want to know. Which act of this movie is actually about rigidity and which is about flexibility, if there even is a difference between them here? Is the escape earned by him getting spontaneous, or by him getting perfect at one memorized day? And what about the parts where the script fails — the old homeless guy dies no matter what he tries, and all the suicides. Those are the only scenes where he isn't running a routine, and they're the ones where he's least free. Does that mean knowing you're in a loop doesn't buy you anything, which is what act two feels like to me?

Also the bit I can't shake: he wakes up on February 3rd and decides to stay, to live in Punxsutawney. That's the first day of his life he has no map for, and the movie treats it as the happy ending and never shows him handling it. Cheat, or the whole point?

Give me an actual reading, not a plot summary, and give it with a spine — tell me what you think the movie is claiming about rigidity and freedom and whether that claim is true, and pick a side and defend it instead of laying out both readings and leaving me to choose. Tell me where your reading has to bend the movie to make it fit. And if the wasp story turns out to be one of those things philosophers repeat endlessly without checking, say that too, because I'd rather know.
