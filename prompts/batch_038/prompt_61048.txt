So this is kind of urgent and a little embarrassing, but here goes.

My family has been crawfishing down in Acadiana for three generations. My brother runs the operation now and I spent every summer of my childhood up to my knees in trap lines and bait buckets. Then I went and got a philosophy PhD instead, and I'm now a fourth year working on animal minds. Two weeks ago I got invited to give a 30 min talk at a workshop a fisheries science department is co-hosting with an aquaculture certification body — literally the people who write the audit standards for shellfish. My brother is flying up to sit in the back, which is its own problem.

Here's the thesis, roughly, and it isn't the one the room is expecting:

The question "is species X conscious?" is malformed and we should stop asking it. Consciousness isn't a species-level property with an on/off switch, or even a single scalar; it attaches to particular functional systems. So an animal can have a valenced state — something bad-feeling about nociceptive input, weighed against hunger, remembered, paid for — while having no unified cross-modal picture of the world at all. In the talk I split this into two tests, badly named: gate A asks whether there's a state with felt valence (opioid modulation, self-administered analgesia, tradeoffs where the animal takes a cost now for a payoff later, avoidance learning that can't be reflex); gate B asks whether there's a single subject binding modalities at a time (cross-modal binding, flexible choice under novelty, the global-workspace-ish signatures). My claim is that plenty of animals clear A and not B — probably most insects, probably decapods, maybe fish — and that everything welfare policy actually needs lives at gate A. Which means those reports from the last few years recommending that crabs and cephalopods get treated as sentient on precautionary grounds, with their long lists of criteria, are doing something defensible but conceptually sloppy: they use "sentient" as one flag covering both gates, and the precautionary move lets everyone avoid noticing that the gates come apart.

The audience will be about 25. Mostly comparative psychologists and fish biologists who run welfare-indicator labs — they measure cortisol and operant choice and think this is settled by assay, not arugment. Two invertebrate neurophysiologists who will really not enjoy being told their animals might flunk gate B. One person from the certification body whose entire job is compressing all of this into a yes/no checkbox on an audit form. A few philosophers, none of whom work on consciousness. Nobody in the room is into moral weight or aggregation or any of that, and my brother doesn't know what "phenomenal" means.

Can you build me the Q&A prep? What I actually want: the questions in roughly the order they'll come at me, in the words the person asking would use (the fish biologist and the certification person won't put the same objection the same way), what I should give away in the first two minutes to buy credibility, what I have to hold onto, and — most important — where my argument is thinnest, so I can go fix it before the 15th instead of hoping it flies over their heads. Tell me specifically which of these are going to land and how hard: (a) gates A and B are just their existing indicator list repackaged, so I've added nothing but vocabulary; (b) I'm smuggling in the assumption that valence has to be conscious, when a tradeoff could be entirely subpersonal; (c) if a crawfish clears A and not B, what difference does my brother's boiler make; (d) the decomposition is unfalsifiable, because any evidence against one gate gets reassinged to the other. And then the one I've been dreading, which I'd rather type than say out loud: if this view got into a standards document, would it mostly just function as a sophisticated-sounding way to keep boiling things alive?
