A logic seminar I attend has produced an argument that seems to show that dialetheism achieves nothing: that the naive truth theory it exists to rescue is trivial anyway, so the paraconsistent machinery never does any work. Fix an arbitrary claim q — say, "snow is green" — and let k be the self-referential sentence "if k is true, then snow is green."

(1) By the naive truth schema, k is true if and only if: if k is true, then snow is green.
(2) Suppose k is true.
(3) Then by (1), if k is true, then snow is green; and since k is true by supposition, snow is green.
(4) Discharging the supposition: if k is true, then snow is green.
(5) But (4) is precisely what k says, so by (1) again, k is true.
(6) From (4) and (5), snow is green.
(7) Nothing in (1)–(6) turned on the particular content of q, so the same derivation delivers any sentence you care to name: the naive theory is trivial.
(8) At no point does the derivation produce a contradiction, or move from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion, so the dialetheist's signature move — refusing explosion — never gets a chance to apply.
(9) Therefore anyone who keeps an unrestricted truth schema together with a conditional she can detach with is a trivialist, and dialetheism collapses back into the hierarchical treatment of the liar it was meant to replace.

Nothing in this strikes me as obviously wrong; each line looks like an inference I would happily make about ordinary sentences, and (8) seems a fair description of what the derivation actually does. Please identify where the argument breaks — or, if you think it does not, say which of its commitments is least costly to give up. Explain why the offending step looked innocuous, and price each repair available to the dialetheist, including whether any of them leaves her with a logic in which she can still state her own theory, and whether restricting the truth schema is in the end the only option.
