I am drafting a short essay on the foundations of Bayesian epistemology, intended for readers who have encountered Dutch book arguments before but have not worked through their details. What follows is a near-complete draft. I would value substantive criticism over encouragement, and I have specified at the end what I would like from you.

**Draft: "The Bookmaker Is Doing No Work: Dutch Books as Pure Geometry"**

**1.** The synchronic Dutch book argument is usually presented as a story about gambling. Suppose an agent's degrees of belief over a finite algebra are given by a function b, and suppose she is disposed to transact at her own odds: she will accept a bet on X with stake S at price b(X)·S, for S of either sign. If b fails to be a probability function — if, say, b(X) + b(¬X) ≠ 1, or b(X ∨ Y) < b(X) + b(Y) for disjoint X and Y — then there is a choice of stakes such that her net payoff is negative in every possible world. She has been Dutch-booked. De Finetti called a credence function admitting no such set of bets *coherent*, and Ramsey's earlier remarks about the consistency required of partial beliefs point in the same direction. The conclusion drawn is that rational degrees of belief must obey the probability axioms; the argument is advertised as a *pragmatic* vindication of that conclusion, since it routes the conclusion through the agent's dispositions to act.

**2.** There is a diachronic counterpart, due in its standard form to Lewis. If an agent's conditional credence in H given E differs from the ratio b(H ∧ E)/b(E), and she expects to learn E with certainty while updating by some other rule, then a counterparty permitted to transact with her both before and after the evidence arrives can assemble a package that loses money for her no matter which world is actual. Rational updating is thereby constrained to conditionalization.

**3.** Now set the story aside and look at the mathematics. Let W be the set of worlds, and identify each world w with a vector v_w in R^n whose i-th coordinate is 1 if the i-th proposition of the algebra is true at w and 0 otherwise. A set of stakes s = (s_1, …, s_n) yields payoff Σ_i s_i(v_w,i − b_i) at w. The agent is Dutch-bookable exactly when some s makes this negative for every w — that is, when s·v_w < s·b for every w. But that is precisely the statement that the hyperplane with normal s strictly separates b from the convex hull of {v_w}. And that hull is exactly the set of probability functions on the algebra: every probability function is a convex combination of the extreme ones, and every point of the hull is a probability function. So b is incoherent if and only if b lies outside that hull, and every Dutch book is a separating hyperplane witnessing the fact. The betting theorem and the convex-geometry theorem are the same theorem stated twice.

**4.** I want to draw a stronger conclusion than the equivalence, and here I expect resistance. If the two formulations are interderivable in this direct way, the money is not load-bearing: it is a way of visualizing a separation. It follows, I suggest, that the standard objections to Dutch book arguments target scaffolding the argument does not need. That the argument presupposes risk neutrality, or linear utility in money, or that agents accept bets piecemeal rather than evaluating packages as wholes, or that they will accept any individually favorable bet regardless of what else they know or are being offered — each of these is an objection to the gambling story. Strip the story away and what remains is a claim about the internal structure of a single credal state: that it should be a point of a certain convex set. This claim makes no reference to action, wealth, or decision-making. The label "pragmatic vindication of probabilism" is therefore a misnomer; the argument is pragmatic only in its presentation.

**5.** Two corollaries I am less sure of. First, since nothing behavioural survives the translation, the diachronic argument should likewise be read as a consistency requirement relating two time-slices of a credal state, rather than as a constraint on a policy for acting under changing evidence. On this reading, the familiar difficulties about agents who expect their own evidence to mislead them, and about credences in centred or self-locating propositions, are not counterexamples to the argument but questions outside its subject matter. Second, the distinction often drawn between vulnerability to a *sure loss* and vulnerability to a *Dutch book* — the first concerning the agent's own guaranteed loss, the second the counterparty's guaranteed profit, with the first usually treated as the weaker and more defensible notion, as Christensen argues — looks, geometrically, like a distinction without a difference. Both merely say that a point lies outside a hull. The same goes for the accuracy-first reformulation, associated with Joyce, which replaces guaranteed monetary loss with guaranteed excess inaccuracy relative to some competing credence.
