I teach an aesthetics course and this term every student has read the same glossy Sunday-magazine essay on forgeries, the kind that arrives with its conclusion pre-attached, so I need to be able to discuss it fairly rather then just sneer at it. Here's the argument, or my compression of it:

"A forgery that looks exactly like the original cannot be aesthetically worse than the original, because anything you can't perceive in front of the picture can't make a difference to what you see. The extra millions are paid for a story, not a surface, which is why the value we attach to authenticity is really the value we attach to a relic — the reason you'd want your grandmother's ring rather than an identical replacement. Besides, for most of the history of art nobody minded: a master's workshop sent pictures out under his name with the assistants doing most of the brushwork, and the cult of the unique hand is a nineteenth-century invention of dealers and auction houses who needed something scarce to sell. It follows that museums are being dishonest when they label copies, and the clean experiment would be to hang a first-rate forgery beside the original with the labels taken off — since visitors can't tell them apart, the difference in value is exposed as a fiction."

I count five moves in there and I'd like an audit of each, in the order they appear. I'm inclined to think the original matters and the essay is overconfident, but I know some of it has to be right — a decent defender of originals should be happy to grant that copies can be gorgeous and that hammer prices track stories — and its not like I want to wave a slogan at a student who has actually read the thing.

Two things to fold in wherever they belong. First, a colleague of mine insists the whole dispute turns on whether the copy is even the same work, on the grounds that what a picture looks like depends on the category you see it in, so a forged Rembrandt seen as a Rembrandt is aesthetically a different object from that same canvas seen as a 1930s imitation; tell me whether that's a point the essay is obliged to answer or a distinction that does no work here. Second, I have a half-memory that Renaissance contracts sometimes stipulated the master's own hand, which would be awkward for the third move — but don't do history for me, just say whether that move has to be true for the essay's conclusion to stand.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per move, a sentence or two each. Tag each holds / half-holds / fails, name what a defender of the original should concede inside it, and point to the sleight of hand if there is one. No preamble and no wrap-up paragraph — but do make the fifth item settle the thing I actually care about, which is whether the museum recommendation still follows once the first four are cleaned up.
