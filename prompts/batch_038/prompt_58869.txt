You're a metaphysician who works on causation day to day — treat this like a supervision session where I've brought you a view I'm personally committed to and I want you to try genuinely hard to break it rather than nod along. My position: omissions really do cause things. When the nurse fails to check the infusion pump and the patient dies, her not-checking is a cause of the death, not merely a backdrop against which the real causes did their work; absences are bona fide relata of the causal relation, and the folk way of talking about them isn't a loose manner of speech that a careful theory should paraphrase away. I've also got my own wrinkle on it, which is what I most want pressure-tested. To handle the obvious proliferation complaint — my not watering your plants in Peru, my not preventing some disaster in 1789, the endless stream of things everyone fails to do at every moment — I say an omission is causally relevant only relative to a role or standing expectation the agent actually occupied: duties, appointments, contracts, promises, job descriptions, the kind of normative structure the situation genuinely has, not just what happens to strike us as salient. So the same bodily non-event is a cause under one role description and not a cause under another, and I take that to be a feature, not an embarrassment.

Here's the case that's been keeping me awake, and I made it up myself so it shouldn't be one you've seen: a valve has to be inspected once between 6pm and 6am. Keeper A is on shift till midnight, keeper B from midnight onwards. Neither inspects it, the vessel overpressurises at 4am and floods the plant. On my account, whose omission caused the damage — A's, B's, or a single extended omission that isn't owned by anyone? And does the answer change if their roles overlap between 11pm and 1am because of a botched handover?
