I spent thirty-eight years as a physicist in a national measurement institute, most of it in the electrical quantities group, and I have been retired for six years. Only since retiring have I begun reading philosophy seriously, and the argument I keep returning to is the one between moral realists and moral anti-realists. I read it as a layman, which is to say I have the labels but not the instincts, and I find I understand a position far better once I can see it in the vocabulary of my own trade. So I want to ask you to perform a translation, and — more importantly — an honest account of what the translation loses.

Here is the vocabulary I want you to work in, and since I am thoroughly fluent in it, please do not simplify it: measurand; quantity and unit; primary standard and the chain of traceability; calibration and calibration drift; systematic error as distinct from random error; an uncertainty budget and its coverage factor; the distinction between the true value of a quantity and our best estimate of it; interlaboratory comparison; the reference value such a comparison produces and the degree of equivalence assigned to each participating laboratory; and, where it bears on the case, the question of what is fixed by definition rather than measured, as when a unit is tied to a constant of nature.

What I want translated, item by item, is this. First, the realist's core claim that moral facts obtain independently of what anyone thinks or feels about them. Second, the anti-realist family in its several forms: the view that moral sentences do not express beliefs at all but something more like attitudes or practical commitments; the error theory, which holds that ordinary moral claims are systematically false and which is usually associated with Mackie's argument from queerness; relativism; and fictionalism, on which moral discourse is useful but not to be taken at face value. Third, the persistent and apparently intractable character of moral disagreement, which both sides deploy as evidence. Fourth, the claim that the moral supervenes on the natural. Fifth, the claim — pressed hardest by anti-realists but accepted in some form by philosophers on both sides — that sincerely judging an act wrong is not a purely descriptive state of mind but carries some motivation to act accordingly. Sixth, the conceptual point that no list of natural properties seems, by itself, to settle a normative question.

For each of the six items I want a verdict: whether it translates into my vocabulary cleanly; whether it translates only with distortion, in which case tell me precisely what the distortion is; or whether it has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Where an item does translate, state the mapping rather than offering an analogy — which metrological notion stands in for which philosophical one, and whether the two play the same role in their respective arguments.

I have three questions of my own that I could not settle. The first: is the realist's independence claim the analogue of a quantity's being invariant across laboratories and methods, or is it something structurally different? The second: when moral disagreement persists, does it have the shape of a systematic error that could in principle be calibrated away, or the shape of a genuine underdetermination in which the disputants are estimating different measurands? My hunch is that the debate slides between these two without noticing, and I would like to know whether the hunch survives scrutiny. The third: an interlaboratory comparison ends not with a ruling on which laboratory is correct but with a reference value and stated degrees of equivalence. Is that a model of what anti-realism proposes, or does it quietly presuppose realism about the measurand and therefore cannot represent anti-realism at all?

Two cautions. Please stay alert to the fact that my discipline assumes what these philosophers are disputing: metrology takes it for granted that there is a value to be measured and that procedures answer to it. If importing my vocabulary settles the debate by force, say so at each point where that happens, and say which side it favours. And please do not force a mapping where there is none; I would much rather be told that my vocabulary has no resources for a certain claim than be handed a flattering comparison. Wherever you report a failure of translation, give me your assessment of what the failure shows — that my framework is impoverished, or that the philosophical claim was less determinate than its proponents assumed. I do not expect you to be neutral between those two readings, and I do not expect your answer to be the same in every case.
