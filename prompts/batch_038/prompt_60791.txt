Here's the ask: analyze the April 1986 Chernobyl accident as a case study in the shutdown problem.

The facts as I understand them — please correct me where I'm wrong rather than quietly going along with it. Reactor 4 was being taken down for maintenance and they used the outage to run a test: whether a coasting turbine, with the grid gone, could generate enough power to run the coolant pumps until the diesels came up. The test had been scheduled for the day shift but the grid controller wouldn't release the unit, they needed the power, so it slipped like nine or ten hours and ended up being run overnight by a shift that hadn't been prepped for it. During the run-down the power collapsed to something like 30 MW, xenon built up, and to climb back they pulled out most of the control rods and ended up way below the minimum operating reactivity margin. Then at 1:23:40 somebody pressed AZ-5, the emergency shutdown, and the rods went in — and because they had graphite tips that displaced water, inserting them from fully-withdrawn added reactivity in the lower core for the first seconds instead of removing it. Power ran away, fuel channels ruptured, the rod channels jammed, and the reactor came apart in two explosions. Afterwards the crew on the panel didn't believe it had happened, the instrumentation was telling them the reactor was still there, and they spent hours manually opening valves and pumping water into a destroyed core. And there's something about tests at another RBMK plant a couple of years earlier — Ignalina, 1983? — that had already shown the tip effect, and the finding never made it into the operators' hands, so the people pressing the button had no idea what the button did. Also, from what I've read, there's still an argument about whether pressing AZ-5 started the excursion or whether it was already running away on the void coefficient and the rods just finished it.

What I care about is not the physics and not a retelling. It's this. Every treatment of the shutdown problem I've read treats shutdown as the safe, boring direction, and the whole puzzle is whether the agent resists it, games the operator, or leaves behind a successor that won't. Chernobyl is a case where the shutdown was itself the most dangerous action available, and where the humans were doing exactly what corrigibility theory wants — deferring to the principal who had authority, not resisting, pressing the button when things looked wrong.

Then the bit I made up, and I want you to be blunt. I keep calling it the Dyatlov problem. Corrigibility is always defined relative to one operator with legitimate authority to shut the thing down. But real systems have several principals pulling in different directions, and an agent can be perfectly corrigible toward one — the crew deferring to the grid controller, and to Dyatlov on the phone — while being completely uncorrigible toward another: the young reactor operator who said we shouldn't do this, the instrument readings, the written regulations. So "is this system corrigible" isn't a property of the system, it's a property of the system plus whoever happens to be holding the button.
