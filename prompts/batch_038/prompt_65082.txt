I am preparing a talk for our department's philosophy-of-computation group on whether interpretability results should be read realistically — that is, on whether claims about a trained network's internal states describe features the network genuinely has, rather than useful fictions we impose on it from outside. To motivate the realist reading, I intended to rehearse and then disarm the following argument against it, which a colleague constructed. On working through it privately, I found that it seems to establish considerably more than my colleague wants, and I would like a diagnosis before I present it.

The setting is meant to be ordinary. Take a network N with a 512-dimensional hidden layer, trained on a supervised classification task, and write h(x) for the hidden state produced by input x. Let P be some property of inputs that is not part of the training label: for instance, that a handwritten seven carries the horizontal crossbar common in parts of Europe, or that a sentence contains a typographical error. N is in superposition with respect to properties like P — no individual hidden unit answers to P — so any account of P's presence must instead locate a direction in activation space, typically by fitting a readout or by training a sparse autoencoder with an overcomplete dictionary and inspecting its atoms.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) An interpretability claim attributes a representation to N: it says that some part of N's internal state represents P.

(2) Such a claim is true only if there is a decoding map D from hidden states to values of P such that (a) D is accurate — D(h(x)) matches P(x) across the inputs that matter — and (b) N's downstream computation is sensitive to what D reads off.

(3) Condition (a) can always be met. A linear readout has as many free parameters as the hidden layer has dimensions, so it can be fit to reproduce any assignment of P-values across as many sampled inputs as there are dimensions, provided those hidden states are in general position; with a learned readout of sufficient capacity, any finite sample can be fit, whatever the labeling.

(4) Condition (b) can always be met as well. N's downstream computation is a function of h(x), and D is a function of h(x); so the computation proceeds on the basis of the very states from which D reads P. If D is accurate, the computation is being carried out on states that carry the P-information, and that is all sensitivity can amount to.

(5) Hence, for any network N and any property P of its inputs, there is a decoding map that makes it true that some part of N represents P.

(6) So either representation attributions are trivially true and interpretability discovers nothing, or representing P requires some further ingredient beyond the existence of an accurate and sensitive decoder — but interpretability methods deliver nothing over and above decoders: a probe, a dictionary atom, a steering vector. Any further ingredient would lie beyond the reach of every such method.

(7) Therefore interpretability realism is either vacuous or empirically empty.

I would rather surrender (5) than (2), since (2) is what keeps representation talk tethered to the network at all; but I cannot see how to surrender (5) while retaining (3) and (4), and (3) looks to me like arithmetic about parameters while (4) looks like a remark about functions. Denying either in front of the group would need a reason I do not currently have.

Please tell me where the argument breaks. Specifically: which step you reject, and whether you take the argument to be invalid or valid but unsound; why the step you reject seemed to me compelling, and what would have to be true for it to hold; whether your diagnosis carries over to the older trivialization objections to ascribing computations and representations to physical systems generally, or whether something about learned networks changes the case; and what each available repair costs the realist — in particular, whether the tightened criterion can leave some real interpretability claims indeterminate, given that superposition removes any privileged unit basis and an overcomplete dictionary can split one candidate feature across several atoms or fuse several into one.
