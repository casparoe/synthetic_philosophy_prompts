I am the county archivist in the seat of a mid-sized Southern county, and in six weeks I will give the address at the rededication of the courthouse square. I would like you to write that address. Here is the situation in full, followed by what it must and must not do.

The square's centerpiece is a bronze Confederate private on a granite pedestal, put up in the early 1900s by a ladies' memorial association; their dedication plaque is still bolted to the north face. After six years of hearings, the commission voted 5–4 not to remove the figure but to change the ground around it. What they built is this: the lawn on the north side has been raised into a berm, so that a visitor can now stand at eye level with the soldier and read every inscription at arm's length. The original plaque has been left in place, unaltered and unrebutted on its own face. Three new bronze panels have been set into the berm's retaining wall, each written independently and each permitted to contradict the others — one by a member of the local heritage association, one by a woman whose great-great-grandmother appears as a line item in a plantation inventory twelve miles from here, and one by the eleven public-school students who spent two years in my reading room transcribing deeds, ledgers, and coroner's reports. Then the commission, on a tie, declined to write a fourth panel reconciling the other three.

That refusal is why I was asked to speak, and it is what my address has to defend without turning into a celebration of indecision.

In the front row: both panel authors, the students, five commissioners who voted yes and four who voted no, and a delegation from our sister city in Baden-Württemberg, including the stonemason who cast the panels. Behind them, several hundred people who came because there are folding chairs, shade, and a church choir afterward. At the rehearsal dinner the stonemason told me, in substance, that in Germany the country decided once what the crimes were and the monuments assume that decision, and that what we have built here is a railing around an argument. My address has to answer him. It may not answer him by proposing that America do what Germany did — partly because that would be false to this room, and partly because it would be false about Germany, where the consensus he describes was not a decision but a fight conducted for decades against arguments remarkably like the ones made in our own hearings.

Requirements:

1. Length: 1,000 to 1,200 words, roughly eight minutes aloud. Write it in American English, and write it to be spoken — short clauses, concrete nouns, places to breathe. No headings and no stage directions beyond an occasional bracketed pause.

2. State the principle on which the missing fourth panel counts as a decision rather than a failure. Then use that same principle to say one thing the second panel's author and her allies will find unwelcome, and one thing the heritage association will find unwelcome. Those two unwelcome things must fall out of the one principle; they must not be a balancing act, and the speech must not soften either of them.

3. The comparative material is a tool, not the subject. Give Germany one substantive passage, because of who is sitting in the front row. Give Japan and Turkey no more than four sentences between them, and make those sentences do one specific job: showing what happens to a public memory when the verdict is issued by the state and then enforced — whether by statute, as with the Turkish penal code provision used to prosecute people for denigrating Turkishness, or by the settled framing of a national story, as when a country's commemorative center of gravity comes to rest on what was done to it rather than on what it did. Everything else belongs to this county.

4. You may invoke exactly one precedent, and if you do, describe it rather than cite it: the column installed in a Hamburg suburb in the 1980s, clad in lead, which residents were invited to sign and which was lowered into the ground in stages over several years until nothing remained above the pavement but a notice.

5. No citations, no footnotes, no bibliography, no naming of books or scholars. I cannot look anything up on the day, and neither can my audience.

6. Do not use the words healing, closure, reconciliation, or never again; do not use any variant of the saying about those who cannot remember the past being condemned to repeat it; do not apostrophize the dead; do not build anaphoric lists of what we will do together. I am white and my people have been in this county longer than the courthouse has stood, and I want that stated once, plainly, early, and then never raised again.

7. End at the berm, at eye level, with something in the room — not with an abstraction and not with a benediction.
