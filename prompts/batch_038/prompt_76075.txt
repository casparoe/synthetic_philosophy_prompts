I co-teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of time, and every year the week on future contingents goes the same way: somebody brings in Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" — or, worse, the film Arrival — and the discussion dissolves into a vague meditation on determinism versus free will in which nobody engages the argument Aristotle actually runs in De Interpretatione 9, or the machinery that has accumulated on top of it (bivalence and its denial, the direction-of-explanation reply to the fatalist, hard versus soft facts about the past, supervaluation over branching histories, the thin red line, truthmaker pressures on future-tensed propositions). I want to use the novella properly this year, and I'd like a piece of criticism I can hand the students as a model of what a rigorous reading looks like rather than a plot summary with philosophical name-dropping.

The relevant shape of the story, as I have it: Louise Banks narrates in the second person, addressed to a daughter who does not yet exist at the moment of narration — she is writing on the day the child is conceived — and interleaves the history of the heptapod contact (Louise and the physicist Gary Donnelly at their looking glass, the two heptapods nicknamed Flapper and Raspberry, the discovery that Heptapod B is a semasiographic system of semagrams composed all at once rather than in sequence) with what Louise experiences as memories of her daughter's later life and death. The pivot is Fermat's principle of least time: Gary's account of refraction becomes the breakthrough because a variational principle describes the same physics teleologically rather than causally, and Louise infers that the heptapods experience events all at once. Chiang frames the free-worry explicitly, in the direction of wondering whether knowing the future might not paralyze an agent but instead evoke "a sense of obligation to act precisely as she knew she would." The film changes a great deal — it names the daughter and alters her fate, and adds the weapon/tool mistranslation subplot and a stated heptapod motive for coming, where the novella's visitors simply leave without explaining themselves — but please work from the novella, and where you are unsure of a detail in it, flag the uncertainty instead of filling the gap.

What I want from you is a reading that does four things at once. First, locate Louise in the sea-battle argument with some precision: which of her forward-looking thoughts are future contingents in Aristotle's sense, and is the crucial one better treated as a general prediction or as a singular claim about an individual who does not yet exist — and does that difference matter, given that her narration is addressed to a "you" with no present referent at all? Second, decide whether the story endorses the familiar reply that truth about the future is not a constraint on it (the battle does not happen because the claim is true; the claim is true because the battle will happen), or whether it goes further and supplies the future with a metaphysical ground available now — the variational picture, on which the whole trajectory is fixed by a global condition rather than pushed along by antecedent causes. If it does go further, say whether that converts soft facts into hard ones, whether an Ockhamist about the past could accept it, and whether Chiang has thereby made the fatalist's premise more defensible than it was for Aristotle. Third, work out what the story commits to about truth versus knowledge: it may not be a thesis about bivalence at all, but about the phenomenology of memory, and I suspect that conflation is exactly where the popular treatments go wrong. Relatedly, notice that Louise's relation to her daughter's future is modeled on her relation to the past; ask what would have to be true for the future to acquire the modal fixity of the past, whether the story earns that or simply assumes it, and whether her grief depends on it. Fourth, take seriously the deliberation question: is there any real choosing going on when Louise decides to have the child, and is the "obligation to act precisely as she knew she would" a recognizable position in the foreknowledge debate or something the fiction contributes that the arguments have not? I want you to say what the story adds to the dispute that argument alone cannot deliver, and equally where it gets away with something an argument would not be permitted — the heptapods' unexplained departure, Louise's continuing to plan and hope, the real possibility that the novella is about mourning and acceptance and that the physics is decoration rather than load-bearing structure. If the reading breaks down somewhere, say so and say where.

Please close with the practical part: two or three episodes from the novella you would actually put in front of the class, and one question you would ask them that cannot be answered without doing the philosophy. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and I do not need you to quote the novella — I have the text and will check passages myself. I would rather have your own formulations of the positions than a survey of who has held them, and I would rather you argue for a reading than balance two.
