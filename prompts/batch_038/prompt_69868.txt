**Elasticity: why more humane pest control might mean more dead insects**

**1. The shape of the standard debate.** Nearly every argument I see about insect suffering in agriculture runs the same way. Take some probability that insects are sentient, or at least capable of something like nociceptive suffering; multiply by the enormous number killed by insecticide applications, harvest, tillage, and stored-product protection; multiply by how bad a given death is; conclude that expected harm is staggering and that practice should change. Call this the weighting view. I don't want to attack the arithmetic — I think it's fine. My worry is about which term gets treated as a constant. The weighting view treats N, the number killed, as something you look up. But N isn't exogenous. It responds to how we feel about the killing.

**2. Two levers, not one.** It helps to separate:
(a) *intensity* — how bad each individual death is. Method matters a lot here: a fast knockdown contact spray versus a slow-acting bait, versus exclusion netting, versus sterile-insect release, versus just modifying habitat so nothing wants to live there in the first place.
(b) *volume* — how often we intervene, over how much area, and for how many species we consider worth bothering about.

Essentially all welfare-motivated reform pushes on (a). Almost nothing pushes on (b), and (b) is bigger.

**3. The elasticity claim.** Define *deployment elasticity* as the responsiveness of total kills to a fall in the per-kill cost of the act, where "cost" bundles money, labor, and the moral or psychological drag on whoever is deciding. My claim: in pest control, elasticity is plausibly above 1 across a wide range, so making killing feel cleaner increases total harm. It's the Jevons structure — efficiency gains consumed rather than banked, like fuel economy buying more miles, or safer cars buying higher speeds.

Three features make pest control a specially good home for this:
(i) **Invisibility.** The deaths are unobserved. There's no audience whose sympathy could put a brake on volume the way it can for, say, slaughter.
(ii) **Non-culpability.** Pests aren't aggressors in any sense that supports a self-defense model. They're organisms doing what organisms do. So the operative justification isn't defense against a culpable attacker — it's a public-harm justification, structurally the same as flood control or weed management. Public-harm justifications are cost-benefit shaped, and anything cost-benefit shaped expands as the cost term shrinks.
(iii) **Threshold logic.** Integrated pest management already tells growers to act once pest density or damage reaches the point where the value of crop destroyed exceeds the cost of control — the economic injury level, with the economic threshold sitting just below it as the action trigger. That rule *is* a comparison of harm against cost-of-intervening. Lower the moral cost of intervening and you lower the threshold. The rule literally moves.

**4. What I think follows.** Not "stop improving methods." Rather: any humaneness improvement should be coupled to an explicit volume constraint — a kill budget, or a cap on treated area — because uncoupled, the improvement functions as a subsidy to more killing. And reform should prefer abatement to extermination: exclusion, sanitation, habitat change, biological control. Those reduce the number of occasions on which a kill-decision arises at all, and they're closer to one-time than to per-season. The morally decisive variable is intervention frequency, and nobody measures it or reports it.

**5. My doubts.** (a) If the elasticity claim is just "efficiency gains tend to get consumed," it's a generic point about technology and licenses nothing specific about insects. (b) Baseline problem: maybe the humane method simply substitutes for the inhumane one at constant volume, in which case elasticity never bites and the paper is about a counterfactual that doesn't obtain. (c) Norm shift: humaneness reform might be a wedge that eventually delegitimizes routine killing rather than entrenching it. I have no idea how to argue this either way.
