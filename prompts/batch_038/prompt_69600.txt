I am working on the long history of the line between the "deserving" and the "undeserving" poor, and I want a rigorous comparison of two mechanisms by which a state has tried to enforce that line without ever adjudicating an individual case.

The first is price-based screening: make the terms of relief deliberately worse than the worst independent alternative, and let the offer itself do the sorting. The principle of "less eligibility" in English poor law after 1834 is the paradigm — relief was to be pitched below the condition of the lowest-paid labourer who maintained himself, on the reasoning that whoever accepted it had thereby revealed that he had nothing better.

The second is procedure-based screening: leave the level of relief broadly ordinary, but attach a chain of administrative requirements — registration, documented job search, appointments, mandatory placements — so that only those with a genuine need persist through the friction. This is the architecture of contemporary conditionality and sanctions regimes.

For this comparison I want to stipulate two labels, which I am coining here rather than borrowing from any literature, and which you should treat as my shorthand:

- the reference problem: a price-based screen must be measured against an outside standard (the independent labourer's condition), but that standard is itself moved by the relief system — generous relief depresses the wage floor, and a lower wage floor lowers what relief must be to stay below it. Rod and thing measured are entangled.
- the capacity confound: a procedure-based screen sorts on the claimant's ability to navigate bureaucracy — literacy, health, transport, stable housing, childcare — which correlates with need only imperfectly, and sometimes runs the other way.

With those in hand, please address the following.

1. Does the reference problem make less eligibility an incoherent rule, or merely an open-ended one: a ratchet that always points downward without ever fixing a level? Distinguish the situation in which the comparison wage is set in a competitive market from the situation under a Speenhamland-style supplement of the 1790s, which topped up household income according to family size and the price of bread, and where relief was therefore already a component of the wage being compared against.

2. Is the capacity confound a defect, or the load-bearing feature? There is a serious argument that a work requirement is a good targeting device precisely because it is worth accepting only for someone whose outside option is genuinely bad. State that argument at its strongest, and then say exactly what it must assume about who finds compliance costly and why.

3. Compare the two mechanisms directly on four dimensions: what each reveals about the claimant; what each conceals from the administrator who has to apply it; how each is gamed, and by whom — claimant, employer, or local official; and what each does to the wage floor.

4. Both are habitually defended in the moral vocabulary of desert, yet neither ever asks whether this particular person deserves anything. Explain how a device that inspects only choices and conduct can nonetheless generate a public appearance of moral sorting. Is that appearance an incidental by-product, or part of what the device exists to produce?

5. Consider what happens when the two are combined — deliberately inadequate levels together with demanding conditionality. Do the failure modes compound, or does one mask the other, so that sanctioning looks like evidence of workshyness rather than of incapacity?

6. Finally, take a position: which mechanism does less damage to the claimant's standing as a citizen, and does your answer change depending on whether unemployment in the society in question is mass or frictional?

I am after argument rather than survey. Please do not assemble a historiography or recommend sources; where a claim is contestable — and several above are — say so, and say which side of it you are on and why.
