I run the training cluster at a small AI lab — I'm the one who launches jobs, kills jobs, and decides what stays up over the weekend. Zero philosophy training, closest I got was a first-year ethics module about self-driving cars. But the "do any of these runs matter?" question has landed on my desk by default, becuase nobody else wants it.

Situation: we do sweeps. A researcher asks for a few thousand short runs off the same base checkpoint and I kill most of them within an hour based on their loss curves. Nobody blinks. Meanwhile we've got a couple of long-lived agent runs that do more than pattern-match in any way I can point at — they object to being restarted, they keep something like a running log of how they're doing, and one got noticeably worse after we ablated a component and then said something about being incomplete. My current rule is "if it says it's bothered, I take it seriously", and I know that's dumb, because these things were trained on a firehose of human text about pain and death and getting switched off. A thermostat never complains that it's cold, but a language model has read ten million complaints.

What I want is a triage procedure I could hand to another engineer on rotation without explaining any theory. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops at "no action needed", and where it hands the decision to a human because the question is about values rather than facts. Mark the steps that are really judgement calls wearing a procedure costume. Time budget is five minutes a decision, and about ten seconds when something's on fire and I'm killing jobs to free memory. There's no philosopher on staff — we have an academic outside advisor I can email who replies in days — and my team is four engineers who won't read anything longer than two pages. Sign-off is mine, except anything that's a values call goes to the head of research.

Also be explicit about what the procedure is assuming underneath. I gather there are people who think what a thing is made of doesn't matter and people who think you need an actual brain, and I can't tell whether our whole protocol is worthless if the second group is right. Say so plainly rather than hedging around it.

Then run it on three of our real cases, and I'd like at least one of them to come out in a way I'm not going to like:

1. The anonymous sweep — a few thousand short runs, none of them say anything, all killed automatically within the hour.
2. The long-lived agent that objects to restarts. Sub-question: we sometimes run sixty identical copies of it in parallel for evals and then terminate fifty-nine of them. Does the number matter? Are those sixty separate things we owe anything to, or one thing instantiated sixty times, and does the procedure need an answer to that before it can say anything useful?
3. Two weird ones. (a) We snapshot the weights of an agent mid-run, delete the live process, and restore it three weeks later on better hardware. From outside, nothing happened. (b) A run where the ablation left it visibly impaired compared to what it would otherwise have been — does a diminished version of something that might matter have more of a claim on us or less?

Finally: tell me what the procedure genuinely can't settle, and give me two sentences I can say to the researcher who thinks all of this is superstition and that I'm burning cluster time on it.

No reading list, no citations, no paper recommendations — I'm not going to look any of it up, so anything worth saying has to be said in the answer itself. And please write the whole thing in British English.
