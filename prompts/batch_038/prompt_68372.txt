For my seminar on the philosophy of wellbeing I have to defend a version of tranquilism — the view that what makes a life go well or badly is the absence of craving and of suffering, rather than the presence of pleasure — and before I settle on a formulation I would like to know what my own judgements already commit me to. They are as follows.

1. A woman who has never wanted anything she lacks, and who is in no pain: fully well off, and not merely neutral.
2. A man in no pain but consumed by a craving he knows cannot be satisfied: badly off, though less badly than someone in pain of comparable intensity.
3. A monk who has extinguished craving but has an untreated abscess: worse off than the woman in (1), and I do not think the difference is exhausted by the quantity of unpleasant feeling involved.
4. A patient given a drug that removes her craving to be near her children along with her chronic pain, leaving her content and undisturbed: harmed, I think, though I cannot say why, since both craving and suffering have fallen.
5. A person whose every craving is perfectly satisfied inside an experience machine but who feels a low continuous ache, against a person with one raging unsatisfied craving and no ache at all: I genuinely cannot rank these.

My working formulation is that suffering is bad in itself, whereas frustrated craving is bad only in so far as it hurts; case (4) is what makes me doubt it. Is there a single principle that delivers all five verdicts? Please state it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each of its clauses, and tell me what it implies for two further cases of your own choosing — one of which should concern whether we ought to reduce a child's capacity to crave. Where no principle can save everything, say which of my verdicts must go and why, and whether the simpler principle that sacrifices it is to be preferred to the more complex one that retains it.
