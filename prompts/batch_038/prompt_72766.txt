Last night a collaborator and I argued until two in the morning about whether perception is cognitively penetrable, and by the end it became clear that we had never actually disagreed about any empirical finding. We disagreed about what a finding would have to look like in order to count. He holds that beliefs, expectations, and expertise can change the character of visual experience itself; I hold that every result he appealed to is either a change in what information the subject sampled — fixation, attention, eye movements — or a change in how willing the subject was to say "yes, I saw it." Neither of us could produce a case the other would concede was decisive, which is precisely the problem I now want to hand to a room full of other people.

We are co-hosting a two-day interdisciplinary workshop next month. The audience is mixed: vision scientists and experimental psychologists on one side, philosophers of mind on the other, and a number of graduate students who have never read anything by the other camp. I want to open the first session with a sorting instrument that shows each participant which position their own judgments about cases commit them to, before anyone learns the labels and starts defending a team. Please write it. I want three deliverables, in this order.

**1. The axes.** Before any questions, lay out the dimensions along which positions in this debate genuinely come apart. Candidates I have in mind: whether an effect is on the subject's sensitivity or on their willingness to report; whether putative penetration happens online, in the moment, or accumulates over learning and development; whether cognition enters by altering sensory processing or only by altering what gets sampled; whether the properties at issue are low-level (size, hue, orientation) or high-level (kind, causality, threat, affordance); whether penetration, if real, undercuts the justificatory standing of experience or strengthens it; and whether there is any fact of the matter about where perception stops and cognition begins. Give me five to seven axes with their available values, and for each say whether it does independent work or is a redescription of another axis.

**2. The instrument.** Nine questions. Each is a single concrete case with two or three answer options, to be completed on paper in about fifteen minutes by someone with no background in the debate. No question may ask a participant to endorse a thesis in the abstract — nothing of the form "do you think early vision is informationally encapsulated?" — and no answer option may contain vocabulary that signals which position it maps onto. Participants should be sorted by what they say about cases.

Four of the nine must be built from the following, which I stipulate; treat them as hypotheticals rather than as studies you should recall, and do not go hunting for real-world counterparts.

- Two groups of speakers, one whose language has two basic terms dividing the green-cyan region and one with a single term, are tested on same/different discriminations for pairs straddling that boundary and for pairs equally distant within a category. Both groups show a cross-boundary advantage under brief masked presentation. It vanishes in the two-term group under concurrent verbal shadowing, and it vanishes in both groups when the arrays are presented peripherally under a demanding attentional load.
- An expert and a novice each view a midgame chess position for 200 milliseconds. They are equally accurate at reporting whether a given square was occupied, and equally accurate at matching the shade of a square to a swatch wheel. The expert is far more accurate at reporting which pieces attack which.
- A person with a marked spider phobia and a control participant view the same specimen behind glass and both are asked to estimate its length against a ruler. The phobic participant's estimate is systematically larger.
- Two groups perform a yes/no detection task in which a faint word is briefly presented and they must say whether they saw a food-related word. One group says "food word" substantially more often. Three possible accompanying data patterns: the excess is matched by a comparable excess on blank trials where nothing was presented; the excess is confined to trials containing a faint real food word; and the excess disappears entirely when the task is switched to a forced choice between two alternatives. Which pattern, if observed, would show that the groups differ in what they see rather than in how liberal they are with "yes"?
- Two radiologists view the same ambiguous shadow. One has been told the patient has a strong family history; the shadow presents itself to her, she says, as a nodule. Her colleague reports only a smudge.

Use at least four of these five, and you may add your own cases for the remaining questions.

**3. The scoring key.** Six to eight leaves. Name each position descriptively — no attributions to individual philosophers or psychologists, no references of any kind, since half the room will not know the names and I do not want anyone's guesses on the record. For each leaf give the answer pattern that indicates it, one sentence stating the position, and one sentence naming the most awkward commitment the position has to swallow. I want at least the following represented, though you may merge or refine them: penetration is illusory and every candidate result is report bias or post-perceptual judgment; penetration occurs only through the sampling route of attention and eye movement; penetration occurs only over learning and expertise, never online; genuine online alteration of phenomenal character; alteration of experience that degrades its justificatory force; alteration of experience that improves it, as in expertise; and the view that the boundary question itself is misconceived.

Then close with two short sections. First, the empty cells: because your axes generate far more combinations than there are positions, identify at least three that are logically available but unoccupied and diagnose each — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has tried it? Second, tell me which pairs of leaves my nine questions cannot actually separate, and what a further case would have to do to separate them. Do not paper over these.

End with one paragraph addressed to my argument rather than to the room: which single question in your instrument most cleanly decides between his position and mine, and why the other eight fail to.

I would like roughly 1,300 to 1,700 words total, formatted so that the participant-facing pages could be photocopied as they stand and the key kept separate.
