I write a weekly column on science and the ideas underneath it, and for some months now I have been circling an argumentative structure I cannot name. I would like to know whether it has a recognized name, or whether I should coin something I can defend in print.

Here is the structure. Someone offers an account of why a particular intuition feels compelling: the intuition, they say, is what you would expect from a mechanism that does such-and-such, and once you watch that mechanism produce the feeling, the feeling stops looking like a window onto anything further. The account is offered as dispelling rather than merely causal. It does not say the intuition is false; it says there was never anything there for it to be about.

Two instances, both from material I have covered recently.

First, in consciousness research: every report people make about the inner character of their experience — the ineffability, the vividness, the sense that something is going on over and above the information processing — can be traced to attentional and self-modeling machinery that would produce precisely those reports whether or not anything further were happening. Trace it, and the report no longer seems to point beyond the trace.

Second, in the debate over choice: the feeling of having deliberated is explained as a narrative the brain composes after the relevant activity has already occurred, so that the sense of agency turns out to be a description of a result rather than an awareness of a cause.

What unsettles me is not either account in itself. It is the distribution of the account. The machinery invoked is the same machinery the explainer is using while explaining: composing the account, weighing whether it fits the evidence, noticing that it seems to dissolve the intuition. If the account is right, it applies to the explainer's own conviction that it is right. Yet in these exchanges that conviction is never treated as a target. It is treated as the standpoint from which targets get identified. When I press people on this, I get one of two replies: either that their account is supported by everything else we know about brains and behaviour while the intuition it dissolves is supported by nothing but its own felt force, or that the dissolved intuition is special precisely because it claims to reach beyond the physical. Neither reply, so far as I can tell, states a criterion that would distinguish the two uses of the same machinery from a mere preference for one's own.

I know roughly where this sits, and I want to avoid conflating it with its neighbours. It is not the ordinary point that a belief's origin does not settle its truth, because these explainers are not resting on causation — they are claiming the causal story leaves nothing for the intuition to be about. It is not a straightforward charge of circularity, since the account can be internally coherent and well evidenced. And it is not the sweeping sceptical worry that if our faculties were shaped for survival rather than truth we cannot trust them anywhere, because that version indicts everything equally, whereas what I keep meeting is selective.

What I need is the carving, not a label. If there is an established concept for an explanation advanced against a target while its own delivery is exempt from it, tell me what it is called, how it is defined, and what its stated conditions are — in particular, under what circumstances an explainer is entitled to exempt their own use of a mechanism they say dissolves someone else's, and whether that entitlement changes depending on whether the target intuition purports to reach beyond the physical or merely reports a felt datum. Tell me which of my two instances it genuinely covers and whether the second is a weaker case than I assume. If nothing in the literature picks out exactly this configuration, say so plainly and propose a term with a definition and boundary conditions, together with one case that looks like it but is not, so the piece does not overclaim.
