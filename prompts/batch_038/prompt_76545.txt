The situation. I sit on the faculty of an interdisciplinary program that teaches ethics to people who will build and deploy machine learning systems. A colleague has circulated a draft essay arguing that Plutarch's *Parallel Lives* — and, he says, hagiography too — are early instances of imitation learning on a curated demonstration set. His mapping, as I reconstruct it, runs like this: the biographer is the dataset curator; each Life is a demonstration trajectory; the paired Greek-and-Roman structure supplies contrastive pairs so the learner can see the same virtue realized under different conditions; the reader who "absorbs" the exemplar without ever being told a rule is doing behavioral cloning rather than inference from principles; and the fact that ancient lives contain embellishment, anachronism, and (in the saints' lives) outright miracle is just label noise, which he claims exposure-based learning tolerates gracefully. He supports the reading with Plutarch's own language — in the preface to the paired Lives of Timoleon and Aemilius Paulus he describes "using history as a mirror and endeavouring in a manner to fashion and adorn my life in conformity with the virtues therein depicted," and elsewhere in the same preface speaks of "calmly and dispassionately turning my thoughts away from them to the fairest of my examples."

The analogy is doing two pieces of work, and those are what I need audited.

- Conclusion A: moral education should be exemplar-based rather than principle-based, because that is demonstrably how virtue is actually acquired; teaching ethical theory to people who will wield powerful technology is a detour, and what we should be curating is a set of lives.
- Conclusion B: factual accuracy in a life is largely beside the point. Since the mechanism is exposure rather than inference, an embellished or even partly invented exemplar transfers the virtue just as well; Plutarch's inaccuracies and the hagiographer's miracles are equally harmless, and equally unobjectionable.
