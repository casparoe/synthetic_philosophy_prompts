A public history museum where I work is preparing an exhibition on how historians distinguish a well-supported revisionist claim from a pseudohistorical one. The curators have decided against a gallery of famous bad claims; they want visitors to leave able to apply a rubric themselves. The draft instrument we have been circulating scores any claim on six indicators, one point each:

(1) The claim cannot be formulated in a way that specifies what evidence, if it turned up, would count against it.
(2) Anomalies are accumulated without reference to base rates — every unexplained inscription, out-of-place artifact, and gap in the record is entered as positive evidence, with no comparison to the amount of unexplained material the field contains in general.
(3) Its proponents address a general audience and do not engage the technical objections raised in the specialist literature.
(4) The claim becomes more convincing to its adherents as further anomalies are attached to it, rather than being constrained by them.
(5) Its proponents have a stake — professional, national, or ideological — in the claim's being true.
(6) Its core has remained essentially unchanged for decades while the counter-evidence around it has accumulated.

These are loosely adapted from the falsifiability and research-programme criteria in philosophy of science; the exhibition text will not attribute them to any particular author, and no source apparatus is needed from you.

My task is to advise which indicators survive. Please do the following.

First, rank the six by discriminating power — how reliably each separates pseudohistory from legitimate revisionism that happens to be unfashionable, in both directions. Assign each a score from 0 (no better than a coin flip) to 1 (near-decisive), arrange them in tiers, and give one sentence of justification per indicator.

Second, for each indicator supply the most damaging counterexample you can construct: either a genuine historical claim the indicator would wrongly flag, or an acknowledged pseudohistory it would let through. State which of the two failure modes that indicator is more exposed to, and if in your judgment the false-positive rate on real scholarship exceeds the true-positive rate on pseudohistory, say so explicitly and mark the indicator for deletion rather than mere down-weighting.

Third, answer two structural questions: should the rubric be scored conjunctively, so that a claim counts as pseudohistorical only if it trips several indicators at once, or additively as drafted; and which single indicator, if struck, would most weaken the instrument?

Fourth, propose at most one replacement indicator to fill the gap you identify, and subject it to the same counterexample test you applied to the original six.

Finally, run your finished rubric on the two cases the curators are already quarrelling over — the scholarly reception of the Vinland sagas' account of a Norse presence in North America in the decades before the excavations at L'Anse aux Meadows, and Fomenko's New Chronology, which applies statistical methods to ancient astronomical records and to recurrent patterns of dynastic names to argue that events attributed to Rome, Greece, and Egypt actually occurred in the Middle Ages. Report the score each case receives and whether you think that score is correct. If the rubric misfires on either case, identify the indicator responsible and say what the misfire reveals about the rubric as a whole.
