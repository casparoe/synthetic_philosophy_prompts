I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I am the teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course on the metaphysics and science of consciousness while also sitting in on a graduate seminar on temporal experience, which is where I first met the material below. Our instructor has delegated one question of the closed-book final to me, and I would like help drafting it properly. The question concerns the color-phi phenomenon: the display, going back to the work Kolers and von Grünau published in 1976 at Nelson Goodman's prompting, in which two differently coloured dots are presented in rapid succession a few degrees apart, and subjects report a single dot travelling smoothly between the two locations that changes colour abruptly at the midpoint of its path — a time at which the colour information carried by the second dot has not yet arrived. I want the question to be about Dennett and Kinsbourne's two ways of accommodating that report: the Orwellian model, on which the subject's experience is initially veridical but the record of it is rewritten after the fact, and the Stalinesque model, on which the brain waits, edits, and only then produces the illusory content that is experienced. My instructor has told me in no uncertain terms that the question must not be answerable by defining and comparing those two labels, since roughly half of my discussion section can already do that from the lecture slides.

The difficulty I keep running into is that my drafts are survivable by a good memoriser, so I would like the question built around a design that has to be argued with rather than recalled. In section last week an undergraduate proposed what struck the room — including me, wrongly, as it now seems — as a clean way to tell the two models apart, and I want it presented to students in polished form as a research team's proposal that they must evaluate. The design runs as follows. The display is standard: a red square at the left location, then, sixty milliseconds later and four degrees to the right, a green square. On probe trials a thin grey vertical line is flashed for eight milliseconds at the exact spatial midpoint of the induced path, timed to be physically simultaneous with the onset of the green square. Immediately afterwards, and before any general question about what the subject saw, a single forced-choice item appears: at the moment the moving square crossed the line, was it red or green? The response is a keypress under an eight-hundred-millisecond deadline. The team's reasoning is that this narrow, cued, speeded judgement differs in kind from a free retrospective narrative report — it is too fast and too specific to have been reconstructed after the fact, so an Orwellian rewriting of the story should leave it untouched. On their prediction, an Orwellian subject answers red and a Stalinesque subject answers green, and the models are thereby distinguished. They have added a control condition in which the same midpoint question is asked at leisure after a ten-second distractor task, on the theory that the delayed condition should come out green on either account. My flatmate, who works on motor control, thinks the whole debate is idle because the visuomotor channels driving a speeded keypress need not be conscious at all; I suspect he has put his finger on the flaw but cannot yet state it in a way that would grade well.

Please write three things. First, the exam question itself: a stem that presents the design in the terms above, states that it is worth twenty-five marks and that students have forty minutes, and asks them (a) whether the design discriminates between the two models, (b) if it does not, which premise in the team's reasoning is doing the work they wanted the experiment to do, and (c) what kind of measure, if any, could settle the matter without begging the question about when and where experiential content is fixed. Second, a model answer of about seven hundred words that I can hand to the graders as a calibration text. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five that names the specific moves a first-class answer must contain — I expect these to include the observation that both models already concede the report is postdictive, since the second colour is not available at the reported moment of change; the observation that the probe response is nevertheless still a memory-based report and so still rewritable on the Orwellian side; the observation that substituting a non-report measure, whether a saccade, a keypress latency, or a neural signal, requires a linking hypothesis identifying that measure as a marker of conscious content, which is exactly what the dispute withholds; and an explicit, defended stand on whether the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction is empirically empty or merely hard to test. For each item give the marks available, a one-line statement of the characteristic error that should cost those marks, and — since the grading team is two undergraduates and me — a note separating a solid B, which recites both models and pronounces the design inconclusive, from an A, which says why it is inconclusive and states what would have to be true for it to be decisive. A bonus mark or two for an accurate connection to Libet's backward-referral results would be welcome, but only if the connection is load-bearing rather than decorative. Finally, if you think my probe design has a feature that makes part (a) either too easy or too subtle for this level, say so and propose a variant before committing to the final version.
