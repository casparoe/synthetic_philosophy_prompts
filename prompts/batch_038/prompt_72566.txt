I run model risk at a mid-sized consumer lender, and before next month's fairness review I have written down the position I intend to defend. What I want is an audit of that position, not a survey: assume familiarity with the impossibility results concerning calibration, error-rate balance, and statistical independence, and do not restate them for me.

My commitments:

1. The object of fairness assessment is the score, not any particular decision. A score is fair when it is calibrated within groups — the probability of default given score *s* is the same function of *s* for every group we monitor. If that holds, whatever thresholds downstream users choose is their business, not a property of the model.

2. Precisely because the score is the fair object, models should not be graded by decision-level quantities. Nevertheless, for our flagship product I require the deployed rule to hold the false negative rate on approvals within two percentage points across groups: an applicant who will in fact repay should have very nearly the same chance of being approved whichever group they are in.

3. Default rates in our book differ substantially across those groups, and I do not think the differences are artifacts of the label. We code default from observed repayment behaviour, and I see no reason to suppose that measurement is systematically distorted by group.

4. To keep the model from learning historical disadvantage, we train on a class-balanced sample, defaults and non-defaults at fifty-fifty, and we exclude group membership — along with any variable our counsel regards as a proxy — from the feature set.

5. I will not adjust, shift, or otherwise post-process scores after training. A score that has been nudged into looking fair is, on my view, itself a form of statistical discrimination, and I would rather ship an honest number.

6. I reject demographic parity as a criterion of fairness; equal approval rates irrespective of qualification is not what fairness demands. That said, I am entirely comfortable with the credit committee setting a somewhat lower threshold for one group this year to absorb the fallout from a regional downturn.

Tell me which of these pull against each other and how badly, which pairs only look like they are in conflict, and for each genuine tension the cheapest revision together with what that revision costs me in principle or in practice. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and end by naming the single change that would clean up the most. Keep the whole response under 600 words, and put your bottom line — is this package tenable as stated? — in the first two sentences.
