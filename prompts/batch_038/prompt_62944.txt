I have been going around in circles on a taxonomy problem, and I would like you to answer as a philosopher of biology who is equally at home in the adaptationist and the pluralist camps — someone who can take a trait apart without reflexively reaching for either "it must be an adaptation" or "it is just a spandrel." The context: I came at this from the other direction, from arguments about whether the modules posited by evolutionary psychology are adaptations at all, and discovered that I could not evaluate those arguments until I knew what I was demanding of an adaptation claim. I accepted what I took to be the central point of the spandrels critique, that a spandrel in the strict sense is a necessary architectural consequence of some other design decision rather than a general-purpose label for anything non-adaptive (and I am aware that the surfaces in the basilica that inspired the metaphor are pendentives rather than spandrels in an architect's vocabulary, which is part of why I distrust the word). What troubles me now is that in my own usage the strict sense has quietly collapsed back into the catch-all sense, and that my working inventory — adaptation, exaptation, spandrel, vestigial trait, developmental constraint, phylogenetic legacy, neutral trait, drift — is doing far more work than I can justify. A friend of mine in comparative morphology tells me I am using these words the way philosophers do rather than the way biologists do, which is fair but does not tell me which usage to keep. I want a taxonomy that predicts, not one that merely sorts.

Here are my verdicts, which I have tried to hold fixed rather than curate. (a) The navel: not an adaptation, and I resist calling it a spandrel, because it follows from no design decision at all — it is simply where the cord was attached. (b) The human chin: a byproduct of the retraction of the face relative to the tooth rows; I find the proposed masticatory and speech-related functions unpersuasive. (c) The male nipple: a byproduct of a shared developmental program, no function, and again not a spandrel in the architectural sense, since there is no arch. (d) The route of the recurrent laryngeal nerve: the nerve is plainly an adaptation and does its job, and its detour is plainly historical accident; I want both descriptions to apply to the same trait and I refuse to be forced to choose between them. (e) The inverted wiring of the vertebrate retina: legacy rather than optimum, yet I reject bad design as a summary, because the eye as a whole is superb — I seem to need a category for good design assembled out of inherited poor materials. (f) Wisdom teeth: the trait is not obsolete, the environment moved underneath it, and my word vestigial covers that situation and situation (g) alike, which makes it useless. (g) The appendix: I now lean toward currently useful — lymphoid tissue, some kind of reservoir for gut flora — without being confident that it is maintained for that use. Note that (b) and (g) are the pair I cannot live with: I dismiss a functional hypothesis about the chin almost without argument and accept one about the appendix on the strength of a suggestion. (h) Feathers: co-opted from insulation to flight, no difficulty. (i) Reading: an exaptation of visual object-recognition circuitry, full stop. (j) Arithmetic: I want to treat it differently, as an exaptation sitting on top of something that does look selected, since approximate number sense is present in infants and in other animals — and I cannot articulate what makes (j) two-tiered and (i) one-tiered. (k) The free versus attached earlobe: not an adaptation, not a spandrel, not a constraint; I am reduced to saying drift, and I suspect that drift is my old catch-all wearing a different hat. (l) Goosebumps: the pilomotor reflex made sense for insulation and threat display in a furred ancestor, and I cannot decide whether it persists because emotional piloerection still signals something or merely because it costs nothing to keep.

My tentative rule is this: a trait is an adaptation for effect E if its present form is explained by selection for E; a spandrel if it is a necessary geometric or developmental consequence of a feature that is such an adaptation; an exaptation if it originated under selection for, or as a byproduct of, something other than its present effect and was later co-opted; vestigial if its original effect is gone; a constraint if history has foreclosed the alternatives that would have been available; and drift otherwise. Please test that rule against (a)–(l) rather than admire it. Tell me the smallest set of categories that survives my verdicts, the single decision procedure for assigning a trait to one of them, which of my verdicts has to be dropped and why, what the procedure delivers for two cases I have not raised — the near-universality of seven cervical vertebrae in mammals, giraffes included, and the biconcave shape of the red blood cell — and where a residue remains that the procedure still cannot classify. Think the cases through carefully beforehand, and if it helps, brainstorm two or three competing taxonomies before settling on one, but do not show me that work: no literature review, no reading list, no names or dates, only the concepts. Answer as exactly five numbered items, in the order just given — smallest taxonomy, decision procedure, verdict to be dropped, the two new cases, the residue — each item one or two sentences long, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no comment on the format.
