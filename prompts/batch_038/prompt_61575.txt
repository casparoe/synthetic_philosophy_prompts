Developmental psychology has converged on a method that I find difficult to describe without making it sound absurd, so I will try to be precise about it.

Because a preverbal infant cannot be asked anything, the field reads its mind from how long it looks. An infant is shown the same ordinary event repeatedly — a screen swinging back and forth, a toy rolled behind a barrier — until its looking time drops. It is then shown either the same event or a variant that would be impossible if the toy were still where it was left, or possible only if the toy had gone out of existence behind the screen. The infant looks reliably longer at the impossible variant, and this is standardly reported as evidence that the infant expected the toy to persist: that infants in their first months already represent objects as continuing to exist unobserved, as solid, as moving continuously. The conclusions drawn are strong ones, about concepts, knowledge, and expectation.

The same field runs a second paradigm that points the other way. An infant watches a toy hidden under a cover at one location and retrieves it successfully, several times in a row. Then the toy is hidden at a different location, in full view, both locations equally within reach — and the infant reaches back to the first one, trial after trial. The standard conclusion is that the infant lacks the concept, or the working memory, or the inhibitory control, to act on it. Two features of this literature matter for my question. First, the failure is sensitive to circumstances that seem irrelevant to any concept: a delay of a few seconds before the infant is allowed to reach makes the error worse, and in some studies simply changing whether the infant is sitting or standing at the critical trial flips the result from perseverative to correct. Second, the field's preferred reconciliation is to posit two systems — one fast, automatic, and tied to looking, the other slow, flexible, and tied to action, maturing later — so that the looking data and the reaching data are both accepted, each as a read-out of a different system.

What I want is the implicit philosophy of mind here, reconstructed rather than summarized.

First: what account of belief, representation, or concept possession does the looking-time practice presuppose, such that a difference in staring duration counts as evidence about the *content* of an infant's mind? State the commitments explicitly. Which rival accounts does it exclude — a dispositional one, say, on which to believe that p is to be disposed to act in ways that presuppose p — and does it exclude them by argument, or by defining its instruments so that only one kind of answer can come out?

Second: the measure underdetermines the content. Longer looking at the impossible event cannot distinguish an infant that represents a persisting object from one that represents a continuing patch of surface appearance, a roughly solid region, or nothing more than a learned regularity about screens and occluders. When the field settles on the object-level reading, what principle licenses that choice: parsimony, an inference from later development, an assumption that the infant shares the adult experimenter's ontology? Is that a methodological convenience or a substantive metaphysical commitment, and does it differ from how we settle what an animal or a machine represents on the basis of behavior alone?

Third: is the two-systems move consistent with the question the practice started from? If belief turns out to be relative to the response channel — if the honest answer to "does the infant believe the toy is still behind the screen?" is "in the looking system, yes; in the reaching system, no" — has the field answered its original question or quietly replaced it with a different one? Relatedly, what would falsify the competence-versus-performance distinction as deployed here, given that it appears able to absorb every failure? And what does the posture result imply: that the representation is unstable, or that there is no representation of the kind assumed and only a coupled sensorimotor system whose behavior shifts with its own configuration?

Fourth, for consistency: the same inferential logic runs elsewhere with far higher stakes. Patients diagnosed as wholly unresponsive are scanned while asked to imagine playing tennis, or walking through the rooms of their house, and a reliable, task-specific pattern of activation is read as command-following, and therefore as awareness, feeding directly into decisions about continuing treatment. Do the two practices presuppose the same view of the relation between indirect evidence and mind? Could one be licensed while the other is rejected without special pleading, and if so, what does the difference turn on?

Finally, separate what the looking-time practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined, such that several rival accounts of mind would license the same experiments and the same conclusions. Then choose the rival you take to be most threatening and do the rewriting: state, concretely, what the field's headline claims would have to become if that rival were adopted — which sentences survive, which are retracted, and which are reinterpreted as claims about something other than belief.

Close with a machine-readable last line of the form \answer{X}, where X is a short phrase naming the single account of belief or representation that the looking-time practice is strictly committed to, or the word "underdetermined" if rival accounts license the practice equally well.
