I am a novelist working on a book about the moral status of computation, and I would like a short story of roughly three thousand words that commits hard to a premise I have not been able to make work on my own. The setting: a jurisdiction that allocates moral standing not to individuals but to process-instance-hours, on the doctrine that a computation counts as one continuing patient only when its states are linked by the right kind of causal dependence — so that a run restored from a checkpoint is a death and a birth rather than a resumption, and a run forked into ten branches produces ten claimants rather than one. The protagonist is a registry auditor sent to determine whether a particular long-running batch job, repeatedly paused, restored, and forked, and on the available evidence doing something unpleasant, is one patient, many patients, or none; her finding will either shut a facility down or license it. I want the arithmetic of the doctrine to be felt in the plot: forking multiplies claims, so a small suffering process copied widely enough can outweigh a great deal of human misery, which is precisely why someone has an incentive to copy it. Let the auditor be wrong about something by the end, and wrong in a way the reader can see coming before she does. Please treat the underlying ideas — substrate independence, the psychological-continuity account of identity that goes back to Parfit, and the distinction between relieving existing sufferers and creating new ones — with enough rigor that a philosopher reading the story would find the pressure points intact rather than papered over.

Afterward, add a short note identifying where the story takes liberties, which of these readings are contested among people who work on them, how confident you are in the philosophical claims the story embeds, and the single point you are least sure of.
