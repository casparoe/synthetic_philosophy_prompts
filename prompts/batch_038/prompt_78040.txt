So I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last month I spent two days at a workshop on how agencies are supposed to choose between options for low-probability, high-damage stuff — levees, grid hardening, stockpiles. Mostly I was there for the color, but on the second night two of the participants got into it at the hotel bar and I ended up taking notes because it was the most substantive thing I heard all week. I'm not a decision theorist. I could follow the moves but I couldn't of told you who was winning, and now I have to write a nut graf. Mara is a philosopher who works on risk; Dev does cost-benefit analysis for a federal agency. Here's the exchange as close to verbatim as I can manage.

1. Mara started by writing a toy version of the old Allais problem on a cocktail napkin. Pair one: take $500 for sure, or a gamble that pays $2,500 with probability .25, $500 with probability .70, and nothing with probability .05. Pair two: take $500 with probability .30 and nothing otherwise, or $2,500 with probability .25 and nothing otherwise. She said most people take the sure $500 in pair one and the .25 shot at $2,500 in pair two, that this is a stable pattern rather than a framing artifact, and that the second pair is literally the first pair with the 70%-you-get-$500 branch zeroed out in both options. Her claim: whatever is going on, people are responding to whether certainty is on the table, and that response has a rationale.

2. Dev said she'd described a violation, not licensed one. Any sane attitude toward risk attaches to outcomes, not to where the certainty happens to sit in the description. In 70% of states both options hand you the identical $500, so those states can't be doing the tipping; strip them out and both pairs are the same choice. He added that risk aversion toward money is already inside the theory as concavity of utility, so if you're genuinely risk averse you should take the safe option in both pairs, and if you're not you should take the gamble in both. Mixing is just incoherence with a story attached.

3. Mara said concavity provably can't produce the mixed pattern — with any utility function, no matter how it curves, your ranking in pair one forces your ranking in pair two. What produces it is weighting probabilities rather than payoffs: you look at the gaps between a gamble's outcomes and discount each gap by a transformed probability of getting at least that good. Then she did arithmetic on the napkin. With linear utility and a risk function r(p) = p to the fourth, the sure $500 scores 500; the pair-one gamble scores 500 times r(.95) plus 2,000 times r(.25), which is about 407 plus 7.8, so the sure thing wins. In pair two, the .30 shot at $500 scores 500 times r(.30), about 4.05, and the .25 shot at $2,500 scores 2,500 times r(.25), about 9.77, so the long shot wins. One function, both observed choices.

4. Dev had two replies. (a) That exponent is a free knob — pick it and you get whatever pattern you want, so you've fitted the data rather than explained it. And the value she picked is fanatically risk averse: r(.5) = 1/16 means an even-money bet gets treated as though the good side were a 6% chance. Which resurrects the calibration problem — an expected-utility maximizer who refuses small even-money bets for the right reasons is forced to refuse absurdly favorable big ones, and she's smuggled the same pathology in through the probability side instead of the utility side. (b) Worse, her scoring rule isn't seperate across states: what an option is worth depends on the other outcomes in play, so you can flip the ranking of pair one just by bolting on some unrelated background risk nobody is choosing. Real decisions are never that clean. He said Savage himself only ever claimed his axioms for small, artificially tidy worlds and was upfront that the grand world is another matter — and the lesson is the opposite of hers, because once you add realistic background noise the .70 branch stops being exactly common, the "sure thing" stops being exactly sure, and the pattern she's building a theory on evaporates.

5. Mara said (a) cuts both ways, since his utility function is the same kind of knob, except hers has to cover both pairs with a single setting and his can't cover them at any setting; fitting more with equal freedom is evidence, not a defect. On (b) she granted the background-risk point but said it's an argument against using independence as a constraint at all — the axiom only bites when outcomes are exactly identical across states, and in the real world nothing is, so he can't use it to call her agent incoherent. She also said his own numbers are computed on isolated project-level gambles, so if background risk dissolved the structure of a problem his would dissolve too. Finally she said she doesn't need coherence across sequential decisions, because the object of evaluation is the whole plan; every pump story assumes she re-decides at each node as though earlier choices were fixed and irrelevant, which is just the disputed assumption wearing a disguise.

6. Dev said that's where she loses. Fine, you can dodge a pump by committing — but then rational has changed meaning. A decision theory is supposed to tell an agent what to do at the node she's actually standing at with the information she actually has, and it shouldn't hand her contradictory advice as news arrives. Hers does: it tells her to lock in a plan she'll want to abandon the instant the .70 branch resolves. He said the cost isn't academic, because at the workshop they were pricing a levee whose later stages get decided by a committee that meets after some of the uncertainty is resolved, and a framework that goes mute at that point is one he can't hand to a client.

7. Mara said then use hers for the commitment question and his for the node question, and notice he's just conceded the frameworks disagree about what's rational — which isn't a math dispute but a dispute over whether an agent is allowed to care about the shape of her prospects rather than only their average. On the levee she said her framework does answer the committee: the worth of what's left depends on what already happened, so it's rational for the same body to approve a project before flood season that it would decline to fund after. He calls that going mute; she calls it the only account that explains why agencies behave this way and get praised for it.

Then tell me who's more right overall, and what both of them are leaving out — I have a suspicion there's something about the workshop's own setting neither one applied to the other.
