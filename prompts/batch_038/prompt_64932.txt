So my brother-in-law runs a Tuesday-night history thing at his place, and last month they got onto the old argument about who started the First World War — specifically Fritz Fischer and the fuss his book made in Germany in the early sixties. Two of the regulars, Nadia and Pete, went at it for the better part of an hour while I ate cheese and took a few notes. Roughly how it went:

1. Nadia said Fischer basically settled it. You line up three things: the December 1912 meeting at which the Kaiser and his senior officers treated a coming war as something to prepare for rather than avoid; the assurances given to Austria in early July 1914; and then the September 1914 memorandum of annexation aims. Put together that's a state that wanted a continental war and had a list of what it was for. So saying Germany is the culprit rather than one culprit among five is a claim with a truth value.
2. Pete said she'd stacked the deck by mixing two different questions. The September memorandum is six weeks into the fighting, drafted by a chancellor's secretary at a moment when everyone expected a quick win — every belligerent improvised aims like that once it was in, including the ones we call victims. Aims tell you what a state wanted after it was in, not what made it go in. So the real evidence is just the 1912 meeting and July, and the 1912 meeting produced no decision at all.
3. Nadia said fine, drop the September document, July is enough — and then she proposed a test, which she called the personnel-swap test. A state is the right target of blame when the decision would probably still have been made with different people in the key offices. In Germany's case, she said, the drift toward war in July 1914 was robust to personnel: it came out of general staff assumptions, the timeline of Russian rearmament, the way the whole apparatus framed the choice. Swap out individuals in Vienna or St Petersburg and you plausibly get a different July.
4. Pete said that's a nice test and probably false, and even if true it doesn't get her what she wants, because the same description — an alliance structure that converted a regional crisis into a general war — fits everybody, and the fear-of-the-future-balance argument was being made in Paris and Petersburg too, just pointing the other way. So her test either yields a specific verdict she hasn't earned from the evidence, or it yields the same verdict for all five powers, at which point responsibility has stopped doing any work.
5. Then Nadia made what I thought was her best move. Nobody in July 1914 chose a world war, she said; they chose actions that raised its probability. Blame by outcomes and you're just doing moral luck. Blame at all and you have to apportion by expected contribution to the probability — keep a ledger of it. On that ledger the assurances to Austria are a big entry, everyone else's entries are smaller but nonzero, and the annexation aims matter not for whether a wrong happened but for how bad the wrong was that the gamble got taken for.
6. Pete said she'd quietly changed the subject. A ledger of probability contributions is a claim about individuals making bets and doesn't need nations at all. And he pressed the deeper point: Germany didn't decide anything. Some dozen men did, badly, under no single decision procedure, and calling the state the agent is either a metaphor for those men or a claim about the character of a polity that nobody has argued for. Which would mean the whole debate is malformed rather than merely unsettled.

I couldnt tell you who won and I've been chewing on it since. Can you take the six moves one at a time and rate each — clearly right, defensible, shaky, clearly wrong — and say what each one has to assume to be true? I'm especially interested in (a) whether move 5 actually rescues move 3 from the objection in move 4, or just relocates the problem somewhere less visible, and (b) whether move 6 works against every account of national responsibility or only against one particular kind. Then tell me what the two of them left on the table together — I have a suspicion they're arguing past each other about what a blame assignment is even for, but I couldn't have said it at the time. And close with your own verdict: is there a non-vacuous sense in which a nation can be responsible for a war, and if so, what does it commit you to that you might not want to be committed to?
