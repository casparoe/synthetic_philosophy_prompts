I teach a 75-minute session in an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of animal minds: 18 students, all with at least one prior philosophy course. I want a structured in-class deliberation that makes them feel the underdetermination of consciousness attributions — that the standard behavioral and comparative-neuroanatomical indicators fail to discriminate between sophisticated cognition with phenomenology and sophisticated cognition without it, and that the indicator set loses its warrant precisely where it is most needed, i.e. once you leave the mammals. They should hit this themselves: no one hears the term, or any philosopher's name, until the debrief.

Build it around real, small stakes. I will commit a fixed sum — say thirty dollars — to be split across animal-welfare causes attached to the species under discussion, so the allocation is zero-sum and generosity to one dossier is a direct loss to another. There are six sealed dossiers, each an anonymized indicator profile: mark-test performance, learning flexibility and reversal, modulation of nociceptive responses by analgesics, self-directed comfort behavior, play, willingness to pay a cost for enrichment, plus neuron count and degree of centralization. Assign roles, including students whose standing job is to press the reading on which every indicator has a non-phenomenal explanation, and at least one who is given resemblance-to-mammalian-neuroanatomy as an explicit rule of inference to apply mechanically.

Please give me: guidance on constructing the dossiers, including which indicators are empirically shaky enough that I should flag them to the room afterward, and how to keep students from treating the task as guessing the species; the round-by-round timeline; the reveal; and a twist that lands after it — I want a seventh profile, indicator-for-indicator identical to one they scored generously, whose identity makes them want to retract the score. Tell me what that seventh dossier should be, and give me a fallback in case the room simply refuses to budge.

Then the debrief: map each round onto the structure of the problem, list what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect it flattens graded phenomenology into a binary and cannot stage the first-person asymmetry that makes the inference from our own case the only non-arbitrary starting point. Say whether that diagnosis is right, and what I will have to supply by hand.
