You are a philosopher of history with serious training in twentieth-century historiographical method; please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate diagnoses before settling on any of them. I am an undergraduate in a historiography seminar, and for six weeks we have been circling an argument that will not resolve. One half of the seminar follows Braudel in holding that the real explanations of large historical outcomes live in slow structures — climate and terrain, demography, trade routes, the mental habits that barely move across generations — and that his layered account of the Mediterranean world gets the ordering right: the near-timeless relation of people to their environment first, the slower rhythms of economies and societies second, and individuals, decisions, and events only as the fastest and least explanatory layer. The other half holds that this is an abdication rather than an explanation: structures are never visible except through what particular people did, the structural account never tells you why anything happened when and how it did, and a history with the agents drained out of it has no explanandum to be tested against. Each side restates its best argument, nobody converts, and the session ends where it started.

I have come to suspect that the impasse is not about history at all but about what a historical explanation is supposed to deliver — that the structuralists are describing slowly varying parameters of a system while the event-historians are describing its states and trajectories, so the two camps are answering different questions about the same object under a single name. Treat that as a hypothesis I want tested, not as a conclusion I want endorsed. Is this in fact a verbal disagreement about the word "explanation," a substantive disagreement about causal relevance and timescale, a difference in what each side will accept as evidence, a difference about the purpose of writing history, or some combination that needs to be teased apart? Diagnose it by reference to what each side actually says and what each is doing with the concept of cause, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a well-chosen case, a formal result, a change of question — or, if you think no progress should be expected here, argue that and explain why. I want the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of schools; no bibliography or reading list, please.
