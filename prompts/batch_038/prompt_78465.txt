I am at work on a novel whose central character is a cognitive scientist — a man of sixty, recently widowed, who has spent his career studying animal navigation and now finds himself applying his own theories to his grief. In the middle of the book he gives a lecture, and I want him to articulate, in his own voice and to a lay audience, the view of mind associated with Daniel Dennett. My editor pressed on me an essay from a glossy weekend magazine, written by someone who plainly knows the terrain, and I have been working from a paragraph in it. The paragraph runs as follows:

> Dennett's signature claim is what he calls competence without comprehension, and it is the hinge of everything else he thinks. Darwin performed what Dennett describes as a strange inversion of reasoning: before him, the only way to account for the order and design in a thing was to trace it back to a mind that had planned it; Darwin showed that the design of living things could be produced by a process that is not a mind at all and foresees nothing. Turing performed the same inversion on the mind itself, in Darwin's Dangerous Idea and again in From Bacteria to Bach and Back, where Dennett argues that a machine which shuffles symbols by purely mechanical rules, understanding none of them, can nevertheless do anything a mathematician can do — which is why Dennett holds that a program robust enough to hold its own in conversation with a fluent speaker really is a fluent speaker. Put the two inversions together and you get the real conclusion: comprehension is not a distinct kind of thing in the world, but simply what we call competence once the competence is intricate enough that we cannot see the machinery. This is why Dennett dismisses the Chinese Room. Searle asks us to imagine a man in a room who does not understand Chinese and concludes that no arrangement of that kind can understand, which merely assumes what was to be shown; there is no theatre in which the show of experience is screened for an inner audience, and there is nothing it is like to be a chess engine. What survives of understanding is a predictive strategy — to say that a creature believes something is only to say that anticipating its behaviour this way pays off. Dennett is therefore an eliminativist about meaning and about inner experience: there are no genuine beliefs, only regularities we find it convenient to treat as beliefs, and any remaining talk of minds is a courtesy we extend to complicated machines.

I would like an accuracy audit of that paragraph, because I intend to put a compressed version of it in my character's mouth and I do not want an expert reader to catch me out. Please take it sentence by sentence, or claim by claim if the sentences run together, and sort each into what a philosopher of mind broadly sympathetic to Dennett would sign off on, what is a defensible compression for a magazine, what is a misreading that happens to sound right, and what is simply false.

Then test the two inferences that carry the weight of the paragraph. First: does the denial of an inner theatre, plus the claim that the intentional stance is a predictive strategy, actually entail that there are no genuine beliefs — or does it entail something weaker, and if weaker, what exactly? Second: is the line about comprehension being what we call competence whose machinery we cannot see Dennett's position, or is it close to the reverse of his position? I ask because it strikes me that on the magazine's version, comprehension would grow out of the observer's ignorance rather than out of anything in the observed system, and that seems to make it a fact about us rather than about the animal or the machine. If that is right, say what Dennett thinks does the work instead. I would also like to know whether the claim about Turing's proof is stated accurately, and whether a machine that cannot see the machinery inside itself is a case Dennett would count as comprehension or not.

Finally, and this is the part I most need. I confess I prefer the harder, colder reading the paragraph ends on; it would give my character a knife, and I want him to turn it on his own sorrow — to tell his daughter that her mother's lifelong certainty that she understood Schubert was only competence without comprehension, and that his own grief is a regularity in a machine. Is that knife made of steel or of stage tin? If the dramatic effect depends on the eliminativist conclusion, and that conclusion does not survive the audit, then tell me what a character who gets Dennett right would say instead in that scene, and whether the corrected version is more or less devastating — and if it is less so, tell me plainly, because I would rather know now.
