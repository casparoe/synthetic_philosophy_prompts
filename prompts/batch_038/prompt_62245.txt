Right, so about three weeks out I'm giving a talk at a joint session between our law school's international criminal law reading group and the genocide studies centre that shares our corridor, and I want to walk in with the question period already rehearsed rather than improvising it. Please write everything in British English — half the room will be British and I'll be reading this sheet off a printout, so I don't want to be tripping over Americanisms.

The position I'm defending, in rough form: the reason the label "genocide" misfires so often isn't the special intent requirement that everyone blames. It's that the Convention criminalises the destruction of a group's *existence* while the wrong that actually mobilises people politically is the destruction of a group's *pattern*, and those two come apart precisely in the cases where the naming fights happen. My proposal is to read "in part" not as a numerical or substantiality test but as a functional one — intent is satisfied where the acts are aimed at whatever structures whose loss makes the group's continuation as a group impossible, whether or not anyone is killed. The consequence I bite the bullet on: forced assimilation programmes can be genocide, but also plenty of things that get called genocide in advocacy — a famine caused by indifference, a mass rape campaign with no group-destruction plan behind it — would not be, and I say that out loud in the talk. Then the second half: the fight over the word is not a distortion of legal judgement, it's the predictable result of one term doing double duty as a term of art with a defined actus reus and mens rea and as the highest condemnation available in political speech, so every legal finding of "not genocide" gets heard as "not that bad". My fix is to split the vocabularies — keep "genocide" as a technical term with no honourific load, and give advocates a separate graded political vocabulary for group destruction.

I open by reading out Lemkin's line from chapter IX of Axis Rule in Occupied Europe: "Genocide has two phases: one, destruction of the national pattern of the oppressed group: the other, the imposition of the national pattern of the oppressor." I then argue the second phase is what the treaty text dropped, and I note in passing that Lemkin himself rejected the older vocabulary of denationalisation as inadequate — partly because, on his account, it didn't capture the biological dimension and partly because it didn't capture the imposition of the oppressor's pattern. That's a paraphrase, not a quotation, so don't tidy it into a quote.

The room, as best I can predict it: two doctrinal ICL people who did stints as legal officers at the tribunals and will come at me on the elements of crimes, on the deliberate deletion of cultural destruction during the 1948 drafting, on nulla poena and the claim that you can't stretch a criminal definition by philosophising; three or four historians from the centre who are Lemkin partisans and will say the Convention is itself the betrayal, so anchoring anything to treaty text is a category mistake, and they'll want to know exactly where my functional test lands on residential schools and on frontier colonial violence; a grad student who's written on whether groups are defined by self-ascription or by the perpetrator's classification; one NGO person from atrocity prevention who only cares whether any of this changes early warning or referral practice, and who will say splitting the vocabulary just hands governments one more word to hide behind; and possibly a philosopher of language who'll ask whether my two-vocabulary proposal is even coherent given that meaning is fixed by use.
