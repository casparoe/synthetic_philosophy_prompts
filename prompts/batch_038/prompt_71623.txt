The test our panel applies to alteration applications is that the works must preserve the building's special architectural or historic interest. Nowhere does it say how much alteration that interest can survive, and I have come to think that gap is where most of our difficulties live.

I am the conservation officer for a regional heritage body. We decide roughly forty applications a month to modify buildings on our register: re-pointing, roof and window replacement, energy-efficiency upgrades, extensions, and now and then something as drastic as rebuilding after a fire. Each application is judged on its own, and each, judged on its own, is a small step — this course of stone, that bay of timber, one window on a rear elevation. Over twenty years the approvals accumulate into a building in which almost none of the original material remains, though no individual approval was unreasonable. Attempts to write a numeric limit into our guidance have failed, correctly, on the grounds that any such limit is arbitrary. But arbitrariness is not the same as caprice, and I suspect a panel can defend a line it concedes to be underdetermined, on grounds of consistency, administrability, and the absence of a better alternative. I would like to know whether that defense is coherent, and whether it would survive an appeal.

Please write a decision procedure my panel could run on an application. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, where it terminates in an approval, and where it hands the matter to a vote because the question is one of value rather than fact. It must fit inside thirty minutes per application, be usable by members with no philosophical training, and produce a written rationale that can be shown to an owner who is spending money and to an appellate body that will read it cold.

Four things it must handle explicitly:

First, the ledger. Give us a mechanism for recording cumulative loss across successive applications, and a way for the procedure to reach a different verdict on the forty-first application than on the fortieth without pretending that the building itself changed decisively overnight.

Second, the baseline. Decay and neglect alter a building too, so leaving things alone is not a neutral comparator. The procedure must state what it is measuring change against, and I expect that choice to do more work than our guidance admits.

Third, the object of protection. The same building gets different verdicts depending on whether what is being protected is the material fabric, the form and geometry, the evidence of particular hands and craft, the continuity of use, or the building's contribution to its setting. Say which of these your procedure presupposes, at which step each one enters, and what would change if the panel adopted a different one.

Fourth, precedent. To the extent that the boundary is made determinate by our own accumulated decisions rather than by anything in the building, tell me what discipline that imposes: how reasons should be given so that later panels are bound in the right way, and when a line should be reopened rather than followed.

Then run the procedure on four cases:

1. An eighteenth-century stone farmhouse with failing mortar. The available contractor proposes a hard cement-rich mix that will darken the wall and alter how the fabric manages moisture; the visually and physically compatible lime alternative costs three times as much and the owner cannot raise it.

2. A timber-framed hall where, over five applications in eighteen years, we have approved piecemeal replacement of rotten members. Some 45 percent of the frame is now machine-cut new oak. Geometry, joint types, and appearance are faithful, and each new member is discreetly stamped with the year of its insertion.

3. A village chapel gutted by fire. The owner proposes reconstruction to the pre-fire appearance on a steel frame with prefabricated stone cladding, arguing that the building's significance lies in its silhouette in the landscape and in its continuing use for worship.

4. Concealed, fully reversible secondary glazing and roof-slope solar panels, approved building by building across a conservation area, where no single installation is visible from the street and the aggregate effect after a decade is a district whose fabric and character have both shifted.

At least one of these verdicts should run against what a heritage panel would expect, and I want you to say which and why the procedure produces it.

Finally, be candid about the limits: identify the steps where the procedure relocates the judgment rather than resolving it, and say where the residual arbitrariness ends up. I am not looking for a survey of the philosophical literature on vagueness or for references of any kind — I want the procedure, the reasoning behind each step, and the honest account of what it cannot do.
