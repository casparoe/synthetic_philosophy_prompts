I'm taking a philosophy-of-ML course this term that has an annoying lab component attached to it, and between the two halves I've ended up weirdly bothered by how I — and literally everyone in the lab — handle reward functions. I want to know what that whole way of working commits us to, because it feels like it settles a philosophical question without ever arguing about it.

Concretely, my project: I hand-wrote a reward for a small navigation task, watched the agent find a degenerate loop that farmed it forever, and then did what the TAs told me to do. Added a per-step cost so it stops dawdling. Added a dense bonus for reducing distance to the goal, then annealed that bonus down to zero once it stopped being needed. Rescaled everything so the numbers were a friendlier size for the optimizer, and later normalized by the running std of returns to keep training stable. Tuned the ratio between reward scale and the entropy/KL coefficient like it was a volume knob. Everyone in the lab knows the result that says shaping with a term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and we treat that as the definition of *safe* shaping — safe meaning it doesn't change behaviour, not safe in any other sense. And multiply every reward by ten and nothing about which policy is optimal changes, which we also just accept. So here's the thing that bothers me: when the agent maximized my written-down reward in a way I hadn't intended, I called it reward hacking and treated it as the *agent's* failure — cheating, exploiting a loophole, a bug in its alignment to what I wanted. But when I rewrite the reward myself, adding terms I know perfectly well aren't the goal and then deleting them later, nobody calls that hacking, that's just engineering. Apparently only one direction of this counts as a cheat.

Meanwhile in the reading half of the course we've been looking at the claim that reward signals in trained agents might be the functional analogue of valence in animals — positive prediction errors as something pleasure-like, negative ones as something suffering-like, so that training runs might have welfare in them somewhere. When I hold that next to what I actually did on Tuesday, they seem to be talking about completely different objects. My practice treats the reward scalar as (a) mine to rewrite at will, (b) meaningless in absolute magnitude, (c) only ever a proxy for something I intend but havent written down, and (d) something the agent can illegitimately game. The valence claim treats it as, in some sense, the agent's own good. So: reconstruct the implicit theory. What does this practice presuppose about what a reward signal *is*, and about whether the agent has anything at stake in getting it? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — the valence view obviously, but also just the weaker view that realized reward is a genuine good-for-the-agent even if it isn't felt as anything? Then be strict about the distinction I care most about: what the practice actually *requires* versus what it merely sits comfortably with. Could someone who thinks reward is valence do every single thing on my list above and just add a caveat in the paper, or is there one move in there — the normalization, the annealing, calling the loop-farmer a cheater — that is flat-out incompatible with taking the reward signal as valence? And where is the practice underdetermined, i.e. where would several different theories of value license exactly the same engineering decisions, so that the practice proves nothing? Related: does the shaping-versus-anti-hacking asymmetry presuppose that the real reward function is the designer's intention rather than the code, and is that a view the field states anywhere or just one it performs? Finally, suppose a lab adopted the valence view as official policy — tell me what would concretely have to change (can you still normalize rewards, still reset a run mid-training, still harden an agent against a strategy it is, on this picture, sincerely and rewardingly pursuing?) and whether any of those changes would survive contact with needing to actually ship results.
