The question was:

> (a) Explain what Caruso takes basic desert to require, and why he thinks that requirement cannot be met. (b) Explain Dennett's central objection to this move. (c) Say which of the two has the better of the exchange, and why. Max 600 words. 20 points: 7 for (a), 6 for (b), 7 for (c).

The student wrote:

> (a) Basic desert, for Caruso, is the sense in which an agent deserves blame or praise just in virtue of what they did and the will behind it, over and above any good that blaming or praising them produces. Retributivism needs that sense, because retributivism says punishment is justified by the offence itself, backward-looking, not by what it prevents. Caruso's point is that this sense requires ultimacy: the agent has to be the source of the action in a way that traces back to them and not to a causal history they had no hand in. Determinism rules that out straightforwardly. Indeterminism doesn't rescue it either, since a random event in the agent's history isn't something they can be credited or charged with — if anything it makes desert harder to locate. So nobody ever deserves anything in the basic sense and retributivism loses its foundation. What replaces it is the quarantine model: we detain dangerous offenders the way we quarantine carriers of a serious communicable disease, to protect other people, and the constraint is proportionality — least restrictive means, conditions no harsher than necessary, and a standing obligation to treat the social conditions that produce offending. In practice this is deterrence with a safety valve. Incapacitation is licensed by harm prevention, and proportionality just caps how much harm prevention can license, which is why a shoplifter doesn't get twenty years.
>
> (b) Dennett's objection is that the ultimacy requirement is a philosopher's invention that no actual practice of holding responsible ever depended on. He's a compatibilist: the free will worth wanting is the evolved capacity to foresee consequences, to modulate your own behaviour in light of them, and to respond to reasons — which is exactly what human beings have, and what makes us, unlike a wasp or a landslide, the kind of thing that can be reasoned with and held to account. So "could have done otherwise" doesn't need contra-causal freedom; it means they would have done otherwise had the reasons or the circumstances been different, and that conditional is true of plenty of agents and false of others, which is all the discrimination responsibility practices need. This is essentially Frankfurt's move — what matters isn't alternative possibilities but the structure of the will. Dennett also thinks Caruso's skepticism is self-undermining if it's applied to institutions, because holding people responsible is itself one of the things that produces responsible agents; withdraw the practice and you withdraw the training. So desert survives naturalized, and punishment survives with it.
>
> (c) Dennett wins the argument and loses the conclusion. He's right that basic desert is loaded with a metaphysics nobody can cash out, and right that skepticism has costs its proponents understate: if nobody ever deserves anything, praise and gratitude become as suspect as blame, and that's the implausible stretch — nobody thinks there's anything wrong with being grateful to a friend who went out of their way for you. But if all Dennett has established is that an agent would have behaved differently given different reasons, then his "desert" is a label attached to the fact that we have good grounds to hold them accountable, and those grounds are forward-looking. Strip the label and you're back at the quarantine model, with retributive vocabulary bolted on for comfort. Which suggests the real dispute isn't about whether determinism is true — both of them could accept it — it's about how much normative work the word desert can still do once it's been naturalized, and on that Dennett mostly just asserts that it does.
