Here's roughly my package, numbered so you can point at things:

1. At the level of moral status I'm a cosmopolitan: borders and shared citizenship have no intrinsic moral weight, and every person's claims count the same in the fundamental account.
2. But compatriots defintely do have special claims on me and on my state, and these aren't useful fictions. I specifically reject the story where we look after our own only because that's an efficient way of getting everyone looked after — I don't want the special duties to be a division of moral labor in disguise.
3. What grounds them, for me, is the value of the relationship itself: a shared history of building and sustaining institutions together. Not consent, not benefits recieved, not reciprocity kept as a ledger.
4. On distributive justice specifically I lean practice-dependent. What justice requires is fixed by the institutions that actually exist — the practice determines the content of the principles, it isn't that there's a ready-made egalitarian principle lying around waiting for some practice to trigger it. That's also why I'm sceptical of global egalitarianism: there's no practice of the right kind, so there's nothing determinate for it to demand at the global level.
5. That said, I think a genuine world state — real taxation, real enforcement, real courts — would be governed by egalitarian principles with basically the same content as ours. Nothing exotic happens when the practice goes global.
6. Case verdict: if my state has a limited supply of something rescue-ish, say vaccine doses whose research it funded with public money, it can permissibly prioritise its own residents even where the total harm falling on excluded foreigners is somewhat larger. But past a threshold of dire need, need trumps association — I won't let compatriots queue-jump over people who'd otherwise die.
