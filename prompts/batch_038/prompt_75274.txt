Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words on Molyneux's problem — the classical question about a person blind from birth who is later made to see and is asked whether he can identify, by sight alone and without touching, the shapes he previously knew only by touch. I want a serious piece of fiction, not a dialogue dressed up as one, and I want a version of the problem you are unlikely to have seen handled before.

Here is the setup I want you to use.

The protagonist, Ines, has been totally blind since birth and is in her mid-thirties when a cortical implant gives her vision for the first time. The unusual part is her upbringing: she has never learned shapes by touch. Her parents were both geometers, and out of something closer to principle than cruelty they refused to let her education run through her fingertips — spatial understanding, they held, should be built from definitions and deductions rather than from groping at models. So she learned solid geometry verbally. She can state the defining properties of a cube, a sphere, and a cone; she can give their volumes and surface areas; she can reason about their cross-sections and tell you that a cube viewed exactly along a body diagonal presents a hexagonal outline. But she has never held one, never traced an edge, never been permitted a tactile model. Her hands know cups, doorknobs, keys, and the spines of books; they have no acquaintance with geometric solids as such.

In the weeks after activation her vision is crude: a narrow field, no dependable color, edges and brightness contrasts, and no immediate sense of depth or of how far away anything is.

The central scene is this. Ines is seated at a table on which three solids have been set — a cube, a sphere, and a cone, all of comparable size and painted the same flat white. She is asked to say which is which, without touching them and without further instruction.

What I want the story to accomplish:

1. Commit to an answer. She identifies them correctly, or she does not, or she does so for reasons that should not count as getting it right. Pick one and let the story's internal logic carry the weight of that choice. Do not hedge by having the scene interrupted, the objects removed, or the question politely withdrawn.

2. Render the inside of the experience. The interest lies not in the verdict but in the texture: what her visual field actually gives her, what she recognizes and what refuses to organize, how the sentences she memorized as a child sit against what is now in front of her face. If she succeeds, show the bridge — what connects the definition she possesses to the shape she sees. If she fails, show what she reached for and did not find.

3. Dramatize, without explaining, the difference between genuine cross-modal recognition and a shrewd inference from some incidental feature — choosing the object that has corners, say, or the one that is not the round one. To an observer across the table these look identical. I want the story to make the reader feel how much turns on the distinction and how little access anyone has to it.

4. Include at least one scene before activation, showing how she thinks about shape when shape is only language — and let that scene plant whatever pays off at the table.

Craft constraints: a single close point of view, sustained throughout; the philosophical problem may be named aloud at most once, in dialogue, and preferably not at all; no expository passages about the history of the question; no character explaining the debate to another character; no closing in which anyone articulates a lesson. Give the parents intelligible reasons and let them be more than strawmen.

After the story, add an author's note of no more than 150 words stating which outcome you committed to and the single consideration that decided it for you — what you take to be required for knowledge of a shape to be available to a sense that has never met it. Do not cite sources in the note; just give your reasoning.
