Every entry our panel publishes gets used downstream in ways we cannot retract: state emergency plans cite them, the operating rules of two regional utilities cite them, and one term is now incorporated by reference into a funding statute, so that whether a given day is recorded under that term or another decides whether cooling centers open and whether a shutoff moratorium takes effect. I chair the terminology panel of a national heat-health data standard. We publish two drafts a year; each cycle produces four to eight contested terms, and a typical meeting disposes of two of them in ninety minutes.

The panel is two meteorologists, an epidemiologist, a utility regulator, two public-health officers, and a community-advocacy representative. None have philosophy training and none will read a methodology paper. What I need is a triage procedure we can actually run in the room: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what the exits are, and which exit routes the term to whom.

Build it on the diagnostic I half-remember from a course years ago: take the contested word, ban it from the discussion, and require each side to restate its position in substitute terms the other side will accept — then see whether the disagreement survives the substitution, and if it does, what it is now about. But do not assume the panel can produce neat substitutes on demand, and do not stop at the two-way verdict that test seems to promise. At minimum, I want the procedure to distinguish and route differently: a disagreement that evaporates under substitution; one that survives because the word was carrying two senses and the entry should be split in two; one that survives because the predicate is vague and the panel simply has to fix a boundary, the facts being settled while the choice of cutoff is not; one that survives because the parties differ about the world and what is needed is evidence rather than definition; and — the case I most need handled — one that is merely verbal between the disputants and yet still demands a binding decision, because the term has legal effect and someone must say what it picks out. State plainly that a verdict of "verbal" is not a verdict of "unimportant," and build a guard against my deploying it to shut a colleague down, since in practice declaring someone's position merely verbal is a way of dismissing them. Also tell me what the procedure does when a member refuses to offer a substitute term and the test cannot be run at all.

Then:

- Give me the one-page version the panel can hold in their hands: questions in order, jargon-free, with the sufficient-answer test attached to each.
- Say which distinctions from the underlying method you deliberately dropped in translation and why, and which you kept at the cost of making the procedure clumsier.
- Mark every step that is a judgment call dressed as procedure.
- Run it on the three terms below. For each, give the classification, the exit, the glossary action, and the sentence that goes into the minutes — which must be defensible to a lawyer reading them cold. No member will have produced a number on the spot, and my signature is what carries the decision.
- Close with what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of terminology fight it will misclassify, and how I would recognize that I am in one.

The three terms:

1. **Excessive heat event.** One member insists on dry-bulb temperature with a duration clause — three or more consecutive days at or above a fixed threshold. Another insists on a humidity-adjusted index, on the ground that the dry-bulb version scores as unremarkable exactly those nights that track mortality. Both have submitted data; the argument has now spanned two draft cycles.

2. **Heat emergency.** This is the statutory term. Two members agree on every forecast and every mortality estimate in the case file and differ only on whether declaration should be triggered by forecast conditions, so that centers open in advance, or by observed excess deaths, so that funding follows harm. The statute says only that a heat emergency must be declared. Our sponsor has told me the panel is expected to settle this on scientific grounds.

3. **Vulnerable population.** One member treats it as a property of persons — age, specified comorbidities — and produces a registry. Another treats it as a property of circumstances — no working cooling, living alone, outdoor work, no vehicle — and produces a map. Each says the other's list omits the people who actually die. A third member argues the term is advocacy language and should be struck from the standard; the advocacy representative argues that striking it removes the only place the standard acknowledges inequality at all.
