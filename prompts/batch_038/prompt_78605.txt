Three months ago I got talked into being the intake coordinator at a small housing-crisis nonprofit — people show up, we have a handful of emergency beds and a little short-term rent money, and somebody has to say who gets what. Up until now that call has basically been made by whoever is on shift, on instinct. Then we had one really ugly night where two volunteers made opposite calls on two families that looked similar on paper, one of them complained to the board, and now I've been asked to write a standardized scoring rubric. Points for this, points for that, threshold decides, appeals allowed.

I've been trying for six weeks and every version I write breaks on the next real case. Half the time I end up adding a clause that only exists to handle one family's situation, and then the rubric is four pages long and nobody will use it. So I've started to suspect the whole project is either impossible or going to make us worse at the thing we're actually decent at — but honestly I can't tell whether that's a real insight or just me not wanting to be pinned down by an accountability document I'd have to live under. Both of my instincts look self-serving from where I'm sitting.

So instead of deciding it alone I want to run it past the volunteers and see what happens when they try it.

Here's the setup: 14 people, 75 minutes, Tuesday night in the back room of a church, no projector — whiteboard, paper, pens, and I can print stuff at work. Mixed room. Four have been doing this five-plus years and are deeply suspicious of being scored at all; the rest are newer, and two of them are temperamentally rule-followers who I think will love the rubric and get annoyed with the veterans. I'd rather the activity not turn into old guard versus newcomers, so please build something that gives both temperaments a job to do where each one's strength becomes a liability at some point.

What I want you to design is the whole session: an activity where they actually write rules, apply them, and hit the wall themselves in the doing, rather than me describing the wall and then asking how they feel about it. The labels shouldn't appear until the debrief — no talk of particularism or generalism or anyone's name beforehand, they should arrive at the end and by then feel like names for something they already did.

Mechanically, a few things I care about:

- A deck of case cards where the same handful of features keep recurring but do different work. I want a feature that reads in card 3 as clearly counting toward helping to be the feature that makes card 11 feel wrong to help on. And I need the flip to feel like the feature genuinely changed sides, not like it just got shouted down by something louder in the total — I've gathered that distinction is where the whole argument hides, and if the deck only ever produces outweighing, the game quietly proves the rule-writers' point. So build at least two flips of the real kind into the deck and tell me which ones they are and why they're the real thing.
- A scoring scheme that punishes bad calls and also punishes rule bloat, so patching a rule has a price and they can feel the tradeoff between fitting the cases and keeping the thing usable. No real money, and please don't make it competitive enough that people start gaming it.
- A round where a team has to defend a call without appealing to any rule at all, and another team has to cross-examine them for arbitrariness. I want the accountability pressure to be felt from inside, because that's the part of the generalist side I actually find persuasive and I don't want the session to be a hit piece on rules.

Deliverables: materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, roles and team sizes, the rule-writing format, and the actual case cards written out concretely — at least eight of them, with enough detail that two different volunteers would read the same facts off the card. Don't tell me to invent the deck; the deck is the hard part and it's the part I can't see yet. Then debrief questions tied to specific moments in the room ("when team B patched their rule after card 9, what exactly did that patch cost them?"), what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — I'd bet money someone says "just add more conditions until it works" and someone else says "the deck is rigged, you wrote the cards," and I need a line for each — and finally an honest note on which part of this debate the activity cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the last ten minutes.

One last thing I want the debrief to actually settle rather than gesture at: whether the conclusion we land on should be the strong one — no rule set can capture this, so the rubric project is mistaken — or the weaker one, rules are useful crutches that need judgment to apply them and the board's request is fine as long as nobody confuses the rubric for the reasoning. Those feel very different to me and I can't tell them appart yet, and which one it is determines whether I write this document or go back to the board and argue against it.
