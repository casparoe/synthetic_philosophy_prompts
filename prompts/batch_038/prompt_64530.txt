Two months ago I took over as director of a regional folk-song archive, and my first commission is a small travelling exhibit built on our ballad collection: some four hundred variant texts of thirty-odd ballads, taken down from singers between the 1930s and the 1970s, a great many of them from people who insisted they had learned a song once only, from one other person, and had never seen it printed. The wall text is mine to write. My two lead collaborators — a folklorist who has spent years with the singers' families, and a computational social scientist who has been modelling the corpus — got into an argument about the framing that I could not settle, and it now determines everything I put on those panels. I will report it as evenhandedly as I can manage, though I suspect I have failed at that in places.

(1) The modeller said the ballads are as close to a textbook case of memetics as any corpus gets: each singer learned from an earlier singer, introduced variation, and the variants that were singable and memorable persisted while the rest died out. The archive therefore records a genuinely Darwinian process — variation, differential retention, heritable transmission — and the exhibit should present the songs as branching, competing lineages rather than treating the biology as decoration. What is happening in our collection, she said, is what Dawkins meant, not a loose analogy to it.

(2) The folklorist said this has the mechanism backwards. Singers do not copy; they reconstruct. A singer hears a ballad once or twice, retains a gist, and rebuilds it from stock phrases and story-patterns available to any singer of the tradition. What we read in the corpus as descent is mostly convergence: many minds arriving independently at similar forms, because those are the forms minds find easy to build. He pointed out that our own field notes are full of singers who produced near-identical stanzas with no plausible chain of transmission between them.

(3) The modeller replied that descent and convergence are empirically separable: she builds trees from shared variant features, and the trees come out with strong, stable signal, which is not what independent reconstruction would produce. She added that even pure convergence would be a selection result — it would mean certain forms are likelier to be re-produced, which is precisely what memetic fitness is supposed to name. And if reconstruction makes a process non-Darwinian, then nothing cultural can ever count as Darwinian, in which case his objection is true by stipulation rather than by argument.

(4) The folklorist said the trees are trees of texts, and that she chose the characters knowing which conclusion they would yield. More fundamentally, selection requires something that persists through variation — a thing that is copied and that can out-reproduce its rivals. In reconstruction what persists is a gist, and a gist is not a particular; it is a pattern re-derived afresh by each singer, and re-derivation is not replication. Once fitness means nothing more than tends to recur, the theory cannot fail, and a theory that cannot fail does not explain.

(5) The modeller said his alternative does the same work with the same weakness. Attractors are posits too; they explain recurrence by appeal to properties of forms and of minds; they are no more directly observable than fitness. So the real disagreement, she said, is not whether a selection-shaped account is legitimate but whether the causal work happens between singers or inside them — a much smaller disagreement than the one he thinks he is having.

(6) He said the size of the disagreement is measured by what the exhibit would have to claim: if descent is real, we trace lineages and tell visitors which variant begat which; if reconstruction is real, we set five unrelated singers side by side making the same stanza and say nothing at all about ancestry. We cannot do both honestly, and he would rather the panels understate the pattern than misdescribe it.

Please adjudicate move by move. For each of the six numbered steps, say whether it tells against the other side, merely restates a position already on the table, or backfires on its author — and justify each verdict briefly rather than tallying them at the end. Then tell me where the genuine crux sits: is this a dispute about mechanism, about what counts as an explanation, about the evidential value of phylogenetic trees, or about something neither of them has named? Say also what each of them is missing that would actually have moved the argument forward. I am not after references or a survey of the literature; I want your judgment of these arguments exactly as they were made. Finally, and briefly: given material documented as thoroughly as ours is, what should the wall text claim?
