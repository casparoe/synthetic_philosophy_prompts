I build agent-based simulations for a living and have no formal training in philosophy, so please tolerate the engineering vocabulary; I will state my problem concretely and then ask you for a number.

The setup. Our team maintains a simulation of a regional housing market: two hundred thousand heterogeneous agents, each with a bounded search radius, simple heuristics for bidding and for relocating, and no access to any aggregate variable — no agent can observe or represent the city-wide price index, the vacancy rate, or the construction pipeline. We run the model many times under different random seeds. In essentially every run the same macro pattern appears: a coupled price-and-vacancy oscillation with a period of roughly eleven simulated years and an amplitude band that is tight across seeds, even though we have swept the individual-level heuristic parameters across a factor of four with no qualitative change in the pattern. We have no derivation of that period from the agent rules. We can only produce it by running the model. Our macro-level description is markedly more stable under perturbation of the micro rules than any individual-level description we have been able to write down.

A colleague who trained as an economist says this is simply an unfinished reduction: there is in principle a derivation from the agent rules, we merely lack the mathematics and the compute to find it, and once it is in hand the macro description turns out to be a dispensable heuristic. I think the eleven-year period is a genuine higher-level regularity that the individual-level story does not, in any useful sense, contain.

Here is what I want. Please give me your credence — a single number together with its derivation — in the following proposition, which I have tried to state precisely:

P: In any world containing agents of the kind described, every true statement about the aggregate pattern (its period, its amplitude band, its robustness to changes in the individual heuristics) is entailed by the totality of true statements about individual agents and their interactions; and every correct explanation of such a statement is reducible, in principle and without loss, to an explanation that quantifies only over individuals.

Note that P bundles two claims I suspect come apart: an entailment claim about what the individual-level facts fix (call it A), and a claim about explanations being reducible without loss (call it B). Give me your number for P, then numbers for A, for B, and for whatever further sub-claims P genuinely depends on — for instance, whether "facts about individuals" is meant to include relational or institutional facts such as one agent's taking a certain token to count as money; whether the vagueness in the boundaries of the aggregate pattern makes A truth-apt at all; whether the greater stability of the macro description under perturbation is evidence against reducibility or merely evidence about our modelling tools; and whether derivations that are in principle available but permanently infeasible can do the philosophical work my colleague wants them to do. One or two sentences of justification per term, and then show me explicitly how you combine them into the number for P.

Then answer three further questions. Which term is P most sensitive to — that is, where would a small change in your probability produce the largest change in the final number? Which term are you least confident in? And what would actually move you: not more simulation runs, since I can produce those all day, but which argument or piece of evidence would shift the number most, and in which direction?

Finally, my credence in P is about 0.35 and my colleague's is about 0.85. Given your decomposition, where does our disagreement most likely live — which single sub-claim, if settled, would close most of the gap? And please be blunt about the possibility that one of us has failed to notice that P is not determinate as stated; if that is so, say what the nearest determinate version is and re-estimate that instead.
