Been going back and forth on price controls for months and I've decided to stop arguing and just write down what I actually think about particular cases, to see if there's a principle hiding in the mess. Answer in British English please — I keep getting American spellings in these things.

My verdicts:

- Rent control in a city where housing is genuinely tight: not fine. I sympathise with the tenants completely, but I think it ends up making things worse and landing on the wrong people.
- A cap on the price of insulin — one manufacturer, nobody can go without it: fine. Not just permitted, positively required.
- A law stopping petrol stations and hardware shops raising prices in the three days after a hurricane: fine.
- Surge pricing on the wholesale electricity market during a heatwave: fine, doesn't even register. I'll admit those two look like the same thing on paper — a shortage, a necessity, a price held down by law versus one let rip — and I can't say why they come out different for me. My flatmate reckons there's some fairness research about people judging price rises against a reference price, sellers being entitled to protect their normal profit and buyers their normal price, and that this is what's doing the work in that pair. If that's real and if it's load-bearing for me, tell me.
- A minimum wage at a level that doesn't cost many jobs: fine. A minimum wage of £100 an hour: obviously not fine. Which makes me worry my whole position is just "controls are fine when they dont bite", which cant be a principle, it's a fudge.
- A price floor on milk that produces surpluses and gets dumped: not fine. But that's a floor, same shape as the wage floor, and I like one and not the other.
- Ceilings and rationing during a war: fine. The exact same ceilings left standing ten years later: not fine.
- A shop voluntarily capping its own prices during a crisis: fine, admirable. Several shops in the same trade agreeing between them to cap prices: not fine, that's just a cartel with better PR.
- Banning ticket resale above face value: not fine, I think, but this is the one I'm least sure of and I can't say why it bothers me less than generator prices.
- A cap on the interest rate for payday loans: fine — even though I've read the argument that it pushes the worst-off borrowers towards worse alternatives, and I still say fine. Tell me if I'm being dishonest about that one.

My own tentative principle, for you to break: controls are alright when there's no real competition on the other side *and* when the thing being priced is something people can't just go without. But it fails my own cases. Insulin and generators pass it, fine, but so does a wartime ceiling on bread, which I also like for what feels like a completely different reason, and it says nothing at all about why I'm happy with a wage floor and not a milk floor.

So what I want is the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, that actually generates these verdicts. State each one properly rather than as a slogan; say which of my cases is doing the work for each clause of it; then run it on cases I haven't listed — a minimum price per unit of alcohol, a cap on executive pay, a rent cap that only bites on people who've been in the same flat more than five years, a legal ban on paying blood donors, the tariff a monopoly water company is allowed to charge, and a floor on the price of carbon. And be explicit about which of my verdicts each candidate has to overrule. I'd rather have a simple principle that costs me a verdict or two than a baroque one that saves everything, but show me the baroque one anyway, because I suspect it's the one I'm actually living inside. Finally, flag where my "not fine"s are really "wouldn't work"s in costume — I think several of them are empirical claims pretending to be moral ones, and I want to know which.
