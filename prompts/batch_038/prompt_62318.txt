I'm trying to work out whether I actually have a coherent idea of what makes a kind natural instead of just gerrymandered, going by my gut on cases. Diamond and graphite: different kinds, even though both are pure carbon. Carbon-12 and carbon-14: same kind, even though they differ in mass and one of them is radioactive. Water: one kind, even though every real sample of it has some semiheavy water mixed in. Jade: not a kind at all — nephrite and jadeite are two unrelated minerals that happen to look and carve alike. Planets: Pluto isnt one, and the bit about clearing your orbital neighborhood is obviously a threshold on a continuum, but I still think the verdict comes out right. Dwarf planet: a gerrymander, not a kind. Electrons: a kind, even though nothing tells one electron apart from another except where it is and where it's been. Biological species: kinds, even though hybridization makes the edges leaky. Lab-grown diamond: still diamond, full stop — the mineralogists' rule that a mineral has to form by natural geological processes looks like bookkeeping, not metaphysics. But zeolite A, whose structure has only ever been built in a lab and never found in any rock: I dont want to call that a mineral kind.

My rough guess at a principle is something like "a kind is natural when its members share a microstructure that explains the properties we group them by," but that already seems to break on the isotopes and on electrons.

So: is there a single principle that fits the whole list? Give it clause by clause, and for each clause say which case above it's there to handle. I'm pretty sure you can't save every verdict, so tell me plainly which one or two go, and whether you'd rather hand me a simple principle that drops them or a complicated one that keeps them all. Then run whatever you end up with on three cases I didn't list: "gene," where the molecular notion and the Mendelian notion come apart; quark color; and one more you pick yourself. Please write the answer in American English.
