I am a novelist at work on a book whose principal institution is the Directorate of Public Confidence, the press and information arm of an invented state, and I need a chapter-opening artifact: a found document, reproduced in full, that the reader encounters before meeting any characters. I would like you to draft it.

Please work in two capacities at once — as a political theorist who knows Hannah Arendt's two essays on this subject (her 1967 piece Truth and Politics and her 1971 piece Lying in Politics, on the Pentagon Papers) and as a satirist with a good ear for bureaucratic prose. Think carefully before you begin writing. First, give me a short brainstorming section of no more than 200 words, in bullet form, in which you sort out which of Arendt's distinctions the Directorate has understood correctly, which it has inverted, and which it has simply never encountered; I want to see your reasoning about the concepts before you commit them to a voice. Then deliver the document itself.

The document is Standards of Practice, Fourth Edition, issued by the Directorate to its own officers, roughly 900 words, in numbered sections with bureaucratic headings, plus two boxed sidebars and a closing revision note signed by the Director-General. The register should be that of a mid-century civil service — internal memoranda, filing cabinets, chained circulars, the passive voice of the permanent official — rather than that of a contemporary social-media team; I do not want a lazy post-truth op-ed in costume.

The satire's target is a practice and a style of discourse, not any real person, party, government, or news organization. Keep the state unnamed and every named individual invented. The Directorate's founding authority is Arendt, cited by name only; the handbook's authors believe they have absorbed her and are, in their own estimation, defending the political realm. They are sincere, articulate, and not villains. What they have taken from her is the claim that factual truth is despotic in a specifically political sense — that it arrives without argument, forecloses opinion, and belongs to the truth-teller who stands outside the space of politics — together with the idea that lying is a form of action, an intervention that changes the world rather than merely reporting on it, and that a well-made falsehood is likelier to be believed than the thing it replaces because it has been designed to fit what an audience already expects. What they have never absorbed, and what the handbook cannot therefore say, is that opinion depends on factual information, that facts can be destroyed but not substituted, and that a record no one can rely on eventually takes the office's capacity to promise, to act, and to remember along with it.

Those last points must emerge only through the handbook's procedures and its unexplained failure modes, never through authorial commentary. Concretely, I would like the standards to legislate: a two-tier classification of statements, separating rational truths (freely arguable, no clearance required) from factual truths (handled exclusively by licensed officers); the deprecation of concealment as a legacy practice, on the reasoning that hiding an intention leaves the world intact, whereas the office's remit is the world itself; a prohibition on flat denial, since denial preserves the authority of the thing denied, with proliferation offered in its place; a commissioning procedure for what the Directorate calls a replacement fact, and at least one audit or quality-assurance finding reporting that the replacement never arrived and that the vacancy has begun to spread to adjacent entries; and a self-deception clause, framed as a housekeeping rule about officers keeping private notes. Include one invented quotation from an earlier Directorate circular, attributed to a named official of your devising, and let a sidebar cover induction training, including what a new officer should do when a member of the public insists that something happened.

Two hard rules. Do not quote from any real text anywhere in the document or in your brainstorming notes; paraphrase Arendt's ideas in the Directorate's own vocabulary, and let every quotation mark in your answer enclose words you have invented. And do not append a bibliography, a list of sources, further reading, or any citation to a real work.

The tone must stay deadpan throughout and the piece must remain legible: a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without the author ever winking. If you have done this well, a specialist in Arendt will laugh at the exact place where the handbook's own logic begins to consume its author, and will judge the satire fair.
