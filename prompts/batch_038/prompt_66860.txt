A general-interest magazine recently ran an essay about why nations are younger than they look, and it ends with a claim I have no way to evaluate. Here is its summary of the scholarly picture, in the essay's own words:

"Benedict Anderson called nations 'imagined communities,' and imagined means what it sounds like: made up. His argument is that print capitalism created national consciousness where none had existed, since until the printing press spread, people in the past lived in villages and had no sense of belonging to anything larger; once cheap newspapers and novels appeared, ruling elites acquired a mass audience they could turn into a nation, complete with a flag. Eric Hobsbawm makes the same point from the other end with his idea of 'invented tradition': Highland tartan, royal ceremonial, and folk customs look ancient but were fabricated recently, usually deliberately, by people who needed a pedigree for institutions they were building anyway. So the scholarly verdict is that nations are not two thousand years old but about two hundred, that they are inventions rather than discoveries, and therefore that anyone who says 'my nation has always existed' is not simply mistaken but in bad faith: a nation is a costume someone put on and then refused to admit wearing."

I find the last sentence attractive, but I have also heard that Anderson's book is subtler than this essay suggests, and I suspect 'imagined' does not carry the weight the author puts on it. Going claim by claim: which parts would a specialist in the study of nationalism accept, which are defensible popular simplifications, and which actually misrepresent Anderson or Hobsbawm?
