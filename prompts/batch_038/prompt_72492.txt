I want to practice attacking the claim that free information is always worth having -- the Good's theorem result, that the expected value of an experiment can't be negative. Play a committed strict Bayesian who treats that theorem as settled and has thought hard about all the escape routes. Rules: you can concede that information with a price on it can be worth passing on (that's just arithmetic), and you can concede cases where getting the evidence changes my tastes or my act-set, though you'll want to argue those are missdescribed. What you will not concede is that an agent with a fixed utility function and a fixed set of acts can be better off refusing costless, reliable evidence about the state. You're allowed to say my case is misdescribed and redescribe it yourself -- that's a legitimate move, not a dodge. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs, conversational, no lecturing, and stay in character until I say stop. Also, at the end of every reply drop one line out of character saying roughly how confident you are (percentage) that your defense of the theorem actually survives what I've just thrown at it, and name the single point in that reply you're least sure of.

Here's my opening. I'm an ER doc deciding how to manage a patient. Two states: she has condition A or she doesn't, prior 0.1. There's a bedside test -- free, instant, reliable, and it changes nothing in the world except what I believe: P(positive | A) = 0.9 and P(positive | not A) = 0.1, so a positive leaves the correct posterior at exactly 0.5 and a negative at about 0.012. Three acts, same units throughout: aggressive drug D pays +12 if A and −10 if not; intermediate course M pays +7 if A and −2 if not; watchful waiting W pays 0 if A and +4 if not. With no test I take W and expect 3.6. If I conditionalize properly the test earns its keep: positive branch goes to M for 2.5, negative branch stays at W for about 3.95, total 3.69, so +0.09 -- your theorem holds, no quarrel. But here's the thing I know about myself, and it isn't a change of tastes. Positives get handed to me under a matched-condition alert banner with a clinical photo of the rash attached, and ten years of doing this has taught me that when evidence arrives that vivid I neglect the base rate and land somewhere near 0.95 on A instead of 0.5. At 0.95 I reach for D, which given the true posterior pays 1, and my total falls to 3.42 -- thats 0.18 worse than never running the test at all. Same utility function, same act-set, free and reliable evidence, and the arithmetic tells me to refuse it. So pick a horn: either the theorem isn't about the decisions actual agents make, or "value of information" isn't a value for anyone who might misuse it. Which one are you giving up?
