I'm drafting the opening section of a paper on Dennett's coffee-taster thought experiments from "Quining Qualia," and I'd like substantive critical comments rather than encouragement. Below is the argument in condensed form; please treat it as the real thing.

**§1. The cases, and the reading I want to reject.** Chase drinks coffee he used to love and now reports finding it unpleasant; pressed, he cannot say whether the taste changed or only his reactions to it. Sanborn, a seasoned taster, has grown more discerning over the years and reports that the coffee tastes different to him now, though nothing need have changed in his sensory apparatus. The standard gloss is epistemic: introspection cannot adjudicate between these competing descriptions, so the introspected intrinsic property earns no explanatory keep, and qualia go. I think that gloss is too weak for the conclusion it is supposed to support. Underdetermination of theory by evidence licenses agnosticism, not elimination. What the cases actually establish is stronger and different: there is no fact, even in principle, about which description is right, because same-quale-again has no determinate truth value once the subject's discriminative and judgmental economy changes. The failure is not that we cannot locate qualia; it is that the identity conditions for them are missing.

**§2. What the reframing buys.** If the problem is individuation rather than access, then the coffee cases and the inverted-spectrum cases are one argument, not two, and the familiar realist response to the latter — that inversion is conceivable, so qualia are real but not functionally determined — does nothing for the former. The realist owes a criterion for when an experience now has the same quale as one earlier that is not simply "it seemed the same to the subject," since that is exactly what the cases put in doubt. My claim is that any such criterion is either question-begging or collapses into a functional one.

**§3. My positive proposal.** Keep what the cases leave standing and call it a *taste commitment*: the comparative and reidentificatory verdicts a subject is prepared to defend under repeated attentive exposure, together with her dispositions to be surprised by her own verdicts. Sanborn's coffee did not change its character; Sanborn acquired commitments. This is meant to preserve the one thing worth preserving in talk of experience — the authority gap between novice and expert, which on this account is just a difference in the stability of commitments — without positing intrinsic properties.

**§4. The objection I address, and the reply I distrust.** Taste commitments look like a redescription of judgments plus dispositions, so the view seems to lose any resources for explaining why there is something it is like to taste coffee badly rather than merely to misjudge it. I reply that surprise at one's own verdict does the whole of that work. I am least confident here, and I suspect the reply is either empty or a surrender.

Please comment on the following, in this order:

1. Is the distinction I draw in §1 — epistemic underdetermination versus missing identity conditions — a real philosophical difference, or have I merely relabeled Dennett's own point? Say what would settle the matter, and whether anything in my §1–§2 actually turns on it.
2. Test the §2 claim that the realist has no non-question-begging, non-functional criterion of diachronic sameness of quale. Give me the best candidate you can construct and say whether it survives.
3. In §4, diagnose precisely which of the two failures my reply suffers from — emptiness or surrender — and say whether a third option is available to a view like mine. If not, say so plainly.
4. Flag any place where my paraphrase of the two coffee cases distorts what Dennett is doing with them, and any place where I credit or charge him with a claim he does not need.
5. Name the strongest objection from a defender of qualia that this draft does not raise at all, and state it in a form I could not easily absorb into §3.

Close with a short verdict: is the paper worth continuing, and if so which of §2 or §3 should be cut to make room for the other? I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list — only your own reasoning.
