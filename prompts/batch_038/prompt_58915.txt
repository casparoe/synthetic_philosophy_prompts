Our office café changed its cold-brew board three weeks ago, and two colleagues of mine have not stopped arguing about what the change proves. I was the only other person in the kitchen when it came to a head, so I have been drafted as referee, and I am not equipped to referee it. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can, in order, and I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than simply telling me who won.

The facts, which neither of them disputes: the café sold cold brew in two sizes, twelve ounces for $4.50 and twenty-four ounces for $7.50. Sam, a third colleague, bought the twelve-ounce every morning for two years. Then the café added a third line to the board — twenty ounces, also $7.50. It has never sold once; the barista confirms this. Sam now buys the twenty-four-ounce every morning. Tobias thinks the twenty-ounce line was put up deliberately by someone who had read about how menus get built; Hana says it makes no difference either way.

1. Hana: Sam's preference between the twelve- and the twenty-four-ounce turns entirely on an option he would never choose. So there is no fact of the matter about what Sam prefers, only about what Sam picks. "Preference" is a word we stick onto the picking, which means no possible behavior could contradict the theory — and a theory nothing could contradict tells you nothing. Utility is bookkeeping wearing a causal costume.

2. Tobias: The opposite. The framework makes a precise, checkable claim: if you choose x out of a set, you must choose x out of any smaller set that still contains it. Sam chose the twelve-ounce from {12, 24} and now chooses the twenty-four from {12, 20, 24}. That is a violation, and violations are what falsifiable theories deliver. The framework has been refuted for Sam. That is an empirical finding about a person, not a tautology.

3. Hana: Then be honest about what your utility is. Nothing checks the numbers except further choices. You cannot ask Sam how much he wants the twenty-four-ounce and get anything that is not itself behavior — his answer to the question is a selection of words. The number summarizes what he does and then gets introduced as the reason he does it.

4. Tobias: Choice is evidence for utility, not a definition of it, the way tracks are evidence for the animal. There are other sources: what he will do to keep the option open, what he will pay to hold it, whether he regrets it afterwards. And the framework is not idle. That a strictly worse third option would overturn a two-year habit is not obvious; it is exactly the sort of thing the theory lets you anticipate and the café exploit.

5. Hana: Then "Sam prefers the twenty-four-ounce" was false for two years and is true today, though nothing in Sam changed — only the chalkboard did. So the preference belongs to Sam-plus-menu. If preferences are properties of pairs like that, utility names nothing inside the person and we are back to bookkeeping. If you insist they are properties of the person, your framework has been refuted by a piece of chalk.

6. Tobias: Neither. Sam is not choosing between coffees in isolation but between prospects that include the comparison. A twenty-four-ounce sitting next to a twenty-ounce at the same price is a different object of choice from a twenty-four-ounce sitting next to a twelve-ounce at nearly half the price. Described at that grain, Sam's preferences are stable and transitive, and there is no reversal left to explain.

7. Hana: There is the emptiness charge, demonstrated rather than asserted: any pattern of choice at all can be made consistent by re-cutting the options finely enough. And it is not a victimless move. Sam is exploitable. The café could take his twelve-ounce, hand him the twenty-four for fifty cents while the decoy is on the board, then erase the decoy and buy the twenty-four back for fifty cents by returning his twelve. He makes both trades and ends a dollar poorer, holding exactly what he started with. That is what a genuine defect looks like, and no redescription of the options removes it.

8. Tobias: The pump only works on someone who does not see it coming. Sam, told the plan in advance, declines the first trade, because what he is then choosing between is not a cup and a cup but a sequence of cups-and-fees, and the sequence is bad. So the pump establishes that a context-sensitive chooser can be milked by whoever controls the menu — a claim about the café's conduct — not that Sam is incoherent.

9. Hana: A rationality that depends on whether an adversary happens to be in the room is not rationality, it is luck. And notice what you have already surrendered: there is no standing ranking in Sam for a pump to contradict, so utility maximization describes what a person would do if he had one.
