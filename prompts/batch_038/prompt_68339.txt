I'm a third-year graduate student in a developmental lab that works with twin and adoption data, and I've been asked to write a two-page primer for our department's first-year methods sequence on what a heritability estimate does and does not license. Before I write it, I want to know whether my own working intuitions are coherent, because I suspect they are not. A friend in the statistics department read a draft and told me I was "using heritability as though it were a property of traits rather than of datasets," which I think is the same complaint as verdict 5 below, but I could not reconstruct her argument and she would not spell it out. Here are my verdicts, in no particular order:

1. If a trait has h² = 0.85 in some population, nothing whatever follows about how much of any given person's trait came from their genes.
2. Two populations, both with h² = 0.80 for the same trait, differing by a full standard deviation in the mean because one has had worse nutrition: heritability is silent on the cause of the gap.
3. In a population where every child receives essentially the same schooling, a heritability of 0.9 for a wholly school-taught skill is perfectly possible, and I'm comfortable with that.
4. The same trait can be highly heritable in one population and barely heritable in another, so the number belongs to the population-in-its-environments rather than to the trait.
5. But: if h² is low — say 0.15 — in a population where school quality varies enormously, that is decent evidence that improving schools will raise the mean substantially; and conversely, a trait that is highly heritable in a population with uniform schools is thereby harder to move by policy.
6. Identical twins reared apart who correlate 0.72 on a trait give me more or less directly the heritability of that trait, and a better handle on it than sibling or adoption designs, since their rearing environments were not selected to match their genotypes.
7. A study I read put twin-based heritability for a psychiatric trait around 0.6 and SNP-based heritability around 0.2. I want to say the twin number was inflated and 0.2 is the honest figure, because the SNP estimate rests on fewer assumptions.
8. Where there is genotype-by-environment interaction, heritability is not so much high or low as undefined: you cannot decompose what exists only in combination.
9. A metabolic disorder that is entirely genetic in origin but whose severe phenotype all but disappeared once newborn screening and dietary treatment arrived: I want to say its heritability fell toward zero, and that this shows heritability measures how locked in a trait is. This is the verdict I most suspect, but I cannot see what is wrong with it.
10. If a trait is highly heritable within each of two populations, a large mean difference between them is likelier to be partly genetic than if the trait were barely heritable within them. I have been told this is a fallacy and I accept that it is one, but I want the exact reason, and I want to know whether any weaker residual claim survives — for instance, that high within-population heritability is at least an existence proof that the trait is developmentally sensitive to genetic variation.

My tentative principle, which I want tested against all ten, is this: a heritability estimate tells you what fraction of the variance in a trait, in one population, over the range of environments that population actually occupies, is attributable to genetic differences among its members — and nothing else. Is that enough to deliver every verdict, or does it need further clauses (about narrow versus broad sense, about what counts as the range of environments actually occupied, about the difference between a variance decomposition and the causal effect of a specified environmental change, about the fact that the estimand is defined only relative to a model)? Please assume I know what a variance is and what an MZ and DZ correlation are, define narrow-sense versus broad-sense the first time you use them, and give me the reasoning rather than references — I want no reading list and no citations, since I have no way of chasing either. Verdict 10 is the one I care most about getting exactly right, so treat it at length wherever it fits best.
