Every year our admissions committee proposes a new ordeal for applicants — a non-refundable fee, a trial residency, a public confession of some past error — and I have grown suspicious of my own enthusiasm for these. I direct a small residential fellowship, roughly thirty places a year from several hundred applications, and "it's a costly signal, so it must select for the right people" has become the committee's substitute for an argument. Write a decision procedure we could run on any proposed costly requirement before adopting it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure terminates in rejection, and the points where it hands the decision back to us because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Ground it in what signaling theory can actually hand a non-specialist. I want the differential-cost condition made usable — a requirement is informative only if it costs the applicants we want less than the applicants we don't — along with the difference between an unfakeable index and a handicap that can simply be bought, what makes unenforced talk credible when interests coincide, what a receiver's response does to a signal's value across successive cohorts, the gap between evidence of a trait and possession of it, and the possibility that the candidates we most want are the ones least inclined to signal at all. Flag which steps are judgment calls dressed as arithmetic, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle — in particular, what to do when a cost works not by revealing a type but by changing the person who pays it.

Then run it on three proposals: a $500 non-refundable application fee; a required public essay describing something the applicant got badly wrong; and an unpaid two-week trial residency. At least one should come out "no" despite the committee's fondness for it, and I want the reasoning shown rather than the verdict alone.
