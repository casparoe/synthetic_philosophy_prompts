I spent most of a dinner last week arguing with a friend, a philosopher of biology, about what my employer's database actually asserts when it attaches a functional term to a gene product. I curate annotations for a small model-organism database that contributes to a large cross-species gene-function resource. Our consortium's own definitions are written in the vocabulary of activity and contribution — what a gene product does at the molecular level, and what larger process that activity helps accomplish — and our evidence codes simply record what somebody measured and where. My friend's charge was that this is a disguise: that in practice we refuse to propagate perfectly well-supported activities, that we treat a demonstrated side reaction differently from a canonical one, and that our guidelines quietly invoke selection history while our documentation disowns it. I defended our practice as a neutral, evidence-driven enterprise; he said neutrality was exactly what a functional vocabulary cannot deliver. I am no longer certain who won, but I am certain that my curators cannot run this argument every time a gene comes up.

Please write a decision procedure that my annotation team can run on a proposed function claim. The constraints are these: each curator has a doctorate in molecular biology and no training in philosophy; the procedure must be executable in about fifteen minutes per gene without reading anything theoretical; two curators must reach the same verdict independently, or the case escalates to me; and the output of a run is one of four things — annotate at this term, annotate at a broader or narrower term, record no functional data, or flag for adjudication with a stated reason.

The procedure should give the questions in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark explicitly, for every step, whether that step is doing causal-role work (what the product contributes to the system it is in), selected-effects work (what the trait's presence in the lineage is explained by), or neither — and where the two come apart so that the choice is conceptual rather than evidential, the procedure should say so rather than pretend the evidence settles it. Tell me which distinctions from the philosophical literature must survive the translation into a checklist and which can be dropped, and what the checklist loses by dropping them. Be candid about the steps that are judgment calls wearing the costume of rules, and about what the procedure cannot do — in particular, it should not be asked to decide whether our resource ought to redefine function.

Then run it on three cases. First, a gene in a bird whose single-copy product serves as a housekeeping metabolic enzyme in most tissues and as a structural protein in the lens, where both roles are experimentally well supported. Second, an enzyme with a canonical activity plus a much weaker secondary activity demonstrated only in vitro, where one lineage shows signs of having been under recent selection on the secondary reaction. Third, a primate gene disabled by mutation whose orthologs in other mammals carry out a biosynthetic step the primate lineage no longer performs. On the third case, my own instinct is that there is still something true to record about what that gene was for; if the procedure tells me to record nothing, I want the run to show me exactly which commitment of mine it is overriding, and whether that commitment is doing any work elsewhere in our guidelines.

Finally, give me a one-page version the curators can keep beside them, and a short list of the three or four disputes this procedure will not resolve and that I should therefore expect to keep adjudicating by hand.
