A bioengineering consortium is preparing human trials of a gradual neural replacement procedure, and before it can draft an informed-consent document or advise a court on the personhood question, it needs a specification of what any account of survival-through-replacement must deliver. The procedure, which the consortium calls the Night Shift, is fixed; treat every detail below as stipulated and do not spend any words on its feasibility.

1. Each night for three hundred consecutive nights, while the subject sleeps, roughly a third of a percent of the neurons in the subject's cerebral cortex are removed and replaced by synthetic modules calibrated that same evening to reproduce the input–output profile of the neurons they replace.
2. The order of replacement is spatially scattered by design: at no point is any contiguous cortical region more than five percent synthetic, so there is never a stage at which the original tissue occupies an identifiable subregion of the brain.
3. The removed biological neurons are kept viable in culture for the duration of the procedure and could, on the consortium's own account, be re-implanted at any night up to the last one.
4. The modules are permitted to correct measured deviations from the target transfer function within a bounded tolerance. The cumulative effect is a real, measurable functional drift: by night three hundred the subject's working-memory consolidation is better than it was at night one, and no part of the system is exactly what it was.
5. At night one hundred and fifty a full functional snapshot is taken and archived. Two hundred nights after the procedure is complete, that snapshot is instantiated in a second substrate, interviewed for one hour, and deleted. The subject of the original procedure is alive, unchanged, and unaware throughout that hour.
6. On a standing battery of psychological tests, memory, character, and self-conception are preserved at every stage. But from around night one hundred the subject reports a persistent and quite specific feeling that the one doing the remembering is not the one who had the memories. There is no behavioral or test-score correlate of this report, and it does not remit. Treat the report as a sincere and accurate report of how things seem to the subject — it is a datum for the account, not a symptom to be explained away.

Write the desiderata for an account of personal survival that is adequate to this case. State the requirements such an account has to meet, numbered, and attach to each one the consideration or case that motivates it: the phenomena it must explain, the verdicts it must return on each of the six features above, the distinctions it may not collapse (for instance between numerical and qualitative identity, between identity and what matters, between a process being gradual and its being continuous, between the person and the organism), the biological and neurological facts it must not contradict, and the constraints imposed by the intended use — a consent form and a legal finding, and therefore something an engineer and a judge could each apply without philosophical training, night by night, with no appeal to intuitions they cannot check.

Then do three things with the list. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp — psychological-continuity accounts, biological or animalist accounts, four-dimensionalist accounts, and accounts on which identity is not what matters — and say why the marking goes that way. Identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied given the Night Shift and given the intended use; if the list is itself the result, show the derivation rather than asserting the tension. And name the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive candidates.

Close with no more than a paragraph, and not a scorecard, saying which requirement does the damage to each of the four camps named above.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on whether the requirements are jointly satisfiable, and which one does the most work — and then give the list and the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of the setup. Take a position throughout: wherever the camps disagree about what should even count as a requirement, say which side you are on and give your reason, rather than presenting the disagreement as a menu for the consortium to choose from.
