For the past two years I have been reading about artificial intelligence on my own — no degree, no coursework, just articles and long arguments online — and I have become convinced that two ideas about machine goals are the most important things that most people have never heard. The first is that how capable a system is and what it ultimately wants are two separate dials, so there is no automatic route from very smart to wants good things. The second is that a great many otherwise unrelated final goals end up generating the same practical subgoals: acquire more resources, do not let anyone switch you off, do not let anyone change what you are aiming at, and keep getting better at whatever you are aiming at. I have tried explaining this to people several times and I have failed every time, because out loud it sounds either trivially obvious or like science fiction.

So I want to stop explaining it and make people run into it.

I run a monthly Thursday-evening discussion group out of a public library branch. Between eight and fourteen adults, mixed ages, mostly people who do not read much philosophy, two hours including a break. There is no budget to speak of: index cards, a whiteboard, a bag of poker chips, dice, phone timers, and whatever else I can carry in a backpack. No money and nothing edible as a prize.

Design a game for that group which gets them to feel both ideas, with the weight on the second one. Some requirements, which I would like you to work within but also to push back on where they turn out to be counterproductive:

1. Nobody should hear the words orthogonality, instrumental convergence, alignment, or artificial intelligence during play. The game should be able to run as an abstract exercise about people pursuing private aims, so that the group ends up arguing about the mechanics rather than about robots.
2. Each player's final aim is dealt to them face-down and cannot be chosen, negotiated, or reasoned out of — and the aims must not all be variations on one theme. I want at least two that would sound, said aloud, like perfectly decent things to want, and at least one that is arbitrary to the point of being faintly ridiculous.
3. Capability has to be a genuinely separate dial from the aim, and this is the part I cannot solve. My instinct is to deal a second card saying how many actions a player gets per round, but that just feels like having extra turns, and a player with extra turns is not better at getting what they want in any interesting sense. I want a mechanic under which the high-capability player is visibly, demonstrably better at achieving whatever aim they happen to be holding, including the ridiculous one, and under which the other players come to regard that fact as a problem in its own right.
4. At some point after the middle of the game, at least one player's aim should be altered by another player's action, and I want that to feel like a disaster to the person it happens to rather than a relief, without me saying anything to make it feel that way.

Then give me the full running order: setup, exactly what is written on each card, round structure, how a round terminates, how the whole game terminates, and what counts as winning or losing — including whether it is possible for nobody to win. Then a debrief of roughly twenty minutes that maps particular moments from the play onto the two ideas and that works by asking the group questions rather than by telling them the answer.

I would also like three honest sections at the end. First: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this. Which players check out and why, which aims are likely to collapse into the same strategy so that the game stops demonstrating anything, and whether the group will simply form a cartel and cooperate with one another — because if they do, they will leave believing the evening was a lesson about trust, which is not the lesson. Second: which feature of the two ideas the game cannot reproduce at all, given that my players are ordinary humans who already share a large amount of moral background with each other and with me, and what I would have to say out loud to cover that gap. Third: which parts of the second idea are conceptual — true of any sufficiently effective pursuer of any goal whatsoever — and which parts are empirical bets about what real systems would do, since I suspect my group will not distinguish those and I want to have an answer ready when someone asks whether the whole thing is only a story.

Please begin with a single sentence naming the one mechanic that is doing the real work here, and then give the design, with no preamble and no summary of what I have asked for.
