I've spent the last year on a novel about a girl from this little harbour town who supposedly swam out to a wreck in 1798 and brought an infant back to shore. What's actually left to go on: one three-line newspaper notice that doesn't name her, a ballad written some fifty years later that adds the baby and the storm, a parish regiser entry proving somebody by that name existed, and what old men in the pub still say. The local museum has a plaque with the ballad version on it. The curator read my draft and liked it, but she's asked me not to cut against the plaque — her argument being that the story people have been telling for two hundred years is by now the real history of the place, whatever happened in the water, and that a novel which scraps it takes something away from the town.

The other thing I cant shake: in another century somebody will quote my book. My invented bits — the colour of the shawl, what she says to the man on the beach — will end up in a heritage pamphlet with no trace of me attached to them.

So what do I actually owe her, the thing that maybe happened or the thing everyone since has believed happened? And is the curator right that I'd be stealing something?
