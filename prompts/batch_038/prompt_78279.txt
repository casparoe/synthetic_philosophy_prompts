Three months ago I was appointed to the advisory panel in my German Land that reviews applications by religious bodies for the status of a corporation under public law — the status which, among other things, allows a community to have contributions levied on its behalf through the state's income-tax machinery, with the tax offices collecting and remitting for a fee. My training is in political philosophy rather than church law, and the file now before the panel has surfaced a problem I cannot settle from the handbooks.

The applicant is an umbrella association of local Islamic congregations, on the order of forty thousand adherents across the Land, with a stable organisational structure, published statutes, an elected council, and a fifteen-year history of operating in the region. Two features make it unusual. First, it keeps no membership register of the kind the collection mechanism presupposes. Affiliation in its congregations is a matter of practice, family and local recognition; some mosques maintain enrolment lists, most do not, and the association says that compiling a definitive roster would require it to decide, for its adherents, who they are — something its own self-understanding does not license it to do. Second, it proposes a flat per-capita contribution rather than the income-proportional levy used by the churches, and it asks that the state's collection machinery not be used on its account for the first five years; instead the Land would remit the flat sum multiplied by a figure the association itself certifies and which would be audited externally.

The panel is split, but not into a simple for-and-against. One half argues that the whole point of a status of this kind is functional equivalence rather than identical form: the association has governance, permanence, doctrinal cohesion and a willingness to be audited, and insisting on a parish-style register would mean that only communities already shaped like the churches could ever be recognised — equality as a demand that others become us. The other half argues that the register is not an incidental administrative formality but the load-bearing element of the arrangement: it is what makes the levy something the community has defined over a bounded group of people who can, by formal declaration to a state office, leave it. Strip that out and the state is no longer administering a community's own contribution but financing a self-certifying body whose numbers nobody outside it can check — and granting it would create a template every other applicant will invoke.

My difficulty is more specific than either framing. The contribution mechanism rests on the assumption that a community is bounded: that it can say who belongs, that belonging carries at least a notional opportunity of exit, and that the state's role is ministerial. If the applicant genuinely cannot say who belongs, that assumption fails at the point where it matters. But if the requirement that it say who belongs can only be met by the applicant reorganising itself into something it is not, then the requirement is doing quiet theological work under an administrative name, and I would like to know whether there is any formulation of it that does not.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should come to a clear recommendation — grant, deny, or grant under stated conditions — and the conditions must be ones this applicant could realistically satisfy within a few years rather than ones it could satisfy only by ceasing to be itself. Set out the reasoning so that members of both halves of the panel can see what would have to change to move them, engage the strongest version of the losing side's case rather than a caricature of it, and say explicitly what precedent the recommendation would set for the next applicant, since that is the objection I expect to face. Where the recommendation turns on a distinction — between administering a levy and certifying a number, say, or between recognising a community and defining one — name it and defend it rather than assuming it.
