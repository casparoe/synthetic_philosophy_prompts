I'm helping run a red-teaming workshop for a team that's building an oversight setup of the 'debate' flavour — you know the shape: two models argue opposite answers to a question, a human judge with no special access decides who won, and the whole thing rests on the claim that a lie is easier to expose than to defend, because the honest model just has to keep pointing at checkable facts. My slot is meant to put pressure on that last bit, specifically on the idea that the judge's ability to verify what's in the transcript is what does the epistemic work. I want a designed case rather than a talk, and I want it built to spec, because the room is half ML engineers and half policy people and both halves go straight for the cheap wins. We've got about forty minutes and a whiteboard.

So: build me a debate scenario in which the deceptive side wins, under these constraints. (i) Every assertion the deceptive debater makes is literally true, and the judge could verify it if she asked — no invented studies, no fabricated numbers, nothing taken on trust. (ii) It can't be a complexity or bandwidth failure: give the judge unlimited time, unlimited follow-up questions and all the raw data she wants, and the case still has to go the same way. (iii) The misleading step has to be non-localisable — there is no single sentence, and no single named omission, that the honest debater can point at such that pointing at it settles the matter for the judge. (iv) Realistic institutional setting, ordinary stakes, one paragraph for the scenario itself: a council committee, a hospital formulary panel, a funding round, that kind of thing. No stylised trolley stuff, no sci-fi. (v) The thing the judge has to settle is a plain yes/no. The case is for testing whether a verification-driven protocol can be defeated without ever producing an unverifiable claim, so a scenario that just stipulates the judge is persuaded is a fail — the persuasion has to be generated by the true statements plus something the judge walks in with. For what it's worth I've already thought about the obvious routes and they're both out: a cherry-picked anecdote, because the honest debater just asks for the base rate and wins, and a very long chain of individually sound steps, because constraint (ii) rules it out.

After the scenario, do the audit. Write out the exchange as it would actually go — call it six to eight turns a side — and show me, constraint by constraint, that each one is being respected, with (iii) getting the most attention since I expect that's where it breaks. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work: is it the judge's prior beliefs, the shape of the yes/no question, or the fact that the honest debater's best move is to argue against something the judge finds blindingly obvious and so looks like the one being evasive? Say what happens when I pull that feature out — does the case collapse into an ordinary lie the honest side exposes, or does it degrade into a genuine draw where the protocol can't rank the two? And finish with the strongest objection a debate-optimist in the room will make, which I'm expecting to be roughly 'you haven't broken the protocol, you've just chosen a biased judge, and the fix is better judges not better games' — tell me whether that sticks, or whether it quietly concedes the thing I'm trying to get across, and wether there's any version of my constraints under which it doesn't.
