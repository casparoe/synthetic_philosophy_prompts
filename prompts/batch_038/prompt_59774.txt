I run trust & safety for a mid-sized VR social platform (call it Meridian — persistent shared world, people build structures that stay up unless destroyed, there's an in-world currency called scrip that can be cashed out for real money above a threshold but crafted items can't be, and there are zones flagged "anything goes" where you opt into PvP and griefing on entry). Every week we get maybe fifteen escalated appeals that all come down to the same question: was the thing that happened in the virtual world a real harm to a real person, or is it just pixels? My team has no philosophy training, they've got about thirty minutes a case, and anything where we treat a virtual object as property has to be signed off by our general counsel, so the reasoning has to be short and written down in a way a lawyer can follow.

I know there's a live debate underneath this — roughly, one side says a virtual world is a genuine reality of a digital kind, so virtual objects are real objects (made of bits rather than atoms, but real for all that) and virtual acts really are the acts they look like; the other side says none of it is real, it's shared make-believe, and the only thing that can be harmed is the flesh-and-blood person behind the avatar. I want you to take that argument and turn it into a decision procedure my appeals officers can actually run without reading a word of theory.

So give me the steps in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which steps can be discharged mechanically (did a server snapshot restore the object, is the item cashable, was the zone flagged, is there a pattern of following the user across servers) and which can't, and the exact points where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to a human because what's left is a value question rather than a fact question. Wherever the two positions above would give different answers at a given step, tell me which one the procedure follows and what that choice costs us — who gets let off, who gets over-penalized. Build in the mechanics I described, because a procedure that ignores persistence, recall, the cashable/non-cashable split and the consent zones won't survive contact with our queue for a day.

Then run it end to end on four cases:

1. Someone burned down a memorial another user spent months building for a dead sibling; our recall system restored it from snapshot in under an hour, undamaged.
2. A player's avatar was groped and chased through an "anything goes" zone by someone who had already been following them across seperate servers for a week.
3. A crafted item that can't be cashed out in-world was stolen and resold on a third-party site for actual money.
4. An NPC companion another user talked to every day was permanently deleted by a stranger who never met them.

For each, give the verdict the procedure produces, the two-or-three-sentence justification the officer would file, and make sure at least one of the four comes out as something I'd personally hate — I'd rather see the procedure bite me than see it quietly bend to my intuitions.

Finally, be straight with me about the failure modes: which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, what the procedure will systematically get wrong, what incentives it creates for bad-faith appeals, and what it simply can't do — I already suspect it can't tell us how severe a penalty should be, and I'd rather you say so than fake a severity scale.
