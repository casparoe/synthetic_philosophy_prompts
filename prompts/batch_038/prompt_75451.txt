so I'm a junior and I'm one of the two student members on our district's technology advisory panel. Mostly it's boring, but in five weeks we vote on what to recommend to the school board about a tool the district wants to keep paying for. It's an early-warning thing: it takes 9th graders' attendance, credits earned, and a couple of 8th grade test scores and spits out a number for how likely each kid is to fall off-track — behind on credits by the end of 10th grade, basically on a slow path to not graduating. Counselors use the number to hand out the extra tutoring slots, and there are only like 120 slots district-wide, so it's really a ranked list and the top 120 kids get in.

The mess is that there are three promises floating around and I'm pretty sure they can't all hold at the same time, but nobody on the panel will say that out loud. (1) The board told the community at the spring meeting that the tool would be "equally accurate for every group," and in the minutes that got written down as equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates across groups. (2) The superintendent said at the budget town hall that the tool doesn't use race at all, not even to set different cutoffs for different kids. (3) Last month a student group filed a complaint pointing out that among the kids who get flagged, the share who actually do end up falling off track is way lower in one group than in another, so getting flagged means something different depending on who you are, and they're calling that profiling dressed up as accuracy.

The vendor's own numbers are what make my head hurt. They say the share of kids who actually fall off track is about 18 percent in one group and about 31 percent in another, and they say their score is calibrated for both, meaning if you take all the kids at a given score level, roughly the same fraction of them go off track no matter what group they're in. One board member's proposed fix is to set the cutoff for each group so that the number of flagged kids matches that group's share of enrollment, and the vendor wrote back that this would require race-aware thresholds, wich sounds like it just breaks promise (2) instead. Another member wants to solve it by asking for 300 more slots. Nobody has said what they'd give up.

Can you draft the memo I'm going to hand in and read aloud? It has to actually land somewhere — renew, renew with conditions, or don't — and it has to tell the board which of the three promises to stop making and what to do instead, with a real timeline, since the vote is in five weeks and next fall's scores land in August. I also want it to be honest about the strongest case for the promise I'm telling them to drop, because half the panel is going to be annoyed no matter what and I don't want it to look like I ducked the hard part. And please be concrete about what a false positive and a false negative even mean here, because nobody is getting punished — a kid who gets flagged gets tutoring, and a kid who gets missed just doesn't get it. I keep having the feeling that this flips which error is the bad one compared to all the courtroom and hiring examples people bring up, but I can't articulate how, and I need the memo to do that work for me. Same question for the "flag everyone proportionally" fix: my gut says it sounds fair and is actually worse for somebody, and I want to know if my gut is right.

Write the whole thing as straight continuous prose, like a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. My advisor says anything with bullets gets skimmed and then nobody reads the reasoning. Put the recommendation in the first paragraph so people can't miss it, and keep it to about a page and a half since I have to read it out loud. Also don't send me off to go read studies or papers, I have zero time and no journal access — just explain whatever I need to know inside the memo itself.
