I have taught the adaptationism debate for a number of years, and I have come to occupy a position considerably stronger than the one usually attributed to the critics of the adaptationist programme, which I should like pressure-tested. It is this. When a trait is fixed throughout a lineage, shows no intraspecific variation, is developmentally coupled to other features whose construction the organism cannot avoid, and has no demonstrated current utility, the correct default is not merely that the trait may be a structural byproduct of development and phylogenetic history; it is that the byproduct hypothesis is objectively preferable, and that once the developmental and historical causes are specified there is no residual explanandum left for selection to discharge. On my view, those who reply that such a trait must nonetheless have been shaped by selection owe a positive demonstration rather than a plausible story about what it might be for, and a great deal of what passes for functional explanation — including much of what is offered in psychology and the social sciences for features of human cognition — is a well-written description of a consequence dressed up as a cause. I am aware of the obvious embarrassment about the architectural metaphor itself: the structures in question are pendentives rather than spandrels, and there is a serious argument that the builders chose those surfaces deliberately and decorated them for their own sake, which would make the analogy not loose but actively misleading. I do not think that bears on my thesis, though I should like to be told if it bears on it more than I suppose.

What would count against the position as I have stated it? I want the map of its exposure, not a survey of the quarrel. Which observations, experimental results, and arguments should move me, sorted by how decisive they are — and where does the line fall between a defeater that would oblige me to abandon the thesis and one that is merely awkward for it? What does my thesis predict about the distribution of traits across lineages, about the relationship between form and utility, and about what comparative and experimental work will turn up, that the adaptationist alternative does not; and what does that alternative predict which I cannot accommodate? I should also like to know which counter-evidence a committed defender of my view would concede as damaging and which they would dismiss, and on what principled grounds, since the difference between those two responses is where my position either survives or begins to look like an article of faith. Then the harder question: does the thesis risk anything at all? Each time functional evidence appears — a demonstration of current utility, a heritability estimate, a fitness differential, an optimality argument — I can reply that this shows what a trait does now and not why it is there, and retreat to a claim about the burden of proof. Is that legitimate for a thesis of this kind, or has the position been qualified into a shape no evidence can reach, and if the latter, is that a genuine failing here or only a failing for a view that purports to be empirical? Connectedly, consider whether what I have stated is one thesis or three — a claim about how the world is, a claim about how evidence must be ordered, and a claim about what explanation consists in — say which is most exposed, and whether they can come apart. And please take seriously the possibility that my strong version collapses into the moderate one everybody already accepts, namely that adaptationist hypotheses require rigorous testing: tell me what would distinguish the two, and whether collapse would be fatal or merely deflating.

For concreteness, suppose a genus of small burrowing beetles in which every species carries, without exception, a pair of raised hexagonal bosses on the thorax — perfectly symmetrical, invariant within species, attached to no muscle, bearing no obvious relation to digging, fighting, or load-carrying, yet closely predictable from the geometry of the cuticle-folding pattern that the developmental system must use if the wing cases are to close properly. My colleague insists the bosses are adaptations and offers three candidate functions; I insist they fall out of the geometry and that the candidate functions are at most uses the beetles have stumbled into. Tell me what would settle that argument and whether what would settle it is the kind of thing that could ever be had — I suspect that what would persuade me most is a demonstration that my thesis is indistinguishable from an adaptationism that concedes everything I say about development, which would leave me holding a vocabulary rather than a view. I am not after references or a reading list; I know the literature and would rather you argued than cited. Please write in British English.
