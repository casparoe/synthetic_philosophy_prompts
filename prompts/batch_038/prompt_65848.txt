In England and Wales the reduction in sentence for a guilty plea is governed by a published tariff. A defendant who indicates a plea at the first hearing receives one-third off the sentence the offence would otherwise attract. After that stage the maximum reduction drops to one-quarter and slides down a scale to one-tenth on the first day of trial, and to nothing if the plea arrives during the trial itself. The guideline insists that the size of the reduction turns only on when the defendant gave up: it must not vary with the strength of the prosecution's evidence, and it must not reflect any demonstration of remorse, which is to be handled separately as personal mitigation. The same document also holds that a person is entitled not to admit the offence and to put the prosecution to proof of its case, and that nothing in the guideline is to be used to pressure anyone into pleading guilty.

American doctrine points the same way from the other end. Where a prosecutor warns a defendant that refusing a plea offer will prompt far graver charges — a five-year recommendation against a threat of a mandatory life term — the resulting plea is treated as voluntary, so long as it was a knowing and intelligent choice among the alternatives actually on offer. Yet a defendant who robs a bank because a stranger held a gun to his head is excused, his choice not counted as free at all; and in many common-law jurisdictions that excuse is simply unavailable to him if what he did at gunpoint was kill.

Reconstruct the theory of choice these practices presuppose, arguing from the description given rather than from any case law.

1. What distinguishes a threat that defeats voluntariness from an offer that merely makes one alternative expensive? Is it the source of the pressure, the offeror's standing to make it, the entitlement baseline against which the option set is measured, or something about the quality of the will itself?
2. There is a philosophical dispute about whether holding someone responsible requires that they could have done otherwise. Tell me whether these rules have already taken a side in that dispute, and which side; and say whether the plea cases in particular are best read as denying that acceptable alternatives are needed, or as redefining what counts as an alternative.
3. Separate what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with. The tariff's exclusion of evidential strength and remorse as reasons for the discount rules some familiar justifications out — say which, and what survives: a price the state pays to purchase the waiver of a right, a refund of costs saved, or a claim about desert. Is the practice philosophically underdetermined between these, or does one of them alone make sense of a scale that runs on timing?
4. Are the three practices consistent with one another? If not, identify the precise point of collision and what principle, if any, could reconcile them.
5. Finally, say what would have to change — in the tariff, in the voluntariness inquiry, in the availability of duress, in the design of plea negotiations generally — if we adopted the rival view that an agent left with only one tolerable option does not choose freely.
