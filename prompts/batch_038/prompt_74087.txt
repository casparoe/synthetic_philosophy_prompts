"Taurek starts with a case where you have a limited supply of some medicine. One person needs the whole supply to survive, and five others each need a fifth of it, so you cant save everybody. Taurek says the normal reaction is that you obviously have to give it to the five, becuase five deaths is worse than one death. But he says that's exactly the mistake. You can't actually compare one person's suffering with another person's, there's no scale you could put them on, so there's no fact of the matter about whether five losses outweigh one loss. That's why the numbers don't count: it's not that they count less, it's that you could never know how much to count them by.

He also says that if one of the people is your friend, you're allowed to just save your friend and let the stranger die, since it wouldn't be irrational to care more about someone you know. Taurek thinks you're actually required to save the friend in that case, because the friendship is a real moral reason and the strangers' claims can't be measured up against it anyway.

When everyone is a stranger though he says you should flip a coin, heads you save the one, tails you save the five. I think this shows he does let the numbers count a bit. He's not saying ignore the five completely, he's saying give them a bigger share of the chance. So his view is sort of in between the utilitarians and the people who say only the individual matters. And if you wanted to be even fairer to the five you could weight the coin, like 5/6 against 1/6, and Taurek would probably be fine with that too since it's still about chances rather than outcomes."
