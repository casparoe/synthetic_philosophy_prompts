I came to philosophy late and sideways. Thirty-odd years in a plant physiology lab — mostly stomatal conductance, which is about as exciting as it sounds — and since I retired I've joined a reading group at the local library that works through one book a term. This term it's Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I'll confess I expected to find it soft and have instead found it the most empirically minded moral philosophy I've come across: a man doing careful observation of a phenomenon and trying hard not to over-fit his theory to it. What I'm stuck on is the impartial spectator, and specifically the distinction Smith draws between the judgement of the actual people around you and the judgement of the man within the breast. My daughter, who works in communications and so would, says the whole distinction is a comfortable fiction, that any institution that talks about conscience is really just doing reputation management with better vocabulary. I want to test that on a case I remember vividly, because I was there, or at least in front of the television, for it.

The case is the week after Diana died in the car crash in Paris in the small hours of Sunday 31 August 1997. As I recall it: the Queen was at Balmoral with William and Harry, boys of fifteen and twelve, and she stayed there for most of that week. The Palace line was that she was putting her grandchildren's grief first and that they were private people at a private moment. Meanwhile the country was performing its grief very publicly and very visibly — the mountains of flowers at the palace gates, the queues, the books of condolence — and the papers turned on her for her silence, asking where the Queen was, with opinion apparently running strongly against her by the middle of the week. On the Friday she came back to London, a day earlier than planned, and gave a live address to the nation from Buckingham Palace, which was a genuinely unusual thing for her to do; and a Union flag flew at half mast over the palace, which I believe departed from the usual practice of flying the royal standard only when the sovereign is in residence and never at half mast. The funeral was the Saturday. If I've got a detail wrong, just ignore it — don't hang anything on the specifics, it's the shape of the week I care about.

So: analyse that week through Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, and do it in both directions. First the mapping, and please be exact about it — who is the spectator in each of the several judgement relations running at once (the nation judging her, she judging herself, the household judging the nation, and whatever the press was doing in manufacturing a spectator out of nothing much), what sentiment is being sympathised with, and where the business of changing places in fancy broke down and why it broke down in that particular direction, i.e. towards visible grief at the gates and away from invisible grief in Scotland. Smith thinks the sufferer has to bring his passion down to the pitch the spectators can enter into, and that this is where self-command comes from; tell me whether the Friday broadcast is that process working, or that process being corrupted into performance. Then the crux my daughter would press: can Smith's machinery distinguish consulting the man within the breast from caving in to a newspaper campaign? He also thinks the wise and virtuous man sometimes has to stand against the actual multitude, so the theory seems able to recommend both yielding and defying, which looks to me like a problem — say whether you think it's fatal, whether there's a properly Smithian answer, and what would count against your reading. Then the scale question, and here I want you to be honest rather than generous: Smith's spectator mechanism presumes proximity and a trickle of information, and this was a live national broadcast watched by essentially everyone at once, with a 24-hour news cycle in between. Does that break the theory, extend it, or merely change the inputs? And where Smith simply has nothing to say — about a constitutional monarch as a public symbol, about institutions, about media — tell me he has nothing to say rather than making him speak. Two smaller things I'd like folded in: Smith surely predicts that we approve of self-command in the bearing of grief, yet her self-command was read as coldness, so is that an anomaly or does his own account of distance and visibility explain it; and is there a Smithian way of granting my daughter her point — the distinction between the love of praise and the love of praiseworthiness seems made for exactly this — while still insisting the week had moral content and wasn't only PR. Structure it however suits you, though I'd like the mismatches kept seperate from the places where the mapping actually works, and finish with your verdict on whether the episode confirms the theory, strains it, or is a kind of case Smith never anticipated. Don't send me off to secondary literature or give me a bibliography, I've got the book and I want your analysis; and please write the whole thing in British English — after five years of American paperbacks my eyes still snag on every 'behavior' I meet.
