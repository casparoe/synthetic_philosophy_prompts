Context: I'm a software engineer. My company runs a reading group that's been working through Kuhn's book on scientific revolutions. Two of the regulars — Nadia, who does ML research, and Owen, a postdoc in history of science — got into it about whether the shift in AI from hand-built symbolic systems to learned neural networks was a Kuhnian paradigm shift. Here's roughly how it went, paraphrased from memory:

1. Nadia: yes, obviously. The two camps didn't just disagree about answers, they disagreed about what would count as an answer. The symbolic people wanted a system whose behavior you could trace through explicit rules; the learning people said trace it or don't, what matters is whether it holds up on data it hasn't seen. Words like representation and generalization meant different things in the two camps, and the old guard's complaint — "but it doesn't understand anything" — wasn't something the new camp could even engage with as an argument. That's incommensurability, straight up.

2. Owen: no. Whatever else incommensurability is, it has to be consistent with the fact that these people talked to each other constantly — same conferences, same journals, same benchmarks — and the symbolic folks understood perfectly well what was being claimed and said why they thought it wasn't enough. If you can understand a claim well enough to reject it, the meanings weren't incommensurable. What actually happened is that one side started winning on shared empirical ground, which is the opposite of a gestalt switch.

3. Nadia: "shared empirical ground" is precisely what's in question. You're judging the transition by the standards that won it. The symbolic camp didn't accept "it scores better on the test set" as a reason, because for them a result you can't explain isn't a result. Of course everything looks neutrally adjudicated from inside the winning framework — that's just the winners doing the bookkeeping.

4. Owen: that argument proves too much. If "the losing side didn't accept the winners' criteria" is enough to make an episode a revolution, then every change of fashion in every field is a paradigm shift and the phrase stops picking anything out. You're also skipping Kuhn's actual machinery: he wants anomalies the reigning paradigm can't absorb, a crisis, and then a rival. You've described a rival getting better results. That isn't a revolution, it's just winning.

5. Nadia: there was a crisis. The winters. Nobody could get hand-coded knowledge to scale, the systems fell over the moment the world got slightly messy, and the field kept producing the same failures for decades — combinatorial explosion, the frame problem, the fact that you can't hire enough experts to write down what a four-year-old knows about chairs. That's anomalies piling up with no fix in sight, which is exactly his setup.

6. Owen: a crisis in engineering throughput isn't a crisis in explanation. His revolutions are about a field changing what it takes the world to be like — the target of explanation moves. Here the target never moved: make machines behave intelligently. Same goal from 1956 to now. So at most you swapped tools inside one paradigm, which is normal science, not revolution.

7. Nadia: but a paradigm isn't just the goal. On Kuhn's own wider gloss it's the disciplinary matrix — the values, the model problems, the exemplars students learn from, the instruments. And the exemplars completely changed. A student in 1985 learned from logic puzzles and semantic networks; a student in 2015 learned from image classifiers and language models. Different exemplars, different picture of what a solved problem looks like. Different paradigm.

8. Owen: and there's the move I was waiting for. Widen paradigm until it means "whatever a field does," and everything becomes a paradigm shift. The interesting question was always the narrow one — did the meanings shift so far that the two sides literally couldn't compare thier claims — and you've quietly stopped answering it.

Neither budged, and at some point they both accused each other of defining the term in whatever way made them win, which honestly might be true of both.
