I am a second-year graduate student in philosophy working on explanation in biology, and I took one seminar in philosophy of physics as a courtesy requirement, so my grasp of the foundations literature is thin but not zero. Two nights ago I got into a three-hour argument with a friend who does quantum error correction — she is technically excellent and openly impatient with metaphysics — and we ended up talking past each other about whether the wavefunction is "real." I wrote her argument down afterward in my own words, partly from notes and partly from memory, so the phrasing is mine and may have sharpened or softened things in ways I cannot now detect. Here it is, as she would have put it:

1. Harrigan and Spekkens divide interpretations into ψ-ontic and ψ-epistemic: on the first, the quantum state is part of the physical state of the world; on the second, it encodes only what an observer knows, so one and the same underlying physical situation can correctly be assigned many different wavefunctions.
2. Every ψ-epistemic view is therefore a hidden-variable theory in disguise — it posits some real underlying state and treats the wavefunction as a probability distribution over it — which means it inherits every no-go result: Bell kills the local ones, Kochen–Specker kills the noncontextual ones, and there is nothing left over.
3. The Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem settled the remainder anyway: provided two systems are prepared independently, distinct pure quantum states cannot have overlapping distributions over the underlying states, so ψ-epistemic models are ruled out outright and the quantum state is an element of reality in the same way a classical field is.
4. Consequently the wavefunction is a real physical object spread out in space, the survivors are Everett and Bohm (both ψ-ontic), and views like QBism are simply refusals to do metaphysics.
5. The measurement problem was never a problem about what the wavefunction is; it is a problem about which ψ-ontic story you prefer, and the epistemic camp had no answer to it, since calling the wavefunction "information" does not tell you why we observe one outcome rather than another.

At one point I pushed back with my own move, which I am not sure was any better than hers: I said that a ψ-epistemic partisan could accept the theorem, deny the preparation-independence premise, and appeal to retrocausal influences from the measurement events back to the preparation; she answered that this is just superdeterminism under a nicer name, because it amounts to giving up the independence of the experimenter's settings, and no respectable person accepts that. Please assess my move too.

Act as a philosopher of physics who works on ontological models and no-go theorems and who has explained this material to non-specialists many times. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short section — no more than a few paragraphs — that lays out the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic distinction as the literature actually defines it, in terms I can follow without a textbook open, including where the taxonomy itself is contested or thought to cut things badly. Then go through her five claims one at a time and, for each sentence or clause, tell me what a foundations researcher would accept, what is a tolerable simplification, what is subtly wrong (especially where two claims the literature keeps distinct are being fused, or where a conditional result is being read as unconditional), and what is flatly mistaken. Pay particular attention to claim 3, where she appears to state the theorem's assumption and then immediately forget it, and to claim 4, where I suspect the list of survivors is not exhaustive and QBism may not even belong in her framework. Tell me which parts of claim 5 are actually correct, because I suspect she is not entirely wrong there. Then answer two further questions: what survives of her overall conclusion once the errors are corrected, and what is the strongest reply she could make to my corrected account without changing her mind. Finally, state how confident you are in each verdict — distinguishing the ones you would defend in front of a foundations seminar from the ones that are genuinely matters of interpretive preference — and single out the point in your entire response you are least sure about. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I cannot check them, and I would rather you say plainly when you are reconstructing than guess at a source. Close with two or three questions I could put to her next time we argue that would actually move us forward rather than repeat the impasse.
