I am drafting a chapter of my dissertation on the person-affecting restriction in procreative ethics, and I have reached a difficulty I would like an outside diagnosis of. My working assumption, which I share with Benatar and which I want to interrogate rather than defend, is that the asymmetry between the badness of pain and the goodness of pleasure carries over into an asymmetry between their absences: the absence of pain is good even where there is nobody to enjoy that good, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody for whom it is a deprivation. To test whether that position can be held without collapsing into something monstrous, I constructed the argument below. My reading group thinks it is sound; my supervisor thinks it is a sophism but will not say where; I have not been able to decide between them, and I would rather know exactly what I am committed to than win the exchange. I do not think the standard replies, DeGrazia's rebuttal and Benatar's response to his critics, engage this version, since neither of them addresses the general principle I extract in the first premise.

First, a stipulation. Call a good orphaned when it obtains although there is nobody whom it is good for, nobody made better off by it and nobody deprived by its absence. The asymmetry's second half cannot do any work unless it concedes that orphaned goods exist; call that concession the Orphan Clause. Now the argument. (1) The Orphan Clause is a general account of when states of affairs carry value rather than a local stipulation about procreation: a state of affairs is bad only if it is bad for somebody, but a state of affairs may be good without being good for anybody. (2) For any merely possible person P, either the life P would have had would contain significant pain or it would not. (3) If it would, then P's never existing prevents a bad, and by the Orphan Clause that prevention is good. (4) If it would not, then P's never existing forgoes goods, but by the Orphan Clause it forgoes nothing bad, there being no subject who is deprived. (5) So for every merely possible person, that person's never existing is either good or at worst neutral, and is in no case bad. (6) Since value on this account attaches to states of affairs rather than to relations between states of affairs and subjects, a world is assessed by the goods and bads it contains; the world in which no sentient being ever exists contains no bads at all, and the goods contained in other worlds cannot be entered against it as losses, because entering a missing good as a bad requires an owner and an empty world has none. (7) Therefore the empty world is better than every populated world, and better by a margin that grows with the suffering those worlds contain. (8) A betterness that could never generate a reason to prefer or to act would not be the betterness the asymmetry needs, since the asymmetrist's own case against procreation requires that the goodness of absent pain give prospective parents a reason not to conceive; so wherever one world is better than another and the transition is possible, there is at least a pro tanto reason to bring the better world about. (9) Hence there is pro tanto reason to bring about the empty world, and reason to hasten the painless extinction of sentient life, since each further day of sentience adds bads that a world without it would never have contained. (10) Yet (9) cannot be acted upon without killing, and killing is bad for its victim, so the Orphan Clause that generated (9) condemns every means available to it, and a principle that mandates an end it renders impermissible to pursue is not a principle anyone can hold. (11) Therefore the Orphan Clause is false, and the asymmetry with it, leaving a choice between a symmetric person-affecting view, on which neither absent pain nor absent pleasure counts without an owner, and a symmetric impersonal view, on which both count.

Tell me where this goes wrong, if it does. I need two things. First, a verdict on validity: if the argument is valid and the only question is which premise to abandon, say which one, and defend that choice by pricing the alternatives, that is, say what the symmetric person-affecting view costs its holder about the intuition that it can be better never to have been, and what the symmetric impersonal view costs about mere addition and the repugnant conclusion. Second, if some step is invalid rather than merely false, name the step, state precisely which term's sense or which quantifier's scope the inference illicitly switches, and explain why the step looked legitimate to me when I wrote it down. My supervisor locates the trouble at (8), on the ground that betterness is simply not reason-giving where there is no subject; part of my reading group suspects a slide somewhere between (5) and (7) but cannot articulate it; and I myself am most attached to (1), because I still cannot bring myself to believe that a world containing nothing but bliss is in any respect worse than a world containing nothing at all, which appears to be what surrendering (1) would require. Please reply in continuous prose, as one would write a considered letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists of your own, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Referring to the premises by the numbers I have given them is all the structure I need.
