I run a small weekly seminar on minds and machines that I started after I retired from a national lab (thirty years of condensed matter, mostly transport measurements in systems where the sample is coupled to a bath you can't fully isolate). I've only been reading philosophy seriously for about four years, so treat me as smart but new — and act as a professional philosopher who works on personal identity and philosophy of mind and has sat through a hundred of these talks.

Here's my situation. I'm presenting my own view next month. The room is mostly analytic philosophers of mind, one philosopher of cognitive science who leans enactivist, a computational neuroscientist who builds cortical microcircuit models and thinks whole-brain emulation is an engineering problem with a timeline, two metaphysicians who do persistence over time, and a handful of grad students who've read their Parfit. Nobody in the room is a transhumanist and nobody is a dualist, so please don't hand me the "but is the copy really YOU" question or any soul stuff — they're way past that.

My claim, roughly: gradual replacement of a brain, part by part with functionally equivalent prosthetics, preserves the person — but not for the reason everybody gives. It isn't that the pattern or the information is substrate-independent. I think that's exactly wrong, and it's the same mistake that makes copy-and-run uploading look plausible. My reason is that in a gradual replacement there is never a single instant at which the question "is it still the same mind?" could have come out otherwise, because the causal coupling between the new part and the rest of the system (and through it to the body and the world) is never let go of at any moment. Identity rides on the coupling being continuous, not on the arrangement being repeatable. I call it the no-detachment principle in the draft. This is supposed to explain why gradual replacement is fine and why scan-copy-destroy is not, without any appeal to how similar the end states look from outside.
