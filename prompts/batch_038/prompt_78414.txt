I have just taken over the comment desk at a regional paper — four of us and a string of freelancers — and my editor has asked me to draft a house position on when we may fairly describe a government's conduct as democratic backsliding, on the grounds that we have been accused of partisanship twice in one month, once by each side. The request is not wholly academic for me: my parents still live in a country that most comparative indices would now classify as no longer a full democracy, and I have found that this makes me both quicker to reach for the term and more suspicious of my reasons for reaching for it. So, in preparation, I wrote out my own verdicts on a set of cases, deliberately stripped of country names — I noticed that I reversed myself on two of them when the names were put back — and I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases and what I said about each. (1) A governing party, elected freely with 44 per cent of the vote, replaces the members of the electoral commission one at a time with people who have held party office: backsliding, clearly. (2) The same party redraws constituency boundaries in its own favour at the scheduled decennial review, using the ordinary procedure and a commission it appoints under standing rules: distasteful, but ordinary politics, not backsliding. (3) A government lowers the voting age to sixteen in a country where the young vote predictably for the left, and enrols them through schools: not backsliding, since the franchise has been widened — though I cannot say why this differs in kind from (2), as both tilt future contests towards the people doing the tilting. (4) A government lowers the retirement age of judges so that a third of the supreme court must stand down within a year, and fills the vacancies: backsliding. (5) A government simply declines to implement one final judgment it calls unconstitutional, changing no law and professing to abide by all the others: backsliding — although nothing institutional has altered here at all, which is what I said mattered in (2). (6) A president loses an election, alleges fraud without evidence, and concedes and transfers power on schedule: not backsliding, mere words. (7) A president loses, alleges fraud without evidence, and telephones a regional official asking him to find the votes needed; the official refuses and the transfer happens: backsliding, even though the institutions held and the outcome was identical to (6). (8) A government bans a party that advocates violence and commands 2 per cent support: not backsliding. (9) A government bans the largest opposition party after a court finds that its leader's speeches incited hatred, though he has never been convicted of an offence: backsliding — and I honestly do not know whether the difference from (8) is the size of the party, the absence of a conviction, or my sympathies. (10) A government channels all state advertising to friendly outlets and subjects critical ones to repeated tax inspections, all of it lawful: backsliding. (11) A government declares a state of emergency after an attack and renews it eleven times over four years, each renewal formally voted by parliament, governing by decree throughout: backsliding. (12) A ruling party requires university rectors and the directors of state theatres to be appointed by ministers: I lean towards yes, weakly, and I suspect I lean that way only because I dislike the party in question.

My tentative principle is that a measure counts as backsliding just in case it makes it less likely that the losers of the next election could become the winners of the one after. I can already see that it misfires: it treats (2) and (3) as equivalent, it cannot separate (6) from (7), and it seems to have nothing to say about (12). So I should like the following, please. State a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precise enough to be applied to a measure nobody has yet thought of, and for each clause say which of my cases earns it and which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; I would rather be told that (3) or (7) has to go than be handed a principle with eleven escape hatches in it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for two situations I have not listed: a long-established democracy whose ordinary machinery has always translated 44 per cent of the vote into a durable legislative majority, with an unelected second chamber and judges chosen by the governing party of the day — is that backsliding, or merely non-ideal, and if the latter, what supplies the baseline against which sliding is measured; and a national court annulling the result in one constituency three months after an election because the winning candidate overspent, thereby depriving the governing party of its majority. Finally, address the question my editor actually cares about: must the judgement be available to a reasonable observer at the time the measure is taken, on the information they then have, or is backsliding essentially a verdict of hindsight — and if it is only visible in hindsight, is it defensible to print the word at all? Please close by saying what would change your mind: which single one of my verdicts you would overturn with the greatest confidence, and what argument would persuade you that no non-partisan principle is available here, so that the term does nothing but mark the other side's moves. I would also like you to flag where your own reasoning is weakest — in particular, whether you have smuggled in a substantive view of what democracy is for in order to make my list come out tidy.
