I work as a software engineer — mostly data pipelines and internal tooling, with no formal training in history or philosophy — and last year our regional history museum asked me to join the advisory panel for a new gallery on the western Roman world from roughly AD 250 to 700, largely because I built its interactive centerpiece: a bank of touchscreens where visitors drag a timeline slider across a map of the western provinces and toggle layers of evidence (pottery types and where they are found, roof tiles from rural sites, cattle bone measurements, coin hoards, dated building inscriptions, lists of bishoprics) to watch what appears and what disappears over four and a half centuries. The gallery opens next spring, and at our meeting on the 14th the panel has to vote on its title and on a framing statement that will go on the wall at the entrance.

Three positions have hardened. The family foundation underwriting about half the gallery has written to say the title must be recognizable to a general audience, and their marketing contact argues that the fall of Rome is the phrase people search for and the phrase county sixth-grade teachers use on their worksheets, since the district unit that supplies most of our school bookings is taught under that name. Our academic advisor, a late-antique archaeologist at the state university, says she cannot sign off on the word fall at all: she argues that half the layers in my own interactive show continuity rather than rupture — bishoprics carrying on, the eastern empire intact, law codes and church institutions absorbing Roman ones rather than replacing them — and that fall smuggles in Gibbon's moral story about lost civic virtue while casting the Germanic peoples as destroyers rather than as participants who were themselves becoming Roman; she recommends the framing associated with Peter Brown's The World of Late Antiquity (1971), in which 250 to 700 is one continuous period of transformation. Another panelist says the opposite: that transformation is a euphemism, and that the material record visible in the very same interactive — smaller cattle, the disappearance of mass-produced pottery, roof tiles vanishing from ordinary rural buildings — is evidence that ordinary people lost a great deal, so softening the story is its own kind of dishonesty; he cites Bryan Ward-Perkins's The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization (2005) as a direct rebuttal. A third proposal, which I admit I like because my software already supports it, is to refuse to settle the question and make the gallery about the question: let visitors choose collapse, continuity, or transformation and show them how the same layers look under each label. The director's objection is that a gallery which declines to say anything will read as evasive to reviewers and confuse school groups, and she has asked each of us to circulate a memo recommending one course of action.

Please draft that memo — long enough to argue, short enough that people will actually read it before the meeting, so a page or a page and a half. It needs to land on a concrete recommendation: a title or title-plus-subtitle, the entrance framing statement, and what the interactive should do, since that choice drives my remaining build time. I would like it to engage the substantive question rather than split the difference out of politeness: is fall a false description of what happened, or a loaded but defensible shorthand? Is transformation a neutral technical term or a euphemism? And are the two sides even disagreeing about the same thing, or are they using different kinds of evidence and different accounts of what counts as an event — a date like 476 versus a century-long drift in cattle and pottery? Please give the strongest version of the case for the recommendation you reject, including the donor's practical concern, which I do not think is silly, rather than a strawman of it. Do not include a bibliography or reference list — I cannot verify one offline and I would rather have your reasoning; name a historian or a book only where you are confident it is real and the attribution is right. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation you make, on whatever scale feels natural to you, and single out the point in it you are least sure of: the place where, if a panelist pushed hardest, the memo would be most likely to come apart.
