I work as a software engineer on the research-platform team at an academic medical center. My training is in computer science, my statistics is entirely self-taught and job-acquired, and I have never taken a philosophy course, so please calibrate the register accordingly. In three weeks I am giving a forty-minute talk in the department's metascience seminar, with about an hour of discussion afterward. I was invited because I circulated an internal write-up of some simulation work, and the write-up got read by people who are far better at statistics than I am. The room will be roughly fifteen people: two clinical-trial statisticians who spend their working lives on group-sequential designs, interim monitoring, and alpha-spending; three or four psychologists who run preregistered studies and have recently started reporting Bayes factors alongside their p-values; one faculty member who teaches the department's foundations-of-inference elective and who holds that the point of a statistical procedure is that it would have detected an error had one been present, rather than that it licenses well-calibrated degrees of belief; and the rest graduate students. None of them work in machine learning, and none of them will enjoy being told that what they do is broken.

The thesis I am defending is narrower than a general case for or against either statistical tradition, and I want the questions aimed at it rather than at a strawman. I claim that the frequentist-versus-Bayesian dispute is downstream of a problem both sides leave untouched: a reported p-value or Bayes factor is interpretable only relative to the size of the space of analyses the analyst actually searched before choosing the one to report, and that space is essentially never bounded or recorded. On my account, preregistration does its real work not by increasing transparency or by disciplining motives, but by imposing a budget on that search space, in the same way a multiplicity correction bounds a list of comparisons, except applied to the analyst's upstream decisions rather than to a list of outcomes. My evidence is a simulation in which a scripted analyst-agent reprocesses the same generated dataset under varying numbers of permitted degrees of freedom; the false-positive rate degrades smoothly as a function of the log of the number of analyses tried, and it degrades about the same way whether the reported quantity is a p-value or a Bayes factor. So preregistration, on my view, is an error-control device rather than a sociological or ethical one. I already expect one attack and have no answer to it: that this is multiplicity correction with extra steps, and that the trial statisticians have had rigorous error control under optional stopping for decades. There is also a line of criticism I have been avoiding, which concerns what I fixed in my simulation before running it and what I adjusted after; like most such simulations, mine has its own degrees of freedom.

What I need is a preparation document for the question period, not a defense of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ranked by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly, and ordered roughly as they will come. Each one should be written in the first person, in the vocabulary the objector would genuinely use, with the technical content stated plainly enough that I can check my own understanding of it, rather than by pointing me to papers. For each: a reply I can deliver aloud in about two minutes, a frank note on where that reply is softest, and a flag if the objection is one I should simply concede rather than fight. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes of the talk so that two or three of these never get asked, what I must not give up even under pressure, which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the hour, and what my graceful fallback is if the question I have been avoiding does get asked.
