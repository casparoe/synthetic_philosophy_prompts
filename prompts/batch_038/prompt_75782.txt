I run localisation engineering at a mid-sized software company, and my days are spent among locale identifiers and BCP 47 tags, resource bundles and fallback chains, CLDR plural categories and script subtags, translation memory with fuzzy-match thresholds, glossaries and style guides, string freezes ahead of a release train, pseudo-localisation to catch hard-coded text, and post-edited machine translation for the long tail of our markets. What started this is a small thing. Marketing asked us to split a single pan-regional locale into five country variants on the grounds that customers perceive a national difference even where the text is nearly identical; once the split was done, the translation memory back-filled each new variant from the same source segments, and we ended up with five variants that were something like ninety-four per cent identical to one another, while everyone in the review meeting insisted the differences were the ones that mattered and that the new variants somehow felt older than they were. It struck me afterwards that this is precisely what Hobsbawm and Anderson claim nations do, and that I may have been watching an invented tradition get assembled out of a style guide, a match-percentage report and a terminology base.

I read both authors seriously enough as an undergraduate to know the shape of the argument, but my working instincts have since been thoroughly colonised by the machinery above, and I now suspect that the debate about how old nations are can be restated in that machinery with disconcerting ease. I would like you to attempt the restatement as faithfully as you can, and then tell me where it fails. Please take, at minimum: Hobsbawm's distinction between tradition, which is invariant and ritual, and custom, which is a motor of change and is easily confused with it — and map it onto the style guide, the glossary, and translation memory, asking which of my artefacts is invariant and which merely accumulates precedent until new text sounds inherited; Anderson's account of print capitalism, of vernaculars being fixed into unified fields of exchange and communication, of certain dialects being promoted because they sat closer to the print-language, and of the administrative circuits along which creole functionaries and newspaper readers learned who their peers were — mapped onto the base locale and its fallback chain, onto externalisation and string ownership, and onto the console and release channel as the pilgrimage route by which a distributed population discovers its own boundaries; the retirement of one language tag for a pluricentric language in favour of separate tags for each of its standard varieties, as with Serbo-Croatian giving way to Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian and Montenegrin, mapped onto a standards body ratifying nations that its own registry helped to constitute; and then Smith's ethnosymbolist reply — that nationalism is modern while nations are not, because an ethnie supplies a named population, myths, memories, symbols and values that the modernisers can rearrange but not conjure — rendered as the untranslatable residue sitting in a legacy corpus of idioms, proverbs and local forms, which any pipeline can standardise and promote but cannot originate.

Then give me the part I actually care about: the residue report, item by item. Which of these translations is faithful, which had to be bent or padded to fit, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I expect Anderson on simultaneity and on homogeneous, empty time to be the first casualty, since a release train is a schedule and not a shared present, but I want to know whether there is more, and in particular whether the nation's being imagined as both limited and sovereign — finite against other nations, and self-legitimating rather than ordained from above — has any counterpart whatsoever in a system whose variants are defined by inheritance from a source. Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts. Does it show that localisation engineering lacks the concepts needed to see what it is doing, that my vocabulary has no term for the experience of belonging to a community and only terms for delivering strings to it? Or does it show that Anderson and Smith are describing the same pipeline and merely disagreeing about whether the corpus was there before the tooling arrived, which would make their disagreement considerably less deep than it sounds? I would also like you to flag any place where the mapping is suspiciously smooth, since a translation that costs nothing is usually smuggling something in. Please write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with a bibliography or a reading list; I want the analysis, not the references.
