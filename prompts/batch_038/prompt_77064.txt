I'm about three years into a PhD on low-impact AI and I keep hitting a wall in the writing, so I want to sanity-check a claim before I put it in a paper.

Setup: finite deterministic MDP, agent gets some task reward R that we're allowed to have badly misspecified. My penalty term is its not a function of states or of R at all. For each step of a trajectory, I BFS out n steps from the current state, weight every state by 2^(−d) where d is its shortest-path distance, sum those up, and compare against the same sum computed from the state you'd be sitting in had the agent idled instead. Penalty is the log-ratio of the two sums, clipped at zero so that gaining reachability doesn't get rewarded. λ trades the penalty off against R.

Here's what I want to claim: this measure contains no value function, so the standard objection — you can't single out which changes are "side effects" without knowing what matters — simply doesn't reach it. I ran a little check. I had three people with radically different ethics (a total-view utilitarian, a deontologist, someone who heavily discounts the far future) each compute the penalty by hand on the same short trajectory, and they got bit-identical numbers. There's no slot left for their disagreement to live in. Whether a state is reachable is a structural fact about the transition function; you either can get there or you can't.

So: are there places where values still sneak in that I'm not seeing, or is that objection really only aimed at the the older count-the-changes measures and at AUP-style approaches that lean on auxiliary reward functions? If it's the former, where exactly does it enter? I'd like to know because the only thing I currently treat as free is λ, and I want to describe that honestly as a calibration problem rather than an ethics problem.
