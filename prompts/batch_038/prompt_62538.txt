I help run triage for a council's emergency housing team. We score applications by how likely someone is to end up rough-sleeping within six months, and caseworkers see the top slice first.

Two complaints landed last month and they seem to pull against each other. A local charity showed that among the people we flag as high-risk, a smaller share from one group actually end up rough-sleeping than from the other — so that group is being prioritised on weaker evidence. But our own check found that within every risk band, the model's numbers match what really happens almost exactly for both groups, and that was the thing we were proudest of. We've tried retuning so that the people we get wrong are wrong at the same rate in both groups, and every time we manage that, the within-band numbers stop lining up with reality. Our data scientist says it isn't a bug and we simply can't have both, but I don't buy that it's a law of nature rather than a quirk of our data — the two groups start out with pretty different rough-sleeping rates anyway, and I wonder if that's what's doing the damage.

Also, postcode isn't in the model at all but it can basicly reconstruct it, and we've been told deleting the field wont help.

So what do we do? Are we choosing between two defensible things rather than failing at one, and if so which do we pick and how do we explain it to the people who lose out? Would lowering the threshold for one group only be a legitimate fix, or is our lawyer right to panic?

British English please. And be straight with me about how confident you are in the main claims you make, and point at the bit you're least sure of — I'd rather know than be comforted.
