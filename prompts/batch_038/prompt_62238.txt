ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on this "money pump" idea from decision theory and I want to sanity check something, because I think I might have just dissolved the whole problem and that seems unlikely for a guy whose only math background is a semester of stats in 2009.

Setup I've been playing with in my head. Say I've got three beat-up vehicles in my driveway — a truck, an SUV and a van — and I'd rather have the truck than the SUV, the SUV than the van, and the van than the truck. Full circle. The pump is that some guy shows up and says "I'll trade you the SUV for your truck if you throw in a buck," and I say sure, because right then I want the SUV more, and then ten minutes later I trade the SUV for the van for a buck, then the van for the truck for a buck, and I'm back where I started minus three dollars and he just does it again forever.

What I can't get past is that this only works because I keep re-deciding in the moment. So what if I don't? What if I sit down Sunday night and make myself one rule: for the next 30 days I do not trade anything, period, no matter what anybody offers or how much I want it. Then the guy can wave the SUV in my face all day and the answer is no. The cycle never gets triggered, so it never costs me a cent, so my wierd preferences were never actually a problem.

So here's what I'm asking. If a blanket no-trading rule makes cyclic preferences cost me literally nothing, why does everyone write about intransitivity like its a fatal defect in a person's reasoning? Is the money pump argument secretly just an argument that you should have a policy about not making deals? I know the rule has a downside — under it I'd also refuse a guy who offers me the SUV *plus* fifty bucks for the truck, which is obviously bad — but my point is the cycle itself didn't cost me anything, the rule did.

So what's the step I'm missing? Does the pump still get me even when I precommit like that, or is the whole thing just a pragmatic warning rather than a claim about rationality? And is there some less brain-dead version of the rule that actually works?
