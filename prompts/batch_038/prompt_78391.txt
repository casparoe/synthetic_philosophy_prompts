Since I hung up the lab coat I've been sitting on the grants panel of a small community fund here in town — six of us, mostly volunteers, we hand out chunks of $2k to $15k a year for disability and access stuff, and then we vote. Its a tiny operation. I've also, rather to my own surprise, gotten deep into political philosophy in retirement, and I've noticed our minutes are basically a running experiment in the question of what justice is even supposed to equalize: happiness, resources, or what people can actually do and be. So I want to know whether our record settles that, because next month's application has me properly stuck.

Our verdicts so far, with the reason whoever took the minutes gave:

1. Power wheelchair for a woman with MS who was adamant she'd made her peace with it and didn't want anyone's pity. Funded. Minute: "whether she minds being unable to walk isn't the question; whether she can get to the shops is."
2. Former racer with bad arthritis asked for a bespoke lightweight road bike. We funded the adapted trike we'd normally give and refused to cover the difference. Minute: "we fund getting around, not liking it."
3. Grief counselling plus a year of bus fares for a man with no impairment whatsoever, whose wife had died and who hadn't left the house in months. Funded. Minute: "isolation disables a person as surely as anything with a diagnosis code."
4. A sign language interpreter for a Deaf applicant who told us flatly that he didn't consider himself disabled and objected to the form he'd been made to fill in. Funded. Minute: "he gets to say what he needs; we just pay for it."
5. Refused an experimental nerve treatment for chronic back pain — good anecdotes, no trial worth the name — when our standard physio package was available to him. Minute: "we are not spending six applicants' grants on one uncertain bet."
6. Refused the identical hearing aid to a comfortable retiree who simply didn't want to draw down his savings, having funded it months earlier for a young applicant with nothing. Minute: "for people who can't, not for people who'd rather not."
7. Funded a ramp and a counter conversion for a woman who uses a wheelchair and runs a bakery, though she could have rented an accessible unit for less. Minute: "her standing behind her own counter is the whole point of the thing."

The new one. A woman in her thirties lost her left hand in an accident two years ago. The functional hook we'd normally fund does everything she needs — she teaches high school, she can grip, carry, mark papers, drive. What she's asking for is the cosmetic myoelectric hand, which runs about eleven times the price and does essentially the same physical work but looks like a hand. Her letter says she's fine about how she looks, genuinely, and that she isn't asking out of vanity; but parents at her school flinch, kids stare, and she's been passed over twice for the pastoral lead role and is fairly sure why. She also says that if we turn her down she'd rather the money went to someone who needs it more.

What I'm after:

- Sort out which of the seven minutes are consistent with which candidate rule. I can see at least four that look like they fit everything: equalize what people can do and be; equalize resources but don't pay for tastes; do whatever the applicant says they need; and don't let anyone be pushed into an inferior position. My worry is that all four fit all seven, in which case our record is worthless as evidence and I should say so at the meeting. Tell me whether that's right, or whether some precedent quietly kills one of them — I have a suspicion about number 2 and another about number 3, but I want the argument rather than my hunch.
- Then predict the vote on the new case, rule by rule, and tell me which way you'd bet and why. I particularly want to know what her closing line does to each rule: three of them seem to be moved by it and one of them seems to think it's beside the point, and I can't tell which is which.
- Which of the seven is the closest analog, and exactly where does the analogy snap?
- If the record genuinely doesn't decide it, spell out what principle we'd be committing ourselves to by voting yes and what by voting no, in language a room full of tired volunteers could actually argue about — and tell me which of those two commitments would be harder to walk back from later, given that we're a small fund and everyone in town knows our decisions.
- Finally, design me one more hypothetical application that would seperate the surviving rules cleanly, something I could put to the panel in the abstract before we vote, so we find out which rule we're actually running instead of discovering it by accident.

I'm not after references or a summary of what Sen or Dworkin or anyone else wrote — I can read them myself, and I have. I want the reasoning worked through on our own minutes. A tight answer with the structure of the argument visible beats a long one that hedges.
