Right, I've been on the jury for a national documentary prize for four years running now — nine of us — and I've been trying to write down what I actually believe about how we decide, partly because last year went strange and I still can't place why. Here's the list. I want to know whether it hangs together.

1. We vote the three criteria separately, simple majority on each, and the award then just falls out: whichever film meets all three gets it. We never hold a vote on the award itself. I think that's right and I'd fight to keep it.

2. The point of doing it that way is accountability. We publish our reasoning, and I think reasons only bind us if they're actually held by the panel — a written justification that no individual juror would sign is decoration.

3. But I also think a decision is legitimate only if most of us would endorse it all-things-considered. A verdict not one juror actually holds can't honestly be called ours.

4. Ballots are secret, and I've always taken it that secrecy plus the fact that each question is a plain yes/no rules out horse-trading. There's nothing to trade: you either think a film is original or you don't. That sort of thing is a problem for legislatures, not for us.

5. We deliberate openly for two days before voting and I think jurors should move in that room. Arriving settled and never budging is doing the job badly.

6. The criteria get fixed about eighteen months before the shortlist goes out and I'd resist touching them mid-round — though if a round shows a criterion its not meaning what we took it to mean, we should rewrite it for the following year.

7. Three of the nine rotate off each year, and I think we're bound by what earlier juries published: if last year's jury held that a film of a certain kind met the significance criterion, this year's can't hold the opposite about a like film without explaining itself. Consistency across years is part of being answerable too.

What set this off: last year originality carried 7–2, craft 6–3 and significance 5–4, and the prize went to a film that, asked one by one, not a single juror would have picked. Four of us still can't explain it to each other. We've half-settled on calling it a fluke.

So — which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real ones by severity rather than giving me a flat list, say what would have to go in each case and what that costs us, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Don't read anything into how fondly I've worded each one; I've no idea which I'd drop.

One-sentence bottom line first, then the audit, and keep the whole thing under 400 words.
