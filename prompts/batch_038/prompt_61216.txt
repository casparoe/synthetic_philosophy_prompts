I'm in a Stoicism club at my school and we're split down the middle. Half of us use the modern trichotomy version — things fully up to you, partly up to you, not up to you at all — where you handle the middle category by internalizing the goal, like turning "hit the target" into "shoot well." The other half say that's a cop-out and you should just stick with the strict two-way split from Epictetus. I want a thought experiment that comes out in the strict-dichotomy direction, but a fair one, not rigged.

So: build me a case where a competent trichotomy follower ends up with worse advice than someone running the strict dichotomy — worse for the agent's own actual aim, not just less tranquil — and both of them agree on every fact of the situation. Constraints: one paragraph, ordinary realistic setting (driving test, team tryouts, college app, a shift at a part-time job, whatever), no sci-fi, no psychic coaches or mind readers. The trichotomy person cant be careless or badly informed: the bad result has to fall out of the internalizing move itself, applied exactly how a good Irvine-style Stoic would apply it. And the strict person can't win by being checked out or not caring — they have to be at least as invested as the other one.

After the scenario, walk me through what each version tells the agent to do and why, name the one feature of the case that does the work, say what happens to the verdicts if you delete that feature, and then give the best comeback a trichotomy defender could make. If the case only works because I basically stipulated who loses, that counts as a failure — the losing advice has to be generated by the theory, not by the setup.
