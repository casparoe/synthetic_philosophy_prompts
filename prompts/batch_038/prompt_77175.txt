You are to act as a philosopher of technology who also has a genuine working familiarity with the technical and conceptual literature on AI corrigibility — someone comfortable moving between engineering practice and the analysis of agency, control, and responsibility. Please treat what follows as a request for analysis rather than for a survey, and think it through carefully before answering. In fact, begin by brainstorming a list of candidate theories that could license the practice I describe, including at least two you will go on to reject and one you suspect is right for the wrong reasons; only then commit to a reconstruction.

I cover science and engineering for a general-interest magazine and I am working on a piece about off-switches. Two field notes frame the puzzle.

First note. The dead man's switch on a locomotive, and the operator-presence bar on a walk-behind mower, work the same way: the machine runs only while a human is continuously, physically affirming that it should. Let go — because you have decided to stop, because you have fainted, because you have been killed — and the system applies the brakes or stops the blade. The default state is off, and running is a condition that must be renewed moment by moment.

Second note. On newer equipment the requirement is generally no longer a steady hold. The system asks for an acknowledgment at irregular intervals, and in some designs the acknowledgment must vary rather than be a single repeated gesture. The rationale given to me is that a fixed requirement gets metronomed: the operator stops deciding anything and starts answering. So the device no longer merely checks whether the human is present; it checks whether the human is still paying attention, and it does so by asking questions the human cannot pre-rehearse.

Third, and this is what snagged me: these devices are defeated constantly, with wedges, tape, weights on pedals, and zip ties. In almost every case I could trace, the defeat was by the operator, not against the operator. Nobody's machine fought back.

The contemporary discussion of building an off-switch into a sufficiently capable AI system borrows this vocabulary wholesale, and treats the central problem as the possibility that the system will find it instrumentally useful to avoid or disable the switch. But when I compare that framing to the practice it is borrowed from, three things fail to line up. Aviation, which is at least as safety-critical as rail, does not require continuous human affirmation of the flight at all. Railway practice was designed around a single operator alone in a cab with no one to consult and no one to bill, whereas a deployed system has many operators, an owner, and a party who loses revenue every time the switch is thrown. And the railway practice assumes that stopping is roughly free — an abrupt halt on track is an inconvenience, not a catastrophe — which is precisely what cannot be assumed of a system running something continuous.

So, in order:

1. What theory of agency, control, and responsibility does the dead man's switch presuppose? Be specific about what it takes the human to be doing while holding it: authorizing, witnessing, or merely not being dead.
2. What does the randomized vigilance variant presuppose, and is it consistent with the first? It looks to me as though the older device treats the human as the author of each moment of operation, while the newer one treats the human as a component to be interrogated — a distrust of the controller that the older design simply does not contain. If they are inconsistent, say which one the AI off-switch rhetoric is actually inheriting, since it uses the vocabulary of the first to describe something closer to the second.
3. Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? I have in mind, among others, the view that safety is a property of a system's design and can be settled once rather than renewed, and the view that the human's role is to intervene exceptionally rather than to sustain continuously.
4. Does the notion of corrigibility presuppose that the difficulty of throwing the switch lies in the machine's dispositions rather than in the presser's incentives? My field notes suggest the opposite: the historical failure mode is accommodation, not resistance. Work out what follows for the concept if the switch's real function is partly to reassure the person holding it, so that they keep engaging rather than walking away.
5. Where is this practice philosophically underdetermined? Tell me plainly which of my questions several incompatible theories would answer identically, so that the mechanism itself decides nothing.
6. What would have to change — in the design, in the paperwork, in who bears the cost of a halt, in who counts as the operator — if we replaced the presupposed view with one on which the operative fact is that pressing is expensive for the person who presses?

Throughout, please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and flag the difference explicitly. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want your reasoning, and I want you to argue rather than to catalog. Finally, tell me if my framing is wrong — if one of these field notes is doing more work than I realize, or if the aviation contrast does not mean what I assume it means.
