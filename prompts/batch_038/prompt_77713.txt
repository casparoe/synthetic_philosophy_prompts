**Nobody ever told you not to be rich**

The standard comparison pairs two temperaments: the American Dream, in which the successful are celebrated, and Jantelagen or the tall poppy syndrome, in which they get cut down. The comparison assumes the variable is an attitude to inequality — does this culture want the top to be high? I think that's the wrong variable. Both cultures police success pretty hard. What differs is who is permitted to *say*, in the first person, that they have done well.

Start with the actual text. Sandemose's Jante is a fictional small **Danish** town, modelled on his own hometown, even though the phrase now gets waved around as Swedish and Norwegian (I keep slipping between *janteloven* and *jantelagen* myself, which is sort of the point). It comes as ten rules, and the first, in the usual English rendering, is "You're not to think you are anything special." Now read down the list. Not one of them forbids wealth, property, rank, or high earnings. Every one of them forbids a first-person comparative belief: don't think you're as good as we are, don't think you know more than we do, don't think you can teach us anything. It's a grammar of self-reference, not a distributive principle. And the eleventh rule — the one Sandemose calls the penal code of Jante — isn't an injunction at all, it's a question: "Perhaps you don't think we know a few things about you?" That's an audit, not a tax.

Sandemose also didn't claim to have invented the rules; he said he was writing down, satirically, norms that had been around for centuries. That matters, because the phrase has since been conscripted as an explanation of Nordic politics: Scandinavians redistribute, the story goes, because they resent the top. But there is nothing in the code about redistribution, nothing about the shape of a distribution, nothing about money. Using it to explain a tax system is like explaining a speed limit by appealing to table manners.

The tall poppy has a different structure again, and its own history undercuts the envy reading even harder. The image is Livy's: Tarquin, asked by his son what he should do now that the son has made himself all-powerful in Gabii, says nothing, walks into his garden, and knocks the heads off the tallest poppies with a stick. The son gets the message and kills the eminent men of the city. The cutter is the tyrant. This is not the resentment of the crowd toward the successful — it's the successful removing rivals. (The earlier Greek versions, in Aristotle and Herodotus, use a grain field instead and reverse who does the cutting, which tells you how little the details mattered.) The phrase only became the name of a national temperament in Australia and New Zealand in the 1980s, and somewhere in that move the agency quietly flipped: from what the powerful do to the eminent, to what the mediocre do to the successful.

So I want two axes instead of one. Axis 1: is unequal success *legitimate* — may you have it? Axis 2: may you *name it yourself* — may you say "I did this"? The usual contrast collapses them into a single score. My claim is that American culture is permissive on both, which is why self-promotion there is a virtue rather than a tolerated vice, and that Nordic culture is fairly permissive on axis 1 and strict on axis 2: Swedes and Norwegians are glad about companies and athletes who win, so long as the winning is narrated by someone else, in the third person, or by the collective. An actor who takes home an Emmy and then can't bring himself to mention it is not evidence that Swedes want him to lose. The US has sanctions too — but they mostly land on the parvenu, on the person who "acts like they're better than you," which is an axis-2 offence wearing an axis-1 costume.

Once you separate the axes, Jantelagen stops being a theory of equality and becomes a rule about *attribution*: who is licensed to assign credit. And that gives you a testable prediction — the same culture should be perfectly comfortable with enormous prizes and salaries, provided the announcement comes from an institution, and absolutely ferocious about the person who says "I earned this" out loud.
