A colleague and a graduate student in my department argued after a reading group on the Allais paradox, and neither would let the other have the last word. Both were working from the standard setup: in the first choice most people take 1A, the certain $1 million, over 1B, which pays $5 million with probability .10, $1 million with probability .89, and nothing with probability .01; in the second most people take 2B, $5 million with probability .10 and nothing with probability .90, over 2A, $1 million with probability .11 and nothing with probability .89. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

(1) The student said the pattern refutes the independence axiom as a normative principle and not merely as a description of behavior. The axiom is justified by the thought that the .89 common consequence should cancel out of the comparison, but cancellation is precisely what is at issue: what makes 1A attractive is the elimination of a one-in-a-hundred chance of going home empty-handed, and that is a real feature of the prospect rather than a mistake about it.

(2) The colleague replied that this proves too much. Considered singly, neither choice is objectionable; what is objectionable is holding both, since together they breach a constraint the student invokes without complaint everywhere else. He added that when subjects are shown the two problems side by side and their own pattern is pointed out to them, a good number revise, and that people who make a choice and then disown it on reflection have given evidence of an error, not of a discovered truth.

(3) The student said the revision data cut no ice: embarrassment at an apparent inconsistency shows only that people want to look consistent. Coherence constraints, he said, tell you that your attitudes must hang together but not which coherent set to have, and in this case the charge of incoherence smuggles the axiom back in, because the inconsistency alleged is inconsistency with the very principle under attack. He noted as well that 1B has the higher expected payoff, so the preference for 1A cannot be written off as ordinary prudence about money either.

(4) The colleague then pressed the money pump. An agent with that pair of preferences, confronted with a suitable sequence of offers, can be walked around a cycle and end up paying for nothing, strictly worse off at the end. That, he said, is not a fastidiousness about axioms but money leaving the agent's pocket, and it shows the pattern to be normatively defective on grounds that never mention independence.

(5) The student answered that the pump catches only a myopic chooser who takes each problem in isolation as it arrives. A sophisticated agent who foresees the sequence can adopt a plan at the outset and hold to it, at worst paying a small premium for the commitment; that premium is the price of myopia, not the price of the preference. If the pattern is condemned even in such a planner, he said, the charge is no longer about what anyone prefers but about a demand that preferences be nothing over and above dispositions to choose.

(6) The colleague's closing move: a planner who must purchase protection against his own later choices has already conceded the point, because the premium measures a cost that the alternative preference set would not have incurred, and a settled plan to keep paying it is simply a revealed preference for that other set.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify the rating rather than restating the move. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what each of them is overlooking — in particular, whether either has established anything about the axiom as such, as distinct from something about the choice pattern it forbids.
