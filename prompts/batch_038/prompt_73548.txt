I have no formal training in philosophy — I left school at sixteen and have done my reading on my own, mostly from library books and long podcasts — so please treat what follows as an honest attempt rather than an exam answer.

Some months ago I came across Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain example from Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974): a society begins with some distribution of money that everyone agrees is just, a star basketball player arranges to receive a small extra payment from every fan who chooses to buy a ticket to watch him play, and by the end of the season the distribution no longer has the shape it started with. Nozick's charge, as I understand it, is that any principle of justice that looks at the shape of a distribution rather than at how it came about must keep interfering with people's ordinary voluntary dealings, because those dealings constantly break the shape.

Two conclusions I drew from this now seem to be in tension with one another, and I cannot see my way out of them.

First, because the argument grants at the outset that the starting distribution is just, it looks to me as though it can only ever establish something about the made-up society in the story. In the actual world nobody can show that the holdings people began with were justly acquired — there is conquest, enclosure, slavery and inheritance behind most of what exists — so the example appears to leave the real distribution of wealth completely untouched. It seems to me to be a story rather than an argument.

Second, though, if justice really does depend on how each holding came about, then Nozick's own view looks like the one that requires the most continuous interference of all. Someone would have to keep a record of every acquisition, every transfer, every gift and every inheritance, indefinitely, and audit each against its history; the patterned theorist, by contrast, only ever has to compare snapshots. On that reading his objection to his opponents turns around and applies with greater force to him.

So my question is this: given that a historical theory of justice seems to demand more constant supervision than a patterned one, and given that no historical theory can get off the ground without a just starting point that nobody can supply, what is the Chamberlain example supposed to have shown in the first place?
