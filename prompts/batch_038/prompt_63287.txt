I am a second-year philosophy doctoral student, and for the past two terms I have been co-convening a fortnightly reading group that is roughly half philosophers and half people from the statistics department. We have just spent four sessions on the two-envelope paradox, and I have been asked to circulate a one-page note stating where I have personally got to, so that the group can spend the next meeting attacking it. Before I send it, I would like a careful audit of whether the package hangs together, because I suspect it does not and I cannot say where it fails. My commitments, as they would appear on the handout, are these. (1) The switching argument — the calculation which, taking the amount in my chosen envelope to be A, assigns the other envelope an expected value of 1.25A — is invalid, and the invalidity is a straightforward equivocation: the A that the argument treats as a determinate sum and the A over which it averages are not the same quantity, and once that is spelled out there is nothing left to explain. (2) A resolution worth having must be distribution-free: it may not appeal to any assumption about how the organiser selected the two amounts, partly because the puzzle is standardly posed without one, and partly because a diagnosis that works only for priors nobody would actually hold seems to me to change the subject. (3) I am an ordinary expected-value maximiser; I have no appetite for exotic repairs such as risk-weighted utilities or non-standard credences over infinities. (4) Before opening my envelope I am genuinely indifferent between the two, and I regard that indifference as correct rather than as a failure of nerve. (5) Having opened it, my credence that the other envelope holds the larger sum should be exactly one half, whatever figure I find: I knew all along that one envelope held twice the other, and the number on the note adds nothing I did not already have. (6) I accept as a mathematical aside that no proper distribution over the possible amounts makes the one-half/one-half assumption hold uniformly across every figure one might observe; but I do not think this touches me, because my credences in (5) are not derived from any distribution over the organiser's choices at all — I take them directly, and I do not see that a rational credence has to be recoverable from some prior in order to be rational. (7) Free information cannot make a rational expected-value maximiser worse off in expectation.

Two further things belong in the audit. First, a friend in the group tells me that (4) and (6) cannot both stand: if, under any proper prior, some observed amounts make switching advantageous, then my ex ante indifference must be a mirage, since I should already have been leaning towards the policy of looking and then switching. I think he is wrong, but I cannot say why, and I should like to know whether the objection has any force at all. Second, I should flag my own contribution, since I want to know whether it earns its place on the handout. Frustrated by (6), I proposed what I called the ceiling variant: the organiser tells you only that the smaller amount is a whole number of pounds, that it lies below some enormous figure, and that you will never be told what that figure is; you then open your envelope and find, say, £7. My thought was that this rescues (5) without abandoning (2), because the bound is known to exist but not known in value, so no prior is smuggled in and yet the mathematics invoked in (6) no longer bites against me. One of the statisticians dismissed this as merely relocating the prior into the ceiling, which struck me as uncharitable, but I could not answer him, and the group has since taken to treating the variant as settled against me.

So please go through the list and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be so, ranking the genuine ones by severity rather than presenting them all as equally damaging. For each, say precisely what would have to give, and what the cheapest revision is that preserves the most of the remainder; then tell me which single revision would clear the largest number of problems at once. Address the ceiling variant explicitly — whether it does what I claim for it, or whether it hides the difficulty rather than dissolving it — and say what you make of the statistician's charge. Throughout, I would ask you to keep a clear line between what is settled and what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this. I have noticed that the textbook diagnosis tends to be presented as though the matter were closed, when I suspect that several of my questions are live: whether the equivocation diagnosis counts as a complete resolution or only as a gesture at one, whether an improper or scale-invariant credence assignment can ever be licensed, what is required for a rational credence to be representable by a prior, and how the variants with unbounded expectations should be handled. Where you are marking a contested point, say that it is contested and indicate roughly which way you lean, rather than writing as though the profession had decided; where you are confident, be confident. Please write the answer in British English.
