Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about whether hypocrisy can cost a person the standing to blame.

One maintains that standing is a real normative relation and not a matter of style: when someone condemns a fault in another while quietly exempting himself from the norm he invokes, he has not merely been ineffective or ill-mannered. He has done something to the moral relationship between the two of them, and his blame is unfitting even when every proposition in it is true.

The other maintains that standing is a philosopher's invention. The tu quoque has always been a fallacy; the truth of a charge is untouched by the record of the person bringing it; and whatever survives of the intuition that the hypocrite may not blame will reduce, on inspection, to ordinary facts about his persuasiveness, his value as evidence about the fault in question, or his manners.

Both should be at the top of their form, and each should be made to press the other with the same two cases.

First: a man who, following a brain injury, has become incapable of the vice he now denounces most eloquently. He cannot be tempted by it, and he has never blamed it unevenly in anyone. Does he have standing to blame it — or has the injury destroyed something that blame requires, and if so, what exactly?

Second: a woman who has never committed the fault she condemns and who condemns it evenly in friend and stranger alike, but only because she has never been in a position to get away with it. Given a chance at no cost to herself, she would take it gladly, and she knows this about herself. Nothing in her behaviour, or in her dispositions toward other people, distinguishes her from the saint. Does she lack standing?

The two should engage each other's actual moves rather than recite parallel monologues; give each at least three substantive turns. Let the exchange end where it honestly ends — a real concession, a sharpened disagreement, a stalemate — and not in a tidy synthesis that neither position licenses.

Afterward, step out of character and tell me which of the two you think is better placed to handle both cases, and how much weight that verdict should carry: is it something you would defend against a good objection, or a close call you happen to be breaking one way? Note also any place where you had to supply a detail about the cases or about either position that my framing leaves open, since I want to know how much of the result is yours rather than the setup's. Finally, if you think the request is built on a false premise — that the two cases do not really pull against each other, that one of the positions cannot be stated without collapsing into the other, or that a dialogue is the wrong instrument for this question — say so plainly and explain, rather than producing a polished exchange that quietly concedes the point.
