A marine institute runs a heat-tolerance breeding programme on a reef coral. Two things are stipulated. First, the coral's algal symbionts are taken up fresh from the water column every generation — nothing about the symbiont community is passed from parent holobiont to offspring, only the host genome is. Second, the institute propagates a holobiont only if the whole community's heat tolerance clears a fixed threshold, and it's indifferent about which strain does the work; any host that misses the bar is culled together with whatever community it happens to be carrying, including strains that look tolerant on their own but are useless in situ.

The question: is there genuine selection at the level of the holobiont in this set-up, or only the appearance of it — an artefact of the breeder's threshold rule doing the sorting? Take both sides seriously (including whether it should make any difference that the sorting is done by a human with a target rather than by the environment), then commit to a verdict and defend it. Please don't just march me through the standard labels — replicator/interactor, MLS1 vs MLS2, hologenome — without actually applying them to the case, since that's exactly what got my last one a C. About 1500 words, and in British English please.
