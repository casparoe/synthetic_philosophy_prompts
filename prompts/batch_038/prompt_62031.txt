I run an upper-level metaphysics seminar on powers and dispositions — 14 students, one 100-minute block. They've had the simple conditional analysis in lecture (call something fragile iff it would shatter if struck, soluble iff it would dissolve if immersed, and so on) and most of them are privately convinced the standard counterexamples are verbal quibbles dressed up as philosophy. I want an in-class game that costs them that conviction, and I want them to lose it in play rather than in a lecture.

The conceal I have in mind — steal it, change it, throw it out:

Teams of 3-4. One team is the **analysts**: they must write down an explicit sorting rule for whether a mystery object has some disposition, using nothing but if-then clauses about what the object would do when tested. The other team is the **workshop**: they get a closed toolbox of imaginary devices and a few minutes to construct an object that either (a) really does have the disposition but the analysts' rule sorts as lacking it, or (b) really lacks it but gets sorted as having it. The toolbox is the load-bearing part, because I want the counterexamples to get *built* rather than waved at, so seed it with roughly the right resources: a field that switches an object's inner structure off the instant anything touches it, a field that switches it on, some packing that stops the stimulus ever arriving, something that neutralises the effect after the stimulus has landed, and a couple of plain-jane tools so the good stuff doesn't stand out. You can add or swap items, but every item should correspond to a real move in the literature and you should tell me which.

Scoring should reward the workshop for counterexamples that are judged valid by some procedure you design (this is the part I'm most worried about — I don't want it degenerating into an argument about whether their object is "really" fragile, so give me a way to settle that quickly and a way to stop teams lawyering the rules). Also: I want a mechanic where the whole class records its gut verdict on each mystery object by secret ballot *before* anyone applies the analysts' rule to it, so the analysts can't quietly retrofit. Points, no real money needed, maybe some symbolic prize.

Two full rounds minimum, so the analysts patch their rule and get broken again. What I'm hoping — tell me if it won't happen — is that their final patch converges on something like Lewis's move: keep the object's own intrinsic makeup fixed while you imagine the stimulus happening. That's a great place to land, because the debrief can then ask what that clause is really doing, and whether they've analysed the disposition or just banned the workshop's best tools. I'd also like the game to end up reproducing the fact that this kind of patch handles the switch-off device but *still* gets beaten by the packing device — that asymmetry is one of the things I most want them to walk out with, and if the ruleset can't produce it naturally, say so.

They shouldn't hear the words fink, mask, or antidote until the debrief. In the debrief I want the map from each toolbox item to its name and to which objection it is, the question of what the analysts' final clause bought them, and an explicit moment where you make them notice that they could have skipped the whole exercise by just looking at what the object is made of — I expect at least one team to reach for microstructure and I want to know whether to allow it, forbid it, or price it as a purchasable card, since appealing to composition is obviously a different analysis and that's a conversation worth having rather than suppressing.

Deliverables: full ruleset, materials list (I have index cards, a whiteboard, and a printer), scoring, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 100 minutes including the debrief, what to do with 13 or 15 students, the debrief script or at least its beats, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover on the fly, and — this matters most — a straight answer on which feature of the real problem the game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in words. My guess is that in the game the students are always allowed to peek inside the object, whereas the actual question is whether there's anything in there to peek at. Tell me if that's the right worry or if there's a worse one I'm not seeing.
