I am preparing a reading-group session on moral desert in which karma will be treated not as an article of faith but as a candidate theory, competing with retributivist and consequentialist accounts of what it is for an outcome to fit an action. Before we begin comparing theories, I want the specification: what any adequate karmic theory of moral desert must be able to do, where adequacy means being good enough for the job of explaining why outcomes can fit actions without anyone making them fit.

Write the desiderata. For each, give (a) the requirement, stated precisely rather than as a slogan; (b) the case or consideration behind it — not an argument for it, but the datum that makes a theory look inadequate if it cannot handle it; and (c) your verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for anything deserving the name, or whether it is a prejudice smuggled in from a neighbouring camp. Where you think an item on my list below is not a genuine requirement at all, say so and say why.

Cover at least the following ground, and add what I have missed:

- The cases. An agent who prospers through cruelty across a whole life and dies comfortably. An infant born into suffering it cannot be said to have earned. The sincere agent whose well-intentioned act produces disaster. The wrongdoer who has since been thoroughly transformed, so that the person who would receive the result is not recognisably the person who acted. A community whose present prosperity rests on the injustices of its ancestors, and whose current members personally committed none of them.

- The distinctions a theory may not collapse. Desert versus mere causal consequence. The fittingness of an outcome versus its instrumental or educative value. Attributability versus accountability. The moral quality of an act versus that of the agent. Backward-looking desert versus forward-looking reform.

- The structural questions. Whether the mechanism can be purely natural, with no apportioner, or whether proportionality between the quality of an act and the quality of its result requires something to do the proportioning. Whether the one who suffers or benefits must be numerically the same agent as the one who acted, and what a theory that denies a persisting self owes here. Whether the series of acts can have a first member, and what follows if it cannot. Whether desert can be pooled or transferred at the level of households, lineages, or populations. Whether a theory that denies the agent any memory of the deed can still call the result a payment rather than an accident.

- The data it must not contradict. In particular, the observed weakness of any correlation between an agent's moral record and their worldly fortune, and the fact that people reach judgments of deserved misfortune quickly, spontaneously, and about total strangers.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of these requirements that you believe is jointly unsatisfiable — a no-go result for karmic theories — and say which requirement you would abandon to escape it, and what the abandonment costs. Tell me also which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and whether the demand for proportionality is doing the work of a theory of desert at all or is a retributivist reflex that a karmic theory would do better to shed. If you think my framing already stacks the list against one kind of answer, say that too.

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases. A single closing paragraph scoring the main contenders — a purely this-worldly naturalist karma, an administered karma with a divine apportioner, a multi-life karma, and a deflationary reading on which karma is not a theory of desert but a discipline of attention — is welcome. No references, no bibliography.
