I run a graduate reading group in the philosophy of medicine, and this term we are working through the naturalist/normativist debate about disease. My problem as a convener is that every illustration I reach for is one my students have already met a dozen times, and the familiarity lets them nod along without doing any real work; so I would like a case built to order rather than borrowed. The specification is as follows. The deliverable is a single scenario, one paragraph of prose, set entirely in the present-day world — no genetic engineering, no future societies, no exotic devices. It concerns two individuals who are biologically identical with respect to one trait: same age, same sex, same trait, same severity, and the trait must be a genuine failure of an evolutionarily designed function as well as a genuine reduction in functional efficiency below what is typical for their age-and-sex reference class. The two differ only in the social and material circumstances in which the trait is expressed, and the difference must be such that the trait severely impairs one of their lives while being close to inconsequential for the other. The trait may not be one of the standard exhibits of this literature — no infertility, no male pattern baldness, no homosexuality, no depression, no short stature, no lactase non-persistence, no sickle-cell or G6PD variants — and it must be painless and outwardly invisible, so that no character in the story holds a belief about being diseased that could do any of the explanatory work. Describe the biology qualitatively but concretely enough that a clinician would recognize the picture; do not invent prevalence figures and do not cite studies.

The scenario must be engineered so that Boorse's biostatistical theory returns the same verdict for both individuals — disease in each case — while Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis returns opposite verdicts — a disorder for the one whose life is impaired, no disorder for the other — and that split has to fall out of the theories' own clauses rather than out of anything stipulated in the story. After the scenario, do five things: (i) walk through each theory clause by clause for each individual, showing exactly where each verdict comes from; (ii) identify the feature of the case that produces the divergence, which I expect to concern how each theory indexes its key term, and say what happens if that indexing is altered — if Boorse's functions were relativized to the actual rather than the species-typical environment, or if Wakefield's harm clause were relativized to a standard social setting — and whether the divergence survives either change; (iii) note any respect in which the two individuals are not in fact perfectly matched, and assess whether that residual difference threatens the case; (iv) give the strongest reply available to a defender of Wakefield, including whether such a defender could press for a more locally drawn reference class, or deny that the harm clause is as socially plastic as the case assumes; and (v) state plainly what the case does and does not establish about naturalism versus normativism — in particular, whether a divergence between Boorse and Wakefield counts as evidence for the normativist thesis at all, given that the latter account is a hybrid, and what a normativist who rejects any dysfunction clause and defines health as the ability to pursue one's vital goals would have to say about this pair.

The purpose of the case is pedagogical, and I would much rather it be honest than dramatic: if any part of the specification turns out to be unsatisfiable, or if the divergence can be produced only by cheating — for instance by describing one individual as worse off in a way that Boorse's own clauses would independently register — say so at the point where the difficulty arises rather than papering over it. No citations and no bibliography; I will supply references myself. Begin your reply with the scenario itself, with no preface, no restatement of the request, and no commentary until the analysis starts.
