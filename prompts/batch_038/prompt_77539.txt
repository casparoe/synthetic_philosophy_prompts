I'm a senior software engineer, and for about two years I've been running an informal discussion group at my company: twelve or so people, nearly all engineers, none of us with any philosophy training. Next month we're doing the Watchmen graphic novel. Most have seen the 2009 film; roughly half have read the book. I want to steer us away from the usual framing — is Veidt a utilitarian, does the end justify the means — which we have all heard many times, and toward a more specific piece of contemporary philosophy that I suspect the book is actually about.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on the governance of advanced technology and who also writes serious literary criticism: someone equally at home in the AI-risk literature and in the craft of reading a text closely. Think carefully before answering. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you sketch two or three rival readings of the book under this lens, say which one you intend to defend and why, and note what would count as evidence against it. Only then develop the reading.

Here is the lens, stated as plainly as I can so that you need not guess what I mean. A "singleton," in this literature, is not the design pattern I know from work; it is a single decision-making authority at the top level of society, one agent or institution that nothing else can override. The associated hypothesis is roughly this: sufficiently advanced technology may make such an authority feasible and even likely, because whoever controls that technology may acquire a decisive strategic advantage over everyone else, and the resulting arrangement could be either very good or very bad. The case for it is coordination — a single authority can halt arms races, prevent catastrophic uses of a technology, and supply global public goods that competing states systematically fail to supply. The case against it is that there is no exit and no external check: risk of capture, risk that the authority's values were fixed once early and can never be revised, and the loss of the discovery and error-correction that come from many independent polities experimenting and being able to fail separately. The failure mode people worry about most is lock-in, a state of affairs that is very hard to leave once entered. My own professional idiom for it is a single point of failure.

My question is whether Watchmen dramatizes this, and how well. Specifically:

1. Is Veidt's act a singleton at all? He never takes office, never claims jurisdiction, never administers anything; he engineers a one-time catastrophe and then relies on secrecy to keep the peace. Tell me whether the book depicts an installed authority or a permanent emergency, and whether that difference matters for the arguments sketched above.

2. Test, rather than accept, a hypothesis of mine: that the book contains two singleton candidates — Doctor Manhattan, who has the capacity but no will to rule, and Veidt, who has the will but only borrowed capacity — so that the plot resolves by their alliance in the Antarctic chapters, including Manhattan's decision to destroy Rorschach rather than let the disclosure happen. If that is right, what is the book claiming about the need for a would-be singleton to have both power and purpose? If it distorts the text, say so plainly.

3. Veidt's plan runs on private laboratories, private capital, and private media, with no electoral legitimacy anywhere in it. Does the book suggest that such an authority arrives through private capability rather than through treaties and institution-building, and is that suggestion philosophically defensible?

4. Read the unresolved epilogue — Rorschach's written account sitting in a newspaper office, its publication left open, alongside Veidt's final plea for confirmation that he did the right thing and Manhattan's refusal to grant it — as the book's verdict on lock-in. Does the book think a peace maintained by controlling what people know can be stable, and does it think stability is the right measure?

5. Say where the reading breaks. Which scenes and characterizations resist this lens — Rorschach's intransigence, the Comedian's nihilism, Laurie's arc — and does the framing illuminate them or flatten them? I would rather be told the lens is a poor fit in places than be handed a tidy argument.

6. Tell me whether my engineering vocabulary is a legitimate bridge to this literature or a smuggling operation: quorums, consensus, Byzantine fault tolerance, redundancy. I suspect it quietly assumes faults are random rather than motivated, and that nodes are interchangeable.

7. Briefly, and only briefly: the film substitutes a different mechanism for the atrocity and relocates the moral burden. Does that change the philosophical claim, or only the staging?
