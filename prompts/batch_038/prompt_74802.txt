An upper-division philosophy of history seminar of mine meets once a week for 75 minutes; next week I have sixteen students and no lecture prepared. I want an activity that walks them straight into the problem of presentism in historiography without the word being spoken until the last ten minutes.

The engine should be a case you invent for me, so that nothing needs looking up: a county historical society's exhibit committee drafting the ninety-word wall label for its centerpiece display on a nineteenth-century physician who founded the town's first hospital. Build the dossier so that four distinct pressures are simultaneously live — the deed he was publicly honored for in his own lifetime reads as monstrous now; some of his contemporaries said so at the time, in print; he justified the deed by invoking a principle he conspicuously ignored elsewhere in his own career; and nearly every surviving document was written by the institution he founded. Give me the dossier itself, a few hundred words of dated documents and excerpts, plus the surrounding activity.

The mechanic I want is a constraint students will not notice until it bites. Four subcommittees are each handed a rule for whose standards govern the evaluation — contemporaneous official opinion, contemporaneous dissent, the subject's own professed principles, and present-day judgment — and each must apply its rule first and defend it only afterward, with the full group comparing four finished labels at the end. The structure should force them to discover that adopting the anti-presentist rule is itself a choice made from where they stand, and that no rule yields a label they would be willing to put their own names under. Give the final vote some weight: the committee has to forward one label, with signatories.

Then supply a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps particular moments onto the distinction between presentism as anachronistic error and presentism as an inescapable condition of asking historical questions at all, the two or three things that will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to steer them, and a frank account of which feature of the real historiographical predicament a staged committee simply cannot reproduce. Please write in American English.
