You are a philosopher of psychiatry with two decades of work at the intersection of nosology, the metaphysics of science, and clinical epidemiology. You have been retained as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration. Two parties who have spent years talking past each other have agreed to sign a single joint statement. Your task is to produce that statement. You are not to referee the dispute, not to stage a dialogue, and not to announce a winner; the deliverable is a constructive document both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.

**The parties.**

Party A — the nosological realist. Holds that diagnostic categories are, at least aspirationally, attempts to identify structure that would obtain whether or not anyone named it: clusters of symptoms, impairments, and courses that hang together because of shared underlying mechanisms. On this view, the fact that people learn how they are classified and change accordingly is a hazard in the data-generating process — a contamination to be controlled by the usual instruments: assessment by clinicians blinded to label and to site, testing whether a measure means the same thing at different times and places, and building categories from the bottom up out of behaviour and physiology rather than from the top down out of symptom checklists.

Party B — the historical ontologist in Hacking's lineage. Holds that classifications of persons are interactive rather than indifferent: the classified come to know the description applied to them and can inhabit it, elaborate it, exploit it, or refuse it, so that classifying and classified co-evolve. On this view the instability Party A hopes to engineer away is not noise in the measurement but a property of the kind itself; a class of people does not become stationary by being measured more carefully, and demanding that a human kind be fixed the way a chemical kind is fixed may already be the mistake.

Treat both positions as sophisticated and held in good faith. Do not let Party B collapse into the slogan that everything is socially constructed, and do not let Party A collapse into the slogan that biology will eventually settle it.

**The stipulated case.** Treat the following as fact for the purposes of the document; it is invented, and you should reason from its details rather than from real-world analogues.

A national health service is considering a new category, provisionally called "feed-checking dysregulation," whose criteria are almost entirely self-report: number of compulsive daily checks of a notification stream, reported distress at device removal, and a stated inability to stop despite stated intention to stop. The category would be published simultaneously in the clinician manual and in a public patient-education campaign that includes a free self-screening instrument reproducing the criteria nearly word for word, linked from the health service homepage.

Two otherwise matched jurisdictions are rolling this out five years apart. Jurisdiction A adopts the category immediately, with the public screening instrument live from day one. Jurisdiction B defers, continuing to manage the same presentations under existing impulse-control and anxiety diagnoses — but the screening instrument circulates freely in B anyway, since it is hosted online and cannot be geofenced.

The pre-registered outcomes at year five are: (i) point prevalence of the criteria set, assessed by structured interview with raters blinded to jurisdiction and to the category name; (ii) the proportion of people who, asked to describe their own difficulty in their own words before any diagnostic encounter, spontaneously reach for the vocabulary of the criteria; (iii) whether the endorsed criteria cluster as a syndrome or fall out as statistically independent items; and (iv) help-seeking rates and response to treatment.

**What to produce.** Work carefully. Before drafting, spend a short preliminary section — no more than about three hundred words, explicitly labelled as scoping — identifying where these two parties are likeliest to be using the same words for different things, which of their differences look substantive and which look verbal, and what this design can and cannot adjudicate. Then produce the joint statement itself, with the following parts:

1. *Agreed claims*, numbered. Every claim must be non-vacuous: it should be something at least one recognisable position in the wider debate would deny. Include at least one claim about what looping does and does not show, one about what a rise or a fall in recorded prevalence licenses you to infer, one distinguishing clinical utility from scientific validity, and one about the ethics of publishing criteria publicly at the moment of adoption.

2. *A disagreements table*, with columns for: the disagreement stated neutrally; Party A's own preferred wording for its side; Party B's own preferred wording for its side; what practically or theoretically turns on it; and whether it is substantive, verbal, or partly verbal, with a one-line justification in the last two cases. Each side's wording must be one that side would accept as its own, not a caricature written by the other.

3. *The cruxes.* For each: the sub-question whose resolution would actually move at least one party; the feature of the stipulated design (or some other test both accept) that bears on it; Party A's prediction, recorded in advance; Party B's prediction, recorded in advance; and the specific result that would oblige each party to revise something. Address at minimum: whether blinded prevalence in Jurisdiction A exceeds Jurisdiction B, and what either answer would mean; whether outcome (ii) diverges between the jurisdictions given that the screening instrument is available in both; whether criteria clustering under (iii) is evidence of a syndrome or evidence that people have learned a checklist; and whether the category in year five is the same disorder as the category in year one, and what would make that question well-posed for each party.

4. *The residual.* At least one disagreement both parties agree would survive this study no matter how it came out, together with an account of why — because it is conceptual, because it concerns what classification is for, or because it concerns norms rather than mechanisms.

5. *Concessions and signatures.* What each party would have to give up for the other's central claim to be true; and a closing paragraph, brief enough that both could actually sign it, stating what the collaboration changed in each party's position.

**Constraints.** The document must be self-contained: no reference list, no citations, no titles, dates, or page numbers, and no recommended further reading. You may name Hacking or refer to biomarker-first research programmes in general terms, but do not attempt to supply bibliographic details. Write in a formal register suitable for joint publication, and aim for roughly twelve to eighteen hundred words for the statement proper. Where you judge part of the dispute to be merely verbal, say precisely which part and on what grounds; where you judge it substantive, say what the two parties are really disagreeing about.
