I'm putting together a session for a history-of-science seminar on how early modern natural philosophers drew the line between respectable celestial theory and astrology, and I want to do it through Kepler's own attempt to reform astrology from the inside rather than through the usual Popper-style verdict from outside.

Here's the contrast as I've got it. Ptolemaic astrology is a causal theory of qualities: the heavenly bodies impress heat, cold, moisture and dryness on the air and on living things, each planet has its own temperament, and a natal chart is essentially a record of the local physical mixture at birth — so the effects are graded, continuous, and run through the same medium as ordinary weather and medicine. Kepler's reformed version throws out most of that inherited apparatus — the twelve signs as carriers of character, the house divisions, the lots — and keeps mainly the aspects, which he grounds in geometry rather than in qualities: only the angles that devide the circle into harmonically privileged arcs do anything, and he adds a few of his own (the quintile, the sesquiquadrate, the biquintile) on the strength of what he took to be years of weather observations. But the reason an angle does anything is not that it transmits a quality — it's that the sublunary nature, the soul of the earth and by extension our own lower faculties, has a built in, non-rational sensitivity to those ratios and gets stirred when they occur, the way someone who knows nothing of music theory still gets moved by a tune.

So compare the two as natural-philosophical projects, and give me your judgment on three things: which one offers the more coherent account of why a celestial configuration could matter at all, and whether Kepler's move trades a physical mechanism for a psychological one at a net gain or a net loss; whether the discontinuity costs him more than it buys, given that Ptolemy's graded qualities sit much more comfortably next to ordinary meteorology and humoral medicine; and — the one I care about most — whether restricting astrology to aspects plus weather records actually makes it more testable, or whether picking which aspects count on a priori geometric grounds first means the records can only ever confirm and never select. Finish by saying which of the two a sharp seventeenth-century skeptic should have attacked, and why.

Bottom line up front: first sentence states your verdict on all three questions, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating what I asked.
