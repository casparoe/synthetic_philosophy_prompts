So my decision-theory reading group has started doing a thing where every other week one of us has to bring in a piece of original fiction instead of a paper, on the theory that the arguments get easier to argue about once somebody's made them concrete. It's my turn next Thursday and I've drawn acausal trade, which is the one topic on the whole syllabus where I think fiction would genuinely beat the papers. The setup I want: two civilisations that each know the other exists — they've both worked out from physics plus the other's early light-leakage roughly what the far side's environment, incentives and computing power are like — but that can never exchange a single usable signal, because the round-trip lag is longer than either of them will last as civilisations. Each can still predict the other decently well, and so each can do the other a favour conditional on the other doing it a favour, and both end up better off without anything ever crossing the gap. That's the part I want to feel uncanny rather than clever: nobody ever gets thanked, and nobody ever gets to check.

Here's the seed I've got — feel free to change the furniture, but please keep the machinery. My protagonist is an archivist of some monastic order, and the order's entire job for eleven generations has been to maintain a ledger: a vast formal document, continuously broadcast at light speed, specifying what her civilisation will do in a very long list of situations it will probably never actually be in, including most of the situations the far side might care about. The trade only works if the ledger is an honest prediction of behaviour, since the other side is reasoning about the ledger and about the kind of people who'd write it. Something like three hundred years ago somebody in her order started quietly revising entries — not exactly lying, more like making commitments vaguer in directions that happened to be convenient — and she turns up an older recension and realises the revisions compound, and that the vagueness has been propagating forward into every entry written since. I'd like the story to sit inside what she does next, and to take seriously the possibility that the far side already knows and already adjusted, so that the deception was never a secret being kept from anyone in particular.

Two requests on execution. Length: one continuous piece, somewhere between 1500 and 2500 words — an actual story, not an outline or a treatment. And no lectures: I really don't want a scene where a wiser senior archivist explains the decision theory to a sceptical novice, or anyone saying the words "acausal" or "expected utility" out loud. The theory should be load-bearing in the plot and invisible in the dialogue. It's fine — preferred, actually — if the story doesn't resolve whether the trade was ever real; I'd rather it end with her hand near the pen. And a bonus if you can find some small physical object that does the job a handshake would normally do, something like the order's habit of sealing each revision with a mark that can't be lifted without destroying the page, so that tampering and honest editing look identical from the outside. My last draft attempt turned into a courtroom drama and I'd rather not repeat that.
