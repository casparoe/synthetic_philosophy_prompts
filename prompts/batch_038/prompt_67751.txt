I convene a masters-level seminar on the metaphysics of properties and powers: fourteen students, one ninety-minute session, movable tables, a whiteboard, no computers for the students and a materials budget of essentially nothing beyond paper and index cards. This week's reading sets out two objections to the view that to have a disposition is simply to satisfy the corresponding counterfactual test. The first concerns an object that genuinely has the disposition, together with something standing ready to remove the very basis of that disposition at the moment the stimulus condition obtains, so that the object is disposed as it is and yet would not manifest if stimulated. The second concerns an object that genuinely has the disposition, with the basis wholly intact, where an interfering condition blocks the manifestation process instead, so that again the object is disposed as it is and yet would not manifest. My students can recite both cases perfectly. What I want is for them to stop reciting and to discover, procedurally and under time pressure, that the objections generalise into a problem about whether any operational test could pin down the property at all.

Please design an in-class activity in which the students act as a standards body drafting a certification protocol for some artefact property, and are then subjected to adversarial audit rounds in which their protocol is broken. Concretely:

1. **The domain.** Choose a fictional certification context well away from the familiar examples in the literature — no fragile glassware and no live wires. The property to be certified should be one where "genuinely has the property" and "would manifest the property on testing" can come apart, and where the artefacts can be described on a card in ordinary language.

2. **The protocol mechanic.** Teams draft a written protocol: a procedure, expressible in a small number of clauses, that assigns a verdict of *certified* or *not certified* to any artefact given only the information a tester could obtain. Impose an explicit complexity budget, and a rule ruling out blanket hedges of the "unless something interferes" or "all other things being equal" form — or, if you prefer, allow them and score them so that the vacuity becomes expensive. Tell me which choice you made and why, since a naive version of this activity collapses the moment a team writes an unfalsifiable protocol and is never punished for it.

3. **The audit rounds.** Structure at least three escalating rounds in which artefact cards are submitted against the current protocol, with roles rotating between certifying team and adversarial team, and a point scheme (paper tokens or a running ledger, no real money) that rewards the certifying team for correct verdicts and the adversarial team for each artefact that defeats the protocol. At least one round must expose the *opposite* failure — a protocol that certifies an artefact which does not have the property — so that students find their repairs overcorrecting as well as undercorrecting.

4. **The card deck.** Supply at least twelve fully specified cards. For each: what the certifying team sees, what the sealed specification reveals, the intended verdict, a one-line justification I could give if a team challenges it, and an honest flag wherever a competent metaphysician could reasonably dissent from that verdict. Say explicitly how the deck is meant to be shuffled, sealed and reused across rounds, and keep total preparation under thirty minutes.

5. **Time-indexing.** Decide, and tell me, whether a verdict concerns the artefact as it is at the instant before the trial or as it is during the trial, and where in the activity you expect that question to surface. This is the point at which the first kind of objection bites hardest, and I would rather the design forced it into the open than papered over it.

6. **Timeline.** A minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, including where the terminology first enters the room. No lecture beforehand and no naming of the two objection types until the debrief.

7. **Debrief.** Map specific moments — particular cards, particular protocol clauses, particular disputes between teams — onto the structure of the two objections and onto the third, opposite failure, and onto the standard repairs (a clause about the intrinsic basis, a weakened modal, a ceteris paribus qualification), showing what each repair costs.

8. **What will go wrong.** Give me a frank account of the predictable first-run failures: the team that writes a vacuous protocol, the adversarial team that submits an artefact whose classification is genuinely indeterminate and stalls the game in an argument about the answer key, the well-read student who smells the trap in round one and short-circuits the discovery for everyone else. For each, say how to handle it in the room without appearing to steer.

9. **The limit.** State plainly which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it has something to do with the fact that my spec cards will quietly presuppose that there *is* a determinate intrinsic basis to appeal to, which is not something the students should be allowed to take for granted — and say what I must therefore supply myself in the closing twenty minutes.

Please write the whole thing in British English.
