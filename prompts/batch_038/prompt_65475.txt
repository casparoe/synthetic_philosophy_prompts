In six weeks I am giving a forty-minute paper, with twenty-five minutes of discussion, in a Science and Technology Studies colloquium series at a university with a strong history-of-computing group. The room will be roughly this: six or seven historians and sociologists of science who work on computing, machine learning, or data, several of whom do ethnography of industrial labs; two philosophers of science from the department, one of them a Bayesian formal epistemologist who regards research-programme methodology as unfalsifiable historicism dressed up as epistemology; two or three economists who actually know the Lakatosian literature in economic methodology; and three or four machine-learning practitioners and doctoral students who came because the title mentions their field. Nobody in the room is a Lakatos specialist, and nobody is friendly to my framework by default.

The paper's thesis is a revisionist reading of Lakatos, plus a live application. My claims, in order:

1. The standard reading treats the hard core as a set of propositions immunised from refutation by methodological decision, with the negative heuristic as a derivative instruction not to aim the modus tollens at the core. I reverse the order of explanation. The primary notion is the negative heuristic, and it is not a rule about which beliefs are immune but a rule about which reported results count as candidate evidence at all: a standing criterion of data admissibility, maintained by the programme's practitioners. Hard cores are the sediment of many such rules, and can only be identified after the fact.

2. The progressive/degenerating distinction should accordingly be redefined. Not "does the shift yield novel facts," but "does the belt-revision widen or narrow the admissible-data set?" A revision that absorbs an anomaly by reclassifying the anomalous result — as an artifact, a contaminated measurement, an ill-posed task, a failure to replicate properly, a mis-specified description of the domain — narrows the set. A revision that absorbs an anomaly by admitting new kinds of result into what the programme must answer to widens it.

3. This makes the distinction prospectively assessable, which on the standard reading it is not. Narrowing and widening are visible in the contemporaneous record, in practitioners' and referees' arguments about what counts as a legitimate experiment, so one need not wait thirty years to see which side a programme is on.

4. Application: the machine-learning scaling programme. I argue that its belt-revisions have been predominantly narrowing — benchmark contamination and the resulting curation and pruning of evaluation sets; the treatment of surprising capability discontinuities as artifacts of scoring metrics rather than of underlying behaviour; the reclassification of failures as ill-posed tasks; the growing reliance on evaluations designed by the developers of the systems being evaluated — while its empirical and technical success is undeniable. I call this state asymmetric progress: rising confirmatory success together with falling admissibility. Lakatos's two-valued typology cannot represent it, and I take that to be a reason to revise the typology rather than to abandon the apparatus.

My argument for claim 2 is that if belt-revisions changed only what a programme believes and never what it must answer to, tenacity would be indistinguishable from dogmatism, and Lakatos needs a criterion that separates the two without hindsight. Admissibility, I claim, is the only candidate that is visible in real time.

Two calibrations. Assume I already know I will be asked how to tell a legitimate tightening of methodological standards from admissibility-narrowing — tightening usually looks exactly like narrowing, and it is usually an epistemic improvement — and that I have a partial answer involving who does the reclassifying and whether the criterion was set before or after the result; tell me how much damage the partial answer really does. Likewise, assume I handle the charge that this is Kuhn relabelled and the Bayesian charge that my criterion is just a redescription of holding priors fixed plus use-novelty; do not spend space on those unless this room would press them in a form my standard replies do not reach.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis and not a survey. Please give me:

1. The eight to ten questions and objections this room will actually raise, ordered by likelihood multiplied by damage.
2. Each phrased in the register of the subgroup that raises it, and attributed to that subgroup. The lab ethnographer, the Bayesian, the economic methodologist, and the practitioner who builds these systems ask the same underlying question in four incompatible ways, and I need to hear the four ways, not a neutralised version.
3. For each, whether it is exegetical (aimed at my reading of Lakatos) or systematic (aimed at the framework), since I answer these differently, and whether my stance of claiming the reconstruction rather than the exegesis leaves me exposed there.
4. A spoken reply of about ninety seconds for each, followed by one line naming the softest point in that reply and the most likely follow-up.
5. What I should concede in the first three minutes of the talk so that two or three of the listed objections are never asked at all, with the concession worded so it does not undermine claim 3.
6. The single reply that has to land for the paper to be taken seriously rather than filed as clever reconstruction.
7. The question I am hoping nobody asks: the one whose honest answer is either that my criterion cannot yet be applied to the scaling case without hindsight, after all, or that asymmetric progress is compatible with my own diagnosis being exactly the kind of retrospective hard-core attribution I criticise in claim 1.
8. Whether there is a legitimate way to set the historians' objections and the Bayesian's against one another — they attack from opposite directions — or whether that move is a cheap trick that will cost me the room, and if legitimate, the sentence that does it.

No citations, no bibliography, no suggestions of further reading: I know the literature and the room does not want to hear it. No generic complaints about Lakatos that any philosophy-of-science audience would raise. Format it as a sheet I could hold at the podium: short labels, the question as it will be spoken, the reply, the soft spot.
