I served eleven years as a homicide prosecutor and now sit on the judiciary committee of my state legislature, which is drafting a narrow capital-sentencing statute for a state that has not carried out an execution in three decades. I am to be the bill's principal witness at the hearings. Before I testify, I have written down in one place, for the first time, the package of moral commitments I intend to defend, and I want a rigorous consistency audit of it.

The bill, so that you have a concrete object in view: capital eligibility is confined to three categories — the murder of more than one victim in a single course of conduct; murder preceded by prolonged torture or captivity; and murder committed by a person already serving a life sentence for a previous murder. Eligibility further requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt plus a judicial finding, resting on forensic or documentary evidence independent of any confession or accomplice testimony, that no plausible hypothesis of the defendant's innocence remains. The prosecution may not seek death over the objection of the victim's immediate survivors, and the survivors may not compel the state to seek it. Victim-impact evidence is excluded from the penalty phase. The governor's power to commute is preserved, unrestricted and unreviewable. Every other murder draws life without the possibility of parole.

My commitments:

1. Justification. The state may inflict a punishment on a person only because, and only to the extent that, he deserves it. I disclaim deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation as justifications — not merely because I take the empirical case for them to be weak, but because justifying an execution by its effects on anyone other than the offender is the wrong kind of reason altogether. Consequences cannot make a punishment right; desert alone can.

2. Mandate. Within the three categories, death is not merely permitted but required. A jurisdiction that uniformly imposed life instead would not be exhibiting admirable restraint; it would be failing the victims, declining to take the wrong as seriously as it deserves to be taken.

3. Gate. No one is eligible unless, over and above proof beyond a reasonable doubt, a judge finds on independent forensic or documentary evidence that no plausible innocence hypothesis remains. I hold to something like the old Blackstonean ratio: I would sooner see a hundred offenders who deserve execution go unexecuted than see one innocent person executed.

4. Residual risk. The gate lowers the probability that we kill an innocent person; it cannot drive that probability to zero, and I will not pretend otherwise. If the system as designed executed an innocent person once every few hundred executions, I would regard that as a grave institutional wrong — but not as a reason to abolish, and I would not vote for abolition on that basis. Every institution we regard as legitimate operates at a nonzero rate of catastrophic error; the question is whether the rate is acceptable given what the institution is for.

5. Clemency. The governor may commute for any reason or for none. I hope that power is used generously, including in cases that satisfy every element of the statute and every evidentiary requirement and nevertheless strike the people in the room as ones where death is too much.

6. Survivors and the record. The survivors may block a death sentence but may not compel one, and no victim-impact evidence reaches the jury. I want the family to have a voice in whether the state seeks the ultimate sanction, but I do not want the sentence to turn on how articulate, numerous, sympathetic, or well organized a particular victim's family happens to be.

7. Equality. Two offenders equally culpable must receive the same sentence. Discretion is what produced the geographic and racial disparities that have discredited capital statutes, and I regard unexplained disparity between equally culpable offenders as a decisive objection to any version of this bill.

8. Method. The position I intend to defend is free of empirical premises. Because I ground everything in desert, I should not have to litigate the social science at all — the deterrence studies, the exoneration tallies, the disparity regressions. I want to be able to say from the witness table that even if every empirical claim my opponents make were granted, my position would stand exactly where it stands.

What I want back:

- An audit, not a polemic. Identify each pair, or larger set, of these commitments that cannot all be held as I have stated them, and rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list of worries.
- For every genuine tension, tell me which of the commitments has to be weakened or surrendered, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me — including, specifically, which sentences I would no longer be able to say in testimony.
- Discriminate carefully between tensions that are real and tensions that only look real. I am confident that at least one pair a colleague has already flagged to me is not a genuine problem, and I suspect that at least one pair I regard as perfectly harmonious is not. Where a pair is benign on one reading of a commitment and fatal on another, say so, and tell me which reading the wording above actually commits me to.
- Finish with the single revision to the package that repairs the largest number and the most severe of the tensions, and state plainly what I give up by making it.

Two constraints on your answer. I am not asking whether capital punishment is justified, whether this bill is wise, or how I should vote; treat my conclusions as fixed and examine only whether the route to them coheres. And I do not want a survey of the literature, a reading list, or appeals to authority — reason from the commitments as I have stated them.
