So my reading group has been stuck for months on Harsanyi's aggregation result — the one where, if individual preferences and the social preference are all vNM-rational over the same lotteries, and you add Pareto plus anonymity, social utility comes out as a weighted sum of individual utilities, and then with the impartial observer having an equal chance of being anyone, the weights go equal and you get something that looks like classical utilitarianism falling out of decision theory. Two camps, and they keep talking past each other:

Camp A ("the theorem does the work"): the assumptions are either technical bookkeeping or things you're already committed to the moment you take the idea of social betterness seriously at all, so the moral upshot is real and not smuggled in. Independence for the social preference is no more controversial than independence for an individual's — it's just what it means to have preferences over lotteries.

Camp B ("a representation theorem in a moral costume"): the math is fine but normatively inert. It tells you what anyone who accepts those axioms is committed to, and the axioms are exactly the contested part of the debate — in particular, summing utilities across people already presupposes a fixed zero and a fixed unit for each person's scale, which is the interpersonal comparability problem the theorem was supposed to solve.

Here's the concrete case I've been using to make them argue about something specific instead of slogans, and I want both camps forced to respond to it in the joint statement. Two people, Ada and Bo, three consequences:

u_Ada: P = 9, Q = 0, R = 2
u_Bo: P = 0, Q = 4, R = 2

Plain sum ranks P first, with Q and R tied. Now rescale Bo alone, v_Bo = 3·u_Bo − 1, which gives P = −1, Q = 11, R = 5. Summing u_Ada with v_Bo gives Q = 11, P = 8, R = 7 — the best and the worst consequences swap places. And both of Bo's scales are legitimate vNM representations of the very same preferences over the very same lotteries, so nothing internal to the mathematics prefers one over the other.

What I want you to draft is the document this collaboration should produce, in American English. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — about what the theorem actually says, what the axioms are and what each one rules out, what's settled by the math alone, and what the numeric case above does and doesn't show. Make these non-vacuous; if a claim is one that only sounds agreeable because it's ambiguous, don't put it on the list.

2. A disagreements table with columns for: the dispute, the wording Camp A would accept for its own position, the wording Camp B would accept for its own position, whether the dispute is substantive or verbal, and which crux it reduces to.

3. The cruxes themselves — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. For each, give a test, argument, or case both camps agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, so somebody can be caught out later. I expect at least three cruxes, and I want these addressed:

- What, if anything, pins down the scales? Camp A has to say how the observer's extended preferences or sympathetic identification is supposed to fix Ada's and Bo's zeroes and units, described in terms Camp B accepts as a fair statement of the burden rather than as a restatement of the conclusion. Camp B has to say what the theorem still buys even if comparability is presupposed — some constraint on aggregators, a consistency requirement, nothing at all?
- Suppose we grant Camp B its point and drop independence for the social preference only, keeping it for individuals, and keeping Pareto, anonymity, and continuity. What do each of them expect to happen: does a wide family of rules open up (rank-dependent, prioritarian, maximin-flavored), or do the leftovers still pin things down near the sum? Have each camp name the specific alternative rule it would endorse as the next-best candidate, and predict what the other will object to about it.
- Whether anonymity and the observer's equiprobable chance of being anyone are the same assumption wearing different clothes or genuinely different ones, and whether one camp is quietly sliding between the many-person aggregation result and the single-observer result. If you think that sliding is happening, put it in the table as a candidate verbal dispute and say so.

4. A short closing section on what turned out to be merely verbal, and what's left over that is genuinely substantive — the residue is the point of the whole exercise.
