Ok so I have a view about audit trails and blame that I keep failing to argue properly, and I want you to make the case for it as strongly as you can.

Setup, roughly realistic: a hospital deploys a sepsis-triage system with a full audit trail — every feature value that went in, every rule that fired, the counterfactuals it weighed, and the provenance of the training data behind each weight. A patient gets triaged as low-risk and dies of septic shock six hours later.

Now two versions of what the log turns up.

Version A: the log shows the system downweighted an elevated lactate reading, because the hospitals that contributed most of that training data mostly drew lactate from patients already in the ICU, so the model learned the reading was less informative than it actually was. Somebody upstream made a corner-cutting call about which sites to pool.

Version B: the log shows the system applying exactly the weighting a careful statistician would endorse, to data nobody gathered badly, under a rule nobody should have written differently. The failure is just that the world had a tail and this patient was in it.

Everybody says the audit trail is what closes the responsibility gap. I want you to argue that it widens it — in both versions, and especially in B. My rough intuition, which I can't turn into an argument: blaming someone requires that they could of judged otherwise, and a complete causal account of how the output came about is precisely the thing that shows nobody was in that position. The better we can say why the decision happened, the less room is left for the counterfactual that blame consists in. Explanation crowds out the thing attribution needs.

So build that into a real argument. Take on the obvious objections: that version A plainly does locate a culpable data team; that the gap is only an epistemic problem and the log solves it; that I'm sliding from causal explanation to justification. Then say how the position differs from the familiar claim that nobody can justly be praised or blamed for what a genuinely autonomous system does, and from the many-hands problem — I think it's neither, and the argument should show why.

Don't hedge at the end. If you think the position is wrong, argue it anyway and then tell me where it's weakest.
