I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on well-being, and I want a decision tree that sorts participants by what they are actually committed to about the nature of pleasure — not by what they say about whether hedonism is true. We have just spent two sessions on the heterogeneity problem: the observation that the pleasure of a ripe peach, the pleasure of a hot bath after a day on cold slopes, the pleasure of finally seeing why a proof goes through, and the pleasure of being admired by someone whose opinion matters to you seem to share no detectable phenomenal ingredient, so it is not obvious what single thing a hedonist is claiming to be good. Half the seminar thinks this is fatal; half thinks it is a pseudo-problem generated by expecting the wrong kind of unity. I want the tree to settle where each of them actually stands.

Please produce the following.

1. First, map the logical space before drawing the tree. Identify three or four dimensions you take to be genuinely independent — I expect at least one to concern whether pleasure is a felt quality or a stance taken toward a quality, one to concern whether being pleased that something is the case is the same kind of thing as enjoying a sensation, and one to concern whether a heterogeneous class can still be a real kind in virtue of the role its members play — and state which combinations of answers those dimensions permit. I want the space first and the positions located in it afterward, not the reverse.

2. Then give the tree: seven to nine questions, eight to twelve leaves. Every question must be answerable by someone who has never encountered any of the positions. Use concrete cases, or a choice between two descriptions of the same situation, or a claim to accept or reject — never a question containing the name of a view, a term of art, or a philosopher. Open with a case rather than a thesis, so that nobody can work out where they are headed.

3. These cases must appear somewhere in the tree, and each must do genuine diagnostic work:
 - the chili burn that one diner reports as delightful and another as intolerable, where both are telling the truth about how it feels;
 - the person who sincerely says she is enjoying a massage, but who, when asked to attend closely to what she is feeling, reports finding nothing except the absence of tension and a strong reluctance to have it stop;
 - wanting without liking: the long-time smoker who insists she no longer enjoys cigarettes at all and reaches for one regardless — and its mirror image, the state someone describes as pleasant but carefully avoids repeating;
 - an animal case: exuberant, play-bowing canine delight in a creature with no concept of pleasure and no means of reporting one;
 - being pleased that a rival's paper was rejected, where nothing at all seems to be happening anywhere in the body;
 - two lives containing, by stipulation, the same total amount of pleasure, one built from many small sensory episodes and one from a few intense intellectual ones — with the participant asked whether there is a fact about which contains more, and if so, what makes the two sums weigh against each other.

4. Every leaf gets three things: a descriptive name of your own devising; a one-sentence statement of what that view says the peach, the bath, the proof, and the admiration have in common, or an explicit denial that they have anything in common; and the case that is hardest for it to swallow, stated in one sentence rather than waved away.

5. One question must force the participant to choose between two ways a heterogeneous class can be unified: as a merely nominal kind, in the way that jade covers two mineralogically unrelated things bound together by a name and a trade, and as a role kind, in the way that predator or money covers wildly different realizers that figure in the same explanations. Say explicitly what a leaf that chooses the second option owes us that the first does not.

6. The tree must be able to place someone who holds that pleasures have no common property and that this is no threat to hedonism at a different leaf from someone who holds that pleasures have no common property and that hedonism is therefore false. Make sure at least one question separates the metaphysical worry from the measurement worry, since I think a good deal of our seminar disagreement is a failure to distinguish them.

7. Mark every cell of the space that no question routes to, and diagnose each vacancy: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? I am particularly unsure about the cell that says pleasure is a single simple quality grasped only by acquaintance while also denying that it is phenomenally detectable — tell me whether that position can be coherently occupied, rather than assuming my hunch is right.

8. Flag every pair of leaves your questions fail to separate, and say whether some further case would separate them or whether they are extensionally equivalent and should be merged.

Two further constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; if you are not confident that a given leaf has actual defenders, label it as possibly unoccupied and move on, because an honest map is worth more to me than a decorated one. And keep the tree neutral on the truth of hedonism throughout: no question and no leaf should turn on whether pleasure is what makes a life good, since that is the argument we are trying to postpone, not to settle.
