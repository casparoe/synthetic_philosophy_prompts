I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for roughly two years I have been reporting on a dispute that I have come to think cannot be won as it is currently posed: the argument over whether socially constructed kinds — race above all, but also gender and money — are real. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it, and not a tour of the positions.

Here is what I have observed from the inside. Both camps appear to run the same battery of tests for whether something is real, namely: (i) does it make a causal difference to what happens; (ii) do its categories support reliable generalizations and predictions; (iii) would it persist, and behave the same way, if nobody believed in it; (iv) can our best explanations of the phenomena in question be stated without it. My working hypothesis — which I want you to test rather than accept, and which I suspect is too tidy — is that the impasse arises because these four tests come apart for constructed kinds, and each side silently treats a different subset as decisive. Those who defend race as a genuine social kind press (i), (ii), and (iv): it shapes health, wealth, policing, and life expectancy, it supports generalizations that hold, and the best explanations of a great deal of social life are ineliminably racial. Those on the other side, who press for elimination or who treat race-talk as I would treat talk of phlogiston, press (iii), plus the collapse of (ii) at the boundaries: there are no non-arbitrary cutoffs, and the kind would not exist without the practices that constitute it. Neither camp ever says which test governs, and when I have put the question directly, the answers have not tracked the verdicts.

Against that background:

- Is this a substantive metaphysical disagreement, a verbal one about the word "real," a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading — one asking what there is, the other asking what we should do with a concept we inherited — or a dispute whose engine is something else altogether, such as what each side fears the answer would license politically? Argue for your diagnosis rather than listing options.
- Test the hypothesis against money. Everyone I have spoken to agrees money is constructed, yet no one wants to call it unreal. Does money pass or fail each of the four tests, and does its pattern of answers match the pattern for race? If the patterns diverge, what does that divergence show about which test is doing the real work?
- Does the same diagnosis carry over to gender, or does gender introduce a structural difference — for instance, that the natural-kind candidate it is defined against is itself contested — that changes the shape of the dispute rather than merely its content?
- Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an argument that one of the four tests is privileged over the others, a stipulation that dissolves the question, some new kind of evidence, or a change of question? If your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say so and explain why.

Please do not supply citations or a reading list; I will handle that legwork myself. What I need is the reasoning, stated in a way I could put to a smart reader who has never encountered the debate.
