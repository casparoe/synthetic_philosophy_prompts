> Goodhart's law is usually glossed as the claim that once a statistical measure is pressed into service as a target for policy, it ceases to be a reliable indicator of the thing it was chosen to track. Explain the mechanism that produces this failure, distinguish more than one way in which it can occur, and say what follows for the use of numerical targets in public services. (800 words maximum.)

And here's the student's answer, in full:

> Goodhart's law says that when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. The basic mechanism is that we can never measure what we actually care about directly — we care about health, education, productivity, public safety — so we pick some observable quantity that correlates with it and manage on that. The gap between the proxy and the underlying goal is what gets exploited, and the harder the pressure on the proxy the wider the gap becomes, because people respond to the thing they are being rewarded for rather than the thing we wanted.
>
> Four variants are usually distinguished. Regressional Goodhart happens when the proxy is an imperfect, noisy measure of the goal, so selecting for high proxy values also selects for high error values — like the standard story of the Soviet nail factory measured by weight, which duly produced nothing but enormous nails. Extremal Goodhart is what happens at the extremes: the correlation that held in the normal range simply doesn't hold out where the policy pushes, because the relationship between proxy and goal was never linear in the first place. Causal Goodhart is when we intervene on something that is correlated with the goal but isn't a cause of it, or is only one of several causes, so the intervention fails to transmit. And adversarial Goodhart is when the people being measured know what the measure is and manipulate it deliberately — the Atlanta exam-cheating scandal, where teachers erased and corrected pupils' answers, is the usual example. Adversarial Goodhart is really just causal Goodhart with a knowing agent: the gamer understands the causal structure of the measure better than the designer does and uses that understanding, so the two collapse into a single phenomenon once you notice that gaming requires knowledge of how the measure works.
>
> A lot of the confusion about the law comes from people treating the regressional case as though it were the interesting one, when its really just measurement error under another name. Noise in an indicator is a statistical nuisance we have known about forever; it is not a discovery about incentives and it is not what the law is about. The actual content of the law concerns agents gaming a system, which is why all the memorable examples are about hospitals and schools rather than about scatterplots. Relatedly, the law is often stated as though nothing could be done about it, which is overdone: if the trouble is a badly chosen indicator, then the answer is to choose better ones. Pick a measure that is genuinely causally downstream of what you want and hard to fake, and the law loses its grip.
>
> Which brings me to the policy question. Since every proxy is vulnerable — noisy ones to regressional failure, causally sound ones to intervention failure, and all of them to gaming — it follows that numerical targets in public services do more harm than good and should be replaced by experienced professional judgement and qualitative review. A matron or a head teacher who knows their patch can see whether care or learning is actually improving, and nobody has an incentive to fiddle with a number, because there is no number. Waiting-time targets in the NHS are the standing proof: they generated trolley waits and ambulance queuing rather than faster treatment. So the remedy is not better measurement but less of it.
