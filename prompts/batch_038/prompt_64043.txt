I sit on the constitutional convention for a federation of worker cooperatives that is separating from its parent body and adopting its own governing charter. Under the draft charter, the Congress of Delegates is the federation's supreme organ: its acts are subject to review by no court or external authority, and the charter is the sole source of its competence. Article 14 of my draft provides that Article 14 may be amended only by a two-thirds majority of delegates voting at two consecutive annual congresses, and that no amendment may delete Article 14 or reduce it to an ordinarily amendable clause. A colleague on the convention, who practises commercial law, has twice dismissed the article as so much decoration, on the ground that "no Parliament can bind its successors." His elaboration is that if the delegates ever genuinely want the provision gone, they will simply strike it out by an ordinary majority at a single sitting, and nothing in the drafting will stop them.

I suspect the remark is carrying considerably more weight than it can bear, and I want it taken apart before the convention reconvenes. Please set out, as a menu rather than as a single gloss, the distinct theses that this maxim could be expressing. I would expect the list to include at least: a claim about the default rules a court uses when two enactments of the same body conflict; a claim about the status of the ultimate criteria of validity in a legal system, and whether those criteria can themselves be laid down by a rule; a claim distinguishing restrictions on the manner and form of lawmaking from restrictions on its content; a claim about the identity or continuity of the enacting body across time; a claim about the political futility of provisions that officials are disposed to disregard; and a normative claim about one generation legislating for the next. Distinguish these carefully if they are not in fact distinct, and add any readings I have missed.

For each reading, state it precisely enough to be assessable, then say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; and say whether, if true, it would actually license my colleague's conclusion that Article 14 is powerless. Flag explicitly which readings are artifacts of the setting the maxim grew up in — an uncodified constitution, a legislature of unlimited competence, no entrenched amendment procedure — and would not carry over to a body that is writing its own highest law and has no superior to repeal it. Tell me also which reading you think he is actually relying on, and whether it is the one that survives scrutiny.

Finally, I would like the self-referential knot addressed on its own terms. The amending procedure in Article 14 is itself part of Article 14, so any use of it must comply with the very clause that forbids the article's deletion or downgrade. Can a rule competently subtract from the amending power it confers, or is the prohibition self-undermining in the way that a rule permitting its own repeal would not be? And if the delegates instead discard the charter altogether and adopt a fresh one, does that vindicate the maxim, or does it merely describe a way of acting outside it?
