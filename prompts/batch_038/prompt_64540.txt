I'm an archaeologist (Bronze Age Aegean, mostly mortuary and small-scale cult stuff) and I've got three months of residency time at a humanities institute next year to write something that isn't site-report-adjacent. I need a proposal to hand to the selection panel, and I want it to work out a problem that keeps biting me in actual practice rather than being a general essay about interpretation.

The problem: we routinely write things like 'these communities believed in an afterlife' or 'the figurines show a shared cult' on the basis of, basically, patterned deposition and repeated iconography. I've started thinking there's three different targets that people slide between without noticing: (1) that a practice was performed regularly by a group; (2) that the people performing it had a stable disposition to talk it up, defend it, teach it to kids, sanction people who did it wrong; and (3) that they held a particular propositional content — a doctrine you could actually write a sentence about. Each step down seems to require more assumptions, and my hunch is that the evidence doesn't get richer as you descend, if anything it gets thinner, becuase a specific content is only fixed against a background of lots of other beliefs and practices that leave no trace at all.

So my half-formed idea is that the real question is whether there's a principled stopping point on that ladder — or whether (3) is simply not something material remains can ever license on their own, and the discipline should re-aim at something like a notion of 'institutional commitment' that is genuinely recoverable and still does the explanatory work we want from belief talk (why this site and not that one, why the practice spread, why it stopped).

Turn that into a proposal. I need: the question stated precisely enough that a sceptical colleague could tell me it's wrong rather than merely vague; why the usual answers — charity, inference to the best explanation, middle-range theory, 'the archaeological record is just underdetermined, so be humble' — don't settle it; the approach, including what I'd actually do month by month, and I think it has to involve taking two or three concrete evidence-types (deliberate burial with goods, near-identical small images turning up across many sites, deliberate destruction or concealment of images) and working out for each what would have to be true for the content-level claim to go through and what competing non-belief explanations do the same work just as well; what a good result looks like and what would count as the project failing, in a way that isn't just 'I'd have learned a lot'; and an honest note about where three months simply isn't enough. No bibliography, I'll fill that in myself. Do be concrete about the argumentative moves, not hand-wavy about interdisciplinarity. Assume the panel is half philosophers who've never been on a dig and half archaeologists who think this sort of thing is a waste of a fellowship — it has to land with both.
