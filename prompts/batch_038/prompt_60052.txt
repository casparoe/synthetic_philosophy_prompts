I lead one of two weekly sections for a third-year philosophy of mind course, and next Wednesday's fifty minutes are mine to allocate. The students have had behaviourism, the identity theory, functionalism, and a week apiece on supervenience and multiple realizability; before this course, most of them sat the first-year core unit on free will and determinism, and in office hours three of them have now told me some version of the thought that the mind–body problem is just the free-will problem wearing a different hat. My brief is to get them through Kim's causal exclusion argument — causal closure, the exclusion principle, the systematic-overdetermination dilemma, and why non-reductive physicalism is supposed to be caught between reducing the mental and writing it off as causally inert — without turning the section into an adjudication of it. I do not want to spend the time on the replies, but by the end they need to be able to say what shape any successful reply would have to take, because that is what the essay question in week nine will ask them.

What I want from you is the architecture of those fifty minutes rather than the content of them. Tell me the order in which the components have to go up on the board and what genuinely depends on what: my suspicion is that supervenience has to be in hand before exclusion can even be stated, but I cannot work out whether it belongs before or after causal closure, and whether closure is something they should be made to feel the force of or simply handed as a premise the argument's opponents already accept. Tell me which single case should carry the whole thing, and what that case must have and must not have — I have ruled out pain, since it detonates into a qualia discussion I am not equipped to run in that room, and I have ruled out intending to raise your arm, since at least two of them will immediately insist the intention just is a brain state and we will spend the period arguing about that instead. Map the misconceptions in the order they will actually arrive, with the point in the sequence where I should get ahead of each: I expect several of them to leave thinking the argument shows the mental does not exist rather than that it does not cause anything; I expect at least one to propose that the mental and the physical each contribute half of the effect; and I expect the determinism conflation to resurface the moment I say the word closure. Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end whose wrong answers would tell me which specific piece failed to land, rather than merely that something did.

Finally, be explicit about what I should cut and say out loud that I am cutting it. I assume the technical literature on counterfactual and interventionist notions of causation goes overboard at this level, but tell me whether there is something else that looks load-bearing and is not, and — more usefully — one thing that looks entirely dispensable to a section at this stage and would quietly wreck the hour if I dropped it.
