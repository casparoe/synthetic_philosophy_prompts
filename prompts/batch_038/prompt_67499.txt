I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on concept acquisition, and I want it in the form of a dialogue rather than an argumentative essay. The occasion: I have built a small Bongard-style problem — twelve figures, six on each side, presented with no instruction beyond "the figures on the left belong together, the figures on the right do not; say what the difference is" — and the seminar will spend an hour trying to solve it before we turn to what solving it is supposed to show.

The problem, exactly as I built it. Every figure contains three to six outlined polygons — triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons — of assorted sizes, some nested inside one another, some merely overlapping, some scattered across the frame; exactly one small filled dot lies inside one of those polygons. On the left, the number of polygons in the figure equals the number of sides of the polygon containing the dot (so: four shapes with the dot in a square; five shapes with the dot in a pentagon). On the right, the two numbers come apart. I then built a thirteenth figure as a probe: a large hexagon, a smaller triangle overlapping its lower-left corner, and a second small triangle lying wholly inside the first, with the dot inside the hexagon and inside the overlapping triangle but outside the inner one. Three shapes, two candidate containers, one with three sides and one with six — so the rule as I stated it does not determine an answer.

Pilot data I can hand them. Of fourteen participants, eleven solved it, median time about six minutes. Two said they could see which side a fresh figure belonged on well before they could say why. One, asked to draw a new left-hand figure, produced seven shapes with the dot in a heptagon — a shape that appears nowhere in my set. Of the four I showed the probe to, three called it a left-hand figure without hesitation and one said it "doesn't count." The three who failed offered rules that were false but perfectly statable: the dot is in the shape with the most sides; the shapes all nest; there are more shapes than sides.

Write the dialogue between two people sitting in front of the printout — call them Ilse and Ravi. Ilse is a philosopher of mind who holds that what gets called concept learning is never the minting of a concept but the selection of one from a space of hypotheses the learner could already represent: the hypothesis-testing argument, with its consequence that the interesting question is not where concepts come from but how the search is constrained. Ravi is a developmental cognitive scientist who holds that solving problems of this sort genuinely constructs a predicate that was not available beforehand — not merely a new combination of available ones — typically by recruiting something already in the system (an analog magnitude, a sensorimotor routine, a partial structural description) into a new role with new inferential commitments, and who takes my pilot data to be evidence of exactly that.

Requirements:

- Both are excellent, and neither may be sloppy about the other's view. In particular, Ilse must press the circularity worry in its strongest form: any account of constructing a new concept must describe the end state in vocabulary the theorist already has, and if that description is available to the theorist it is unclear why it should have been unavailable to the solver. Ravi's answer must be a real one — "it is a matter of degree," or "the concepts were already implicit," will not pass.
- The figures, not the literature, must carry the argument. At least three exchanges should turn on a specific datum — the seven-and-heptagon drawing, the two who saw before they could say, the probe, the statable-but-false rules — with each of them reading the same datum the opposite way, and neither reading a caricature.
- Include the exchange in which Ilse observes that compositionality already yields infinitely many hypotheses, so the unboundedness of the search space cannot by itself show that any of them is new; Ravi's reply must engage the difference between generating instances within a fixed type structure and altering the type structure itself — treating a count as something that can enter into an equation with another count computed elsewhere in the same figure.
- Each concedes at least one point that actually costs them.
- Neither converts. The dialogue should end with the crux stated twice, once in each idiom, so that the reader can hear that the two statements are not translations of one another.

Aim for 900–1,300 words of ordinary prose with speaker labels and no stage directions beyond what places them at the table. They may gesture toward the well-known names on each side, but in passing only: no titles, no dates, no reading list, no survey of the history of the debate. Someone who has never heard of Bongard problems should be able to follow; someone who works on concept acquisition should find both positions recognized rather than name-dropped. Do not have them spend time solving the problem — the solution is given above — and keep the arithmetic of my figures right.
