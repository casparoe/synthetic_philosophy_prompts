Three weeks out from my departmental colloquium and I still haven't nailed down my Q&A, which is a bad sign given that the paper is really just one argument plus a lot of scaffolding. I've been promising my coauthor a prep doc for a week and keep putting it off because writing it makes me realise how thin the third section is. Here's the setup. I'm defending the priority view — telic prioritarianism in Parfit's sense, where the moral weight of a benefit falls as the recipient's lifetime well-being rises — and my central claim is that the leveling-down objection has been made to do a job it can't actually do. Everyone treats it as the decisive argument for priority over telic egalitarianism: leveling down makes nobody better off, so equality can't be what matters, so it must be priority. My argument is that this doesn't discriminate between the two views at all. Both are equally committed to an impersonal betterness that isn't better-for-anyone; they differ only in the weighting. And the objector's real premise — that a betterness verdict corresponding to nobody's being better off is empty or confused — is a person-affecting premise, which if you accept it takes out prioritarianism right along with egalitarianism, since on my view too the claim that one distribution is impersonally better is not the claim that it is better for anyone in particular. So I want to say the leveling-down case at most indicts a family of views, and that the live question between equality and priority has to be settled by the shape of the weighting function in the middle and upper ranges rather than by intuitions about making the already-well-off worse off. I plan to concede right at the top that on my view leveling down is never all-things-considered called for, and that a deontic constraint is doing that work — I'm not commited to any sadistic policy, and I don't want to spend the question period pretending otherwise.

The room is the problem. About twenty-five people for a forty-five minute talk with twenty for questions. The core is luck egalitarians of the Cohen/Dworkin stripe, who will insist the whole dispute is really about responsibility and that my impersonal-value talk dodges the question of whose bad luck is whose doing. Two visitors from the public economics group down the hall, who think entirely in social welfare functions: they'll want to know whether my weighting is concave in well-being, whether it satisfies the Pigou-Dalton transfer principle, whether it's additively separable across persons, and above all what measurement assumptions it needs — they're polite, but they regard impersonal goodness talk as noise and one of them has been known to ask speakers to write their view down as an equation on the board. One person who does population ethics and will try to steer me toward the Repugnant Conclusion and ask whether a concave weighting escapes it or just trades it for something worse. Plus the usual crop of grad students, including one sharp utilitarian who thinks prioritarianism is nothing but utilitarianism with a monotone transform applied and will say so in exactly those words.

So give me the prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Eight to ten objections I should realistically expect from this room and no others, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the argument as sketched, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the economist's version should sound like an economist asking a question from the second row, not a philosopher summarizing welfare economics, and the luck egalitarian's should sound like someone who has heard the priority view a hundred times and is tired of it. For each one: a two-minute reply I can actually deliver standing up, a note on where that reply is softest and what they'll say back, and a flag for which ones I should just fold on. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to buy goodwill cheaply, and name the question I'm hoping nobody asks. I have a suspicion about what it is — something like: if all you've shown is that prioritarianism and egalitarianism are two monotone transforms of the same underlying quantity, have you shown anything at all, or just redescribed the disagreement? — but I'd rather you tell me honestly if the real threat is somewhere else entirely.
