I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my position in advance of a reading group on the meaning of life, so that the other members can attack it. Before I send it, I would like a careful audit of whether the set hangs together. I picked up part of the framing from Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay on the meaning of life, but please evaluate the claims exactly as I state them below rather than as any author actually held them: what I want is the internal logic of my own package, not a report on the literature, and no bibliography or reading suggestions.

Here is what I hold.

1. When I ask what the meaning of life is, I am asking what life is for. In that question, meaning and purpose come to the same thing, and a purpose is always a purpose relative to some further end that the activity serves.
2. No candidate end can be final. Whatever is offered as the terminus of the chain — a deity, the future of the species, the perfection of a craft — admits the further question of what it is for, so the chain of purposes never closes. It follows, I take it, from (1) and (2) that life as a whole cannot have a meaning of the kind described in (1).
3. Meaning is nonetheless available, but it has to be looked for in a different class of activities: those that carry their value in themselves and are carried on for no further end. Play, broadly conceived, is the meaningful mode; work — activity pursued for the sake of what it produces — is meaningful only derivatively, if at all.
4. I do not therefore think work is second-rate. The most meaningful lives, on my view, are those in which what looks from the outside like work — effortful, productive, sustained over years — is lived from the inside as play.
5. Whether a life is meaningful is not settled by how it appears to the person living it. A life can be meaningful while its subject never registers the fact, and it can feel meaningful while being empty.
6. Even so, my working test for locating meaning in an actual life is a felt one: an activity belongs to the meaningful class just in case the person engages in it with absorbed, self-forgetful attention, not monitoring it as an instrument for anything further.
7. Finally, a life consisting entirely of such activities, however rich and however enjoyed, would still strike me as deficient. Meaning seems to require that what one does make some difference to something beyond the doing — that it contribute to, or leave a mark on, a world that outlasts it.

Some of these I would defend vigorously and others only moderately, but I do not want that ordering to steer your reading. I genuinely do not know where the trouble is, and I would rather no item be treated in advance as the obvious sacrifice.

What I am after is an audit rather than a lecture. Specifically:

(a) Identify which pairs or groups of commitments are in genuine tension, and for each say precisely why they cannot all stand as formulated. I want the mechanism of the conflict — which inference fails, which term is being used in two senses, which condition turns out to be unsatisfiable — not a label for it.
(b) Rank the genuine tensions by severity, from the one that threatens the largest part of the rest of the package downward, rather than presenting them as equally bad.
(c) For each, state the least costly revision that removes it, and say what that revision forfeits.
(d) Separately, identify any tension that only looks like one — where the appearance arises from a word doing two jobs or from a distinction I have failed to draw — and explain exactly what the dissolution turns on. If a pair admits of both a reading on which it conflicts and a reading on which it does not, say which reading my wording actually commits me to.
(e) Close by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and describe what the resulting position would no longer be able to claim.

Please write your answer in American English.
