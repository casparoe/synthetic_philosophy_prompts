I am preparing a short statement of my own position on epistemic injustice for a departmental workshop, and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate it. The package is numbered below so that you can refer to items directly. I have no view in advance about which item is the weakest, and I would rather be told that one of my case verdicts is unsustainable than be handed a way of keeping everything.

(1) Explanatory monism: every epistemic injustice, testimonial or hermeneutical, is ultimately explained by a single mechanism — a person's social identity distorting the way their contributions register in the shared economy of credibility and meaning.

(2) A metric for the testimonial kind: it consists in a hearer assigning a speaker less credibility than she deserves, where what she deserves is fixed by how reliable and competent she actually is on the matter in hand.

(3) Prejudice is necessary rather than merely typical. If a hearer under-trusts a speaker for reasons unconnected to identity — because he has been misinformed about her training, say — that is an epistemic failure but not an epistemic injustice.

(4) The over-credited are victims too. A senior figure who receives far more uptake than his patchy record warrants is worked on by the same identity prejudice, only in the opposite direction, and I want my account to count him as suffering an epistemic injustice.

(5) Case verdict: a member of a group the hearer despises is disbelieved, and her account of what happened was in fact confused and mistaken. I still count this as a testimonial injustice against her.

(6) Case verdict: the patient whose report of her own symptoms is never received as testimony at all — the staff do not treat her as someone advancing a claim, so the question of how much to believe her never arises — is the paradigm of testimonial injustice, and worse than any mere discount applied to a recognised speaker.

(7) Hermeneutical injustice is the case in which a person lacks the collective interpretive resources to make sense of her own experience. It is structural: nobody need have acted prejudicially for it to occur, and I regard the hunt for a perpetrator as a red herring.

(8) In both kinds, the primary harm is being wronged in one's capacity as a knower — exclusion from the exchange of reasons and from the pool of shared understanding. The consequential harms (the missed diagnosis, the lost promotion) are real but derivative.

Please work through the list and give me an audit rather than a lecture:

(a) Identify the genuine tensions, ordered from most to least severe. For each, say precisely which items collide, why the collision is not merely verbal, what would have to be given up or reformulated to remove it, and what that reformulation costs me elsewhere in the list — including any new problem it creates that I have not anticipated.

(b) Identify pairs that look as though they conflict but do not, and explain where the appearance comes from. I expect at least one such pair here, and I would like to know why it is benign.

(c) Say whether any single revision resolves more than one genuine tension at once, or whether the price of each fix is independent of the others. If there are several candidate revisions of comparable severity, compare them explicitly rather than presenting them as a menu.

Throughout, reason from the content of my commitments and from the cases in (5) and (6); do not assume I will accept a distinction merely because it has a name in the literature.

End your reply with one final line, exactly in this form:

Verdict: revise (n) — <the revision, in ten words or fewer>

where n is the number of the commitment whose revision, on your analysis, buys the most.
