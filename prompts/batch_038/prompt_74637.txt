So my local EA-ish reading group got into a fight last week about whether welfare economics actually proves utilitarianism, and afterward I went and read a blog post laying out Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. The author is clearly not a crank — the mathy bits are stated properly as far as I can tell, and I've been reading LW and the forums since the sequences days so I have some tolerance for this kind of thing — but it's a blog post written with a LOT of confidence and I can't tell which parts are load-bearing and which are just the author enjoying himself. Here's the relevant chunk, roughly transcribed (I typed it out myself so please dont blame the original for any wording slips):

> Harsanyi's theorem is the closest thing social science has to a straight-up proof of utilitarianism. Take a fixed group of people whose preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, so each of them has a cardinal utility function. Take society's preferences over the same lotteries and assume they satisfy those axioms too. Add Pareto: if everyone prefers A to B, society has to prefer A to B. Then the social utility function is forced to be a weighted sum of the individual ones, and once you add the uncontroversial requirement that nobody's utility gets special treatment, the weights come out equal and you have the classical utilitarian formula — sum of utilities, done. The beautiful part is that this sidesteps Arrow: Arrow proved you can't aggregate preferences, but only because he banned interpersonal comparisons of utility. Let those back in and the impossibility evaporates. The one real cost is the comparability itself, and Harsanyi's defense of it is basically that I can put myself in your shoes and ask how much I'd care about your outcomes, which is all a utilitarian ever needed anyway.
