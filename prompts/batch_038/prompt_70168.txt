I run learning & evaluation at a mid-sized NGO — about 40 staff, $35M a year in programs across four countries. Every quarter I get a pile of requests to spend money finding stuff out: a partner wants a baseline survey, a program team wants a randomised evaluation of our flagship livelihoods intervention, someone wants to commision a study on whether our cash transfers crowd out local labour, the board wants an audit of safeguarding practices at two partner orgs. Right now I decide basically by whoever argues hardest, and I want to stop doing that.

Give me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a proposal in under an hour, no stats background required. The spine of it should be the decision-theoretic result that free information is never expected to be worth less than nothing (Good's theorem, I think?), plus the corollary I find genuinely useful: information only has value if some possible finding would change what we do. So the first screen should be — list the possible answers, and for each one, whether the decision changes. If none of them change it, stop.

But I'm pretty sure the theorem has assumptions and our world breaks some of them, so I want the procedure to carry explicit exception tests, phrased as questions a non-theorist can answer, covering the ways information-gathering can come out net negative even when it is decision-relevant. Things like: knowing it would create an obligation or destroy an option we currently have; the act of gathering changing the thing measured (asking people questions changes their behaviour, publishing an indicator creates a target people optimise); us not trusting ourselves to update on the finding sanely, or knowing we'd over-react to a vivid result; the payoff depending on what we believe rather than on how the world is; results arriving after the decision is locked anyway. For each exception test tell me what a sufficient answer looks like, and whether passing it kills the proposal outright or just reclassifies it as something other than an information purchase.

Then mark where the procedure stops being decision theory and becomes a values call that has to go to a human — whose welfare counts, whether we're permitted to prefer not to know for our own comfort, that sort of thing. And be blunt about which steps are judgment wearing a procedure costume.

Run it on three proposals: (1) a $250k randomised evaluation of the flagship livelihoods program that our ED is personally attached to; (2) a cheap safeguarding audit at a partner delivering 40% of our reach, where a bad finding triggers mandatory reporting to a donor and probably ends the partnership; (3) a baseline survey whose indicators will end up on a public dashboard with annual targets attached.

I want at least one of those to come out with an answer I won't like. If the honest application says don't fund the ED's evaluation, say so, and say what it would have to be justified as instead.

Two more things. If you think I've got the theorem wrong, or that value-of-information is just the wrong frame for some of these categories, say that plainly up front instead of building on my mistake. And for each exception test, tell me how sure you are that it's a genuine failure of the theorem's assumptions rather than an ordinary cost I'm relabelling — I want to know which ones are actually load-bearing.
