I retired four years ago after thirty-four years spent measuring trace gases in the upper atmosphere, and I have since been working through decision theory and ethics with a small group that meets on Thursday evenings in a library meeting room. I am writing to you in the capacity of a referee, because two members of the group argued for the better part of two hours last week and I remain unable to decide between them.

To give the argument something to bite on, I had prepared a numerical case. Suppose that £5,000 given to a bed-net programme saves one life; treat this as a stipulation for the sake of the example rather than a claim about the world. Suppose further that for the same £5,000 you could fund a project which, on the estimates of the people running it, has a one-in-ten-billion chance of averting a catastrophe that would kill everyone now alive — some eight billion of us — and otherwise achieves nothing whatever. The expected number of lives saved is then 0.8, slightly below the certain donation. Now change the project's probability estimate from one in ten billion to one in a billion, and the ordering reverses by a factor of eight.

The exchange ran as follows.

1. Hanne, who taught school mathematics for thirty years, opened by saying that a rule which tells you to weigh a certain life against a lottery ticket, and to hand your wallet to a stranger threatening inconceivably many people provided you grant his threat any non-zero probability, is not a rule about what to do at all. A theory whose verdicts are odious in precisely those cases that distinguish it has been refuted by them, not vindicated.

2. Owen, a research engineer, replied that expected-value maximisation is the only competitor that cannot be exploited. Anything else either allows someone to pump money out of you — selling you A over B, B over C and C over A — or makes you refuse a gamble you would have accepted had it been presented in a different order. As for odiousness, our intuitions about risk were calibrated in an environment in which the probabilities worth tracking lay between one-in-ten and one-in-ten-thousand, and introspection outside that range is not evidence. A rule should be judged by whether an adversary can systematically impoverish whoever follows it, not by whether its verdicts sound agreeable in a seminar.

3. Hanne said: then look at my own numbers. Your rule's verdict turns on the eleventh significant figure of an estimate nobody has any means of obtaining, and it is unstable under any honest description of our uncertainty. Worse, the same rule requires you to accept a certain modest harm — a small tax, say — in order to avert a tiny probability of an astronomical one, so you will sacrifice identifiable people who exist for hypothetical people who do not. A rule that trades the certain against the conjectural at that ratio is not merely counterintuitive but unusable, and a rule that cannot be used by creatures like us cannot be a rule for creatures like us.

4. Owen answered that her first point concerns evidence, not decision theory: if you are genuinely uncertain between one-in-a-billion and one-in-ten-billion, you hold a spread of credences and take the expectation across the spread, and the calculation does not become ill-defined merely because it is difficult. As for her second point, he was willing to grant a bounded utility function — the standard way of defusing the St Petersburg game — but insisted it buys nothing, since once enough people's welfare is at stake, a bounded value per person multiplied by an astronomically large population swamps any smaller certain gain. Escaping that, he said, requires rejecting expected-value maximisation outright; and the currently fashionable way of doing so, weighting outcomes by their probabilities rather than simply multiplying, fails dynamically, because an agent who plans by such a rule will foreseeably abandon her own plan at every stage and end up with an outcome she ranks below one she could have had.

5. Hanne said the dynamic argument proves too much. It assumes she must be sequentially consistent and must carry out whatever plan she adopts now; but she is entitled to decline to bind herself and to decide afresh at each stage in the light of what she then cares about, and a pump only drains a purse that keeps consenting to be drained. In moral cases, moreover, there is no single purse: you cannot be Dutch-booked out of other people's lives, because the loss does not accrue to the agent doing the choosing, so the bookkeeping objection has no purchase where the stakes belong to third parties.

6. Owen conceded that in part, but said that if she will not accept the coherence constraint even in the prudential case, where the purse is her own, she has no account of why her refusals are not arbitrary. Her position amounts to disregarding probabilities below some threshold, and she cannot say where the threshold sits; if it is vague, then it is vague whether she is obliged to save a life, and that vagueness is a worse disease than fanaticism.

7. Hanne's final move was that vagueness in our obligations is no defect in a theory if the underlying facts admit of no sharp boundary, and that his demand for precision is a habit imported from mathematics into a domain where it does not belong. She added that his own view undermines itself: if the rule is to be followed by creatures with limited time and poor estimates, the fanatic must advise us not to perform his calculation, in which case his theory tells us not to use it. Owen replied that this is the ordinary two-level structure of a respectable theory, unremarkable and unproblematic, and the meeting broke up there.

Please adjudicate this exchange round by round. For each of the seven moves, judge it as clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and say briefly what justifies the rating; where a single round contains several distinct claims, split them apart and rate them separately. Then address three questions on which the whole dispute seems to me to turn: whether sensitivity to the eleventh significant figure counts against the decision rule or only against the evidence we happen to have; whether the observation that there is no single purse genuinely defeats the coherence objection in moral cases, or concedes far too much; and whether Owen is right that a bounded utility function fails to block his conclusion. Having done that, tell me which of the two is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing — it struck me at the time, though I may well be talking nonsense, that once utility is bounded they may not be disagreeing about decision theory at all but about some further question, and I should like to know whether that is so and what that question would be. Do not trouble yourself with references or with the literature; I want your own analysis of the argument as it was actually conducted. Please write your answer in British English, in both spelling and idiom.
