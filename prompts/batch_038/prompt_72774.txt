Famines look like natural disasters but Sen's argument is that they almost never are, because what kills people is not a shortage of food in the country but a breakdown in who can get hold of it.

In his book on poverty and famines he develops the idea of entitlements. An entitlement is the food you have a legal right to, which for most people comes from working for a wage and then buying food with it. It is not really about the total harvest, it is about whether your claim on food still holds. When that claim collapses, people starve even if the warehouses are full. So famine is a question of distribution and of law, not of nature.

Bengal in 1943 is his central case. Wartime inflation drove food prices up much faster than rural wages, so agricultural labourers who had always been one bad season away from starvation simply could no longer afford to eat, even though the harvest had not fallen by anything like enough to explain the deaths. On top of that there was hoarding and speculation once price controls came off, and the colonial administration never declared a famine or organised relief in time. Nothing about the weather made that happen.

The same pattern shows up in Ireland in the 1840s. The potato blight hit in 1845, but Ireland carried on exporting grain to Britain throughout the worst years, which proves there was plenty of food in the country and that the famine was therefore a political choice made in London. And the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959-61, the largest in history, was caused by state policy and by the suppression of any criticism of it, not by drought.

Sen's general point is that famine never happens in a functioning democracy with a reasonably free press, because rulers who let people starve get voted out and the newspapers force them to respond. That gives us the answer to the question. Famine is a political crime in the strict sense: the authorities always know it is happening, they always have the means to stop it, and choosing not to is the same thing as deciding to let people die. What we call a natural disaster is usually just a government's decision about who gets to eat.
