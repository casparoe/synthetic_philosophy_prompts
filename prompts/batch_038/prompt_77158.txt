I'm in my second year of a philosophy PhD and I'm trying to write a chapter about how much you can actually get out of objects alone — like, what licenses an inference from a pile of stuff in the ground to a claim about what people believed, or about the institutions they lived under. My committee has an archaeologist on it and she keeps telling me the chapter is too abstract, so I want to anchor it with a concrete foil, and the one I've settled on is R.G. Collingwood. He seems like the perfect test case because he's the rare philosopher who really dug — summers on Roman Britain sites with his father, who was an archaeologist and artist himself — and then went on to write the philosophy of history that got published posthumously in 1946 as The Idea of History. But he died in January 1943, which means he never saw radiocarbon dating, never saw the processual revolution, never saw the post-processual backlash, and obviously never saw ancient DNA. Last term in a reading group somebody argued Collingwood basically anticipated Kuhn with the absolute presuppositions stuff from the 1940 Essay on Metaphysics, and I want to know if that's right or a stretch, because it might matter for how he'd take the rest of this.

So here's what I want: put Collingwood in the room with the last eighty years of archaeological theory and tell me where he lands, item by item, and actually commit to a position rather than defaulting to "he'd be suspicious of scientism." First, the processual program — archaeology as anthropology, explanation as causal and generalizing rather than narrative, explicit hypothesis testing built into excavation design, that whole systems-and-environment framing. I suspect he's a weird half-ally here, because his sharp line between history and natural science pushes one way while his insistence that the scientific historian interrogates the evidence instead of just compiling what the authorities say pushes the other, and I'd like you to say exactly where those two pull apart. Second, and connected: would he treat the naturalistic framework behind processualism as one of those absolute presuppositions that can't be true or false but only belong to a constellation, and is that a way of being generous to the processualists without conceding anything to them? Third — this is the part I care about most — inference to institutions rather than to individual beliefs, which is where re-enactment looks least useful. Nobody's thought is available when you argue that a building was a temple and not a chief's hall, that a site sat at the center of a redistributive palace economy, that a change in burial furnishings tracks a shift toward inherited rank, or that a pottery style change means something about how households were organized. The target is a norm, a regularity, an aggregate over people who never thought about it. Is that genuine historical knowledge for Collingwood, natural science wearing a history costume, or something his categories have no slot for at all? Fourth, evidence that isn't testimony in any sense: tree rings, isotopes telling you where somebody grew up and moved, aDNA telling you about kinship and mass migration. And there's a nice irony I want you to deal with — the culture-historical habit of equating pots with peoples got demolished from the sixties through the eighties, and then genetics partly brought mass migration back into play. Where does he come out? Fifth, the interpretive turn: material culture treated as meaningful and read like a text, multivocality, objects having agency, and then the more recent stuff that wants to erase the nature/history boundary he built his whole system on. Does he think that's the history of thought extended to objects, which is what he wanted, or a category error?

For each of the five, give me a verdict, name the doctrine that's doing the work (re-enactment, the inside and outside of an event, the question-and-answer logic, encapsulation, absolute presuppositions, the scale of forms if it's relevant), and a short argument for it. Then separate three things that I think are going to get collapsed if you're not careful: what he'd have endorsed, what he'd have rejected, and what he'd have been forced to revise — and for the revisions, tell me which specific new evidence or argument does the forcing, keeping in mind that he was moving fast between 1938 and 1940 and the late work is unfinished, so some of these questions may just have no Collingwoodian answer. Where that's the case, say so plainly and say what would have to be added to his framework to make room. I don't want a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, and I don't want a survey of what the secondary literature says about him or about processualism — I want the argument. And if you think any of my framings above are wrong or anachronistic (like if I've mis-stated what the processualists were actually claiming, or if the Kuhn comparison is just bad), push back on me in the answer instead of playing along with it.
