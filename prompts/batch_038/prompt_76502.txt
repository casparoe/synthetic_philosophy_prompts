I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on statements that are literally true and reliably misleading — the press secretary who answers a question he was not asked with a sentence nobody disputes. A philosopher I interviewed sketched the argument below to explain why such statements are supposedly less culpable than outright lies, and I left the conversation uneasy: it appears to sweep away the very distinction it was offered to defend.

(1) To lie is to assert what you believe to be false.
(2) To mislead is to bring it about that your hearer believes something false.
(3) When you assert p, the only thing you contribute is p; every further belief your hearer forms is the product of an inference they drew on their own.
(4) So if you assert only what you believe true, any falsity in your hearer's mind arrived by their inference rather than by anything you said, and you are not answerable for it.
(5) Hence the artful truth-teller escapes blame where the liar does not: she contributes truths, and the hearer supplies the error.
(6) But (3) draws no such distinction. When the liar asserts p while believing not-p, her hearer likewise arrives at a false belief only by inferring from her words to the world, and her contribution too is nothing but a sentence.
(7) If (3) and (4) exculpate the truthful misleader, they exculpate the liar: in both cases the false belief is the hearer's own inferential doing.
(8) So no speaker is answerable for any false belief she produces in a hearer — and since the wrongness of lying is supposed to consist precisely in producing such a belief, lying is not wrong either.

I am confident (8) is false, and if forced to choose I would keep (5)'s asymmetry — a flat-out falsehood still strikes me as worse than a curated truth — rather than (3). But I cannot state a line that does not look arbitrary, and my editor will ask for one. Which step fails? Why did it look acceptable going in? And what does the repair cost: if I give up (3), am I committed to holding speakers answerable for every inference a hearer might draw, and if I instead revise (1) or (2), what do I lose?
