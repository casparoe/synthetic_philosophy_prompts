My old supervisor — emeritus now, don't name him, he'd hate it — wrote a short annual review essay for a journal for something like twenty-five years, and the conceit was always the same: he'd take up some historical or proposed arrangement and rule on whether it counted as having answered the socialist calculation problem. I'm going back through the whole run for a chapter I'm writing and the verdicts are weirdly disciplined, but I can't pin down the rule he's operating with. Here's the record as best I've reconstructed it. On Lange's trial-and-error scheme he says Lange won the exchange as it was originally framed, that the solution is formally valid and practically empty, and that Hayek's 1945 piece moved the target while Lange never followed up. On Soviet material-balance planning: fail, and he is emphatic that the failure was not arithmetic — the balances did get balanced — but that the inputs were systematically falsified by the very enterprises reporting them. On Chile's Cybersyn under Beer: fail, but he calls it the most instructive near-miss in the whole literature and grants that fast telemetry demonstrably carried some of the load people assumed only prices could carry. On the Cockshott and Cottrell line of argument — input-output planning with labour-time accounting on modern hardware — he concedes, in print and cheerfully, that it kills the arithmetic objection dead and that anyone still pressing the intractability line is embarrassing themselves; but then he says it answers a question nobody in the debate ever actually asked. On the internal allocation machinery of the giant retailers: he refuses to call it planning at all, describing it as an island of deliberate direction that only stays rational because it's moored to real exchange prices at its edges. On open-source software production: genuine non-market coordination, real value, and not a counterexample, because in that domain consumption isn't rivalrous, so there's nothing to economize on and therefore nothing to calculate. On the American wartime production boards: the strongest historical case for planning there is, pass — but he adds that they had one overriding objective and could borrow the prewar relative-price structure. On national grid dispatch: also planning, also successful, same two caveats, physical objective plus fuel bought at market prices. On Yugoslav market socialism: fail, though he blames soft budget constraints and politically fixed prices and says explicitly that this is not a strike against markets as such. He's also fond of insisting that the incentive problem and the knowledge problem are two problems and that collapsing them is what ruins most of the writing on the subject.

The reason I care: there's a design proposal circulating from a small think tank that he's been asked to give a formal opinion on, and I've seen it. Post-transition economy, fully digital. Every household has an app through which they state, continuously, what they want out of a menu of concrete bundles; every enterprise is instrumented so that capacities, stocks, labour hours and input coefficients are known in real time rather than self-reported; an engine solves for a feasible allocation that maximizes the aggregate of stated satisfactions and ships the goods. No money, no exchange of any kind — nothing is ever bought or sold by anybody. And the engine does compute a full vector of shadow prices for every good and service, but these are used only for bookkeeping and for grading enterprise performance; they are never consulted to decide what gets produced or who gets it. Two friends of mine are sure they know what he'll say and they disagree. One says he'll dismiss it as Lange with better hardware — the shadow-price vector is the Lange board by another name and the app-stated wants are exactly the data Lange was allowed to take as given. The other says he'll be far more generous, because he already conceded the computational point to Cockshott, he already gave Cybersyn credit for telemetry, and the instrumentation removes the falsification problem he blamed the Soviets for.

Tell me honestly whether the record genuinely underdetermines this or whether you think it leans one way, and say which of his past verdicts is the closest analog and where the analogy breaks.
