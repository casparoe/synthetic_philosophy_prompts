im doing a small sidebar for a piece on how philosophy of language absorbed the shock of necessary-but-only-discoverable truths, and i'd rather open it with a poem than a diagram. Can you write me a sonnet on two-dimensional semantics? Please don't use water or Twin Earth — i want this to feel like a discovery rather than a citation, so invent the case: say a fishing town has a word for the pale green light that shows up in thier harbour on certain still nights, the concept fixed entirely by how it looks from the shore, and then comes the later finding that it's one particular organism doing one particular thing. The octave should be the first dimension — the concept as a rule for picking out whatever would play that role if this world were the one that's actual, something you could deploy without leaving the house. The volta is the slide into the second: the world gets fixed, the reference goes rigid, the identity turns out to be necessary, and you only ever learn it by getting in a boat. Then the couplet should be the diagonal — that third way of reading the grid, where the world you're describing and the world you're describing it from turn out to be the same one, which hands you back something knowable from an armchair after all. No names, no titles, and please don't let the word "intension" show up in the poem itself; someone who knows the literature should be able to point at each move and say which is which.
