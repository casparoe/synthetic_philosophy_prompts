In three weeks I present a paper at a closed two-day workshop on the nature of normativity. There will be about fifteen participants: roughly half work in the fittingness-first tradition in value theory and treat fittingness as the sole normative primitive; three defend a reasons-first, broadly Scanlonian picture on which reasons are unanalyzable and everything else is explained in terms of them; two work on the ethics of belief and on doxastic voluntarism; one is an accuracy-first formal epistemologist; and the most senior person in the room has a standing reputation for arguing that disputes of this kind turn out to be verbal. Nobody in the room works on philosophy of action or on the structure of practical reasoning, which matters a great deal, because my argument is conducted entirely in that vocabulary and will be heard as psychologism.

The thesis. I defend an account of the wrong kind of reasons problem — the familiar demon scenario, in which a powerful being threatens you with punishment unless you admire him, and the threat appears to be a reason to admire him of exactly the sort that ought not to count — which I call the eligibility account. It has three parts.

1. A consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude only if it is eligible to serve as a premise in the first-personal deliberation that settles the question the attitude answers.
2. Wrong-kind considerations are genuine normative reasons, but reasons for something else: they bear on the practical question of what to do about one's attitude — whether to cultivate it, retain it, or bring it about — and they are misfiled when we report them as reasons to have it.
3. It follows that right-kind and wrong-kind considerations never enter a single weighing. The problem is therefore not solved but dissolved: it presupposed a comparison that never arises.

I concede in the paper what the load-bearing wall is. Part (1) is meant to explain what makes a reason right-kind in terms of deliberative eligibility rather than in terms of fittingness, so eligibility cannot be defined by appeal to fittingness without circularity. I also hold that eligibility is capacity-relative but not exercise-relative — it concerns what the agent's deliberative capacities could take up, not what she happens to be attending to — and that which question an attitude answers is fixed by the attitude's type rather than by the agent's preferences.

The Q&A will run twenty minutes. Please prepare a prep sheet, not a defense of the view:

- The eight to ten objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones an action-theory audience or an informal rationality forum would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly.
- Each objection stated in the first-person voice and idiom of the kind of person who will raise it; identify that person (fittingness-first value theorist, reasons-first Scanlonian, voluntarist, accuracy-first epistemologist, the senior deflationist) and use the technical vocabulary they would use rather than mine.
- For each, a reply deliverable in under two minutes, an explicit note on where that reply is softest, and whether anything should be conceded rather than defended.
- Flag separately any objection on which my natural reply risks collapsing the eligibility account back into the fittingness-first view, so that I know where I am trading the paper's distinctiveness for safety.
- The two or three concessions that, made in the opening minutes, would defuse the most fire; and the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the room at all.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks: the one for which I have no good answer, put as bluntly as the most senior person present would put it, together with my least-bad response and a candid estimate of what it costs me if it comes.

Please do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I know the literature and want the dialectic, not the sourcing.
