Retired atmospheric modeller here — thirty-odd years running numerical weather prediction, now spending my retirement on philosophy of mind and AI, which is turning out to be harder than the Navier-Stokes. Last week a young roboticist gave a talk at our local group and he explained the frame problem with an analogy I can't stop turning over. His pitch was roughly: think of a robot's world-model as one enormous spreadsheet. Each cell is some fact about the world — door open, battery on wagon, light on — and each formula is a rule about how facts depend on each other. An action is editing one cell; the frame problem is that you also have to say explicitly which cells stay untouched. But a spreadsheet doesn't need that: recalculate and every formula already knows its own dependencies, so anything you didn't hook up simply doesn't move. Conclusion he drew from this: the frame problem is a solved bookkeeping exercise, a matter of finishing filling in the formulas, and the philosophers who claim it reveals something deep about mind are making a mystery out of a clerical task. He was pretty dismissive about it.

Here's my trouble: the analogy felt *right* to me, possibly too right. In my old field you truncate the system, you write down your prognostic variables, and anything not in the model is by construction not there — no frame axioms needed, because the closed set was the whole point. So I want a proper audit rather than a vibe.

Give me the correspondence table: cells, formulas, the dependency graph, recalculation, locked cells, the circular-reference warning, the human who wrote the formulas in the first place — what does each map onto in the logic/planning version of the problem? Then where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care about most — sort those breaks into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that are just surface differences nobody should weight. What does the spreadsheet picture quietly import that a real world-model doesn't have? Then tell me whether "it's just bookkeeping, now solved" survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole way. He also tossed off two other named problems — a qualification one and a ramification one — and I'd like to know whether the spreadsheet has any room for them at all, and whether it can reach the wider version people use the term for, the one about how you decide what's relevant without already having a completed list of what's relevant (I think there's a thought experiment with three robots that all get blown up for different reasons). And if you think the analogy does more harm than good, offer a better one — maybe a different one for the narrow problem and for the wide one.

Two things on how you answer. First, be clear about where the field is actually settled versus where it's genuinely disputed among people who work on this; I've read that the original logic problem was basically cracked by the end of the eighties, but I also read that claim hedged everywhere, and I don't want a contested position handed to me as consensus. Second, flag every place where I've (or he's) slid between different senses of the phrase — my suspicion is the analogy only works on one of them. No reading list, please, just argue it through.
