I'm auditing a Warring States seminar this term, mostly to keep my classical Chinese from rusting, and the running argument in my apartment has turned into Mencius vs. Xunzi on human nature. My roommate's position is that Xunzi is just a pessimist with better prose and that once you've got the kid-about-to-fall-into-the-well case there's nothing left to say. i dont buy that — I think the Xunzian side has real resources and my roommate is arguing against a bumper sticker — and I'd rather find out by losing an argument to it properly than by reading another summary. So here's what I want: play a committed Xunzian. Not a caricature, not "human nature is evil" as a slogan — someone who genuinely thinks the Mencians are the ones telling a flattering fairy tale, who will cheerfully concede small points (that the alarm at the well-child is a real feeling, say, or that ritual has to work with the grain of something) but never the main one. Stay in character until I type stop, and if you need a fact about one of my modern cases, ask me for it in character instead of breaking out to lecture me. Two house rules: keep each reply to roughly 150 words so this feels like a conversation and not an essay, and no scholarship — no chapter numbers, no translators, no titles of books or articles, no modern philosophers unless I name one first. Paraphrase the text's images and arguments in your own words rather than quoting translations at me, I don't have the books in front of me anyway. I'll be bringing contemporary material at you as we go — the bystander who does nothing at a subway platform, the way neighborhoods either loot or organize after a flood, anonymous pile-ons online — and you should treat those as stuff to reason about directly, not as prompts to cite studies you'd have to guess at.

My opening move, and I want you to take it seriously rather than wave it off. Your whole edifice rests on artifice: ritual and righteousness are made, not grown, like the potter's vessel or the crooked wood steamed straight against a mold, and the reason we want goodness at all is that our nature lacks it, the same way we want wealth because we're poor and want beauty because we're plain. Fine, grant that. Then explain the first potter. If nature is just a tangle of appetites with no tilt toward order whatsoever, where does the capacity to step back and see that the appetites are collectively self-defeating come from — and why isn't whatever enables that seeing, and that stepping back, exactly the sort of thing the Mencians are calling a sprout? You insist the sage's nature is no different from mine and that accumulation of learning is open to anyone, that an ordinary person could in principle become a sage. But that promise seems to me to smuggle in the very thing you deny: something in the ordinary person already pointed at the good, otherwise your accumulation is just one appetite outvoting another and your ritual has no more normative grip than this season's fashion. And the specific thing I most want answered, so please don't skip it: what is the alarm at the well-child on your account? Say it's trained in, say it's the residue of a ritualized household, say it's a lucky accident of appetite — whichever you like — but then tell me why that same account doesn't equally explain away the desire for order that your sages are supposed to have felt, since on your story neither is native. If neither is native, why is one of them the seed of civilization and the other just noise?
