I run a small grad reading group on decision theory, and we've spent the last three weeks on Good's result that free evidence can never hurt you — the one that says, roughly, if you'd have to choose either way but could first learn the answer to some question at zero cost, then planning to learn and then maximize on your posterior is never worse ex ante than choosing now, because the average of the maxima beats the maximum of the averages. The students have gotten good at sorting apparent counterexamples into the three escape hatches we came up with on week one: (i) there's a hidden cost you're not counting (attention, time, delay); (ii) the learning itself creeps into the utility function, so the state of knowing changes what the outcomes are worth — spoilers, dread, suspense; (iii) the agent downstream isn't actually a conditionalizing expected-utility maximizer, so the theorem's own hypothesis fails and there's nothing to contradict. My colleague Tomás thinks (iii) is a cheat, that anything you can build in (iii) is really (ii) wearing a disguise.

Here's the case that broke our taxonomy, courtesy of a student named Dana. Priya is coordinating flood relief and has to pick tonight between sending the shipment downriver or overland. Downriver: 100 units of value if the river's passable at the crossing, 20 if it isn't. Overland: 60 if passable, 55 if not — slow but nearly certain. Her evidence about the crossing is genuinely mixed, so she doesn't have a sharp credence; she carries the interval 0.55–0.8 for passable, and she chooses by maximin over that set, which gets her the river (64 against roughly 57.8). There's a gauge station upstream she could radio for free; it takes five minutes, the operator answers plainly either the water's low or it isn't, so the evidence is a clean two-cell partition, and nothing about the call changes what a delivered shipment is worth to anyone — no suspense, no dread, no spoiler, no cost. But Dana stipulates that Priya knows the gauge is unreliable about *this* reach, and she knows that hearing either answer will leave her unsure whether the reading was current, so whichever way it comes back her interval dilates to 0.3–0.95. Maximin on the dilated set then ranks overland at 56.5 against the river at 44, so she flips. Compared ex ante, taking the free call costs her about 7.5 units, and she knows it, so she doesn't make the call.

So: give me your credence that Priya is instrumentally rational here — that no correct account of instrumental rationality condemns declining that call, given everything stipulated. I want the number and the decomposition, not a survey. Break it into the sub-questions you think actually do the work, each with a rough probability and a line of justification: whether a set of priors plus maximin is a rationally permissible doxastic-and-choice state at all, or whether ambiguity aversion of that kind is incoherent; whether Tomás is right that maximin over a set *is* a utility function with a term for one's own confidence state, which would collapse the case into hatch (ii); whether instrumental rationality is even the right frame when the two options can't be ranked by a single expected utility number, since she has no single credence to compute one with — I worry our whole "she loses 7.5" comparison already smuggles in a way of ranking plans that a set-valued agent doesn't have; and whether rationality should be assessed at the node where she declines the call or across the plan as a whole. Show me how you combine them, tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what would move it, and then — since I'm sitting at about 0.6 that she's rational and Tomás is at about 0.2 — tell me which of those sub-questions you think the two of us are actually disagreeing about. No references or further reading, please; I know where this literature lives, I just want your reasoning laid out.
