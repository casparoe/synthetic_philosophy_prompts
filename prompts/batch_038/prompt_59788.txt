I run corporate strategy at a mid-sized firm that makes industrial control software, and in three weeks our board will decide whether to adopt a proposal I drafted. The proposal is the aggressive one: before the industry consortium picks a reference architecture in the autumn, we would publish our specification under an irrevocable licence, bind ourselves contractually to a fixed deployment date with liquidated damages attached, and release our roadmap in enough detail that reversing course would be reputationally ruinous. We would, in short, burn our bridges in public. A colleague on the executive team thinks this is reckless, and after a month of arguing with him I have come around to a position I cannot yet defend properly and want your help putting into its strongest form.

Here is the situation as I understand it. There are only two serious contenders: us, and a rival roughly four times our size. Adopters — utilities, integrators, the consortium's own technical secretariat — will coalesce around whichever specification looks safe to build against. Before the decision date each firm can do one of three things: (i) commit irreversibly, as my proposal does; (ii) stay silent and keep every option open; or (iii) make a commitment that is legible but explicitly revocable at a published price — announce the same architecture, the same date, the same obligations, but attach a contractual exit with a liquidated penalty large enough to hurt us and to compensate adopters who relied on us. Neither firm knows the other's true costs; both know the structure. The payoffs look like this: if exactly one firm is legibly committed at the decision date, the industry coordinates on it and that firm captures most of the surplus. If both commit irreversibly, neither can back down without destroying itself, the consortium stalls for years, and a slower third firm takes the market. If neither commits, the standard is set by a political process in which the larger rival wins on weight alone.

The thesis I want argued is that (iii) dominates (i) in a race of this kind, and — the more general half, which matters to me more — that the familiar Schellingian insight about commitment, the one that says a party gains bargaining power by visibly destroying its own options, is standardly misapplied. Its force, I want the argument to claim, comes entirely from what the destruction communicates, not from the destruction; so wherever a player can buy the same message more cheaply by pricing an option instead of forfeiting it, the rational player keeps the option and pays for the signal. Irreversibility is then a technology of last resort for parties who cannot otherwise be believed, and treating it as the paradigm case of commitment leads firms to destroy option value they never needed to destroy.

Please make that case as rigorously as you can, in roughly 1,200 words. I am not looking for a literature review or references — argue from the structure of the case itself. I do want four objections confronted rather than noted in passing: that a revocable commitment is only cheap talk, since the opponent can compute that we will pay the exit price when the moment actually comes; that if both firms choose (iii) we have merely relabelled the branch in which nobody commits, and the larger rival wins anyway; that credibility in genuinely high-stakes bargaining often depends on the real possibility of losing control of events, so that my argument would prove far too much and soften every promise, contract, and deterrent threat; and that a firm which publishes its exit terms selects for counterparties who plan to use them.

Then tell me what the argument depends on empirically — the one or two claims about how adopters actually behave that would have to hold for it to work — and how the conclusion changes if the consortium's decision is not a single date but a rolling process repeated annually. Finally, state plainly where you think my colleague is right and my thesis overreaches.
