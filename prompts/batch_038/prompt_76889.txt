Here is the case that most of my commitments turn on, stated self-containedly. It is a version of the Judy Benjamin problem. During a field exercise, Judy must locate a company that is either in the North sector or in the South sector of the operating area, with no other possibility. The hills in which the company might be dug in lie wholly inside the North sector; that sector also contains a valley, while the South sector is flat, so the company can be in the hills only if it is in the North. Judy's prior is P(North) = P(South) = 1/2, and conditional on North she assigns P(Hills) = 3/4 and P(Valley) = 1/4. She then receives a radio message from the duty officer at headquarters, whom she regards as honest and well informed, whose entire informational content as far as she can tell is this: if the company is in the North sector, then it is in the hills. He says nothing about which sector the company actually occupies. The question is what Judy's posterior credence in North ought to be.

My statement of position asserts all of the following:

1. Adams' thesis, understood as a norm of acceptance: it is rational to accept "if A, B" precisely to the extent that one's conditional credence P(B|A) is high. I regard this as the fundamental datum about indicative conditionals that any account must explain, and I deliberately formulate it as a claim about acceptance and assertibility rather than as an identity between the probability of some proposition and a conditional probability.
2. Lewis's triviality results are correct and decisive: no non-degenerate proposition can have a probability equal to the corresponding conditional probability across all credence functions. Indicative conditionals are therefore not truth-apt, and I am a suppositionalist about them.
3. The Ramsey test models what I am doing when I evaluate a conditional, and supposing the antecedent is faithfully represented by conditionalizing on it. Because the procedure iterates, I think compounds and embedded conditionals — disjunctions with conditional disjuncts, conditionals with conditional parts — are handled by iterating it, and I am not worried in principle about assigning credences to such sentences.
4. My norm for updating is uniform: the rational posterior is the credence function minimizing relative entropy to the prior, subject to exactly the constraints the evidence imposes. I hold this because it appears to generate both ordinary conditionalization and Jeffrey conditionalization as special cases, and I do not want a bespoke rule for every kind of evidence.
5. In the case above, the right answer is that P(North) remains 1/2 while P(Hills|North) goes to 1 — this is, so far as I can determine, the answer van Fraassen argues for. My reason: the officer's competence concerns conditionals, and a source that is silent about the antecedent should not move my credence in the antecedent at all.
6. Order and route invariance: agents who end up with the same total evidence should end up with the same credences, so a posterior should not depend on the sequence in which items of evidence arrived. Relatedly, whatever rule governs conditional testimony should be applicable whenever a reliable source asserts a conditional, not only in privileged circumstances.
7. One assumption I have not argued for. A member of my reading group maintains that there are generalized triviality arguments against Adams' thesis that do not presuppose conditionals are truth-apt, so that items 1 and 2 collapse together anyway. I have been dismissing this on the grounds that Lewis's argument quantifies over propositions and so cannot touch a thesis formulated purely about acceptance.
