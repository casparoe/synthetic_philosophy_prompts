Having retired four years ago after a career in marine stratigraphy, I have been working my way through the philosophy of history, and next month I am delivering a paper to an interdisciplinary seminar. The room will be historians and social scientists rather than geologists: a committed Spenglerian, a comparative sociologist who works in the Toynbee tradition, two cliometricians with long-run series on state capacity, and a classicist who reads Polybius in the original.

My thesis is that the cyclical-versus-linear framing is misconceived, and that the stratigraphic record shows why. A cyclic signal is legible only against a monotonic accumulation, and only when an external clock of independent period — orbital forcing — disciplines the reading. Nothing in the human record supplies such a clock. I therefore argue that Ibn Khaldun's dynastic lifespans, Spengler's seasons of a culture, and Polybius's rotation of constitutions are curves fitted after the fact to an unconformity-bounded archive that has been heavily eroded: the historian sees only the strata that survived, and that selection alone biases any morphology toward symmetry. What I want to retain is the claim that civilizations accumulate irreversibly even where they recur locally, without sliding into a Whig progress story.

Give me the question period in advance: the eight or ten objections this particular audience will raise, ordered by the damage each would do to my argument, each phrased as its raiser would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in roughly a minute. Mark where each reply is softest, tell me what to concede in the opening five minutes to buy credibility with the cliometricians, flag any point at which my geological analogy misleads rather than illuminates, and close with the one objection I am hoping nobody voices.
