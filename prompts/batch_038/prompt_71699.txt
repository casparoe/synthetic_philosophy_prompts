I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of a year inside hospitals doing research for a book: ethics committees, ICU rounds, a fortnight shadowing a transplant coordinator. What I have come back with is not medical but conceptual, and I have run into a wall that none of the clinicians I asked could help me over, chiefly because none of them seemed to see it. I would like a philosophical reconstruction rather than clinical detail.

The first thing I found. In most American jurisdictions, a statute descended from the Uniform Determination of Death Act declares an individual dead upon "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem." The justification usually offered is that the brain is what binds the body's subsystems into a single organism. And yet such a body, on a ventilator, is warm; its heart beats; it digests; it mounts an immune response to infection; its wounds heal. There are documented cases of women declared dead in this sense being maintained for weeks, or longer, so that a pregnancy could continue to a caesarean delivery. I understand that one state permits a family to refuse the neurological definition on religious grounds, with the consequence that the same body may be a corpse on one side of a border and a living patient on the other. Meanwhile the same legal system that declares these bodies dead treats the removal of their organs as the harvesting of a corpse and not as a killing.

The second thing I found. At the other end of the spectrum, courts and hospital committees routinely authorize the withdrawal of artificial nutrition and hydration from patients in a permanent vegetative state — a condition in which the organism is plainly alive, breathing unaided and self-regulating, though no one claims consciousness persists. The American case that put this on the map turned substantially on what evidence would suffice about what the patient herself would have wanted. And where an advance directive was written decades earlier by a competent adult, it is frequently treated as authoritative over the contrary wishes that the same body now voices: a patient with advanced dementia who protests that he wants to go on living, whose protests we set aside as the noise of somebody who is no longer, in the relevant sense, there.

These two practices look to me as though they rest on incompatible foundations, but I cannot say which foundations. In the first, we call a living, integrated organism a corpse. In the second, we treat a living organism's continued existence as belonging to no one who matters. Is the first a metaphysical claim about what a person is, or an administrative bright line adopted for the sake of procurement and of switching off machines? Is the second a claim about identity, or only about interests? Tell me what theory of personal identity, and of what matters in survival — Parfit's phrase, which I take to name something he thought might come apart from identity itself — each practice strictly requires, as distinct from what it merely sits comfortably with. Tell me which rival accounts of persistence each one quietly forecloses. Tell me whether a single coherent theory can license both, or whether the law is in fact running two different theories in the same building; and whether either practice is underdetermined, since I suspect several theories would produce identical behaviour and the interesting question is which of them the institutions are unwilling to say aloud. Then tell me what would have to change if a suppressed theory were adopted openly: if a jurisdiction declared death at the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness rather than at the death of the brain, or, going the other way, abolished brain death and insisted that a respirator-supported body is a living human being.

One narrower request that matters to me as a maker of scenes. In a chapter, the sister of a brain-dead man insists that he is still there and that disconnecting him would be a killing; the transplant surgeon tells her she is confusing a body with a person. I want to know which of them, if either, is making a metaphysical mistake rather than a moral or merely emotional one — and whether their quarrel is about the world or about which concept of death our institutions have settled upon. Since I am tempted to write her as right, give me the strongest available version of her position.

What I do not want: a reading list, a bibliography, or citations. I cannot chase sources and do not need to. I want the arguments reconstructed and the concepts sorted out, in prose I could hand to an intelligent reader who has never studied philosophy.
