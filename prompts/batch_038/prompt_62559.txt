I am drafting a chapter for a small workshop on moral desert and the limits of punishment, and I have run into an obstacle that is linguistic rather than substantive. Last term I sat in on a reading group — mostly analytic people, plus one member who had spent several years in Varanasi — arguing about whether an offender who has genuinely reformed, and who has already been ruined socially and financially by his own conduct, can still deserve further suffering. When I suggested that on my account the desert claim survives the reform, another participant waved the point away with the proverb 'you reap what you sow', and the room nodded along. I could tell the sentence was doing at least three jobs simultaneously, and that I had lost the exchange without knowing which of those jobs had been decisive, but I could not pin the ambiguity down under pressure. I would like you to pin it down for me, because the chapter now turns on it.

Please treat the proverb as a compressed argument and unpack it into the distinct, precisely statable claims it might be making, running from the most innocuous to the most ambitious; six to nine readings feels right. For each, give me: the claim in a single sentence phrased so that a reasonable person could deny it without contradicting themselves; which of four categories it falls into — a claim about the causal structure of the world, about the psychology of the agent, about what would be fitting, or about who may legitimately inflict what; whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false, and if false whether it fails empirically or through a confusion between descriptive and normative content; what sort of evidence or argument could bear on it; and, above all, whether it actually supports the move my opponent made, namely the conclusion that no further suffering need be, or may be, deliberately imposed on someone already damaged by his own wrongdoing. Where a reading looks as though it supports the conclusion only by trading on a shift between two of those categories, say so explicitly and locate the shift.

For the raw material, please work from what you know of the karma literature and its internal disputes rather than from the motivational-poster version of the proverb. I have in mind the classical division of karmic stock into the accumulated store from previous lives, the portion that has already begun to fructify in the present life, and the results being generated by current action — a division that matters here because the schools differ sharply over which of the three is avoidable and which must simply be exhausted, and that difference constrains what the proverb can be promising; the objection pressed in some schools that a performed action is inert and its fruit therefore requires an administrator, a deity who allots results, which silently converts a law of nature into a piece of providence; the difficulty, for traditions that deny a persisting self, of saying what carries the fruit of an act across to the being who later receives it; and the Platonic line of argument that doing injustice is worse for the doer than suffering it, which is probably the strongest candidate for a purely psychological reading. Please also include at least one reading on which the proverb is not a prediction at all but a principle of justice or an expressive attitude, and at least one on which it is simply the just-world hypothesis in agricultural dress. Approach this as a philosopher who works both on the theory of desert and on classical Indian moral psychology: think the problem through carefully before writing, and open with a brief brainstorming passage listing the readings you considered and those you discarded as merely verbal. Then present the menu as a numbered list, and close with two short sections — your best reconstruction of the single reading my opponent was relying on and how the argument would have gone had he stated it openly, followed by the most likely equivocation a hearer is invited into. Write throughout in British English, and do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or quotations from primary texts; I want your analysis rather than your references, and I have no means of checking citations.
