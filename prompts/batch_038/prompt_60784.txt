I am a systems designer at a mid-sized studio that makes third-person action games, and last night I spent the better part of three hours arguing with a friend who teaches philosophy about whether anything a player does to our characters could matter morally. Neither of us moved an inch. I have come to think we were arguing about the wrong object, so I would rather hand you the artifact we were actually arguing over and ask what it commits us to — our internal rules were written by designers who hold no theory whatsoever and yet appear to have landed somewhere quite specific.

Here are the rules, in the form they exist in our design bible and QA pipeline. Characters are assigned damage-immunity flags at the asset level: any character flagged "minor" cannot take damage from any source, direct or indirect — melee, projectile, vehicle, environmental hazard, scripted explosion, damage-over-time field, physics object. Companion animals inside settlement zones carry the same flag. Enemy combatants carry no flag and may be killed without limit, including, in one shipped sequence, after they have dropped their weapons and raised their hands; that interaction has its own animation set and its own achievement. Immunity is required to be invisible: no shield effect, no dodge, no tooltip, no impact sound — the weapon simply does nothing and the child does not react. Any route around a flag, including one found by a modder or produced by a physics interaction we failed to anticipate, is filed as a Severity 1 defect, the same tier as a save-corrupting crash or a hard progression blocker; content and legal concerns sit two tiers below, and QA is told to hunt flag circumventions the way they hunt exploits. Separately, we run a post-session survey asking players to select any moment they wish they had not done. On our last title the top-ranked items were a scripted interrogation, the surrender execution, and a side quest in which the player may burn down an occupied building; nothing involving minors appeared, because minors cannot be harmed. When a colleague proposed extending the flag to surrendered enemies on the strength of those numbers, the proposal was rejected on the grounds that it would gut a core mechanic.

So: what view of the moral significance of fictional characters does this rulebook presuppose, if any? My friend's line was that the protected classes are picked out by a criterion that looks moral from the inside — beings who cannot defend themselves, cannot consent, cannot retaliate — and that adopting such a criterion already concedes that something in the fiction has a claim on the player; he also gestured at a puzzle about why people's intuitions split so sharply between virtual killing and virtual harm to children, and said our rules are an admission rather than a policy. My line was that this is player comfort and retail risk and nothing more, and that the bug-tier treatment of circumvention proves it: we do not respond to a modded child-harm exploit as though someone had been wronged, we respond as though the artifact had stopped being the artifact it is supposed to be. Tell me which reading the rules actually support, or whether the practice is underdetermined among several theories. I want you to separate what the rules strictly require from what they merely fit comfortably with; to name at least one respectable account of what fictional characters are that the rules rule out rather than simply fail to endorse; and to say whether the surrender-execution asymmetry is a genuine inconsistency in our implicit theory or something the gameplay constraint can absorb. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the severity taxonomy, the survey instrument, the achievement list — if the studio sincerely adopted my friend's view, and what would change if it adopted the view that nothing in a game matters beyond the dispositions the player is cultivating in themselves. Some of this is surely inherited convention with no theory behind it at all; say so wherever that is the case. Please keep the whole thing under 900 words and open with a single sentence telling me which of the two readings the rulebook supports. I do not want a bibliography or a list of further reading — I want the reconstruction.
