I'm a second-year PhD student in an interdisciplinary lab that sits somewhere between cognitive science and HCI, and I need help preparing for a lab meeting that I expect to be contentious. Our project concerns whether clinicians find a new alerting interface tolerable: hospital administrators want to know before a system-wide rollout, and we have two measurement strategies in competition. The first is a retrospective self-report instrument administered at the end of a shift, asking things like whether the alerts felt intrusive and whether the clinician would want to keep them. The second is passive telemetry: how often alerts are dismissed within two seconds, how long the acknowledgment screen is actually looked at, and how many users quietly opt out within the first fortnight. In our pilot, the two measures diverge sharply and in a way that is hard to interpret. The survey scores are uniformly favourable, but the dismiss-within-two-seconds rate is very high, opt-outs are common, and dwell time on the acknowledgment screen is close to the floor. A complicating wrinkle that I keep coming back to: in the small subset of participants who agreed to be pinged at random moments during the shift and asked what they were feeling right then, those momentary reports tracked the telemetry pattern quite well, and it was only the end-of-shift summary ratings that came out glowing.

At the last meeting, one of our senior colleagues responded to the divergence by saying, in effect, that we should stop agonising and simply ask people, since people know how they feel — and he used that line to argue that the survey should be our primary measure and the telemetry treated as a noisy proxy for something the participants can report directly. Later in the same meeting he used the same sentence again, this time against my proposal to add an experience-sampling component with random in-shift prompts: on his view that is wasted effort, because a considered end-of-shift rating already tells us what the person would have said in the moment. A philosopher on our advisory board then muttered something to the effect that first-person authority isn't a reliability claim at all, which I didn't follow and which may or may not be relevant. I suspect the two deployments of the sentence are not the same claim, and that at least one of them is unsupported by anything the sentence actually says, but I can't articulate the disambiguation cleanly under time pressure.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to the meeting, the distinct precise theses that "people know how they feel" might be expressing?
