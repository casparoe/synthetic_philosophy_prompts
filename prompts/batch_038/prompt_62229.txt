I write about biology and its discontents for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a feature on organs that everyone agrees exist but no one agrees are *for*. The human appendix is my test case. On one side are researchers who argue that it is a sheltered reservoir for beneficial gut microbes: a narrow, blind-ended tube, unusually rich in lymphoid tissue, positioned so that a bout of illness can scour the bowel without dislodging the residents inside it. On the other side are comparative anatomists who point out that the cecal appendix has appeared and vanished repeatedly and independently across mammalian lineages, and that where it does persist it varies wildly in length, shape and position — which they take to show that it is not doing any single job that matters.

Over the course of one week, two sources, one from each camp, closed their explanations with the same architectural maxim — form follows function — and each assured me it supported his conclusion. That coincidence is what I need help with, because my editors will not let me print a phrase that two experts are using as evidence for opposite claims.

So: please take the maxim apart. I want a numbered list of the distinct and precise claims it could be making, running from the least to the most committal — a prescription about how to design things, a claim about covariation, a claim about the direction in which evolutionary explanation runs, a claim that existing form is the best available shape for the job, perhaps something openly teleological. For each reading, tell me whether it is a truism no biologist would contest, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false; and tell me what notion of function it assumes, since I suspect this is where the two camps actually part company — the effect that explains why a trait is there at all, versus whatever the trait presently contributes to the working of the organism containing it. Then say which reading each of my sources needs in order for the phrase to carry the weight he put on it, whether the two are genuinely contradicting each other or only talking past each other, and whether any single reading would leave both of them right.

Finally, deal with the cases that seem to run the maxim backwards: the insulating covering that presumably did something before it flew, the repurposed wrist bone that serves a panda as a thumb, and organs that keep the architecture of a job they have long since stopped doing. Does the slogan survive those cases only by being weakened into something empty, or is there a version of it that keeps some bite?
