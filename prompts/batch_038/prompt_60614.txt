'Lewis showed that causation is just counterfactual dependence: c caused e if and only if, had c not happened, e would not have happened. That's the whole of it, and it's why the older regularity theories were finally retired. Two problems remained. Judging which possible world is closest to ours is a matter of taste, so the account can never be applied in practice. And it collapses whenever a backup cause is waiting in the wings: if the first assassin had missed, the second would have finished the job, so the death would have happened anyway, which means that on Lewis's own definition the first assassin didn't cause it. Woodward repaired all of this by defining an intervention as a change to the cause, brought about by an ideal agent, that doesn't touch the effect by any other route. On his account X causes Y just in case some possible manipulation of X would change Y, so a causal claim is at bottom a claim about what an experimenter could do. Two things follow. Causation is agent-relative: a universe containing no agents would contain no causal facts, however regular it was. And you don't really know that X causes Y until you have actually intervened on X, which is why randomized trials sit above every observational study in the hierarchy of evidence, and why Pearl's do-calculus is simply Lewis's possible worlds made rigorous.'
