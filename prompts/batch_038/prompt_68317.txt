The question as I set it:

> Our engineering teams keep Architecture Decision Records: short documents explaining why a design choice was made. Suppose a service has since been rewritten twice, every engineer who worked on the original design has left the company, and the code no longer resembles what those documents describe — though the documents themselves are still sitting in the repository. In what sense, if any, does the original rationale still exist? More generally: can there be a reason without a reasoner — a genuine rationale for something where nobody has it, holds it, or could ever come to hold it? Argue for a position rather than surveying the options.

The submission, from a senior backend engineer who reads philosophy seriously but has never taken a course in it:

> (1) There are two questions buried in the prompt, and keeping them apart does most of the work. The first is about facts: was there something that made sharding the billing database by tenant ID a sensible choice, and is that something still true today? The second is about minds: does anyone currently believe it? The first can have a positive answer while the second has a negative one. When engineers say the rationale is gone, they are usually reporting on the second question and drawing a conclusion about the first, and that step is not licensed.
>
> (2) On the first question I side with Scanlon, who treats a reason as a consideration that counts in favour of something, and holds that what does the counting is a fact rather than a belief. If keeping the hot partitions small genuinely reduced tail latency, then that fact was a reason to shard the way we did, and it did not stop being one when the people who relied on it moved to other companies. Reasons on this picture are not the property of whoever happens to respond to them. Williams pushes the other way: on his account, someone has a reason to do something only if a sound line of deliberation could carry them from the motivations they already have to doing it, so a consideration that could not move anyone is not a reason for anyone. That seems right as far as it goes, but it only shows that a reason has to be a possible reason for somebody. It does not show that it has to be somebody's reason now.
>
> (3) Dennett has a name for the thing I am reaching for: a free-floating rationale, a reason that is real and doing explanatory work even though no agent in the system has it. His standard cases are biological — the broken-wing display a ground-nesting bird uses to draw a predator off the nest, or the climate control built into a termite mound — where there is a plain answer to what the structure is for and no mind anywhere that represents it. Dennett's point, as I understand it, is that such a rationale cannot in principle be recovered by an interpreter, because there is nothing to recover it from: no beliefs, no intentions, no documentation. That is precisely the situation of our legacy billing service.
>
> (4) Which means the ADR is not carrying the weight people give it. The repository holds the rationale the way a photograph holds a face. The reason is in the artifacts, and the engineer who reads them six years later is not supplying the reason, only noticing it. That is why I resist the intuition that the rationale died with the last person who understood the system. If the code and the documents encode the reason, the reason is there to be read, and reading is discovery. It follows that the rationale would still be a reason if the repository were deleted tomorrow and nobody ever reconstructed it, since destroying a record cannot change whether the fact it recorded was a good one.
>
> (5) There is a Kantian line that supports this. The test the categorical imperative imposes asks whether a maxim can be willed as a universal law, and it never consults what the agent happens to want. A test that runs without reference to anyone's desires shows that reasons are mind-independent: the standard is there whether or not anyone is applying it, and it can be applied to rationales that nobody has ever had. So a design rationale that no one now holds can still be assessed, and can still pass.
>
> (6) The best objection I can see is that a consideration which could never enter anyone's deliberation is just a fact about the world rather than a reason, because reasons belong to the space of things that could move someone. If that is right, my position owes a story about what turns a fact into a reason, and I do not have one. But the objection has a cost of its own: it implies that nothing was a reason anywhere in the world until the first creature appeared that could be moved by one, and it gives no account of what changed at that moment. I do not know whether that is fatal to the objection. It is at least expensive.
