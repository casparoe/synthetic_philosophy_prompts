Our department runs a termly public-philosophy evening — forty minutes of interview, then the floor is opened up — and next month's guest is a philosopher whose new book tries to rehabilitate the idea that prospective parents ought to pick the embryo expected to have the best life, the Savulescu-style procreative beneficence line, but with two twists I really want to hold him to. Twist one: he refuses to state it as an enforceable duty, and instead says it's a moral reason that the state should make genuinely available to everyone by funding embryo screening and selection on a health-service model, because leaving it to the wealthy turns a putative benefit into an inequality engine. Twist two: he thinks the non-identity point is on his side — since bringing one particular child into existence rather than another can't harm the child who gets born, the whole case has to be impersonal, about the world containing better-lived lives rather than about anyone being wronged.

The room will be mixed, and at least one person from the disability studies centre has told me she's comming specifically to argue with him, so I don't want softballs and I don't want anything that lets him just restart the book tour. Could you write me eight to ten questions, each followed by the answer he's most likely to give (a sentence or two in his voice, so I can recognise it in real time) and then a follow-up that only bites on that particular answer? I'd like at least one question on the positional-goods problem — if everyone selects for the same cognitive advantages the relative advantage evaporates, so a universal, publicly funded programme seems to destroy the very benefit that justified it; one on the expressivist objection, and whether his "it's the condition I'm screening against, not the person" move can do any work at all when the decision happens before there is a person; one on the authorship/autonomy objection, asking what makes pre-implantation choice different in kind from all the other ways parents shape a child (schooling, diet, music lessons, which neighbourhood); and one on who actually sets the metric of a "best life" once a public body is paying for the screening, and what that ranking says to the people who come out low on it. If there's a place where the honest move for him is to just bite a bullet, flag it in your notes so I'm not caught out.

Two bits of housekeeping. First, no filler — nothing along the lines of what drew him to the topic, and nothing he could answer with a paragraph that's already in the introduction of the book; every question should spell out the position inside the question itself, since half the audience aren't philosophers and shouldn't have to know the jargon to follow the exchange. Second, no references or suggested further reading at the end, I just want the questions and the notes. And please write the whole thing in British English — I've spent too long this term fixing -ize endings in our publicity material's to do it again.
