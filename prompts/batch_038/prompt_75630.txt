Spent 35 years in comparative physiology and now that I'm retired I read old biology texts for fun, which is how I ended up in Aristotle's Parts of Animals, Book II, the chapters on the brain. I'm working from a creaky public-domain translation and I'll paraphrase rather than type it all out, but the argument seems to run roughly like this: the brain is the coldest of the parts, its whole job is to temper and cool the heat that gathers around the heart, and animals with a lot of hot blood need a bigger brain to do the cooling. Then he says creatures whose brain is large relative to their body are dull and stupid while ones with less of it are sharper — and yet elsewhere he says man has the biggest brain in proportion to his size of any animal.

Can you help me get a handle on this? Three things: (1) what's actually doing the explanatory work for him — absolute size, the coldness, or the ratio to the blood supply? (2) Is the man-has-the-biggest-brain bit a flat contradiction of the dullness claim, or is there a reading that lets both stand, one Aristotle himself would accept? (3) Does any of this touch moral standing — is quantity of brain supposed to track how much a creature counts, or is he just doing thermoregulation and I'm importing a modern question he'd never have thought to ask? Be blunt about where my reading is definately off.
