I'm in a public policy masters program and my seminar prof keeps running with the idea that a democracy is just a scientific community at larger scale. Policies are hypotheses, elections and rollouts are the experiments, legislative debate is peer review, a voted-out incumbent is a failed replication. He uses it to argue two things: that we should be way more relaxed about launching big policy pilots without knowing how they'll turn out, and that polarization is overblown, since a fight over policy is really just a fight about data nobody has collected yet. He also likes to say that in science you don't argue with the results, so in a democracy you shouldn't argue with the vote. Someone in seminar backed him up with the old states-are-laboratories line.

Can you audit the whole thing? I want the correspondence table -- what's playing the role of the hypothesis, the instrument, the control group, the replication, the community of inquirers, the protocol fixed in advance -- and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually undercut his two conclusions and the ones that are only surface. I'm especially interested in whether anything on the science side (blinding, randomization, a measure thats independent of the people being measured, reversibility of the treatment) has no political counterpart at all, and whether that gap damages the argument or just the rhetoric. And does don't-argue-with-the-vote come out of the mapping anywhere, or is it smuggled in from somewhere else entirely?

Separate question. I've been reading some Dewey for another class and my sense is he wanted a comparison like this but would hate this use of it -- for him the experimenting seems to be done by the people it happens to, not on them. Is that a real difference the lab picture hides, or am I reading it in? If the analogy can't carry his conclusions, what could?
