Last spring I became the sole trustee of a small family trust, a post my uncle held for thirty years. His record is a matter of family legend and, increasingly, of my own professional embarrassment. Alongside a plain index portfolio he made roughly twenty genuinely aberrant commitments — illiquid, non-repeatable, each of them argued for at the time as a situation that would not come around twice, and each of them, on the documents, defensible. Four paid off handsomely. One paid for everything else. The remaining fifteen lost most or all of their principal. Now an opportunity of exactly that character is in front of me, I am inclined to fund it, and I have found that I can construct two incompatible accounts of my own situation and no longer know which one is a rational basis for acting.

The first is an outside view. It treats my case as one further member of a reference class of twenty — or, generously widened to comparable commitments by other families and funds, perhaps sixty — and reads the resulting base rate as the estimate I should carry into the decision. The second is an inside view. I have read every memorandum in the files; I know the terms, the counterparties, the collateral, the local market, and the particular respects in which this situation resembles the four that worked rather than the fifteen that did not; and I take myself to be better informed about this case than any statistical summary of its class could possibly be.

I would like you to construct a thought experiment that sharpens this into something I can reason with, because the version in the wild is nearly always too easy. The standard base-rate story explains the triumph of the outside view by an information gap — the naive actor lacks data the statistician has — or by a defect of judgment: overconfidence, motivated reasoning, survivorship bias, a reference class gerrymandered to flatter the conclusion. I want a case in which none of that is available.

The specifications:

1. The case must feature a decision-maker with complete and correct particular knowledge. There is no relevant fact about the present opportunity, and no relevant fact about the prior members of the class, that the decision-maker does not know; and everything the decision-maker believes about the respects in which this case is unlike the failures is true.
2. Notwithstanding (1), the outside view must be the authoritative one. The base rate should govern the decision, and the two views must issue in opposite recommendations.
3. The explanation of why (2) holds despite (1) may not appeal to any gap in the decision-maker's information, nor to any bias, error, or self-deception on their part. The mechanism must be structural, and you must state it explicitly.
4. Realistic and particular. No exotic machinery, no science fiction, no demons or omniscient predictors. Ordinary stakes an individual could actually face. The scenario itself should run to one paragraph.
5. Give the case enough determinate content — numbers, a threshold, something that makes proceeding or not proceeding turn on the credence — that the two verdicts are generated by the facts you have laid down rather than asserted in the telling. If the case only yields the verdicts because you wrote them into it, it has failed.

Then:

- Walk through what each view says, the credence each licenses, and the resulting decision, showing that the case as constructed really does produce them.
- Identify the feature of the case doing the work. Explain precisely why complete and accurate knowledge of the particulars does not license the optimistic credence — what it is that the base rate is tracking which the particulars, however fully known, do not.
- Test the feature. Say what happens if it is removed, and confirm that its removal returns a case in which the decision-maker's complete particular knowledge does license the inside-view credence. If removing it does not flip the case, you have not yet found the feature that was doing the work.
- Answer the inside-view objection on its merits, rather than waving it away: if the decision-maker genuinely knows every fact that distinguishes this case from the fifteen failures, and knows them correctly, then their credence just is a considered judgment about those facts, and deferring to the base rate either double-counts evidence already used or amounts to declining to use one's evidence at all.

One further test, if you would indulge me. When you name the structural feature, say whether it generalizes beyond a family trust — whether the same feature is what makes a base rate of past cases authoritative over the confident particular knowledge of someone assessing the prospects of a rising state, or of their own career. If it does generalize, say what changes when the class members are large and slow-moving rather than small and discrete. If it does not, say so plainly; I would rather know that my problem is a narrow one.
