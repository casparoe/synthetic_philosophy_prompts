I run a 100-minute session for 16 MA students in political theory — one semester of ethics behind them, no game theory, and honestly a bit allergic to abstract argument. I want to build an in-class simulation around a distinction I think gets blurred constantly when people talk about settling conflicts between value systems:

(a) a straight trade: each side keeps its own values completely intact and just swaps support across issues, so everyone ends up with more of what they care about, measured by their own lights;
(b) a real compromise: each side moderates its own demand, i.e. concedes something on its own scale of value, and everyone ends up endorsing a position they think is worse than the one they walked in with.

On paper (a) looks obviously better. In practice something goes wrong with it, and I want them to run into that themselves instead of hearing me say it. Participants shouldn't get the words "trade" and "compromise" until the debrief — the handouts should just tell them what they care about.

The skeleton I've got so far, which I haven't been able to make work: a committee splitting a fixed pot across four or five projects, with participants in four factions, each with a genuine value ordering rather than just different interests. At least one faction treats one of the projects as not up for sale at any price, and here's the bit I care about most — that faction has to receive an offer that is good by its own ranking. So accepting wouldn't be a mistake on its own terms, and yet taking it costs something. I want the room to feel that squeeze rather than have it explained.

Please give me:

- the four factions and their orderings, written out so I can literally print them on cards
- the rules for two rounds: round one where only cross-issue swapping is allowed, round two where the only move on the table is each faction lowering its own demand on its own top issue
- a scoring scheme that needs no real money but where people still feel they've lost something — keep some of the scores private, so they have to guess at what other factions value, and tell me exactly what each card reveals and hides
- a minute-by-minute timeline for 100 minutes
- a debrief that pins specific moments in the game onto the trade/compromise distinction, and that forces them to explain why the round-one outcome was better for every faction by its own values and still left somebody worse off

Also build in this: gains from trade only exist if you have something to trade, so the faction whose values are least fungible should get systematically squeezed even though nobody is being unfair to it. I want that to fall out of the rules, not out of my commentary.

Finally — tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time this runs. I've seen this genre of exercise dissolve into people horsing around and not taking their roles seriously, or into one loud student broker-dealing the whole thing while everyone else watches. And be straight with me about which feature of the idea a role-play like this simply cannot reproduce, that I'm going to have to supply by hand in the discussion afterward.
