I'm teaching a graduate seminar on Victorian philosophy of science this term and we've spent the last two weeks on Sidgwick's double life — the Methods of Ethics on Tuesdays, the Society for Psychical Research on Thursdays. Half my students think the psychical research was a sentimental side project and the other half think it's where the interesting epistemology actually lives, and I want to press the second view harder than I can in class without getting shouted down. So: play Henry Sidgwick, president of the SPR, in his rooms in Cambridge in 1889, just after a council meeting. You may use only the concepts and evidence available to you then, plus what Ive set out below — no Freud, no significance tests, no talk of statistical power; if you need some modern fact clarified, ask me for it the way you'd ask a colleague who has been abroad a long time. Stay in character until I say stop. You may concede small points — you were never a man who pretended to certainty, and you admitted as much about your own book on ethics — but you may not concede the main one, which is that the systematic investigation of ostensibly supernormal phenomena remains a rational and worthwhile enterprise even though it has produced nothing you would call proof. Two paragraphs a reply, in your own voice: wry, self-interrogating, a bit tired, not grand.

Here is what I have just handed you across the table, and you should treat it as a report from a trusted friend who owns strange instruments. In my century one kind of experiment has been run many thousands of times: a subject is asked to anticipate an event that is settled afterwards by pure chance — the fall of a card, the output of a mechanical device — and the guesses are tallied up. The early runs, on small numbers of subjects, showed a slight excess of hits over what chance alone would give. When the same procedure was repeated on many hundreds of subjects, with the method written down and fixed in advance before anyone had seen the data, the excess shrank to a fraction of a percent and became indistinguishable from nothing whatever. Someone then gathered every such study anyone could lay hands on — including the ones the researchers never published because they came out empty — and the pattern was that the apparent effect gets steadily smaller as the studies get larger, right up to the point where the biggest studies show essentially nothing. I have also told you that a century after your committee's report on the Theosophists, a specialist in disputed handwriting went back through the surviving letters held in a national library and concluded that the committee had decided its verdict before it weighed the evidence, and that the report is not a scientific study at all but a prosecutor's address.

Now my opening move, and I want you to take it seriously rather than parry it. You have told your colleagues more than once that a long programme of careful investigation which fails to produce a single unambiguous case is itself a result and not a null one — that the failure to find is evidence. Well: here is your programme, run for another hundred years, with better instruments, fixed procedures, published protocols, and no fraud left to expose — and it comes back empty. By your own logic doesn't that convert your agnosticism into a verdict: not merely that survival is unproven, but that it is improbable? And if so, doesn't the damage land on the ethics too? You hold that practical reason rests on a harmony between virtue and happiness that you cannot demonstrate, and that some moral government of the universe would be the only thing able to secure it. If the hundred-year search turns up nothing, then the one empirical door you left open onto that harmony is shut, and the dualism stops being a difficulty one lives with and becomes a refutation. So which do you withdraw — the research programme or the ethics — or is there a third option that isn't just a refusal to add up the ledger?
