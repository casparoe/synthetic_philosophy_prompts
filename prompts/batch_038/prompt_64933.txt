Okay so I've been having the same argument with my roommates for like three weeks now and I've realised I don't actually have a position on when it's legitimate for one country to use force inside another to stop mass killing — I have a pile of verdicts. Maybe you can tell me what principle they add up to, or which ones I have to throw out. I'm not writing a paper on this, I just got annoyed at myself for not being able to say why two cases that look structurally identical get opposite reactions from me.

Here's the pile. Kosovo 1999: NATO bombed Serbia for months with no Security Council authorisation, and I think that was right. Rwanda 1994: nobody went in while roughly 800,000 people were killed in about three months, and that was the worst failure on the list, no ambiguity. Iraq 2003: also no authorisation, also removed a genuinely monstrous regime, and I think it was wrong — and this is the bit my roommate keeps attacking — I think it would have been wrong even in a counterfactual where the occupation went smoothly, which is a commitment I can't really cash out. Libya 2011: had authorisation, began as protecting Benghazi, ended with NATO functionally helping to hunt down Gaddafi and then the country came apart; I want to say the first phase was right and the whole thing was wrong, which I know sounds like a fudge. Syria 2013: red line drawn, sarin used on Ghouta, the Commons voted down joining strikes, and at the last second everything got traded for a chemical weapons deal that later mostly fell apart — and I honestly think not bombing was right. Then the awkward ones. The Vietnamese invasion that toppled the Khmer Rouge in January 1979 stopped one of the worst killing campaigns of the century, and the Khmer Rouge kept Cambodia's UN seat for more than a decade afterwards anyway; I think the killing stopping was good and that Hanoi's own reasons don't cancel the good, but I also don't want to say the invasion was *right*, and I can't tell you what work that distinction is doing. Same shape with India in East Pakistan in 1971. And Somalia: the operation looked like a success while food was moving and looked like a fiasco after Mogadishu, and I'm pretty sure nothing about the intervention itself changed in between, only the body count did — yet my verdict on it did change, which bothers me more than it should.

The pair I can't reconcile is Iraq and Libya, because for Iraq I'm appealing to something other than consequences and for Libya I'm appealing entirely to consequences, and I don't see how both moves can live in one principle without a special clause for each. Kosovo and Syria are almost as bad: neither had authorisation, both involved stopping or punishing atrocity with air power against a regime that could retaliate against its own population, and I endorse one and not the other. If you asked me whether the difference is that the Kosovo campaign actually worked, I'd say no, because I also think the Syria non-strike was right even though the chemical deal failed — so I appear to be requiring success in one case and exempting failure in the other.

What I want back: one principle, or a small handful of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case nobody has ever described to me, with each clause tied to the specific verdict above that motivates it — I want to know which of my intuitions is buying which piece of machinery. Then run it on four cases I haven't listed and give me the verdicts: (a) a foreign power flies a persistent drone campaign over a region it has no historical ties to and reliably stops militia massacres there, motivated purely by keeping a rival power out, with nobody on the ground either requesting or objecting to it; (b) a regime isn't killing anyone but is forcibly transferring children out of a minority group and banning their language, and a credible threat of limited force would stop it — does that belong in the same category as massacres or a different one, and what does the principle have to say about why; (c) an intervention authorised by a regional body the UN doesn't recognise, which works, which the affected population wants and their own government doesn't; (d) sanctions would definitely stop the killing in about four years, an air campaign would stop it in three weeks but kill a few hundred civilians doing it. And then be blunt about which of my verdicts the best principle can't save — I'd genuinely rather have a simple principle that costs me either Iraq or Libya than a baroque one with an exception per case, but lay out both versions and make me pick, because I suspect I've been arguing for the baroque one without noticing.
