I spent thirty-eight years in an enzymology laboratory, the last twenty of them substantially occupied with cloning and heterologous expression, and I have now been retired for four years, during which I have taken up philosophy more seriously than I ever expected to. What I have found is that the practices I participated in daily were never examined by me at all; I simply inherited their vocabulary along with their pipettes. So I should like to put one such practice to you precisely, and ask what it commits us to.

The practice is this. When my laboratory needed a human enzyme expressed in *E. coli* or in insect cells, we did not isolate the human gene; we ordered it from a synthesis vendor. We specified the protein we wanted and the host we intended to use, and the vendor returned a DNA construct whose nucleotide sequence differed from the human original by something on the order of a quarter of its positions — chosen codon by codon to match the host's transfer RNA abundities and to remove internal restriction sites and cryptic splice signals — while encoding an amino acid sequence identical residue for residue. Everyone, including the vendor's certificate, described this as the same gene, just codon-optimized. Yet the same discipline treats the nucleotide string as the very identity of the gene elsewhere. Sequence repositories index, name, and version the string, and a submitted coding region carries an explicit integer telling the annotation machinery which code table to apply — a qualifier that is silently omitted when the standard table applies, since that is nearly always the case, though two dozen or more variant tables have been catalogued, in some of which TGA specifies tryptophan rather than termination, and in some ciliated protozoa TAA and TAG specify glutamine rather than termination, while a further wrinkle is that in our own cells UGA is read as selenocysteine whenever a particular RNA structural element lies downstream of it. And in patent law the string, not the protein, has been decisive: when the American Supreme Court ruled in 2013 that a naturally occurring DNA segment cannot be patented merely for having been isolated, while a laboratory-made complementary copy of the same coding region could be, the distinction turned entirely on whether that exact string occurs in nature — notwithstanding that the copy carries the same protein-coding content. Meanwhile we still call a substitution that does not change the encoded amino acid "silent," in a field that knows perfectly well such changes can alter mRNA folding, splicing, translation rate, and yield.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the theory this practice presupposes, not a survey of candidate theories. Specifically: what must be true of the relation between codons and amino acids for it to be a code at all — does the practice require the mapping to be arbitrary in the sense that it could have been otherwise, and if so, is that requirement actually met or merely assumed? Does it require a reader, an interpreter, or a recipient distinct from the message, and if it does, who occupies that role in a cell, given that the ribosome and the transfer RNAs are themselves products of the same string? Does it require a sharp separation between the informational molecule and the machinery that acts on it? I would also like you to take seriously the deflationary alternative I find most tempting: that DNA is simply the molecule that happens to be differentially copied and, in being copied and transcribed, causally shifts the rates of countless reactions, so that all the talk of coding, reading, messages, and silence is a dispensable manner of speaking that survives because it is convenient. Please distinguish carefully what the practice strictly cannot do without from what merely sits comfortably alongside it, and tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where two or three incompatible accounts of information would license exactly the same bench behavior and the same database schema. Then address the apparent inconsistency I have described: the string as irreplaceable identity in the repositories and the courts, the string as freely substitutable within an equivalence class at the synthesis vendor, and "silent" as a term for changes we know are not silent. Either show me that these are consistent on one account, or tell me which of them has to give; and if your reconciliation works by distinguishing two senses of information, say plainly which sense is doing the load-bearing work in molecular biology and whether the second is earned or merely decorative. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change — in the way a coding region is annotated, in what a vendor's certificate asserts, in the word "silent," in the legal test — if the deflationary view were adopted rather than merely tolerated. I am asking you to commit: give me your own verdict on whether DNA literally carries a code, with reasons, rather than a balanced inventory that leaves the choice to me, and mark clearly the places where you are relying on a contested reading of the biology or of the notion of information.
