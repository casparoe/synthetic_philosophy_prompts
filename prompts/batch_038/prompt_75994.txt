**The student's answer (632 words)**

The Essay on the Principle of Population (1798, greatly expanded in the second edition of 1803) argues that population, when unchecked, increases in a geometric ratio while the means of subsistence increase only in an arithmetic one. Any rise in real wages above what is needed to keep the labouring population alive leads workers to marry earlier and to raise more children; the enlarged supply of labour then pushes wages back down. Population is held to the level the food supply can support, either by the positive checks — famine, epidemic, war, the general misery that shortens life — or, as Malthus insisted in 1803, by the preventive check of moral restraint: late marriage and celibacy.

On the long view the model looks accurate. English real wages were broadly flat for centuries before 1800 while population crept upward, and the escape from that trap came only with industrialisation, synthetic nitrogen, fossil energy and — decisively — the demographic transition, in which fertility fell as incomes rose. The defence on offer is that Malthus was right about the mechanism and merely early about its end: the reprieve was bought with coal and fertiliser and falling birth rates, none of which is guaranteed to last.

The obvious reply is that this is an unfalsifiable rescue. Duhem and Quine established that hypotheses are never tested singly, so a failed prediction can be absorbed by rearranging the surrounding web — here, by moving the deadline. Lakatos gives the sharper tool. A research programme has a hard core (the asymmetry between the two rates of increase) and a protective belt of auxiliaries (which checks operate, on whom, how strongly, and when). Programmes are not refuted by individual failed predictions; they are assessed by whether their adjustments are progressive, yielding novel facts and new predictions, or degenerating, merely accommodating what has already happened. Shifting the date of the collapse looks degenerating.

But the adjustment is not ad hoc, and that is what gives the defence its content. An auxiliary is ad hoc when it does no independent work. The deadline shift is not like that, because the mechanism Malthus identified is separately and continuously documented: aquifer drawdown, topsoil loss, collapsed fisheries, cereal yields that depend on industrially fixed nitrogen. The mechanism is not in doubt; only the date was. A theory whose mechanism is confirmed and whose timing was off is right but early, and that is a substantive verdict rather than a dodge.

The 1980 wager between Julian Simon and Paul Ehrlich shows the contrast. Ehrlich, who expected mass starvation, picked five metals and bet that their inflation-adjusted prices would rise over the following decade; Simon took the other side and won when the basket's real price fell. That episode shows what a long-run forecast should look like: a dated, definite, measurable quantity, so that the outcome settles the dispute. The Malthusians lost, and lost decisively. What the wager exposed was not that the principle is false — the resource data confirm it — but that Malthus's own formulations were too vague about time to be tested at all.

So "right but early" is available here: the core mechanism is independently supported, and the failure to name the date is a failure of precision, not of content. What would refute the principle is not the continuing absence of collapse, but evidence that the mechanism has stopped operating — that population pressure no longer bears at all on the availability of the means of subsistence. There is no such evidence; the pressure plainly continues. Until there is, the theory remains corroborated.
