so in my early modern course we just did Berkeley's master argument in the Principles (the §23 one, I think) — the opponent says nothing could be easier than imagining a tree standing in a wood with nobody there to see it, and Berkeley's comeback is basically: sure, you've formed ideas of a tree and a wood in your own mind, but in doing that you conspicously didn't leave out your own perception of them, so what you've actually conceived is the tree existing in your mind, not the tree existing unconceived. Contradiction, so esse est percipi. Our prof keeps saying the argument is either valid and interesting or trivially valid, and refuses to say which, which is driving me nuts becuase I have to write the seminar paper on it.

I tried to settle it by pulling out the general form and testing it on other relations, and I've made things worse. The principle I think is doing all the work:

CT: if someone conceives of an x as standing in relation R, then x stands in R — at least when R is a relation to a mind and the act of conceiving is itself an instance of R.

Berkeley, as I've reconstructed it:
1. The opponent conceives of a tree as existing unconceived (granted for reductio).
2. By CT, since conceiving is itself an instance of the conception relation, the tree is thereby conceived by the opponent's mind.
3. So the opponent has conceived a tree that both stands and does not stand in that relation — contradiction.
4. So nothing can be conceived as existing unconceived; whatever can be conceived to exist, exists in a mind.

Now the same form with knowledge swapped in:
1'. I know that some truths are known by nobody — e.g. I know that nobody knows how many dust motes are in this room right now, and I don't know that number myself.
2'. By CT, in knowing that, the fact is known by me.
3'. Contradiction, so I can't know that anything is unknown, and since it generalizes, nothing is unknown.
4'. Which is insane.

And again with tense:
1''. I'm thinking of the Lisbon earthquake as past.
2''. By CT, in thinking of it, it's present to my mind.
3''. Contradiction, so nothing can be thought of as past; everything thinkable is now.
4''. Presentism smuggled in for free.

Here's the wrinkle my friend Rae threw at me. CT only bites if the relation is unindexed. When the opponent says the tree exists unconceived he might mean unconceived by any finite mind, and Berkeley himself says the tree is always conceived by God — so on Berkeley's own view the tree exists unconceived by Hylas, which is perfectly conceivable. Same trick for unknown (= unknown by everyone) and past (= not occurring now). If we index, all three arguments die, including Berkeley's. If we don't, all three run and two of them land on absurdity. Either way the master argument doesn't look special, and I can't tell which premise is the one to blame.

So: which step actually fails — CT itself, the slide from conceiving of x to x being conceived (an act/object confusion? a scope thing?), or the unindexed reading? Is there any reading that keeps Berkeley's argument alive while killing 1'-4' and 1''-4'', or is the honest verdict that they all stand or fall together, and if so which horn of the prof's dilemma do I take? Also I'd like to know what it costs to give up CT, since something CT-ish seems like it has to be true or we could never think about our own thinking at all. I'd rather keep 1' and 1'' than keep CT, but please don't just tell me Berkeley is confused, the man wrote three entire dialogues about this and clearly knew what he was doing.
