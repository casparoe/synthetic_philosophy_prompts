Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about the dogmatism paradox, and somewhere around midnight it became clear that we had been talking past each other for most of the evening. We are the two regular facilitators of a monthly reading group — about twenty people, mostly not philosophers, a couple of nurses, a lawyer, a retired structural engineer — and next month's session is on the argument that knowledge appears to put you beyond the reach of counter-evidence: if you know that p, then anything that seems to count against p is misleading, and evidence you know to be misleading may be disregarded, so knowing something licenses you to refuse to listen. My friend defends the conclusion, at least as a claim about justification rather than about conduct: holding on to a belief in the face of evidence you know to be misleading is not thereby unjustified, and the sense that this is absurd comes from running two questions together, namely what attitude is epistemically justified and what policy an inquirer should adopt. I take the other side. I think the argument fails as guidance whatever it delivers as theory, because a standing policy of disregarding anything one takes to be misleading will be misapplied constantly and the misapplications are not benign; and I suspect the deeper trouble is that the key premise only does work when the subject can actually deploy it, and in the very situations where it would need deploying it leans on the belief it is supposed to protect.

What I want from you is the document the two of us should have produced instead of shouting. Draft the joint statement that a defender of the dogmatist conclusion and its regulative critic could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. It should contain, in this order, the claims both of us can sign — about the shape of the argument, about the factivity of knowledge and what follows from it, about the difference between evidence that bears on whether p is true and evidence that bears on whether one is in a position to know it, and about what actually happens in ordinary cases of inquiry; then the residual disagreements, each stated in wording the side that holds it would accept as its own, with no straw men and no slant; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of us.

For the cruxes I offer four candidates and want you to tell me which is load-bearing, which are downstream, and whether I have missed one: whether epistemic norms govern attitudes only, or also the conduct of inquiry, and so whether an answer about justification answers any practical question at all; whether the knowledge that a given item of evidence is misleading is available to the subject as a reason at the moment she needs it, or only in the abstract beforehand; whether receiving the counter-evidence can itself destroy the knowledge that was supposed to license disregarding it; and whether misleading evidence names anything an agent can recognize from the inside, as opposed to something identifiable only from outside with the facts already in hand. For each crux you identify, give a concrete case or an argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, in a form that would be embarrassing for that side if it turned out wrong. Then give an honest verdict on how much of our argument was merely verbal — my suspicion is that both "disregard" and "should" are doing double duty in the exchange — and say what survives once the verbal part is stripped away.

Run the result through three cases we kept reaching for: the pre-commitment, in which on Monday you know p and write your future self a note instructing them to ignore whatever seems to count against p next week, together with a variant in which, by the time the note is read, its author no longer remembers why she knew p; the clinician who knows a diagnosis from a confirmatory test and the next morning gets an opposite result from a second, ordinarily reliable one; and the case in which the new information does not bear on p at all but on whether she is in a position to know p — that the informant was unreliable, or the instrument was out of calibration when it gave the reading.

I want commitment, not bookkeeping. Take a definite position on which crux is the real one, on which of my four candidates is a distraction, and on where the dispute was about words; give your reasons; and do not balance the ledger and hand it back to us to decide. If you conclude that the collaboration cannot be completed, that one of the two parties would have to sign something false in order to keep the document joint, then say so plainly, locate the exact sentence where it breaks down, and say which side is being asked to swallow what.

No headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables: continuous prose only, in the register of a signed letter, because we intend to read it aloud at the session, taking alternate paragraphs, and the room has to be able to follow who is conceding what without seeing the page. Somewhere around fifteen hundred words. And please no citations, no attributions, no historical placement of the paradox and no suggestions for further reading — we will not have texts in the room and I have no way of checking a reference you give me.
