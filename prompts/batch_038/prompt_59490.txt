Context for the deal on the table: the university offered to divest the endowment from thermal coal specifically (not oil and gas), fund a full-time sustainability office, and give us a seat on a new advisory committee — if we take the tents down within a week and drop the president's name from our press materials. Maya wanted to take it, Dev wanted to refuse, and they went maybe seven rounds. I'm writing this from memory so the wording is loose, but I think I've got the structure of each move right.

(1) Maya: this is a straight gain from trade. We care about coal and about visibility; the admin cares way more about the tents and the bad headlines than about a coal position that's a rounding error in the endowment. When two sides weight the same bundle of goods differently, trading leaves both better off by their own lights — that's just comparative advantage, and refusing to trade because the other side "doesn't value the right things" isn't an argument, it's a tantrum.

(2) Dev: comparative advantage works on goods that are priced in a common currency and can be bought again later. Leverage can't. The tents are the only reason they're offering anything at all, so this deal is selling the thing that sets the price, at a price set by the thing you're selling. Afterwards we're back to zero bargaining power holding a coal-only divestment and a nicer title on somebody's business card.

(3) Maya: leverage isn't worth anything in itself, it's only ever a means to concessions, and if you refuse to cash it in for actual concessions you're worshipping the means. Also the tents are decaying — attendance is down, the op-eds have flipped, and the admin's cost of clearing them by force gets cheaper every week. Refusing the deal doesn't preserve leverage, it just spends it on nothing.

(4) Dev: okay but notice what you just did. If the answer is always "whatever maximizes expected concessions given our current bargaining position," then there's no such thing as moral compromise at all, it's just a bet, and we were never actually disagreeing about principle. The reason this one feels different is that we stood in front of our members in March and said naming the president was non-negotiable, and that isn't a preference with a price attached, it's a commitment people joined on the strength of.

(5) Maya: commitments a group makes, a group can revise — that's literally what a vote is for. And your non-negotiable line got drawn six weeks ago by people who didn't know a sustainability office was on offer. Staying faithful to a March promise by turning down a real concession in June is just preferring your own self-image to the outcome.

(6) Dev: you're also assuming the only thing at stake is this deal. Every compromise teaches the other side what our commitments cost. Sign this and the admin learns that a coal-only concession plus one committee seat buys a public retreat, and that's the price list they'll use on us forever. And separately — if you take this deal you owe the people who got arrested in April an explanation, maybe an apology. Real trades don't leave that kind of residue. A deal that generates an apology afterwards is evidence it was never a trade in the first place.

(7) Maya: the apology isn't residue from the trade, it's just the cost of having made a promise you couldn't keep. The mistake was made in March when we promised something non-negotiable, not in June when we update. If you never make unpriced commitments you never need the apology, so this isn't an argument against the deal, it's an argument against how we wrote our platform.
