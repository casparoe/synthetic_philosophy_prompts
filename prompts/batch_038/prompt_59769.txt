I spent forty years as a sedimentologist, and my working life consisted largely of persuading river deltas to yield up legible records of their own past. It is only since retiring that I have taken up philosophy with any seriousness, and I find I approach it exactly as I approached fieldwork: I accumulate a table of observations before permitting myself a hypothesis. The observations in this case are electoral rules, gathered over the past several months from official sources and reference works, and they have produced a pattern I cannot make sense of. I should say at the outset that a few of the details below may be slightly out of date or imprecise; please correct me where it matters, though I believe the shape of the pattern survives any such correction.

Here is the table. Australia compels both enrolment and attendance at a polling place, has done so federally since 1924, enforces it with a modest fine, and achieves turnout above ninety per cent; yet because the ballot is secret, an elector who attends, is marked off the roll, and deposits an unmarked or spoiled paper has discharged the obligation and cannot in practice be touched. New Zealand compels enrolment but not voting — and compels enrolment for permanent residents who have lived in the country for a year, not only for citizens. Belgium retains the oldest extant compulsory system, introduced for men in the 1893 franchise settlement, but has largely ceased to enforce it; the Netherlands and Venezuela abandoned compulsion altogether, in 1967 and 1993. Now the age dimension. Austria and Scotland have extended the franchise to sixteen-year-olds within voluntary systems, where the question of compulsion never arises; but in the compulsory-voting states of Latin America the two moves come conspicuously apart. Argentina enfranchised sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds in 2012 and attached no sanction whatever to their abstention. Brazil treats voting as obligatory from eighteen to seventy and optional for sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds, for those over seventy, and for the illiterate. Ecuador does something similar, obligatory from eighteen to sixty-five and optional below and above. Finally the membership dimension: Ireland permits any resident, whatever their citizenship, to vote in local elections while restricting the national parliament to Irish and British citizens; New Zealand permits long-resident non-citizens to vote nationally; and everywhere I have looked, the non-citizen who is permitted to vote is never required to.

Tell me, then, what this bundle presupposes. Not what its defenders say in parliament, which I can read for myself, but what the boundaries of the duty itself commit one to: which conception of voting makes it sensible for the obligation to attach to attendance rather than to a genuine choice, to citizens rather than to all those bound by the law, and to the eighteen-year-old rather than to the sixteen-year-old who has just been handed the ballot? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed carefully enough that I can see which rival accounts it rules out — voting as a right or power conferred on the competent, voting as a duty owed by those subject to coercion, voting as a mere instrument for producing an electorate that is statistically like the population, abstention as itself a form of protected speech — and I want you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, since I suspect several of these features are underdetermined and would survive a change of theory unchanged. Then tell me whether a single principle can generate all three boundaries, or whether, as in a core sample where one meets an unconformity between the lower and the upper beds, we are looking at several incompatible principles deposited at different periods and never reconciled to one another; if the latter, say which bed is load-bearing and which is decorative. Two further things. First, is the Australian tolerance of the blank paper a concession that the state wants bodies rather than judgments, and if so can the usual appeals to legitimacy and to civic duty still carry the weight, or does the practice tacitly rest on the quite different claim that unequal turnout biases outcomes toward the already advantaged? Second, suppose we replaced the membership rule with the principle that everyone subject to a state's coercive law ought to have a say in it, and added that everyone who has a say ought to exercise it: what specifically would have to change in Australia, New Zealand, Ireland and Brazil, and which of those changes would be most fiercely resisted — and does the pattern of the resistance reveal what the existing arrangements were actually for?
