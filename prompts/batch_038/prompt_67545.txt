I am drafting the theoretical-foundations section of a protocol for a standing expert panel that must publish, on a fixed schedule, a probabilistic assessment of whether a specified hazard will exceed a regulatory threshold within a given horizon — an assessment that will be cited in subsequent litigation and cannot be quietly withdrawn. The panel is forbidden from reporting precision it does not have, and equally forbidden from reporting so little that its output carries no decision-relevant content. We intend to allow imprecise, set-valued credences, but before committing to a framework I want to know what we ought to demand of one.

Write the desiderata: the specification a theory of rational imprecise belief must meet to be usable for this purpose. For each requirement, give the consideration behind it together with a concrete micro-case — a small worked example in which a framework lacking the requirement visibly fails the panel (an assessment that cannot be updated on the evidence that was supposed to discriminate between its members, a report that dilates on its own data, an aggregation step that manufactures agreement among dissenting members, and so on) — and label the requirement as non-negotiable, negotiable-but-costly, or a preference of a particular camp, naming the camp.

At minimum the list should confront the following. How the theory individuates the doxastic state it posits: a single state represented by a set, or a family of rival precise states, and whether that choice has any downstream consequences. Whether the representation is unique, or whether one state can be captured by several inequivalent sets. What the members of the set are supposed to be competing with, and why the panel's evidence licenses a set rather than a precise probability. The update rule and its behavior under sequences of evidence, including cases in which conditioning leaves the interval unchanged and cases in which it widens, and whether repeated updating is guaranteed to produce learning. The status of structural norms — the Joycean assumption, nonconglomerability, convexity — and which of these a set-valued account should embrace, reject, or reinterpret. The interface with decision: whether the theory owes the panel a rule for acting on a set, which family of rules it should endorse if so, whether that rule belongs to the theory of belief or is bolted onto it, and what the theory must say when every candidate rule licenses nothing at all. The aggregation of several members' sets into one published assessment. And the charge that imprecision is unstable or self-defeating — that a rational agent should simply select a precise credence, or that a credal set condemns its holder to inaction.

Two further requirements I want you to assess rather than assume. The first is a threshold-discipline constraint I have been imposing informally: the theory must make it determinate, for any given action threshold, whether the published set licenses crossing it, and if it does not, must explain why declining to act is a decision the panel owns rather than a suspension dressed up as prudence. The second is a revisability constraint: because our assessments are cited years after publication, the theory must say how a set may legitimately be widened after the fact without that widening being indistinguishable, from the outside, from having been imprecise all along.

Then tell me which single requirement is doing the most work — the one that eliminates the greatest number of otherwise attractive frameworks — and identify any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, showing as far as you can where the inconsistency arises rather than merely noting a tension. If a purported desideratum is in fact a partisan preference smuggled in as a requirement, say so, and say whose.

Close with a short paragraph scoring the main contenders against the list: the coherent lower-prevision approach, the decision-theoretic approach that represents an agent's state by a set of probability-and-utility pairs and chooses by a maximin-type criterion, and the "sharpen up, imprecision is irrational" reply. A few lines each, no more; the weight belongs on the requirements and their motivating cases, not on the scorecard.

Finally, state how confident you are in the list you have produced. Distinguish the requirements you are confident any adequate theory must meet from those on which reasonable specialists would split, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, together with the reason for your uncertainty.
