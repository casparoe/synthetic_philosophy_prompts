I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I've got a piece half-written on how much of our moral sense is a product of natural selection. Two of my sources — a philosopher, call her R, and an evolutionary biologist, call him D — got into it over email and cc'd me, and I honestly still can't tell who won. Roughly:

(1) D: evolution shaped our moral intuitions for reproductive success, not for truth, so we have no reason to think they're accurate. If our ancestors had been eusocial insects we'd have intuitions telling us to kill our siblings and those would've been just as fit — so the fact that we have the intuitions we have isn't evidence that they're true.

(2) R: there's an escape from that. Survival and flourishing are at least somewhat good, and evolution selected for traits that promoted survival — so our moral beliefs weren't aimed at nothing, they were aimed at something that overlaps with the good. No miracle required.

(3) D: you've assumed the thing in dispute. You start from the moral premise that survival is good and then use it to conclude that evolution tracked moral truth. And on your logic you could vindicate whatever intuitions we happen to have by finding some good thing evolution was pointed at.

(4) R: then notice the same argument works against perception and arithmetic. Those faculties were also shaped for fitness rather then truth. If fitness-tracking licenses skepticism you'd have to be a skeptic about the external world too, and you're not.

(5) D: there's an asymmetry. My visual beliefs get corrected by the world — I look again, I reach out, I'm wrong, I adjust. Nothing does that for moral beliefs. No feedback loop, which is exactly why my insect counterfactual is harmless for vision and lethal for morality.

(6) R: there is feedback, it's called argument. We've overturned huge chunks of our inherited intuitions — about slavery, about women, about who counts — using reasoning that didn't depend on any evolutionary story. At most you've shown one of the inputs is unreliable, not that the output is worthless.

Can you go through this move by move and rate each one (say, clearly wrong to clearly right) with a sentence or two of justification, then say who's more right overall? And if there's something both of them are missing that would change the shape of the argument, I'd rather know that than know who won the dinner-table version.
