I'm in a philosophy-of-mind reading group that has drifted, over the past year, into arguments about large language models, and last week one of our members — a doctoral student in the computer science department who is considerably more patient with our intuitions than we deserve — made an argument I have not been able to shake. He walked us through a transcript in which a model had been given an explicit procedural instruction, had then restated that instruction correctly and even approvingly in the middle of its own reasoning, and had proceeded to violate it two paragraphs later. His diagnosis was immediate: this is Carroll's tortoise, exactly. Achilles keeps writing new hypotheticals into the enormous notebook he carries, the tortoise cheerfully accepts each one the moment it is written down, remarks that there are plenty of blank leaves left and that they will need them all, and still never moves from A and B to Z; likewise, he said, you can keep adding instructions, few-shot exemplars, chain-of-thought scaffolds, and constitutional preambles to the context, and the model will accept every one of them without complaint and still fail to take the step. The notebook, on his account, is the context window, and Carroll's dialogue is best read as a prediction about the limits of writing rules down.

From there he drew a fairly strong conclusion, and it is the conclusion I want audited rather than the transcript. Rule-following, he said, is not a matter of what is inscribed somewhere in the system but of how the system is built: you need an execution engine, not a larger data store, and the machine applies modus ponens the way a processor applies an add instruction, because of what it is made to do, not because of any further item it possesses. It followed, he thought, that anyone hoping to close the gap by better prompting, better-specified principles, or clearer statements of the rules is simply Achilles with a bigger notebook, and that the fix has to be architectural. Someone objected that the analogy proves too much, since a processor executing an instruction is itself just another item in the notebook at the level below, and he replied that this was precisely the point: the regress bottoms out in hardware, in what happens, rather than in what is stated.

Could you lay out the correspondence explicitly before evaluating it — a table is welcome — saying what plays the role of Achilles, the tortoise, the notebook and its blank leaves, the premises already accepted, the hypothetical that keeps getting appended, and the step that never gets taken; then give me the disanalogies, and please separate the ones that genuinely threaten his inference from the ones that are merely decorative. I'd like you to put particular pressure on three points. First, the tortoise accepts every new premise without hesitation and its difficulty is that nothing compels the transition, whereas the model in the transcript already possessed the rule and could articulate it, which raises the question whether the notebook is an analogue of anything at all, or whether the model sits somewhere between Carroll's two characters. Second, what the reply that the regress bottoms out in hardware actually amounts to when the original regress was about justification: does pointing at what the machine does answer the tortoise's demand for a reason to make the step, or does it quietly change the subject from why one ought to infer to what will happen next — and if the latter, does the tortoise remain unmoved for all that? Third, whether the analogy smuggles in a picture of a rule as an item stored somewhere inside the system, which is arguably the very picture the regress was constructed to explode, so that his architectural cure may depend on the disease. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives the failure of the analogy — that is, whether it can be supported independently, without the tortoise carrying the weight — and if this is the wrong vehicle for the phenomenon he is pointing at, offer a better one and say where the replacement also strains. Please write in American English, and don't trouble yourself with references, bibliography, or a reading list; what I want is the reasoning itself.
