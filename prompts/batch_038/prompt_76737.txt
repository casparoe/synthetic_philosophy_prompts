I convene a small philosophy-of-economics reading group, and next week's session concerns what utility language can be made to mean once observed choice is all the evidence there is. To frame the discussion, I would like a dialogue between two equally well-equipped theorists, neither of whom should be allowed to lose cheaply.

The scene: a seminar room just after a methods talk. On the table is a printout of fifty-two rows of prices and purchased bundles for one household over a year. The data pass a GARP test, so by Afriat's theorem some increasing, concave utility function rationalizes them; the reconstruction the group ran produces one such function, though of course every order-preserving transformation of it does too. Two participants are still standing over the printout when the dialogue begins.

The characters:

- Selma, who defends the revealed-preference program in its Samuelsonian spirit: consumer theory is about choice; "utility" is at best a numerical index of an ordering recovered from demand data; and the theory's independence from introspective "states of satisfaction" is its principal methodological virtue rather than a defect. She is not a crude positivist, does not think utility talk is literally meaningless, and is perfectly willing to follow the argument into philosophy of science and philosophy of mind.
- Tomás, a preference realist: unless utility terms stand for something with content of its own — a hedonic or conative state, or at minimum a property playing an explanatory role independent of the choice patterns by which it is measured — the theory redescribes behaviour rather than explaining it, and it cannot underwrite the ordinary welfare claims economists nonetheless make (that the household is better off after a price change, that a tax genuinely hurt these people). He is not a hedonist by default, he knows the lottery-based apparatus well, and he will not simply repeat that Selma's theory is empty.

Requirements:

1. Both must argue at full strength. Every move Selma makes should be one a good economist-philosopher would actually make, and every move Tomás makes should be one that cannot be dismissed as nostalgia for the introspectionism that Robbins and Samuelson were reacting against.
2. Genuine back-and-forth: each turn must engage the specific content of the turn immediately preceding it, not a prepared speech. No talking past each other, no restating positions in fresh vocabulary.
3. The dialogue must pass through, and neither side may duck, these four pressure points:
   - the non-uniqueness point — whether the fact that the data fix only an ordering, and that any monotone transformation of the reconstructed function rationalizes equally well, shows that utility is meaningless or merely non-unique, and what Selma's theory is actually claiming when it says the household "has" a utility function;
   - the empirical content point — what observation could falsify Selma's theory, given that she is happy to redescribe any anomaly (menu dependence, social norms, the guest who takes the smaller portion while wanting the larger) as revealing a more finely individuated choice situation rather than as a failure of preference;
   - the cardinal pivot — whether moving from bundles to lotteries buys Selma more than an ordering, since there the representation is unique up to positive affine transformation, and what Tomás says that extra structure does or does not mean if no magnitude of satisfaction is being measured;
   - the identity move — Selma's proposal that utility is not eliminated but identified with a higher-order disposition to choose, on the model of a theoretical property that earns its keep by its explanatory role, and Tomás's reply about multiple realizability and about whether an identity whose left-hand side has no content independent of the right-hand side is anything but a stipulation.
4. At least two genuine concessions, one from each side, each costing the conceder something she or he would have preferred to keep. Make the cost explicit in the dialogue rather than in narration.
5. End at a live impasse in which each states precisely what argument or evidence would move them, and neither is portrayed as having won.
6. Minimal stage directions, no narrator commentary, no moderator, and no summarizing speech inside the dialogue. No bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus; refer to at most the figures already named above, plus Pareto's coinage of "ophelimity" if it earns its place. I want arguments, not references.
7. Length: 1,200 to 1,600 words of dialogue.

After the dialogue, give me a structured summary of its dialectic as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- `turns`: an array, one entry per turn in order, each with `turn` (integer), `speaker` ("Selma" or "Tomás"), `move_type` (one of "assert", "object", "reply", "concede", "clarify", "escalate"), `claim` (one sentence giving the substantive point made in that turn), `responds_to` (the integer of the turn being answered, or null if it opens a new line), and `key_concept` (the technical notion the turn turns on, e.g. "GARP", "positive affine uniqueness", "menu dependence", "dispositional identity").
- `concessions`: an array of objects with `by`, `conceded` (what was given up), and `cost` (what the concession costs that position).
- `pressure_points`: an array of four objects, one for each pressure point listed above, each with `issue`, `selma_best` (the strongest thing she says about it), `tomas_best` (the strongest thing he says about it), `verdict` (one of "Selma ahead", "Tomás ahead", "dead even"), and `why` (one or two sentences justifying the verdict).
- `what_would_settle_it`: an object with `selma_would_need` and `tomas_would_need`, each one sentence.
- `shared_premise`: one sentence identifying the commitment both sides turn out to share that the dialogue exposes.
