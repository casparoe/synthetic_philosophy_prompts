Our student philosophy society runs a staged series in which an actor plays a dead thinker in live, unscripted question-and-answer before an audience of about eighty people. Next term's programme is Kierkegaard, and the actor has prepared chiefly from the 1846 review of Gyllembourg's novel — the third part of which circulates in English as The Present Age — together with the journals from the Corsair affair. He is a good performer, but he has two habits I want broken: he retreats to edifying generalities about the single individual whenever he is cornered, and he treats every question as an occasion for upbuilding rather than as something requiring an answer.

Please draft ten to twelve questions for me to put to him, in the order I should ask them. For each, give a one-line note on the reply he is most likely to give, and then a follow-up that makes that reply uncomfortable to keep. The questions must turn on the content of the position rather than on his biography or his temperament: nothing of the "what first drew you to philosophy" variety, and nothing that simply invites him to restate the view.

At minimum, cover the following ground:

- The mechanism. Everyone repeats his claim that the public is a phantom, an abstraction that never assembles; almost nobody can say how the press is supposed to produce it. Ask him to explain why a numerical totality of readers who never meet differs in kind from a generation, a people, or an actual assembly, and why the daily press is causally necessary to that difference rather than merely its symptom.

- A difficulty I do not think he has an answer to. His mechanism requires that everyone read the same paper. On platforms that feed each person different material, there is no shared readership at all. Does that dissolve the public, or has the instrument of levelling become more efficient by never permitting one to form in the first place?

- Levelling. He identifies envy as the negatively unifying principle of the age, and he also suggests that levelling, driven far enough, is a discipline through which the single individual may become possible. Press him on whether the second claim is anything more than a consolation for the first, and ask what would distinguish it from the rationalisation of circumstances he cannot alter.

- The Corsair affair. He went out of his way, in print, to invite a satirical weekly that had hitherto reviewed his books favourably to attack him; it obliged within days, and the caricatures made his trousers, his cane and his gait into public property. I want a question that requires him to say what in that sequence counts as evidence for the theory rather than as motive for it.

- His own practice. Pseudonyms, newspaper articles, an authorship plainly designed to be talked about. If the press cannot be used without its user being levelled by it, how does his escape?

- Politics. Whether the category of the single individual condemns coordinated public action — petitions, organised labour, resistance to an occupying power — and, if he permits some collective action, what distinguishes a legitimate assembly from the public, given that both consist of the same individuals reading the same papers.

- Refutation. A closing question that demands a falsifiable prediction: what observation would he accept as showing that the public is something other than a word in his own vocabulary?

Two further constraints. Do not place any quotation from Kierkegaard inside a question; the actor works from memory, and I would rather paraphrase than prompt him into inventing a citation. And do not let the sequence finish on the upbuilding note he will want — I want the final exchange to be one he cannot convert into an edifying remark.
