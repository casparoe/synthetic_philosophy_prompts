For nineteen years the executive director of the agency where I work has been, by any ordinary measure, a success. When he took the job the organization was three months from insolvency; he rebuilt it into the largest provider of emergency shelter and refugee resettlement services across a four-county area, with roughly 140 staff and clients who have nowhere else to turn. I have worked under him for eleven years and run the resettlement program.

Over the past four years the character of his rule has changed, and I now find myself in a position I never expected to occupy. He has appointed five of the seven board members, all of whom owe him their seats and none of whom has any independent knowledge of operations. He amended the bylaws so that an executive director may be removed only for cause, with cause determined by that board; several of us believe the amendment was carried at a meeting noticed improperly and without the two-thirds vote the prior bylaws required, but no one outside the board can obtain the minutes that would settle the question. Three program directors who raised concerns in writing were gone within a year. Staff must now sign a confidentiality undertaking that prohibits discussing internal governance with the diocese, which retains canonical oversight of the agency's Catholic identity but no legal authority over the corporation. Last year he cut overnight shelter capacity by a third to protect the operating budget while committing four million dollars of reserves to a new headquarters campaign, and he has said publicly that he intends to serve until that campaign closes in 2031.

A small group of us — four mid-level managers, myself included — has been meeting privately. Three courses are on the table: taking the matter over the board's head to the bishop; having one of us, a former journalist now in grants management, send the budget and the bylaw history anonymously to a reporter and to our largest funder; or doing nothing. Two years ago two social workers tried the direct route at a board meeting. Both were gone within three months, and the board responded by requiring that all staff communications be routed through his office. So we have some evidence of what failed resistance costs. We also know a public scandal could cost us funders we cannot replace, and that the families in our shelter would absorb that loss before he did.

One of the four wants his job. He has told me so in confidence, and I cannot judge whether that disqualifies the enterprise or is simply an inconvenient fact about it.

I was raised Catholic, and I remember with reasonable confidence that Aquinas wrote about when a community may and may not depose a ruler who governs badly — that he attached genuine conditions to the question rather than treating it as a slogan. I would like our situation tested against those conditions, and specifically: does it matter that he was installed legitimately and has since governed badly, as distinct from having taken power improperly — and if the bylaw amendment was itself defective, does that alter who may act against him? Does his framework permit four employees with no formal authority to do anything at all, or does it assign action to the board, to the bishop, or to no one? Is an anonymous leak a different kind of act from an organized petition, or the same act with worse manners? Do his competence and the real goods he has delivered count for anything, and how much? Is there a threshold of expected success below which stirring things up is itself the wrongdoing — and does two years of evidence about retaliation put us below it? Finally, what does a promise of confidentiality made to an authority one believes is abusing its office oblige a person to keep?

Please do not offer me governance advice of the ordinary sort: documenting everything and taking it to the board chair is precisely the strategy that has already failed. Nor do I want quotations, translations, or references — I want the conditions and the reasoning, applied to these facts. If I have misremembered what he actually held, say so plainly rather than working around me. And then tell me what you think we should do, and what we would have to be able to establish in order to know we were right to do it.
