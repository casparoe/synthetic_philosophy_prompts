I serve as the recording secretary on a hospital ethics committee, and after a split vote I was asked to send an outside reviewer a neutral account of the argument so that the reviewer can judge the reasoning rather than the outcome. The referral concerned a prospective mother who carries a mutation in her mitochondrial DNA that causes a severe, often fatal disease in childhood. The clinic can prevent it by replacing the mitochondria in her egg with those of a donor. The protocol is investigational, its long-term risks are unknown, and the alteration it makes is heritable.

Member A voted against approval.

(1) A's opening: the child who results cannot consent to being the first in a line to carry donor mitochondria, so approving the procedure uses that child as a means to the development of a technique.

Member B voted for approval.

(2) B's reply: the objection presupposes someone who is made worse off, and there is no such person. If the procedure is not performed, the child it would have produced never exists, and the child who exists instead — the same nuclear genome, the diseased mitochondria — is a numerically different individual. The choice therefore settles who exists rather than how well off anyone is, and B could not say who has been wronged.

(3) A: that reasoning generalizes further than B wants. If no child can ever complain about the conditions of its own conception, then no reproductive act whatever can wrong the child it produces, and a premise with that consequence has refuted itself.

(4) B: it has shown only that wronging requires a subject. Where an intervention bears on a child who would have existed anyway, the objection keeps its full force; A is applying a person-affecting constraint to a case in which there is no person left for it to protect.

(5) A's last move: then the objection should be stated impersonally. Some outcomes are worse without being worse for anyone, and B's position commits the committee to denying that anything can be bad unless it is bad for somebody — a considerably larger claim than the vote requires.

Please adjudicate each of the five numbered moves separately: is it right, partly right, or wrong, and why? Then say whose position survives overall, name any premise either member asserts without having earned it, and identify what both of them are leaving out of the dispute.
