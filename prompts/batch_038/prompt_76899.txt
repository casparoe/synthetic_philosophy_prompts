I hold a position on biological function that I want stress-tested rather than defended, and I am preparing it for a philosophy of biology working group. Stated roughly: a trait has a function only if there is a history in which the trait was retained by some mechanism that kept reproducing or reconstructing it because that mechanism was sensitive to the effect in question. Three commitments come with it. First, what causal-role theorists call functions are really dispositions — claims about what a part does, not about why it is there — and the two should not be run together. Second, retention does not require anything like the reproduction of a biological lineage; copying, repair, re-derivation, and reinforcement all qualify, so a Hebbian synapse strengthened because it contributed to a rewarded behavior, an antibody clone expanded because it bound an antigen, and a drainage scupper that a bridge inspector keeps specifying because it prevents deck corrosion all fall under one heading. Third, sensitivity does the real work: the retaining mechanism must register the effect, not merely coexist with it.

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure, not a defense of it. Please address the following, and do not supply references, bibliographies, or a citation apparatus — I want the arguments and the cases, not pointers to literature.

1. Cases it must describe and arguably cannot. For each of the following, say whether the view breaks, bends, or survives, and why: a trait now fixed in a population, with no variation left for retention to act on; a novel structure retained by luck for a single generation and then lost; lens crystallins, recruited from proteins whose earlier job was ordinary metabolism; promiscuous enzymes that catalyze a second reaction no history of retention mentions; and vestigial organs that still do something.

2. Whether the view collapses into a rival. Once a critic adds the requirement that the mechanism register the effect, can a purely causal-role account mimic everything the sensitivity clause delivers? And if someone said my account is just selected-effects theory with the population requirement quietly deleted, or just causal-role theory with an etiological epicycle bolted on, what is the strongest available reply, and is that reply decisive or merely dialectically convenient?

3. Malfunction. Does an etiology-free rival actually lose anything here, or can it accommodate every dysfunction case I claim as my advantage?

4. Concessions. Which of the above would a committed holder of my view accept as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as merely awkward? Rank the resulting defeaters from fatal, through seriously damaging, to cosmetic.

5. Vacuity. Is there any observation, experimental result, or conceptual development that would force me to abandon the view? Or have the elasticity of "retention" and the vagueness of "sensitivity" insulated it to the point where nothing could count against it — and if so, is that a genuine defect for a view of this kind, or an acceptable feature of a framework whose job is conceptual sorting rather than empirical prediction?

Finally, state what my view commits me to that its rivals are not committed to, and identify the single commitment you would attack first if your aim were to bring the whole structure down rather than to qualify it.
