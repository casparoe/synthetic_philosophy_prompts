I spent thirty-five years in observational cosmology and have come to philosophy only since retiring, so I should be grateful if you would answer as a professional philosopher of physics who has actually worked through Gödel's cosmological argument, rather than as someone surveying a literature on my behalf. Think the matter through before writing, and open with two or three sentences fixing the standard you intend to apply: what makes a reply count as engaging Gödel's inference at all, as against changing the subject.

As I understand the argument, the rotating solution Gödel published in 1949 contains closed timelike curves beyond a certain radius, and because the field equations permit matter to be arranged so that a journey into one's own past would be possible, he concluded that time in the sense of an objective lapse, a succession of nows, does not exist, and is ideal in something like Kant's sense. The premise strikes me as modal rather than observational.

Please rank the following six replies by how much dialectical bite they retain against an argument construed that way, attaching a number out of ten to each as you go: (i) that the real universe shows no global rotation, so the model is beside the point; (ii) Einstein's own reply, pressing whether such a matter distribution could be physically realised rather than merely consistent with the field equations; (iii) chronology-protection-style appeals to quantum back-reaction; (iv) the charge that Gödel slides from the absence of a global time ordering in a spacetime to the unreality of becoming; (v) the charge of redundancy, that the relativity of simultaneity in ordinary Minkowski spacetime already yields everything Gödel wants, so the rotation contributes theatre rather than argument; (vi) that even if becoming is absent from the geometry, this shows only that it is no part of the physical description, which an A-theorist may happily grant.

Add one entry of my own devising and tell me where it belongs: that (iii) concedes Gödel's point, since if what rules out the journey is a contingent feature of the actual matter distribution or of back-reaction rather than of spacetime structure, the impossibility is not of a kind that could underwrite an objective flow.

Say plainly if any of these is a straw man that no serious person offers, and if some reply I have not listed dominates them all, name it and rank it as well. I am not after references or further reading; I want your judgment. Roughly six hundred words, please, in continuous prose, the way one writes a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
