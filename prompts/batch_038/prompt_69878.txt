I was at a synthetic biology workshop last weekend and got trapped between two people at the coffee break who argued for about half an hour over whether DNA is literally a code, and I honestly couldnt have refereed it. Neither of them is in my field so I'm reconstructing as best I can.

Ravi runs a genome-recoding project. He opened by saying 'genetic code' isn't a metaphor in the way 'cellular machinery' is a metaphor — it's the one place in nature where you actually find an arbitrary mapping table between symbols and what they get mapped onto, and he said the proof is that his lab can rewrite the table. They took a bacterium, replaced every instance of one codon across the whole genome, deleted the release factor that reads it as a stop, and the thing is alive and making proteins. If the assignments were fixed by chemistry you couldn't do that. He also said it isn't even one table: vertebrate mitochondria read UGA as tryptophan, some yeasts read CUG as serine, certain ciliates read the stop codons as glutamine, so the same string of letters gets decoded differently depending on who's doing the reading, which he took to be exactly what a code is.

Nadia is a philosopher of biology. She said he'd shown the correspondence is contingent, not that it's semantic. Her line was that the mapping is just a description of what the synthetases and the ribosome physically do, so DNA doesn't mean protein, it causes protein. She used a lock: the pin configuration corresponds one-to-one with the cut of the key, you could even write out a table of which pin heights go with which bitting, but nobody says the lock contains information about the key. Then she said the reason biologists keep reaching for code talk is that it lets them say 'the gene for X' and quietly import a sender, and in any case the reader isn't in the message — the decoding apparatus is an entire cell, built by maternal RNAs, cytoplasm, mitochondria and a pile of other stuff that isn't in the sequence, and treating DNA as a code is precisely what licenses genetic determinism.

Ravi's reply was that every code needs a reader: Morse needs operators, ASCII needs a machine, so needing a reader can't be what makes code talk metaphorical or nothing would literally be a code. He added that what separates DNA from the lock is that the sequence survives a round trip through arbitrary media — you could print the genome out in a book, chemically resynthesize it from the printed letters, put it into a cell and get the organism back. Locks don't survive that.

Nadia conceded the reader point but said the round trip only shows DNA is a stable, re-inscribable cause, i.e. correlation, Shannon-level stuff. Then she turned the variant codes around and claimed them for herself: they show the string has no meaning of its own, since its contribution depends entirely on the machinery it lands in. She also said the standard table isn't nearly as arbitrary as he implies — similar codons tend to pick up similar amino acids, and the code is unusually robust to point mutations compared to most alternatives you could write down, which looks more like frozen chemistry with selection laid over it than like a free choice of symbols. Ravi said that cuts in his favor: robustness like that is a maintained functional property, misreading is costly, so the table is the kind of thing that can be optimized and re-engineered, which is code-like rather than chemical. And he said selenocysteine is the killer case — a UGA gets read as an amino acid only when there's a particular RNA stem-loop downstream signaling the ribosome to, which he described as the message carrying its own decoding instructions.

They ran out of time and wandered off. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step: Ravi's recoding/contingency move, the variant-code move (they both claimed it, which seemed odd to me), the lock analogy, the needs-a-reader objection and his answer to it, the round-trip-through-a-book argument, the robustness exchange, and the selenocysteine point. For each one, say whether it lands, partly lands, or misses, and why. Then tell me whether any of this was just a verbal dispute about the word code — and if so which moves were verbal and which were doing real work — plus what they were both ignoring. My suspicion is that they slid between two different notions of information without noticing, but I couldn't pin down where the slide happens.
