I am one of eleven trustees on the board of a county hospital system in the upper Midwest — three of us are appointed by the county commission, I am the only clinician on the board, and I am in my second year, which means I am still new enough to be asked to write things like this. The system runs a 400-bed safety-net hospital and six outpatient clinics. In August our nurses' union, roughly 2,300 members, opened bargaining on a successor contract; the live issue is minimum nurse-to-patient ratios, and the secondary issue is our reliance on agency nurses, which now absorbs about eighteen percent of the nursing budget. Talks broke down at the end of October. The union has given ten days' notice, as its current agreement requires, that an indefinite job action begins on the fourteenth.

Here is the part I need help with. Union leadership has told the board privately that it is not calling a strike. It is calling a work-to-rule: no voluntary overtime, no coming in on scheduled days off, no covering vacant shifts, no deferring meal breaks, strict compliance with every documented policy, checklist, and handoff protocol, and complete contemporaneous documentation of any request that conflicts with a written standard. Their own estimate, shared with us, is a thirty percent reduction in throughput at the main campus, with elective surgery and infusion scheduling hit first. Our general counsel thinks a slowdown of this kind is probably unprotected activity, but she also says the position is unsettled for public employees in our state, and I do not want the memo to relitigate that; treat the legal question as counsel's problem and the ethical question as ours. Several of my colleagues think the work-to-rule is worse than a straightforward strike — it keeps the doors open so that patients and the press never see the action, it degrades care gradually instead of announcing itself, and it strands people who have no way to redirect to another facility, since we closed the region's only other inpatient safety-net service line two years ago. A friend of mine who practices labor law thinks this is sentiment: labor is priced, the work-to-rule is simply the union charging full price for the first time, and any further difference is aesthetic. I do not accept that, and I cannot yet say why.

Draft the memo I will circulate to the board before Thursday's meeting. Put the recommendation in the first paragraph, choosing among at least these three courses or constructing a better one: (a) treat the work-to-rule as a strike and respond as we would to a strike; (b) treat it as a tactic within a still-open negotiation, continue bargaining past the fourteenth, and impose no discipline and no outside relief; (c) trade a conditional truce for something structural — the union suspends the action, and the board commits to a staffing floor with a defined funding mechanism, or to binding interest arbitration. Give reasons my colleagues can actually argue with, and attach a time frame to whatever you recommend. Then do the conceptual work explicitly: what distinguishes withdrawing one's labor from withholding the discretionary, uncontracted cooperation that a thirty percent throughput loss suggests the institution was silently relying on; whether the visibility of the harm has moral weight or only political weight; whether the patients' inability to exit changes what the union owes them, and if so whether that is a fact about the tactic or a fact about who the employer happens to be; and what the case says about the scope of the right to organize when the thing being withheld is not labor but goodwill. State the strongest case against your recommendation better than I could state it, and answer it rather than dismissing it. If you think my framing is wrong — that the question the board actually faces is not the one I have posed — say so in a short paragraph at the end, but still deliver the memo as specified. Please skip background on labor law and on the history of collective bargaining; assume the board knows the basics. Write in American English, roughly 900 to 1,200 words for the memo itself, and then append a summary table with exactly these columns: Option (labeled as above or with your own label); What It Requires of the Board (one or two sentences); Expected Effect on the Bargaining; Who Bears the Harm, and How Visibly; Strongest Objection; How the Memo Answers It; Verdict (recommend / fallback / reject). Keep each cell to a phrase or a single sentence so the whole table fits on one page.
