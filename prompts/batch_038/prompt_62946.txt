I work in insurance claims, and I keep needing a distinction I don't have a word for. Two adjusters look at the same storm. One is assigned a levee, doesn't reinforce it, and the town behind it floods. Another walks past an unassigned levee, doesn't reinforce it, and a different town floods. Physically these are the same sort of thing: something that could have been strengthened wasn't, and water got in. Yet we say the first adjuster caused the flood and the second merely failed to prevent one — and it isn't only his employer's assignment doing the work, since a bystander with no duties at all still doesn't strike us as a cause, even though counterfactually the damage would have been avoided had he acted. What seems to matter is whether there was a standing expectation that made the absence noticeable rather than just another thing that didn't happen.

But it can't be only that. When the bystander is the one person positioned to act, knows it, and is quietly glad of the outcome, my intuitions start sliding toward calling him a cause after all, with no role or expectation in sight.

I gather that causation by omission, and the worry that otherwise every absence becomes a cause, is a live topic in philosophy; I also gather that the same appeal to what is expected or normal gets used to explain which of many positive factors we single out as "the" cause of something. So: is there an established name or position for the view that whether an omission causes anything is settled by roles and expectations rather than by counterfactual dependence? How does it differ from the general business of picking salient causes out of many conditions, and where does my second case fall — a counterexample to that view, or something it has resources to absorb? And if there is no established term, what would you call it, and what would you take its boundary conditions to be?
