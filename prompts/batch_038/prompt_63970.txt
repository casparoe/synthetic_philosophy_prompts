so this has gone from something I read about to something sitting on my kitchen table, and before I make a decision with my own views I want someone to check whether they actually cohere.

context: I'm 39, my wife is 42. There's a late-onset condition that runs down my side of the family — usually starts in your fifties, no cure, shortens a life and roughens it considerably, but I don't think it makes a life not worth living. My mum had it. I got tested in my twenties and I have it too. Our clinic says we could do IVF with embryo testing to avoid passing it on, but at her age that's realistically 18 months of cycles for maybe one or two usable embryos, and every month we spend on that is a month where the kid who would have existed doesn't. I'm leaning toward just trying naturally now. And I've noticed I've been leaning on a pile of philosophical claims to feel okay about that lean, so I want the pile audited rather than the decision.

here's roughly where I am:

1. Wrongness has to land on somebody. An act is wrong only if it's bad for some particular person — someone who ends up worse off because of it. I don't accept "the world is worse" as a free-standing verdict when there's nobody in it who's worse off.

2. So you can't wrong someone by making them, provided their life is worth living. And there's no duty to make happy people — I'm not obliged to have six kids. Though I do think a life that goes well is genuinely good *for the person living it*, not just a neutral event.

3. Above a floor — a life that's really worth living — I've done my bit by that person. I don't think you owe your kid the best possible start, only that you don't start someone below the line.

4. Except: if you're already going to have a kid, and you could instead have one whose life goes noticeably better, and it costs you nothing to do it, then not doing it seems like a real failure. This is the intuition that keeps pushing me toward the IVF.

5. For choices that settle *who* exists, the test I keep coming back to is this: would the person who ends up existing, as an adult looking back on it, endorse the choice? Not "would they wish they'd never been born" — that's the wrong question and almost everybody answers it the same way — but "would they say, yeah, go ahead, do that."

6. My mum knew she carried it before she had me and went ahead anyway. I think what she did was wrong. I'm also glad she did it, I'd endorse her choice today, and I'd want my own daughter to make it in her position. Both of those are things I believe and I'm not especially bothered by holding them.

7. I don't think intentions carry any weight in these cases, and I don't think the doing/letting line does either. All that counts is how the lives of the people who end up existing go. And separately: a couple who deliberately transfers the affected embryo does something worse than a couple who just declines to test. I'd defend both of those sentences.

8. I also think this decision is mine and my wife's in a way it wouldn't be a hospital ethics board's, even though I don't think any of the facts about the kid change depending on who's doing the deciding. Someone at a dinner party told me that was obviously inconsistent and I didn't have anything to say back.

what I want is an audit, not a lecture on the nonidentity problem — I know the shape of the standard moves and I'm not asking you to run me through them. So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which ones only look like they do? I'd rather you got the second part right than be thorough about the first, because I suspect at least two of these are fine and I've been worrying about nothing. Rank the real tensions by how much they'd cost me to fix, most expensive first. And for each one, tell me specifically which sentence has to go or be rewritten and what replaces it — not "reconsider your person-affecting commitment," but the actual edit. Where an apparent tension dissolves, say what the distinction was that I couldn't see.
