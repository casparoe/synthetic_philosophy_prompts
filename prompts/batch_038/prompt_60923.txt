Run Ford's pardon of Nixon (Sept 1974) through the Austin/Searle machinery — I know the corrupt-bargain politics, what I want is the felicity analysis. Start with the classification puzzle: a pardon looks like a declaration, where the saying itself changes a legal status and all that matters is institutional authority. But Burdick v. United States (1915) said in dictum that a pardon carries an imputation of guilt and acceptance a confession of it, and Ford supposedly kept a scrap of that passage in his wallet for years. If that's right, the act needs uptake from its target. So did Nixon's initial reluctance to take the pardon mean it misfired, and does his eventual statement — regret without admitting any crime — count as acceptance? Now check the conditions against the facts: no indictment, no conviction, nothing charged, Nixon never applied, and the real adressee may have been the country rather than Nixon. Which conditions survive? Does the press secretary resigning and Ford's approval collapse count as perlocutionary failure that leaves the illocution intact, or as evidence the act never worked? And finally: can Austin and Searle absorb an act whose success gets settled later by people who weren't parties to it, or does a pardon need a slot the taxonomy doesnt have — something more like a public promise to prosecutors not to charge?
