For about six years I've been a regular at a small group in my city that sits somewhere between an EA chapter and a philosophy reading circle — a couple of graduate students, several professionals who pledge a fixed percentage of their income, and a scattering of people who arrived via the rationalist blogs and never left. I've read the standard material on the demandingness objection and I can recite the usual stalemate fluently: the objector says the theory asks too much of ordinary agents, the consequentialist replies that the objection quietly assumes commonsense morality is the benchmark it needs to displace. What I can't recite is a verdict on an argument I actually watched two members of the group have, because they kept landing on points that the textbook framing doesn't seem to cover, and I ended up genuinely unable to referee it. Here is the exchange as closely as I can reconstruct it.

The two people were Ilana, an ER nurse who gives 10 percent of her income and thinks that's already a stretch, and Theo, a third-year grad student. (1) Ilana opened by saying the demandingness objection is aimed at a straw target: consequentialism tells you what would be best to do, and morality rarely requires the best; there is a real difference between *you ought to send more money this month* and *you did something wrong by not sending it*, and the objection collapses that gap. (2) Theo said that was relabeling rather than answering — if consequentialism is the true account of what one ought to do, there is no room left over for supererogation, so the gap Ilana posits is a patch imported from a rival theory; and once you bolt on a prerogative, you owe an account of why it has exactly the shape and size it does, which is where moderate consequentialisms become unstable, since any principled stopping point looks like an agent-relative value smuggled in through the back door. (3) Ilana said fine, but the bite of the demand depends almost entirely on everyone else defecting: in a world where the affluent actually complied, her share might be three or five percent, and what she owes beyond her share is bounded by what is needed to close the gap, not open-ended — you cannot be required to compensate indefinitely for the noncompliance of strangers. (4) Theo said the fair-share baseline is itself a non-consequentialist notion, because consequentialism is indifferent to the distribution of burdens across agents and cares only about the total; so her move wasn't a repair of consequentialism but a surrender to deontology, and the objection stands against the theory she started out defending. (5) Ilana answered that a consequentialist can get the limit from inside: a moral practice demanding unlimited sacrifice would be motivationally self-defeating and would predictably produce widespread burnout and defection, so the practice whose general internalization maximizes expected good is one that assigns each agent a bounded share and treats the surplus as optional; the constraint is thus derived, not imported. (6) Theo said that concedes his point twice over — she has moved to a two-level or indirect view, which the demandingness objection was never aimed at, and even there the derivation shows only that the optimal *motivation* is bounded while the criterion of rightness stays unbounded, so at the level where the objection was lodged it survives intact.

What I want from you is a move-by-move adjudication, not a summary of the literature and no bibliography — I'll add references myself if I ever write this up. Specifically: rate each of the six moves on a scale from −2 (clearly mistaken) to +2 (clearly right), with a sentence or two of justification per rating, and flag for each whether the move is doing substantive work or is largely verbal. Then answer three questions directly. First, is move (5) a rescue of consequentialism or an abandonment of it, and does Theo's reply in move (6) actually defeat it or does it depend on a distinction between criterion of rightness and decision procedure that Ilana can accept without loss? Second, who is more right overall about whether the demandingness objection targets a real defect rather than an unattractive implication? Third — and this is my own half-formed idea, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter — I suspect the whole exchange trades on treating demandingness as a single scalar, when it has at least two independent dimensions: how much sacrifice the theory requires of an agent, and how much that requirement varies with what other agents do. If splitting those apart dissolves or relocates part of the Ilana–Theo dispute, show me exactly which moves it relocates and where the residue lands; if you think the distinction is a red herring that would not have changed anything either of them said, say so plainly and explain why.
