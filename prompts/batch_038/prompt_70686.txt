I am preparing a paper that sits at the junction of the philosophy of economics and literary criticism, and I would like a close reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day through the problem of what revealed preference can and cannot tell us about a person's good. Please treat this as criticism rather than summary. I know the novel: the 1956 motoring trip to Cornwall, the retrospection about Darlington Hall in the 1920s and 30s, Miss Kenton and her letter, Lord Darlington's postwar disgrace, the closing scene on the seafront. Do not attempt to quote it, and do not offer a bibliography; work from its structure and episodes as you recall them, and flag any detail you are unsure of rather than inventing it.

Please organize the reading around a three-way distinction that I am stipulating for this purpose. These labels are mine, not established terminology, and part of what I want from you is a judgment about whether the novel forces a fourth stance or collapses one of these into another:

1. Definitional revealed preference: choice constitutes the ordering. A preference is nothing over and above a disposition to choose, so the claim that Stevens did not really want what he chose is either false or empty.
2. Evidential revealed preference: choice is fallible evidence of an independently existing ordering, which leaves room for self-deception, repression, and weakness of will — behaviour can misdescribe the mind.
3. Reflective or endorsed preference: an ordering counts as the agent's own only insofar as she would stand behind it under informed and unpressured reflection, which makes the specification of the option set and the conditions of choice theoretically load-bearing.

Three episodes seem to me to separate the stances sharply, and I would like each taken in turn. First, the international conference at the Hall during which Stevens's father is dying upstairs while Stevens goes on serving dinner: under (1) this is a clean revelation of what he preferred; under (3) the question becomes what the relevant menu was at all, since an agent who experiences alternatives not as forbidden but as unthinkable may not face a two-option choice. Tell me whether the theory has the resources to handle that, and how much work the distinction between an unavailable option and an unconsidered one can bear. Second, the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids on Lord Darlington's instruction, and the way the 1956 narration retrospectively reframes Stevens's attitude toward it. Third, the seafront, where Stevens concedes that at least Lord Darlington made his own mistakes, takes up the stranger's suggestion that the evening is the best part of the day, and resolves to acquire the skill of bantering for his new American employer: is that a preference revision that retroactively revalues the earlier life, a late-formed preference ranking an earlier self, or a capitulation that reveals nothing?

Then I want the harder problem addressed directly. The novel is a first-person retrospective narration in which the narrator repeatedly corrects, minimizes, and reinterprets his own past. That produces two agents — the butler of 1923 and 1936, and the narrator of 1956 — whose choices and whose avowals are competing data. Under each of the three stances, say whose preferences the earlier conduct reveals, whether the later narration supplies new evidence about the past ordering or constitutes a change in it, and whether a theory built on choice data can represent an agent extended in time without either arbitrarily privileging one temporal slice or dissolving the person into a series.

Finally, and most importantly: where does the novel resist all three readings, and what does it put into the debate that formal argument does not? I am particularly interested in whether Ishiguro's device of a narration that keeps revising itself performs a distinction that revealed preference theory struggles to state — between his choosing it, his wanting it, and his wanting to want it — and in whether the novel's own emotional register endorses a welfare verdict on Stevens's life or refuses to supply one. Conclude by saying plainly which of the three stances the novel, taken as a whole, puts the most pressure on, and whether a reader who arrived holding that stance should leave it modified, abandoned, or merely better informed about its limits.
