Our state science and engineering fair closes each year with a public lecture session at which three student finalists present their projects to the judges and to a community audience. I am one of the three this year, and I would like to prepare properly for the question period, which runs about twenty minutes.

My project defends a deliberately restricted version of the "F-twist" position associated with Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics. The thesis is that "your model's assumptions are false" is not, by itself, an objection to a scientific model. My argument has four steps. First, every model of anything complicated idealizes, so the falsity of assumptions cannot mark out the bad models. Second, what matters is whether a particular idealization is licensed, and I define one as licensed when relaxing it, holding the rest of the model fixed, would not move the predicted outcome by more than the accuracy required by the decision the model is being used to make. Third, most disputes that sound like complaints about realism are really either disputes about how much accuracy a given purpose requires, or claims that a specific idealization fails my test in a specific regime, and they should be argued in those terms. Fourth, I break with Friedman: predictive success alone does not license using a model to intervene, because success in a stable regime can rest on assumptions that fail precisely when a policy change alters the regime. I also lean on Friedman's own illustrations — the expert billiard player whose shots can be predicted as if he solved the relevant equations, and the tree whose leaves are distributed as if each one sought to maximize its exposure to sunlight — so I expect somebody to go after those.

The panel is not a philosophy seminar. It consists of an experimental condensed-matter physicist, an epidemiologist who builds transmission models for our county health department, a forecasting economist at a regional bank, a semi-retired structural engineer who judges the engineering division, and roughly forty high school and undergraduate students. I have already answered the standard philosophical objections in the paper itself — that "as if" instrumentalism is self-undermining, that Friedman slides between predictive success and truth, that idealization and approximation must be kept distinct — and I do not need more of those.

Please give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do. Attribute each one to the panelist most likely to raise it, phrase it the way that person would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, and pair it with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds plus an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in my opening two minutes so I am not forced to concede it later, and identify the single reply that has to land if the paper is to survive the evening. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one my licensing test cannot answer.
