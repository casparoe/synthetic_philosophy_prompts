I teach van Fraassen's Reflection principle (1984) in my epistemology seminar, and each year I find that my own verdicts about when present deference to a future credence is required refuse to line up with it. Let me report those verdicts plainly and ask whether some principle — Reflection itself, or a restricted or repaired descendant — captures the pattern.

1. Tonight I will have read the whole correspondence in the archive, and my credence about the attribution will have moved. I take it that I should defer now: whatever credence I expect to have then, on the strength of that reading, I should already have.
2. In three hours I will have had several glasses of wine at a department dinner and will feel confident that I can repair the proof. I should obviously not defer to that confidence.
3. Next year, having worked through the statistical literature, I expect to hold a different credence about whether the study replicates. I should defer to that, exactly as in (1).
4. Here is the case I cannot reconcile with (1). Suppose a pill is available which, as I know in advance, will make a certain argument — say, for the claim that we owe nothing to the remote future — strike me as considerably more compelling, and which leaves me afterwards quite unable to tell that the shift was chemically induced. From the inside, my post-pill change of mind will be indistinguishable from one produced by reading a good paper, and my post-pill self will insist, on reflection, that I defer to him now. Even so, I judge that I should not.
5. Tomorrow, under general anesthesia, I will afterwards be certain the procedure went well; that certainty is guaranteed by the protocol whatever the outcome. Conditional on being certain then, I do not think my present credence that it will go well should budge.
6. A decade hence I expect to have thought about the same problem with no new evidence at all and to have drifted toward a different view. I go back and forth: further thinking feels like a kind of evidence-processing, yet input-free drift feels more like (2) than like (1).
7. In an hour I will have forgotten the middle lemma but kept my high credence in the theorem. My credence will not have changed, so Reflection is satisfied — and still something seems to have gone wrong.

Please state the principle, or the small set of competing candidates, precisely and clause by clause, indicating which of these cases motivates each clause. Then say what it implies for cases I have not ruled on — for instance, whether I should defer to a future self who will know the result of a test I have firmly declined to be told, and whether I should defer to a future self who is by my own lights wiser but no better informed. Finally, tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts your account cannot accommodate and would have me abandon. Please write in American English.
