I'm putting together a session for our philosophy society on aggregation and I keep reaching for Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas — the joyful city whose delight is supposed to be impossible unless one child is kept in filth and dark in a basement, everyone learns about the child once they're old enough, and a handful of citizens walk off toward somewhere the narrator can't describe. Everyone teaches it as an anti-utilitarian fable. I want to teach it through Taurek instead, and I want to know whether that actually holds up or whether I'm forcing it.

Taurek's argument, roughly as I have it from that old "should the numbers count" paper of his: if you can save five strangers or one, the five deaths don't add up into anybody's death — there's no supersubject who suffers the total — so the fact that there are more of them isn't the kind of reason it's usually taken to be, and absent special obligations you should give each person an equal chance. Omelas looks like the same shape viewed from the other end: the child's misery isn't cancelled by the citizens' happiness because not one scrap of that happiness is the child's.

Here's some apparatus I made up for this, it isn't from the literature, so feel free to tell me it's a bad taxonomy. I want to split the story's moral work into three: (a) the compensation question — could the many's good ever make up for the one's bad, which is the Taurek move; (b) the permission question — even granting it can't compensate, might the whole package still be permissible given that the story forecloses the trade, since you can't free the child and keep the city; and (c) what I'll call the knowledge condition — the story's insistence that the citizens' joy isn't merely compatible with knowing about the child but somehow rests on it.

So: which of the three is the story really pressing? Is Omelas a (b) case wearing (a)'s clothes? Where do the ones who walk away land — Taurek says give everyone a chance, and they give the child nothing at all, they just remove themselves, so is walking away Taurekian or is it a completely different objection about complicity and integrity? Also, how does all this compare to the ancestor hypothetical the story is usually traced back to, William James's world of millions kept permanently happy on condition that a certain lost soul on the far-off edge of things leads a life of lonely torture — is Taurek's no-supersubject point actually a response to the revulsion James is describing, or is it aimed somewhere else? And finish by telling me what the story shows about aggregation that the drug-dose thought experiments don't, if anything.

Please mark your confidence as you go, and specifically flag which bits of your reading depend on a contested reading of Taurek — whether his claim is about what's better or about what we owe, and whether the coin flip is doing fairness work or something else entirely. And if you think the lens is just wrong for this story, say so plainly rather than politely writing the essay anyway; I'd much rather hear that now than in front of thirty people.
