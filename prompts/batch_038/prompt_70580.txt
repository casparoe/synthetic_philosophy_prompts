In a seminar on the law of assisted dying that I am auditing, one of the regular participants — a colleague who works in criminal law theory — advanced a reading of Hume's posthumously published essay on suicide that I have not been able to dislodge, and I would like it audited rather than either endorsed or waved away.

His claim is this. The hydraulic passage — Hume's observation that if he were able to divert a great river from its course he would thereby commit no crime, and his inference from that to the case of redirecting the small quantity of blood that runs in our own veins — is structurally identical to Rachels' argument from the two cousins and the child in the bathtub: the one who holds the child under, and the one who stands by while the child slips, strikes his head, and drowns. On my colleague's mapping, diverting the river is the killing case and failing to dam it is the letting-die case, and the conclusion is the same in both authors: the difference between acting and omitting, taken by itself, carries no moral weight. He further argues that Hume's leveling of human conduct into the general laws of providence does the same work as Rachels' leveling of act and omission into outcome, so the essay is really the founding text of the killing/letting-die parity thesis. He wants to teach it that way and cite it in a paper on physician-assisted dying.

Could you audit the analogy? Specifically:

1. An explicit correspondence table — what in Hume's passage plays the role of the cousin who holds the child under, the cousin who waits, the child, the inheritance, and the bare difference itself — and where the mapping comes out empty, strained, or many-to-one.
2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that actually matter for my colleague's use and those that are merely historical or stylistic. In particular, consider that Hume is arguing against a charge of impiety and usurping providence, whereas Rachels is arguing against a professional doctrine about what physicians may and may not do, and that Hume's subject is a person's disposition of his own life while Rachels compares what two third parties do to a child.
3. A verdict on the real logical form of Hume's argument, if it is not a case-pair argument: an over-inclusion reductio showing the impiety objection proves too much, a jurisdictional claim about the reach of general providence, something else — and the closest modern analogue you can name for whatever it turns out to be.
4. Whether the essay anywhere limits the reductio so that it does not equally dissolve the objection to homicide, and whether the presence or absence of such a limit helps or hurts the parity reading.
5. Whether my colleague's conclusion survives the disanalogies: can the essay still serve as an eighteenth-century authority for the claim that active and passive assistance in dying stand or fall together, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion from the start? If a better analogy for the passage exists, say what it would be.

Please commit to a judgment rather than hedging in both directions, and finish by telling me what would change your mind — which feature of the essay, or which way of formulating the bathtub case, would make you reverse or substantially weaken the verdict — together with the point in your reasoning where you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. Because you cannot consult the texts, mark clearly which of your conclusions depend on Hume's precise wording and which rest only on the sense of the passage.
