I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I am co-organizing our department's graduate reading group on testimony and speech acts. The membership is awkwardly mixed: two or three people who work directly on epistemic norms, a pair of linguists who model assertion within formal pragmatics and care mainly about what commitments a speech act encodes, and one person who is openly skeptical that "assertion" picks out a unified practice at all. My frustration with the last several meetings is that we sort ourselves by whichever paper we read most recently rather than by what we actually believe, and it has become clear that at least half of our standing disagreements are about cross-cutting questions that nobody has ever stated out loud. So for next week's session I want to circulate a decision tree that sorts each of us by our underlying commitments about what governs assertion — eight to twelve leaves, six or seven branching questions, rendered as an indented outline rather than a graphic.

The crucial thing is that I do not want the familiar three- or four-way map organized by which epistemic property appears inside the norm, because that axis hides precisely the questions we keep tripping over. Please build the tree out of cross-cutting distinctions instead, and let the leaves fall where they may in the resulting space. The distinctions I have in mind include: whether the candidate norm is meant to constitute assertion as a kind, or only to regulate a practice already fixed by other means; whether correctness is a property of the act as evaluated against the speaker's total evidence, or of the act as it in fact stands in the world; whether a violation always licenses blame, or whether an assertion can be improper in one respect while leaving the speaker faultless in another; whether propriety is fixed once and for all at the moment of utterance, or can shift with the conversational context, or even with the context from which the assertion is later assessed; and whether the norm governing assertion is simply the norm governing belief worn on one's sleeve. Add or drop questions as you see fit, but every one of them must be posed as a concrete case or a claim to be accepted or rejected — never as something of the form "do you think assertion requires knowledge?", which would let people answer with a label instead of a commitment.

So that the instrument is testable on the group rather than in the abstract, please make sure the tree can be run on this set of cases: the fourth-grade teacher who rejects evolutionary theory on religious grounds but teaches it, and states it to her class, on the strength of impeccable textbook and expert testimony; the speaker who tells a friend "your ticket will lose"; the physician who predicts recovery on excellent evidence and is simply unlucky; the asserter whose justified belief turns out true only by a fluke in the way the evidence was produced; and the flatly odd-sounding "it is raining, though I do not know that it is." The leaves must include, at minimum: the view that knowledge is necessary for proper assertion; the view that truth is all the norm demands; the view that justification or reasonable belief suffices; the view that assertion is governed only by sincerity together with general norms of practical rationality; the contextualist strategy of explaining apparent improprieties away as conversational artifacts, so that a knowledge norm and a shifty notion of knowledge can be held together; at least one assessment-sensitive or relativist option; and the deflationary option that there is no single norm because there is no single practice to be normed. For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me explicitly which pairs of leaves your questions cannot actually separate — I suspect there is at least one pair on which every case in this literature delivers the same verdict for both, and I would rather the handout admit it than paper over it. Finally, list the combinations of answers your questions permit but that no one seems to occupy, and diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
