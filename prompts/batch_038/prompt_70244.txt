I've been working through Nozick's entitlement theory, and before I decide whether to accept it I want to know whether my own verdicts on particular cases hang together. Here they are, in the order they came to me.

1. A hiker in uninhabited country pries a fist-sized meteorite out of the ground — nothing like it has ever been claimed by anyone — and sells it to a collector for a great deal of money. She is entitled to all of it.
2. A basketball player becomes vastly richer than everyone in his city through nothing but people voluntarily buying tickets. Entitled; and I don't think the size of the gap counts against the arrangement.
3. A firm spends its own capital identifying a molecule nobody else has, protects it, and charges three thousand dollars a dose for something that costs four dollars to make, to buyers who would pay anything to live. Not entitled — and I cannot see which step differs from the basketball case.
4. My neighbor's farm reached her great-grandfather by a deed I'm confident was fraudulent. She is innocent, she bought nothing, and she inherited it; the family that was cheated still exists and could be identified. I think they are owed something, though I cannot say whether the farm, its value, or something else, nor what she loses.
5. A woman wins a lottery and uses the winnings to buy a house that passed through many hands from a thief a century ago, when the victims' descendants can no longer be traced. She keeps it. I notice I am letting the untraceability do the work, and I don't know whether that is a reason or a rationalization.
6. A startup grants options to its first twenty employees; nearly all of the eventual value comes from a sector-wide boom they did nothing to cause. They are entitled to it, I think — which may or may not contradict what I said about the lottery winner.
7. Wage contracts in a town whose mill is the only employer, on land the mill acquired when a treaty was broken: each contract looks voluntary taken by itself, and yet I don't think the resulting distribution is one anyone is entitled to.

Is there a principle that captures these — entitlement theory with something added, or one of the patterned views I've been resisting — and if so, can you state it precisely, saying which of the cases forces each clause? Then tell me what it yields in two cases I have not considered: a landowner who fences off a track villagers have used for a century to reach market, having acquired the land by clean title; and a government that confiscates the hiker's meteorite without compensation on the ground that the find was pure luck rather than anything she did. Finally, be honest about which of my seven verdicts the best principle cannot keep, and in particular whether 2 and 3 can be told apart without also giving up 7.
