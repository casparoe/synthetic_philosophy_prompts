I'm working on a piece about Ayer and life after death and I want to push it past 1989, which is awkward because he died that year — but alot of the stuff that actually matters for his arguments only came later.

Quick version of where he stood, as I read him: his objection wasn't mainly evidential. He didn't just say there's no good evidence for survival, he thought the claim ran into trouble at the level of what would count as the *same person* persisting without a body — no bodily continuity, and memory on its own can't do the work without begging the question. Then in 1988 he choked on a slice of smoked salmon, his heart stopped for about four minutes, and he had a genuinely odd experience that he wrote up for a newspaper: a red light, "exceedingly bright", which he took to be responsible for the government of the universe, plus some ministers in charge of space who had botched an inspection and left space slightly out of joint like a badly cut jigsaw. He didn't convert. In a postscript a little later he said what had been weakened was not his belief that there's no life after death but his inflexible attitude towards that belief. Which is a very Ayers move.

Now the post-1989 material I want you to run him through:

(a) The prospective cardiac-arrest studies — multi-centre, hidden targets placed high up facing the ceiling, standardised interview scales. Thirty-odd years of this and still nobody has verified a report of something only observable from outside the body; what you get instead is a sizeable fraction of survivors with structured, emotionally intense experiences during stretches when the EEG looks flat, and the mainstream reply that the flat window doesn't line up with when the experience is later said to have happened.

(b) Terminal lucidity — advanced dementia patients becoming abruptly coherent hours before death.

(c) The pharmacological and neural models: NMDA blockade, REM intrusion into waking, temporal-lobe and hypoxia accounts.

(d) The bit I think he'd find most interesting: a chunk of the field has stopped arguing for the Christian afterlife and started arguing for consciousness-first metaphysics — integrated-information-flavoured stuff, panpsychism — where near-death reports get used as evidence that experience isn't produced by the brain at all.

And two hypotheticals. Treat these as thought experiments, not as things that happened:

(i) Suppose in 2033 a pre-registered multi-site study gets a small subset of arrest survivors correctly reporting a randomised digit sequence that was displayed on a ceiling-facing screen during a documented period of isoelectric EEG, replicated at three hospitals, and the sceptics' rebuttal is not about fraud but about residual micro-perfusion and about whether reported timing can be trusted.

(ii) Suppose a high-resolution scan taken minutes before death is used to run an emulation that reports continuous memory of the dying moments and insists it is the person.

What I want is Ayer's likely verdict on each, and — this is the real question — whether (i) would move him at all given that his objection was conceptual rather than evidential. Does verified perception during arrest even engage the identity problem, or does he just say it shows an unusual brain event and nothing whatever about a person surviving? And does (ii) cut for him or against him: it looks like a textbook replica case, which is his old argument doing the work, except that the replica's testimony is precisely the sort of memory evidence he thought couldn't establish identity. Also, would he grant the consciousness-first version in (d) a truth value at all, given how far he'd walked back strict verificationism by the seventies? And would (b) bother him more than (a)?

Give me a rough confidence figure for each of the five verdicts, and be explicit about which ones you're genuinely extrapolating from positions he held and which are just you guessing from temperament.
