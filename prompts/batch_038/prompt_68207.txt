I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on liberalism and empire, and I want to read E. M. Forster's *A Passage to India* against John Stuart Mill's writings on British rule in India, rather than against the postcolonial framework the novel usually receives. My working hypothesis is that the novel is, among other things, a portrait of the administrative class Mill spent his working life defending, and that it stages failure modes Mill himself worried about. I want that hypothesis tested, not flattered.

Please begin by stating, in your own words and without quotation, the Millian position the novel is to be set against. I have four elements in mind, and you should correct or refine my summary wherever it is inaccurate or overstated: the distinction Mill draws in his 1859 essay on non-intervention between the rules that obtain among civilized states and those that obtain between a civilized power and peoples he takes to be not yet fit for self-government; the concession in *On Liberty* that despotism may be a legitimate mode of government in dealing with such peoples, provided its end is genuinely their improvement; the argument in the same 1859 essay that a liberty conferred by foreign hands has nothing real or permanent in it, since a people remains free only because it is determined to be free; and the institutional corollary he drew from three and a half decades of Company service, ending with the Company's abolition in 1858, a transfer of Indian government to the Crown that he resisted — namely the claim, developed in the chapter of *Considerations on Representative Government* devoted to the dependencies of a free state, that such a dependency is best governed by a continuous body of professional administrators with local knowledge and secure tenure, insulated from the metropolitan electorate and from the resident British community, and answerable for the improvement of the governed.

Then give me the reading, organized around three questions.

First, who in Chandrapore is a candidate for Mill's professional administrator, and what becomes of each: Fielding at the government college, who travels light and believes in education and personal relations; Ronny Heaslop, the City Magistrate convinced that the British are in India to hold the country rather than to be agreeable; Turton the Collector, who must administer justice and simultaneously preside over the community that expects Aziz to be guilty; McBryde, the police superintendent who fancies himself a scientist of race and whose own conduct is the least scientific thing about him; and the Club, as the institution that binds these men into a single body and expels Fielding from it.

Second, does the novel vindicate Mill's own diagnosis — that the standing danger of tutelage is the drift of the ruling body's ethic from improvement to mere order, and its fusion with the interests of the resident British — or does it strike at the premise on which that diagnosis rests, namely that there exists a class of peoples for whom tutelage is the appropriate relation and a class of guardians capable of exercising it? These are different indictments, and I would like to know which one, if either, the text supports, and where a critic could reasonably argue for the other.

Third, how do the expedition to the Marabar Caves, the trial and its collapse when Adela withdraws the charge, the Club's treatment of Fielding before and after the acquittal, and the final meeting between Fielding and Aziz bear on the question of whether relations of the personal kind Fielding practices can do the political work Mill assigns to institutions? I am particularly interested in whether Forster shows Fielding's liberalism failing because it is too weak, or because it is, in the circumstances, the only honest option available and therefore insufficient.

I also want the resistances stated plainly. Forster published in 1924, describing a Raj shaped by 1857, by the reforms that followed the First World War, and by a nationalist politics Mill could not have anticipated; the novel's register is in part metaphysical, and I do not want the echo in the caves or Godbole's Hinduism flattened into a point about administrative ethics; and Forster is not advancing an argument about trusteeship, so a reading that treats the novel as a refutation of Mill risks importing philosophy into a work that declines to offer any. Tell me where the pairing earns its keep and where I am forcing it, including whether Forster's own liberalism is close enough to Mill's for the comparison to be illuminating or whether it belongs to a different tradition entirely.

Finally, say what the novel contributes to the debate over liberal empire that argument alone does not — whether there is something about the self-understanding of an administrator who no longer believes the improvement rationale but continues to perform it, or about how tutelage is experienced from the other side of it by someone like Aziz, that only a work of this kind can show.

Please structure the answer in headed sections, paraphrase rather than quote from either Mill or Forster, and omit any bibliography or list of references.
