For four years I have shared a three-bedroom flat with two friends, Mira and Jonas, and we run the household by a written agreement that we drafted ourselves, half in earnest and half as a joke. The agreement includes a spare-room clause with four lines. Line one: the second bedroom counts as Mira's personal domain in one respect — as between her using it as a practice room and the room staying locked and unused, her stated preference decides, and neither of the other two may overrule her. Line two says exactly the same for Jonas, as between his using it as a workshop and the room staying locked and unused. Line three is a general housekeeping rule: if the three of us, asked separately, all prefer one arrangement of the room to another, the flat adopts the preferred arrangement. Line four is procedural: where the earlier lines fail to single out an answer, the room stays as it currently is.

Here is where it becomes absurd. Mira would most like the room to stay locked; she practises at her conservatory anyway and dislikes the idea of music in the flat, but if the room is to be used at all she would rather it be hers than Jonas's, whose power tools keep everyone awake. Jonas's ranking is: Mira's practice room first, his own workshop second, a locked door last — he sincerely thinks her use of the room is the best one, but he cannot bear seeing a good room wasted, so he much prefers his workshop to the room standing empty. Mine is: Mira's practice room, then Jonas's workshop, then four months of a locked door. Over the summer the clause went round in a circle. In June we all agreed that Mira's use beat Jonas's. In July Jonas observed that as between his workshop and a locked room his say was decisive, and moved his tools in. In August Mira observed that as between her practice room and a locked room her say was decisive, and since she preferred the locked room, the key went back on its hook; line four then held the room in that state. We have now had the same argument four times at Sunday house meetings, and the room is still empty. Mira maintains that we have stumbled onto something general — that you cannot, she says, simultaneously honour everyone's unanimous preferences and protect small personal domains, and that this is not a drafting failure but very nearly a theorem. Jonas thinks she is being grandiose and that we simply wrote a bad agreement. I am inclined to agree with Jonas, but I cannot identify the line that is at fault.

Could you tell me which of the two is right? Specifically: is the combination of lines genuinely incoherent, or have we mis-specified something a competent drafter would have caught — and where exactly does the reasoning go wrong on the way from our three sets of preferences to the locked door? I would also like practical guidance: what such an agreement can keep, what it would have to give up, and whether there is any way to preserve both the personal-domain lines and something resembling the unanimity rule. Please keep your reply to at most 400 words, open with a single sentence giving your verdict on Mira versus Jonas, and, if the pattern Mira thinks she has spotted is a known result with a name, mention that name in one clause and go no further into the literature.
