Last Saturday a friend and I argued for the better part of five hours about what, on her view, actually makes a life go well, and we reached no conclusion; I should like a considered second opinion before we resume. Her position is a variety of what she calls tranquilism, a word she picked up somewhere and which I do not think is doing any work here, so please do not spend your words on the label, its provenance, or its adherents. In substance she holds that nothing is good in itself, that only the absence of bad things counts, and that the bad things are suffering and unquiet wanting. She is emphatic that she is not a negative hedonist and equally emphatic that she is not a preference-satisfaction theorist, but she cannot articulate the difference between her view and the first of those except by appeal to cases. So I wrote down her verdicts on the cases I put to her, since those are the fixed points she will not move, and I would like you to work from them rather than from her self-description.

1. A patient in unrelievable pain whose family grants every remaining wish. She said the wish-granting does not go on the other side of the ledger at all: the life is bad by exactly the measure the pain makes it bad, and no accumulation of satisfied wishes could ever tip it.

2. A musician who spends twenty years on a single work and completes it to acclaim. She said the completion is not a benefit, formulating it as: his failure would have been a misfortune, his success is not a good fortune. She added that the twenty years of striving were bad only insofar as the striving was itself a restlessness, and that had it been calm, those years would not have been bad at all.

3. A monk in a cell, without pain, companions, or achievements, and with almost no desires. She said nothing is missing from that life and that it is not worse than mine. When I pressed her to say it was better, she refused, and said that comparison was simply not available to her.

4. A heavy smoker who takes a drug that extinguishes the craving outright and leaves everything else untouched: health, circumstances, mood, and, she insisted, the quantity of suffering he feels. She called this a genuine improvement rather than a neutral rearrangement, and she resisted my suggestion that she was smuggling the craving back in as a species of suffering.

5. A woman who wants her husband to be honest with her, is deceived for years, and never suspects it, so that nothing in her experience differs from that of an honestly married woman. She said the frustration counts for nothing; on her view only what is felt has weight.

6. A man rendered incapable of caring about anything by a brain injury: no pain, no distress, no wants. She said this is no improvement on the man he was, and that the disappearance of his cravings is the loss of a person rather than the arrival of peace.

7. A retiree who takes up competitive bridge, develops a fierce desire to win, and wins often. She said the new desire is a new liability, that the winning is not a benefit, and that she could not say whether he is better or worse off on the whole.

8. Asked whether it is better to bring into existence a creature with no suffering and few desires or one with no suffering and a rich set of desires that will mostly be satisfied, she declined the second and would not affirm the first, on the ground that the question presupposes wanting is a way of being better off, which she does not grant.

Here is the case I want to bring back to her. A company has developed a pill that removes a particular ambition permanently and without side effects. The people considering it are ambitious in a peculiarly unagitated way: they want to do distinguished scientific work, they pursue it steadily and with quiet pleasure, they are rarely thwarted, and on the rare occasions when they are, they are unperturbed within the hour. Taking the pill changes nothing about the felt quality of their experience. They cease to want the thing, take up something else with the same mild satisfaction, and report no sense of loss, no relief, and no difference. They ask for the pill because they think wanting less is better, and the desire to stop wanting is of course itself a desire.

What I need from you is the following. First, predict her verdict on the pill case: an improvement, a loss, or exactly neutral, together with your degree of confidence and the reasons for it. Second, set out the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with verdicts 1 through 8; I expect there to be at least three, and I expect one of them to be a refinement of suffering-only hedonism that she would reject in words but that her record may not let her distinguish herself from, and I want to know what each of the candidates says about the pill. Third, tell me where the strain falls: whichever way she answers, which of her recorded verdicts becomes hardest to keep, and in particular what distinction is doing the work in separating 4 from 6, and whether that same distinction decides this case or leaves it open. Fourth, if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say what she would be committing herself to with each possible response, so that I can put the choice to her as a choice rather than as a gotcha. Fifth, name one further case, a single one, that would decisively separate the candidate principles, and state what each principle says about it. And sixth, address the reflexive wrinkle: on her own terms, does the fact that one must want to stop wanting in order to take the pill count against taking it, or is a desire whose object is the extinction of desire a special case, and if so, what makes it special?

Please write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Where you need to keep several candidate principles apart, do it in sentences and paragraphs, and let the order of your exposition carry the structure. A page or two is about right.
