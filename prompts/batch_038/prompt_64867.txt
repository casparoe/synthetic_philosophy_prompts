I'm a backend/SRE-ish engineer, no philosophy training at all, so bear with me. Something at work has been bugging me for months and I finally want it spelled out.

We keep reliability numbers around. For a class of disks, or for a whole service, someone computes an MTBF or a per-hour failure rate — usually just failures-observed divided by total-hours-run across the fleet — and then everybody uses that number as if its a fact about the world. "The chance this disk dies in the next year is about 2%." It goes into capacity models, into the redundancy math, into the 99.9% we promise customers in the SLA. Nobody ever asks "chance according to what, measured how, over what population."

Three things sit weird with me:

1. We never update the number for the individual machine. A disk that's been running clean for four years gets the same 2% as one installed last week. When I asked about this, a coworker said that's correct, because the failure distribution is exponential and exponential is memoryless, so past survival carries no information about the future. But we also do burn-in — we run new hardware hard for a few days and toss the units that die early — which looks exactly like an admission that the survivors are a different population with a different rate. So which is it?

2. Meanwhile, when a particular rack keeps eating drives, we absolutely do treat that rack as having its own failure rate and we quarantine it. So sometimes the chance attaches to the type and sometimes to the individual token, and nobody notices we switched.

3. The contrast with the rest of my job is sharp. If I said "the chance a user clicks buy is 2%", people would instantly ask what model, what features, what data, what interval. Rates about humans get treated as estimates. Rates about hardware get treated as known chances that my beliefs should just line up with. And for the random number generator we don't do statistics at all — we know from reading the code that it's uniform, and we defer to that completely, even though we have gigabytes of its past output sitting right there that we could learn from.

So what does this practice actually presuppose about what a probability is and where it comes from? Reconstruct the implicit theory, including whatever principle is doing the work when we say "the world has this chance, therefore your credence should equal it." Which rival pictures does that rule out — chances as nothing but long-run frequencies, chances as degrees of belief, chances as something we can only ever estimate rather than know? Is the practice internally consistent, or do burn-in, memorylessness, and quarantining lemons only make sense under different theories that we're mixing together without noticing? Please be careful to separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably; I suspect more than one theory could license most of it, and I want to know which parts are underdetermined. Then: if we swapped the presupposed view for one of the rivals, what would concretely change in how we compute, write down, and use these numbers?

American English please. No bibliography and no list of papers — I won't read it. Name positions and arguments if that's useful, but skip the citations. And be self-critical at the end: tell me what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence, if I brought it to you, would flip your verdict — and where you think your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong.
