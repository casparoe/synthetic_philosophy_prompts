Write me an essay on a fairly narrow fight inside structural realism. Quick setup in case its rusty: the epistemic structural realist (roughly Worrall's position) says the scientific realist can only ever lay claim to the *structure* of the world — the relational/mathematical skeleton that survives theory change, like the mathematical relations in Fresnel's aether theory surviving into Maxwell's equations and then into relativity even though the aether itself went — and never to the intrinsic nature of the relata. The standard way of cashing out "structure" here is a Ramsey sentence: existentially generalize over the theoretical terms of a theory and treat the resulting purely relational claim as the content we're actually entitled to believe.

The old objection, due to Max Newman reviewing Russell's *The Analysis of Matter* in Mind in 1928, is that a purely structural claim so understood is trivially satisfiable: given any domain with enough members you can define relations on it by brute stipulation so as to make the Ramsey sentence come out true. So the only non-trivial content that survives is cardinality — how many things there are — which is hardly a realist achievement worth the name. The most common contemporary patch is a naturalness constraint: only quantify over relations that are genuinely natural rather than gerrymandered.

The controversy I want you to adjudicate is whether that patch rescues epistemic structural realism or wrecks it from the other direction. The worry being that naturalness is supposed to be an intrinsic, non-structural feature of properties, so the structural realist who invokes it is claiming knowledge of precisely the sort of thing she said was permanently beyond our reach. I want a real verdict, not a "both sides have points" shrug — it could be "the fix works, but only by collapsing ESR into ordinary scientific realism about natural properties," or "the fix fails and ESR should abandon the Ramsey-sentence route altogether," or something else you argue for.

One hunch of mine I'd like you to pressure-test instead of just accepting: the triviality objection seems to assume that "how many objects there are" is a determinate, metaphysically respectable fact, so cardinality is what's left over once structure is shown to be cheap. But it's far from obvious cardinality *is* determinate — ask how many objects are in this room once mereological sums get counted, or whether the number of spacetime points is a fact of the matter at all. If cardinality is the mushy thing and the structural facts are the sharp ones, does the objection invert on itself? Is that a genuine escape route, or does it just slide into the ontic structural realist's much more radical claim that there are no objects at all, only structure?

Also please engage with: whether the objection bites differently on the epistemic and ontic variants of the view; whether "nobody stipulated the structures that actually get confirmed by experiment, so the choice isn't arbitrary" is an adequate reply or just relocates the problem into the question of *which* structure gets picked out; and whether there's a version of the naturalness constraint that doesn't smuggle in intrinsic natures — naturalness characterized purely by the role a relation plays in successful prediction, say. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I just want the argument laid out and a defended conclusion.
