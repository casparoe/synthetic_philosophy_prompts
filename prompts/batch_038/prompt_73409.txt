Three years into a philosophy of mind PhD, and for our department writing group each of us has to circulate a one-pager on where we actually stand before anyone is allowed to attack it. Mine is about Gödel's theorems and what they do and don't show about minds, and I'd like a consistency check before I put my name on it. Here's the package:

(1) I take the first incompleteness theorem to be exactly what it says: a result about effectively axiomatized theories that can interpret enough arithmetic. Provability, syntax, systems. It is not a theorem about people or about reality or about language, and I'm loud about that.

(2) So the appropriations I see outside logic — the "no system can totalize itself," "there's always a remainder" stuff in some continental writing, and the theology versions — are analogies wearing a lab coat. I've drafted something saying this in pretty blunt terms.

(3) Nonetheless I do think the theorem plus one extra premise gets you a real conclusion about us: no consistent system all of whose arithmetical theorems are true can capture everything that human mathematicians can ideally establish.

(4) The extra premise is soundness — what we can ideally establish about arithmetic, we establish truly.

(5) I know the usual objection, that we can't establish our own consistency and so can't get to our own Gödel sentence, and I dont think it lands. The second theorem says PA can't prove Con(PA); it doesn't say nobody can know Con(PA). Gentzen did know it, by induction up to epsilon-zero, in a theory that doesn't even contain PA. I take my knowledge of my own soundness to work on the same model.

(6) I accept the Church–Turing thesis, and by "machine" in (3) I mean a Turing machine.

(7) On the positive story I'm sympathetic to the physical route: the non-computability has to live somewhere, and objective reduction in microtubules is about the only concrete proposal anyone has actually put on the table. A Gödelian who can't say where the extra capacity comes from hasn't explained anything.

(8) But (3) doesn't depend on (7). Its an a priori point about insight and formal systems, and if the physics turns out to be nonsense I keep the conclusion.

(9) Upshot for AI: not that machines can't do arithmetic, obviously they can, but that no machine can match human mathematical insight in general. We can in principle see the truth of any arithmetical statement we're in a position to establish, whereas a machine is stuck permanently with its own Gödel sentence.

Go through these and tell me which ones actually pull against each other. I'm sure some of it only looks bad and I don't want that flagged anyway — I'd rather the list be discriminating than exhaustive. Where there's a real tension, I want the cheapest revision that removes it and what that revision costs me, and then which single change would clean up the most overall. Don't assume I know where the problems are; I genuinely don't, and I'm not telling you which of these I'd rather keep.

Sort by severity, worst first, and put the apparent-only rows at the bottom.
