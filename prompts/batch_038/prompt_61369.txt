Just spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether real moral dilemmas exist and I need a neutral referee, because we've both decided the other one is being dense. His line: either you can weigh the options, in which case there's one thing you ought to do, or you can't do anything, in which case there isn't — so there is never a case where you ought to do A and ought to do B. My line: that's technically true and completely beside the point. So here are my actual verdicts, case by case, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

- The Sartre one, the student in occupied France choosing between staying with his mother and leaving to fight: genuine dilemma. No fact of the matter about which he ought to do. And afterwards he shouldn't feel relief, he should feel torn, more or less permanently.
- Two strangers drowning, you can only reach one: not a dilemma. Saving either is fine. He didn't wrong the other one and doesn't owe that family an apology, though I wouldn't blame him for feeling wrecked.
- Same setup but one of the two is your kid: also not a dilemma, you ought to save your kid. Except now I want to say the parent isn't exactly clean — something is owed to the other kid's family, not an apology, more an acknowledgment that a claim of theirs got run over.
- The lorry driver, no fault of his own, child dies: did nothing wrong, isn't blameworthy, and his feeling awful isn't just human weakness, it's the correct response. And I would not criticise him for eventually getting on with his life.
- A mother forced by someone else to pick which of her two children lives: did nothing wrong, and yet I think she'd be getting something morally wrong if she said "well, I did nothing wrong, so I'm fine now." So here I want the leftover AND I want to criticise moving on, which is the opposite of what I said about the driver one paragraph ago.
- Two promises that turn out to conflict, and he only discovers it when it's too late to fix: genuine dilemma, and whoever he lets down he owes something afterwards.
- Two promises that conflict where he spotted it a year early and just didn't deal with it: no dilemma any more. He's wronged both of them, one wrong, done. Except I still feel he owes compensation to each of them, which sounds like both oughts survived, and I can't make that square with the previous sentence.
- The official who authorises something monstrous to stop something worse: ought to and ought not to, and it should mark him — I'd want him not celebrated for it later. The identical calculation made by a private person protecting his own family: no dirty hands, just an answer.
- Both kids drown no matter what you do: no dilemma, no residue, nothing was in your hands.

His diagnosis is that I'm running together three seperate things — conflicting obligations, blameworthiness, and which feelings are appropriate — and that only the first one is a dilemma. Maybe. But then cases 3 and 5 look like feelings doing moral work with no obligation left for them to attach to, and I don't know how to cash that out.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that delivers these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed — what about someone who can't tell which of the two drowning kids is theirs? What about the promises case where he spots the conflict early and immediately tells one of them it's off? What about an official who enjoys the monstrous part? And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep: I'd rather have a simple principle that kills two of them than a baroque one that saves all nine, but if you can build both, show me both and say which you'd sign up for.
