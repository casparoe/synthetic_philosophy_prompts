So I've been going back and forth with a friend for a few weeks about suffering-focused ethics, and I finally tried to write down what I actually believe, because every time I explain it out loud it sounds more coherent than I suspect it is — probably because I keep quietly changing what I mean by "threshold." I'm giving a short talk at a local reading group in a couple of weeks and I'd much rather find out where the thing breaks before I'm standing in front of people. Here's the list, roughly in the order I came to believe it:

(1) I'm a one-scale hedonist about value. Pleasure and pain sit on a single intensity axis, and I specifically don't want the escape hatch where intense suffering is supposed to live in a different "dimension" of disvalue than mild discomfort. Same currency, one ruler, all the way up. (2) But there's a threhold T. At or above T an episode of suffering is lexically bad: no finite amount of anything below T — pleasure, mild pain, happy lives, however many, however long — can outweigh a single hour above it. A billion years of pleasant contentment does not pay for one afternoon of genuinely unbearable agony. (3) Intensity is continuous. Between any two intensities there's another, and for two nearby intensities the difference in badness is arbitrarily small; no neighboring pair of experiences jumps by a fixed amount. (4) I have no idea where T is and I don't think anyone does. I'm happy to call that ordinary vagueness, bald-man style — the line is real, it just isn't locatable. (5) I want "at least as good as" to stay transitive, and I don't want to be told that my view leaves huge chunks of the outcome space unranked. Radical incomparability feels like giving up rather than like a solution. (6) I'm not an extinctionist and I'm not a negative utilitarian in the classic sense: a happy life is really good and it's really good that there are more of them, other things equal. (7) Above T nothing compensates — not later joy for the same person, not anybody else's joy, ever. Below T compensation works fine and I don't think it matters whether it happens within one life or across people. (8) I'm a reductionist about personal identity, Parfit-style: identity isn't what matters, there's no deep further fact. (9) Any serious expansion of sentient life — huge future populations, space colonization, loads of artificial minds — carries some chance of producing above-T agony, and I think that chance grows with scale and tends toward 1. Still, I don't think expansion is flatly forbidden: small, well-controlled expansion is permissible, large expansion is very strongly disrecommended. (10) Separately, I say out loud and mean it that pleasure and pain have no exchange rate between them.

Which of these actually collide, and which only look like they do? I want an audit, not a lecture. Go at the pairs that matter, rank the genuine tensions by how bad they are rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say which commitment has to go and what I lose by losing it — including knock-on effects, since I suspect giving one up will quietly take another with it. Please be willing to tell me that some pairs are fine; I don't want everything flagged as inconsistent just because the list is long, and I'm fairly sure at least one of the collisions I'm worried about isn't real. Then end with the single revision that cleans up the most damage. Two constraints on your answer: don't tell me to go be a different kind of consequentialist altogether, and don't propose a fix that requires me to be able to say where T sits, because (4) is the one I'm most attached to.
