Just got off a three-hour call with an old friend from my former unit and I still haven't come down from it, so bear with me. Short version: I spent twenty-two years in strategic forces, most of it aboard boats, and next month I'm giving the farewell remarks at my own change-of-command. The room will be the outgoing and incoming crews, a bunch of spouses and kids who have never set foot on a base, and two or three arms-control academics from a nearby university who are coming for the free lunch.

My friend's position — which I think is wrong but which I cant dismiss — is that the deterrent never actually depended on people like us being willing to do the thing. His claim is that credibility was built entirely out of procedure: checklists, two-man rules, authority delegated downward in advance so that nobody's stomach ever entered into it, and the individual was replaceable. My position is that the whole apparatus rested on some of us genuinely meaning it, and that if he's right then my career was theatre with extra steps.

The deterrence paradox is exactly why this is hard to give as a speech. A threat only works if you'd carry it out; carrying it out would be unforgivable; and then there's the third move, loosening your own grip on purpose so that the chance of an accident does your negotiating for you. So the speech can't do any of the obvious things. It can't say thank god it never came to it and none of us would really have turned a key — that announces to the room's spouses that their whole lives were a bluff. It can't say yes, I would have — that's a monster talking to children. And it can't dodge the subject, because it's the only thing anyone in that room has ever thought seriously about.

Write the speech, seven or eight minutes, warm enough that the families follow it and exact enough that the academics don't feel fobbed off. It has to end up somewhere that doesn't just pick a side in my argument — or if it does pick one, it has to say what that costs the other side.
