I'm a PhD student in aesthetics drafting a chapter on forgery, and the chapter turns on a coinage I want stress-tested before I commit to it. Call a case a **curatorial forgery** one in which nothing about the object deceives anyone: the label, the catalogue entry, and the wall text all correctly state that the canvas was generated in 2025 by a model trained exclusively on one seventeenth-century master's authenticated corpus, then executed by living hands in period materials and technique. The deception, if any, is performed entirely by placement — the canvas is installed in the final gallery of a monographic retrospective of that master, hung as the culminating work of the oeuvre rather than as a commentary upon it, so that visitors leave having absorbed it into the artist's achievement.

Please write an essay that defends or rejects the claim that curatorial forgery is a genuine category: that an object can be a forgery, and can be aesthetically diminished by being one, in the total absence of any deception about its origins. Engage the standard resources directly — Goodman's division of the arts into the autographic, where the work is bound to its own physical history of production, and the allographic, where the work is a notatable structure whose instances are all equally genuine; and Dutton's account of forgery as a crime against artistic achievement rather than against truth-telling — and say for each whether it can be stretched to cover a misrepresentation carried by the frame rather than by the object. You should also confront the opposing line of thought: that if the canvas sustains the same perceptual encounter as an authentic late work, then talk of the history of its making is a confusion about where aesthetic value lives, and the retrospective's hang is at worst a curatorial impropriety.

Do not settle for noting that intuitions divide here. Reach a definite verdict on whether the work's aesthetic value depends on the causal history of its making, and justify that verdict.
