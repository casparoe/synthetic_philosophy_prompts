So I'm about five months into a policy job at a mid-size model lab, roughly 200 people, and I've been handed the deployment section of our internal safety framework, which is basically a rewrite of some bullet points that have been copy-pasted forward for two years. The person above me, who I'll call Dan, has a go-to analogy he brings out in every single meeting: we should run releases the way the FDA runs drugs. Big pre-release eval that functions like Phase III, then you ship, then you set up something like post-market surveillance where incidents get logged, the rate gets watched, and you pull the thing if it climbs. His closer is always some version of "nobody accuses the FDA of being irrational for approving a drug with a one-in-fifty-thousand serious adverse event, so a 2% residual failure rate on our hardest red-team suite is not a blocker." Someone on the technical side, Mara, thinks this frame is doing damage and that drug approval is fundamentally an average-case regime, while what we actually need for a couple of the capabilities in question is something closer to a worst-case guarantee — and that the analogy is quietly smuggling in the premise that average-case acceptability transfers over.

Can you audit Dan's analogy honestly for me? Not a takedown, not a defense. I want the correspondence spelled out first: what plays the role of the active ingredient, the dose, the indication and the trial population, the adverse event, the exposure denominator, the recall, the prescriber, and the post-market phase. Then where the mapping breaks — and the part I actually need is a sorting of those disanalogies into the ones that carry weight against the specific claim Dan is making (that a nonzero residual failure rate is fine and that asking for worst-case guarantees is a category error) versus the ones that are just aesthetically annoying but dont touch the argument. Four I keep circling: whether a deployed model is even a stable artifact the way a tablet is, given fine-tunes, adapters, and how much behavior shifts with the prompt; whether there's any honest exposure denominator to divide by; whether an adversary searching the input space has any counterpart in pharmacology at all or whether it's a thing with no drug analogue whatsoever; and whether pulling a release does the same work as a market withdrawal.

For context, Mara's counter-analogy is that shipping is like introducing a species to a new ecosystem, irreversible and therefore worst-case reasoning is mandatory. My own hunch is that structural engineering is the better frame — you don't certify a bridge by tallying how many bridges have collapsed, you compute a worst-case load and apply a safety factor. Dan says that's naive, bridges fail too and society has accepted it, so engineering is average-case all the way down as well. Is he right about that? And then: which of the three analogies, if any, could legitimately go in the memo and support a deployment threshold, what a fourth one would have to look like if none of them can, and whether the worst-case/average-case distinction is even doing real work here or is mostly a way for the two camps to talk past each other about how much residual risk they're each willing to eat.
