I'm in a reading group this term working through Gödel, Escher, Bach — we did the MU-puzzle back in week 2 and we're now in the stretch about jumping out of the system, Gödel's proof, Lucas, all that. Last night one of the guys in the group (comp sci, has read the book twice, insufferable about it) announced he'd worked out that the whole JOOTSing idea collapses on itself, and he went through it on the common room whiteboard and nobody could stop him, though two of us were sure something had slipped. Here's his argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) The only way to see that MU isn't a theorem of MIU is to stop generating strings and prove something about every possible derivation at once — the invariant that the number of I's in any theorem is never divisible by 3. Inside the system there's no such fact to state, because MIU can't say anything about itself; it just makes strings.
(2) But that invariant proof is itself a proof. It's an induction on the length of derivations, carried out in ordinary arithmetic over numerical codes of strings (the book arithmetizes MIU later on, M=3, I=1, U=0). So "standing outside MIU" is just "standing inside arithmetic," i.e. inside a bigger formal system. Call it A.
(3) Gödel's theorem applies to A the same as to any system that strong: A has a sentence G that's true but not provable in A, and seeing that G is true requires jumping out of A into a still bigger system A′.
(4) Generalize: whenever we establish that some claim φ isn't provable in a system S, we've established it in some S′ extending S, and S′ has its own unprovable sentences. So "φ is unprovable in S" isn't a fact you can stand on — it's a report on how far up the ladder you happen to have climbed.
(5) And since the ladder doesn't stop, nothing about MU is settled in the unconditional way it looks settled. What we actually have at any given rung is "no derivation of MU is certified at this level," not "no derivation exists." Sure, if a derivation existed we'd find it eventually — but "we'd find it eventually" isn't the same claim as "there isn't one," and the invariant argument only ever delivers the first.
(6) Therefore there's no such thing as jumping out of the system, every jump just lands you inside another system, and no unprovability result is ever final — including the one about MU. He freely admits this makes his own argument self-undermining and says that's what you should expect from a correct argument about ladders.

I'm confident (6) is false, and I don't want to give up (1) or (3), since those feel like the actual content of what we read and I'd be sad to lose them. (2) is the one I half-suspect, because "a proof is just a proof" seems too easy a move — but if you deny (2) you're saying arithmetic-with-MIU-coded-into-it isn't a formal system, which sounds insane, and anyway he'd immediately say I'm doing the Lucas thing the book itself takes apart a few chapters later. (5) is the one I can't get a grip on at all: said out loud it sounds almost tautological, "of course a bigger system sees more," wich is exactly why I don't trust my instincts here.

So — which step do you actually reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? Please also price the alternatives rather than just naming a culprit: if I reject (2) instead, what do I inherit, and if I reject (4), what am I committed to about the difference between a fact and my route to knowing it? Two specific things I'd want you to handle, because they're where he kept pressing me. (a) He offered the mutilated checkerboard as a second case: you can't tile an 8×8 board with two opposite corners removed by dominoes, since each domino covers one black and one white square and the two colors are then unequal, so it's the same shape of argument as the I-count invariant and "in the same boat." Is it? If the parity result is real and the MU result isn't, what separates them, and if both are real, what's left of (5)? (b) Does it matter that MIU is actually decidable — I checked, and the I-count condition isn't just necessary for theoremhood but also sufficient, so there's an algorithm for MIU-theoremhood — while G is the kind of sentence that genuinely can't be settled inside A? That feels like a big asymmetry between the two cases and I couldn't work out how to deploy it against him. And finally, if (6) is false, is there anything worth keeping in the original claim that jumping out is something we really do and MIU really can't?
