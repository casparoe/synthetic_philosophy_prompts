Eight months ago I took over the psychiatric advance-directive service at a teaching hospital, and in the spring I found myself defending a decision — to a patient's family, and afterwards to myself — to honor a directive over that patient's furious present objection. The justification I reached for was the standard one, that she had bound her future self while she was well. I have not been able to stop thinking that the phrase described nothing that actually happened, and I have written the worry up as an essay for a clinical ethics journal, using Kavka's toxin puzzle as my frame. What follows is a condensed version: I have stripped out the case material, which is identifying, and most of the clinical detail. I would like comments on it before I submit.

**Binding Yourself to a Self That Will Refuse**

Kavka's puzzle runs roughly as follows. An eccentric billionaire offers you a million dollars, payable tomorrow morning, on the condition that at midnight tonight you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but will not threaten your life or leave lasting effects. He emphasizes that you need not drink the toxin to receive the money: it will be in your account hours before the time for drinking arrives, and you are perfectly free to change your mind. Bluffing is excluded by stipulation — he will know whether you formed the intention, though I am not certain whether that stipulation is in Kavka or in the retellings I have read. The difficulty is familiar: you cannot intend to do what you already know you will have no reason to do when the moment arrives, and once the money has landed there is nothing at all to be said for drinking.

Advance directives in psychiatry are described in the same vocabulary, borrowed from the precommitment literature. A patient with bipolar I, in remission and with insight intact, authorizes involuntary admission and reinstatement of medication if she becomes manic. She knows exactly what the manic state will look like from the inside: she will not experience the early signs as signs, she will experience the directive as persecution, and she will fight it. When we honor the directive over her objection, we tell the family, and ourselves, that she bound her future self.

My claim is that this description is false in an instructive way, and that the toxin puzzle shows why. What distinguishes the two cases is not the presence of a gap between the earlier judgment and the later motive — both have that. It is where the payoff attaches, and who has to produce it. In Kavka's case the payoff attaches to a mental state, and only the agent himself can produce that state; the state is constitutively oriented toward an action he knows he will have no reason to perform, which is why it cannot be sincerely formed. In the directive case the payoff attaches to an outcome — the medication being given — and that outcome is produced not by her intention surviving but by an institution's standing readiness to act. She does not need to intend anything at the later time. She does not need to remember the directive, to believe in it, or to be conscious. Call the first structure internal self-binding and the second external self-binding, the second being really the purchase of a mechanism rather than an exercise of will at all.

If that is right, the moral usually drawn from precommitment in the clinic is backwards. The directive is not a triumph of the earlier will over the later one; it is an admission that the earlier will cannot bind anything, and must instead buy an arrangement that works whether or not the later self endorses it. Kavka's puzzle is not a puzzle about weakness of will or about the authority of past selves. It is about the impossibility of the internal variant, and about how much of our moral language of commitment quietly presupposes the external one.

Two difficulties with my own account, which I would rather raise myself than have found for me. First, if the mechanism does all the work, what remains of the patient's authorship? I want to say that she authorizes rather than intends, and that authorization is a different thing that can survive the disappearance of the intention behind it. But I suspect authorization is doing all the work here and may be empty — a name for the fact that the right paperwork exists. Second, and against my own thesis: there are directives in the clinic with no institution behind them. A patient after a first episode of psychosis, insight intact, resolves to come back to the hospital herself at the first sign of relapse, knowing full well that when the signs come she will not recognize them as such. Here the payoff does attach to her own later conduct and to her own later intention, and the toxin structure seems to bite. If she can form that resolution at all, it looks as though she can form it only by not thinking too clearly about what she will be like later.

Which suggests the general thesis I want to end on, and which I hold only provisionally: the possibility of internal self-binding is inversely proportional to the agent's clarity about the later absence of reasons. Fully transparent agents cannot bind themselves from the inside. The standard philosophical repairs — resolute choice, cultivated dispositions, the rule against reopening a decision once made — all work, as far as I can see, by licensing a measure of strategic opacity, which is why they read as special pleading and why I do not think they can honestly be recommended to a patient.

Four things I want from you, in this order:

1. Whether the internal/external distinction is a real distinction or a relabeling of the obvious fact that a contract with a third party is not an intention. Is there an existing distinction in the literature on intention that it collapses into, or that it is merely restating — something about intending versus causing, about non-reconsideration, or about the means-end commitments built into intending?
2. Accuracy. Does my rendering of the puzzle hold up, including the payment-before-the-drinking ordering and the anti-bluff stipulation I flagged? Have I flattened two different responses — the resolute refusal to reassess a plan, and the view that once one has sincerely intended, reconsideration is excluded — into a single position? I suspect I have, and I would like to know what I have lost by doing it.
3. Whether the transparency thesis is defensible. I want counterexamples: cases of an agent who binds herself internally, in full clarity about her later absence of reasons, with no appeal to opacity, self-deception, or an external enforcer. If such cases exist, say which of my premises they attack.
4. A candid view on whether the essay is interesting or merely decorative — that is, whether Kavka's puzzle is load-bearing in the argument or is there to make a claim about clinics sound more philosophical than it is. Tell me what to cut.

I am not asking for references; I have the literature and cannot chase anything down at the moment. What I do want is an explicit note on where your own confidence should be discounted: which of the objections above you think is decisive, what would change your mind about it, and where you are reconstructing a philosopher's position from secondary summaries rather than from the text. If you think my central distinction survives, say what would have to be true for it to fail, not merely that it survives.
