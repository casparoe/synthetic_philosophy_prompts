A pattern keeps recurring in arguments I follow, and I have never found a name for it. Someone classifies a case under a term that plainly admits of degrees. Asked where the line falls — or what would settle a case near the line — they insist that demanding a line is the wrong kind of request: the term is fuzzy by design, and any bright line would be arbitrary. That reply is treated as closing the matter. Moments later, however, the same speaker applies the term to a neighbouring case that differs only in degree, and does so flatly, with none of the hedging that the acknowledged vagueness seemed to require. The elasticity is thus available on demand: invoked to deflect a challenge to a classification, then dropped whenever a classification is wanted.

Two instances. A commentator declines to say what would make a given regime a democracy, on the grounds that the concept resists criteria, and then uses "democracy" and "not a democracy" as unargued premises throughout the rest of the piece. A lawyer argues that whether a gig worker is an "employee" cannot be reduced to any test, while asserting confidently, about an arrangement differing only in the degree of control exercised, that it is definitely not employment.

Does this have an established name — in the philosophical literature on vagueness, in informal logic, or in rhetoric? I can see that it is not the continuum fallacy, since nothing here denies that a distinction exists, and not plain equivocation, since the sense of the term does not appear to change. What I am after is the carving: what exactly the abuse consists in, how it differs from a legitimate shift in the standards governing a vague predicate, and what makes a given instance one rather than the other. If no term exists, propose one, define it, and tell me where its boundary cases lie.
