My sister stood for a small party in our national election last month and lost, and while everyone else was arguing about coalition maths on the telly I went and actually read the electoral rulebook, which turned out to be about four pages long. We're a party-list country now — we switched from single-member districts roughly ten years ago, mostly on the argument that under the old system half the country's ballots were just wasted. The rule is: count the valid votes, work out each party's exact share of the 10 seats including the fractions, give everyone the whole-number part, then hand the remaining seats to whoever has the biggest leftover fraction. I'm not a mathematician, I do IT support for a chain of dental clinics, but I redid the whole count in a spreadsheet to check the commission's numbers and it matched. There were five lists; one got 4,800 votes, which is just under the 5% cutoff, so it was deleted entirely and the shares were then worked out of the remaining 100,000 — 43,000 / 34,000 / 15,000 / 8,000. Exact shares 4.3, 3.4, 1.5, 0.8; whole numbers first gives 4, 3, 1, 0 which is only 8 seats; the two leftovers go to 0.8 and 0.5. Final result 4, 3, 2, 1, and my sister's party got the 2. Then I looked up how other places do the same job and found this other family of rules where you instead keep dividing each party's votes by 1, then 2, then 3, and award each seat to whoever has the biggest quotient at that moment. Same 100,000 ballots through that rule: 5, 4, 1, 0. The 8,000-vote party gets nothing at all, and my sister's party drops to one. Same votes, two parliaments, and as far as I can tell nobody has ever made a moral argument for either one, it's just written into a statute.

The bit that properly unsettled me: I re-ran the fraction rule pretending the assembly had 11 seats instead of 10, purely to see what would happen. The party that had 2 seats ended up with 1. You add a seat to the pot and somebody loses one. And this isn't an artifact of numbers I made up in a spreadsheet — I read that the Americans hit exactly this in the 1880s with one of the states, it's literally named after it, and they eventually gave up on that rule in the 1940s. Meanwhile the divide-by-1-2-3 rules apparently can't do that shrinking thing, but they can hand a party more seats than its exact share would round up to. So it looks like you can have "everyone gets their fair share" or "the rule doesn't go insane when you nudge the inputs", but not both, and every country has quietly picked a side without telling anyone. I also found a third variant that divides by 1, 3, 5 which agrees with the fraction rule on these numbers but disagrees on others I tried, which somehow makes it worse, because now the outcome depends both on which rule you picked and on the particular split of votes that happened to turn up.

So here's what I actually want to understand, and it isn't which method is best. What does the leftover-fraction rule presuppose about what a vote is and what a parliament is for? It reads to me like it treats each ballot as a claim on a slice of a fixed pie and treats the exact fraction as the thing that's morally owed — but owed to whom, the party or the voter, and what makes a fraction of a seat the right thing to be owed? What does the divisor rule presuppose instead: that what we owe each other is a consistent procedure rather than an exact outcome, so that a rule that never contradicts itself matters more than one that never underpays anybody? Which rival picture of representation does each of those quietly rule out — and does either one commit you to thinking of parties, rather than people, as the units that get represented? I also can't work out whether the 5% threshold belongs to the same theory or flatly contradicts it: we sold the whole switch to PR on the idea that every vote should count equally, and then we delete 4,800 people wholesale, and worse, deleting them raises everyone else's share, so those votes don't just fail to count, they count for the other side. Is that a different value — governability, stability, keeping out cranks — smuggled back into a system justified on fairness, or is it actually required by the same theory? And how much of this is genuinely underdetermined: are there several respectable theories of fair representation that all license the same rule, such that the statute isn't expressing any deep commitment at all, just bookkeeping convention that someone picked in 1947? Finally, tell me what a country would have to change about how it justifies proportional representation if it admitted out loud that exactness and coherence can't both be had — does the whole "every vote counts" story survive that admission, or does it have to be rebuilt on something else, and what would the something else be?
