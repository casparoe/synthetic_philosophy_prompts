Hume's monetary essays are usually compressed into three headline theses — long-run neutrality of money, short-run non-neutrality along the adjustment path, and the self-equilibration of specie among trading nations. That compression obscures the fact that the essays contain several logically separable propositions, some of which do real work in Hume's polemic against the mercantile system and some of which are largely ornamental. Consider the following six, stated in paraphrase and drawn from "Of Money," "Of Interest," and "Of the Balance of Trade":

**P1.** Money is not itself an article of commerce but only the instrument men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another; considering any single kingdom by itself, a greater or lesser plenty of money is of no consequence.

**P2.** The prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money — but the operative proportion is between the metal actually in circulation and the commodities offered together with the briskness of their exchange, so that the same stock of money can sustain very different price levels in a lively and in a languid economy.

**P3.** Where the stock of money grows slowly and by degrees, the interval between the arrival of new money and the full adjustment of prices is a period of quickened industry; it is this intermediate state rather than any endpoint that makes a growing stock of specie desirable, since the real power and riches of a state consist in its stock of labour.

**P4.** Money cannot sink far or permanently below its proper level in any country that has people and industry: a nation losing specie finds its labour and commodities cheap, exports the more, and draws the metal back again; conversely, a nation grown rich is checked by the ability of poorer neighbours to undersell it in foreign markets.

**P5.** The rate of interest is governed not by the quantity of precious metal but by the demand for loans from borrowers who intend to employ them profitably; the long historical fall in interest followed the growth of commerce, frugality, and a middling class of lenders, not the mere arrival of American silver, though a sudden influx may depress rates until prices catch up.

**P6.** Artificially increasing paper credit can never be the interest of a trading nation, since it pushes money beyond its natural proportion to labour and commodities; indeed the most advantageous bank would be one that locked up all the metal it received and never returned part of it into circulation.

The conclusion whose support is at issue is Hume's anti-mercantilist one: that a statesman ought not to pursue a favourable balance of trade or to amass specie as such, because the wealth and strength of a nation lie in the number and industry of its people, and the quantity of money matters only instrumentally.

Three tasks:

1. Assign to each of P1–P6 a number in [0, 1] measuring how load-bearing it is for that conclusion — 1 meaning the conclusion could not be sustained without it, 0 meaning it contributes nothing to it however true it may be — and present the six as a ranking consistent with your numbers. State explicitly where you have placed near-ties and why the margin between them is small.

2. For each proposition, give in one or two sentences the shortest argument by which a Humean could still reach the anti-mercantilist conclusion if that proposition were simply struck from the system, or state that no such argument is available. Judge these detours by whether they remain within Hume's own resources — his account of industry, luxury, the balance of trade, and the psychology of the middling ranks — rather than by whether they could be supplied by later economic theory.

3. Separately from (1), identify which single proposition would, if you were told Hume had got it wrong, do the greatest damage to the remaining five. Here the criterion is not independent plausibility and not importance to the anti-mercantilist conclusion, but the structure of dependencies: which falsity would propagate furthest through the others. Explain the propagation, and note any case where the proposition you select for (3) differs in rank from the one you placed highest in (1) — such a divergence, if it arises, is itself informative about the difference between argumentative centrality and systematic fragility.
