So I cover science and ideas for a magazine — climate and tech mostly these days — and I've been reporting a piece on the running fight inside AI safety between the people who want models to actually be aligned and the people who want to build systems that stay safe even if the model isn't. I sat in on a workshop-ish event and did a couple of interviews, and the phrase that kept coming back, from three different people who I'm pretty sure weren't coordinating, was some version of "assume the model is adversarial." It gets used like a trump card. One researcher used it to swat away my question about whether all that monitoring machinery falls apart the moment the model being watched is smart enough to out-think the watcher — he said you just assume it's adversarial, that's the whole point — and I couldn't tell whether he'd answered me or changed the subject. A panelist the next day used the same words to argue that alignment research is basically a distraction, since we can never verify that anyone's model is really aligned anyway.

My problem is that I'm fairly confident those two uses aren't the same claim, and I suspect at least one of them is definately doing something sneaky with the word "adversarial." My own half-formed list so far: it could mean the model is actually scheming and deliberately deceptive (an empirical claim about real systems), or it could just mean "not aimed at what we want," which is a much weaker and duller thing, or it could be a design stance — the way a security engineer assumes breach or writes a threat model, where nobody's saying the attacker is real, they're just refusing to bet against the worst case. And maybe there's a fourth, more specific version where adverserial means something game-theoretic: a system that knows how it's being evaluated and can shape its behavior around the evaluation, which would be a stronger and weirder claim than any of the others.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, running from the platitudes to the ones that are actually false or unfalsifiable? I'd want to know for each one who or what is supposed to be adversarial (this model, the training process that produced it, the next version of it, frontier systems as a class), and whether it's functioning as a prediction, an assumption, or a design constraint — those feel like they'd cash out very differently. Then tell me what each reading commits you to in practice: does it require monitors and tripwires, sandboxing, checks for sandbagging, or does it make some of those pointless or even counterproductive? And the part I most need: which reading, if any, actually answers my question about a monitor being outsmarted by the thing it's monitoring, and which one, if any, gets you from "assume it's adversarial" to "so alignment work doesn't matter" — because I suspect that second move is a non-sequitur and I'd like to say so with some confidence. No links or citations, please, I'm not going to be able to check them and I don't want a reading list; I just need the distinctions spelled out cleanly enough that I could drop them into a sidebar for readers who've never heard of any of this. A hunch about which reading is the one most of these people actually mean would be useful too.
