I've been reading rationalist blogs since the sequences days, so I come into this with a set of priors that nobody else in the room shares, and I need help writing something that survives contact with seven smart people who don't share them.

Situation: I'm one of seven trustees on a mid-sized family foundation's grantmaking comittee (~$35M/yr). Most of it is boring global-health stuff, plus a $5M "emerging risks" sleeve. The charter says grants are to be evaluated on expected benefit per dollar, and for a decade that has meant our analyst runs expected-value math and we nod along.

This cycle two proposals are fighting over the same $5M:

A — a pathogen early-warning plus surge-response network in six cities. The applicant's own model: 85% chance it saves about 50 lives, 14% about 400, 1% about 300,000. Plain EU ≈ 3,100 lives.

B — scaling up an existing net-distribution and case-management program. 95% chance of about 1,100 lives, 5% about 900. Plain EU ≈ 1,090 lives.

So on EU, A wins by roughly 3x and we were about to fund it. Then one trustee — ex-portfolio manager, reads actual philosophy, definitely not a crank — circulated a one-pager saying we're using the wrong rule. Her pitch is risk-weighted expected utility, Buchak's REU: you run a risk function over the cumulative probabilities, so the weight on the improvement from a worse outcome to a better one depends on the probability of getting at least the better outcome, rather than everything being a probability-weighted average. With ρ(p) = p² she gets A ≈ 88 lives and B ≈ 1,080, and we fund B. She's emphatic that this isn't just "value lives sub-linearly": a concave utility over lives is a different move entirely, and REU is a real departure from expected utility that gives up the independence axiom.

Three of us pushed back and thursday is in four days and the argument has gone nowhere:

1. My reflex is the standard one — risk aversion is for when a loss knocks you out of the game or when you get to re-bet; if you make lots of independent bets, just maximize EV. But our analyst made the annoying counter that the $5M isn't ours: the risk is borne by beneficiaries who each get exactly one shot and can't average anything over repeated trials, so "we make 120 grants a year" might not be the relevant pooling unit at all. I honestly don't know who's right, and the memo can't wave at this.
2. Someone else claims you can't even use REU as a per-proposal scoring formula, because the risk function is supposed to apply to the whole distribution and isn't separable across decisions — which would be a mess for us, since per-proposal scoring is the entire process. Is she right? If she is, what do we do instead?
3. And there's the awkward fact that adopting ρ(p) = p² as standing policy means we never fund a longshot again, on any topic, ever. That feels less like picking a decision rule and more like quietly changing the mission without telling anyone.

What I want: draft the ~2 page memo I'll circulate before thursday, addressed to the committee. It needs an actual bottom line — fund A, fund B, split the money, or defer with conditions — plus a recommendation on what our standing scoring rule should be, since we've been asked to settle that too. Give reasons the pro-REU trustee could genuinely respond to rather than dismiss, and a real section on the strongest consideration against whatever you recommend (the strongest one, not a strawman paragraph we can knock over in two sentences). Assume linear value in lives saved for now, but if you think that assumption is doing sneaky work, say so and keep it clearly separated from the risk-weighting question. No bibliography and no reading list, I don't need citations — I need the argument. And if you think the numbers I've given are the wrong things to be arguing about, tell me what we should be demanding from the applicants instead.
