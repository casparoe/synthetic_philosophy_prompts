A colleague who spent most of her working life running ensemble weather forecasts is retiring, and her former students are assembling a short printed volume for her. The editors have asked for verse about the science rather than about the person; her name is not to appear. I have undertaken to contribute a pair of sonnets, and I want them written to a fairly exact specification, because the form is intended to carry the argument rather than decorate it.

The specification is this. Two sonnets, headed I and II, fourteen lines each, ten-syllable lines in a recognizable iambic rhythm — feminine endings and an occasional trochaic first foot are welcome, padding is not — each in the Shakespearean pattern abab cdcd efef gg. The second sonnet is a perturbed copy of the first. It must use the same seven rhyme sounds, in the same order, and no line-ending word may serve in both poems: the scheme is the invariant set, the words are the trajectory, and that contrast is the whole point. Corresponding lines should begin as near-duplicates and end as strangers. Line 1 of II differs from line 1 of I by exactly one word, and that word should be one whose job in I was to guarantee exactness — a measure, a digit, a term of completeness — so the seed is planted at the moment of maximum confidence. The difference should then widen audibly through the second quatrain and read as multiplication, the same factor applied again and again, rather than as an accumulating list of mishaps: a reader ought to be able to feel a doubling time. By the couplets the two poems should be making different claims about different things while still rhyming with one another.

Content. The subject is the atmosphere, and one weather system should run through both sonnets so that the separation is visible in a single image. I states the confident picture: the state given at one instant, the laws given completely, every later instant thereby settled. It must sound like triumph, not like a straw man. II is that picture with one digit rounded, and its turn at line nine should be the moment the reader realizes that nothing has become uncaused, no law has been suspended, and nothing here licenses a claim about uncaused human action; what has died is only the permission to pass from exact law to exact foreknowledge, since the state can never be given to more than finitely many digits. The harder point belongs in II's closing couplet: the separation cannot grow without limit, because the system wanders inside a bounded region, so what is lost is where it will be and what survives is the shape of that region and the frequency of its visits there — the distribution rather than the day.

Vocabulary: the poems may not use butterfly, chaos, entropy, quantum, Heisenberg, uncertainty principle, or Laplace, and may not call the system random. Determinism, deterministic, predictability, and predictable may appear once at most between them. The constraint must not show.

After the sonnets, add a note of no more than eighty words saying where the perturbation enters, what the two couplets assert survives, and what philosophical work the shared rhyme scheme is doing.
