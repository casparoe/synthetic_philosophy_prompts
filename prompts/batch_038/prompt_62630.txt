hey — i'm a backend engineer, my physics is whatever a podcast left in my head, and i have no philosophy background at all. i want you to write a piece of satire for my team's offsite and i have a pretty specific format in mind.

Write a blameless incident postmortem, in the exact corporate genre my company uses — Summary, Impact, Timeline (all times UTC), Root Cause, Contributing Factors, the Five Whys, Lessons Learned, and an Action Items table with owners and due dates — for a production incident at a startup that has been running a Maxwell's demon in prod. Call the service SZILARD-1. It's a sidecar that runs a one-molecule engine: it measures which half of the box the molecule is in, drops in a partition, lets the thing expand isothermally, and skims work off a single heat bath. It's been quietly taking a bite out of the datacenter cooling bill for two quarters and nobody asked questions because the dashboard was green.

The incident: the demon's measurement buffer is unbounded, it fills up, the on-call flushes it, and the heat from the flush takes out a rack. Around 800 words.

the joke has to come from the physics being exactly right, not from anybody hand-waving it. specifically:

- the five whys should walk all the way down to Landauer: the measurement step can be done for free, the erasure can't, and every bit you flush dumps at least kT ln2 into the cold sink. the team's whole business model turns out to be "batch the erasures into the off-peak window," which is just an entropy line of credit that never showed up on the balance sheet.
- and the real punchline underneath that should be that the net was never positive — a bit extracted and a bit erased are the same kT ln2 — so what they were actually selling was a timing arbitrage, and the reconciliation came due all at once. the doc should get within a sentence of noticing this and then not notice it.
- deadpan the whole way. nobody in the doc realizes they built a perpetual motion machine; they describe it in the exact language we use for a memory leak and an unbounded queue, and the demon has an SLO and an error budget.
- the incident should be classified as a capacity problem, not a law-of-the-universe problem, and the tone should never break.
- contributing factors should include the design review having been waved through by an intern who had a take about the second law being a statistical tendency rather than a hard constraint — and the postmortem should quietly concede that he's right about that, and that it helps them exactly zero.
- at least one action item has to be a fix that's genuinely correct in principle (something reversible-computation shaped, or just never erase at all and accept that the demon's memory grows forever until you run out of universe), and at least one has to be the kind of confidently wrong item a real engineer would absolutely file, like adding a bigger heat sink and calling it "headroom."
- one line in the timeline should be a quoted slack message from the on-call, in that 3am register.

the target of the satire is the genre itself — the way a postmortem turns every limit in nature into a capacity plan with a due date and an owner — not any real person, so don't name a living physicist and don't cite anything or add a reading list. naming Landauer's principle in passing, the way an engineer names a theorem somebody told him about once, is fine and probably funnier.

last line should be the sign-off, something in the shape of "no humans were at fault" that lands the actual point instead of just being cute.
