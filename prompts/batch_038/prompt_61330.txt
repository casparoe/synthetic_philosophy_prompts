So — my name came up off the voter/jury roll last month and our city is running one of those citizens' assemblies on the transit budget. Sixty people, three weekends, recommendations go to the council but aren't binding. I said yes mostly out of curiosity, and then I made the mistake of reading up on sortition beforehand, and now I'm halfway through a stack of stuff about mini-publics, the Athenian practice, the Irish assemblies, proposals for a randomly selected second chamber, and honestly it feels like everyone is answering a slightly different question. One strand says the point of the lot is fairness: everyone gets an equal chance to hold office, and that's the whole value. Another strand says the point is epistemic: you get cognitive diversity, no reelection incentives, no career politicians, so the body decides better. A third says the point is symbolic or expressive — it says something about who counts as competent to rule. Those imply genuinely different designs, and I can't tell which claims are load-bearing for which camp. So before I read any more of it, write out what I should be demanding: the desiderata for an account of when and why a body picked by lot is entitled to make binding decisions. I want the phenomena it has to explain, with the case behind each one. Some that keep nagging at me: why I'm completely fine with a randomly drawn jury but would not accept a randomly drawn judge writing the opinion; why the same sixty people voting on day one seems worse than those sixty people voting after three weekends of testimony, when the selection procedure is identical; why nobody proposes sortition for the executive even when they propose it for legislatures; why the losing side in an election generally swallows the result but I'm not sure they would swallow a result from a body that never had to ask anyone for a vote; and why the mini-publics that actually get run are almost always stratified to match census categories rather than drawn as a pure lottery. Also the distinctions it can't collapse — I suspect equal chance of being selected and equal influence over the outcome are not the same thing, and that a body mirroring the population is not obviously the same as a body representing it.

Then the part I actually care about most: tell me which of the things people demand here are real requirements and which are just the house preferences of one camp, because I think a lot of the literature quietly drops a requirement rather than admits it can't be met. My own guess, which may be wrong, is that "nobody is systematically excluded from office" and "the body has to look like the public it speaks for" pull against each other, since the second one requires sorting people into boxes and topping up whichever box is short — which is precisely not a lottery. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and show me the squeeze, because then the list is the result and I'd rather know that up front. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidate accounts — I want one thing I can hold up against whatever our assembly turns into, not a scorecard of everything.

One concrete thing to run it on, since abstract lists are easy to be agreeable about: a friend of mine is pushing a design where the assembly members serve a single long term, twelve years, specifically so they stop thinking like amateurs and build real expertise, and where anyone drafted can decline and nominate a substitute of their own choosing, who then also serves the full term. I feel like that gives away something important and keeps something important, but I can't articulate either half without just saying it feels undemocratic, and that's not an arguement. So give me the vocabulary for what that design trades — separately for the long term and for the substitution rule, since I think they're not the same kind of problem. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know that's a weird ask for something that's shaped like a list, but I'm going to be reading it on the bus with one thumb and the numbered-list formatting always turns into soup on my phone.
