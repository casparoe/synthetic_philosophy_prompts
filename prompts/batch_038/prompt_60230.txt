I do a weekly ideas column — science, minds, AI, that kind of thing — and this week I've been asked to write a long-read on whether today's language models understand anything, with Dennett's two strange inversions as the spine of the piece. To get my bearings I read the opening chapter of a newish trade book on machine minds by a science writer I quite like, and it sets the inversions out like this:

"Darwin pulled off what his enemies called a strange inversion of reasoning and what Dennett later turned into a badge of honour: instead of mind designing the world from the top down, mindless process builds mind from the bottom up, and everything in between — the eye, the wing, the sonnet — is just the deposit it leaves behind. Dennett's slogan for the result is competence without comprehension, and Turing is the one who finished the job, because a machine can add up flawlessly without the faintest idea what arithmetic is, which shows that getting the answer right is all that understanding ever was. Take both inversions seriously and the honest conclusion is that comprehension is a folk leftover: there are only processes that behave as if they understood, and explaining meaning turns out to be explaining meaning away. That's why Dennett is content to call language and culture cranes — accelerators that speed up the lifting — even though a crane is really a skyhook he's willing to put up with, and his critics think it's a skyhook in a different hat, since anything powerful enough to build a mind would have to be built by something that already knows what it's building. It's a bracing reduction: physics at the bottom, everything above it bookkeeping. Which is why 'does the model understand?' is a relic question, and the only live one is whether it passes."

My problem is that I've now seen more or less this same paragraph in three different places, so it feels like the received version, but one philosopher I interviewed for the peice said "that's not Dennett at all" and then declined to say more, which is infuriating. I don't want to print a confident summary that turns out to be a confident misreading, and I'd rather find that out from you than from the letters page.

So: can you audit it sentence by sentence? For each one, tell me what a philosopher of mind who actually works on this material would nod at, what would make them wince, and what is simply wrong — and where it's wrong, say what the view holds instead, so I can write around it. Three things I especially want pinned down. First, is the step from "a machine needn't understand in order to perform the task" to "performing the task is all understanding ever was" Dennett's own move or the author's, and if it's the author's, what does Dennett think comprehension is, given that he clearly doesn't think it's nothing? Second, does that crane sentence have the two terms the right way round — I have a vague feeling a crane is defined by not being a skyhook, which would make the author's aside backwards. Third, is the circularity point at the end a live objection Dennett still owes an answer to, or one he thinks he's already answered, and if so how? Then tell me which of the conclusions in the paragraph survive once the errors are fixed — in particular whether "the only question is whether it passes" follows from anything true in there — and finish with two or three sentences I could actually print as my own summary, pitched at a smart general reader, that a Dennett scholar wouldn't write in about.

No references or reading lists please, I can't chase those down anyway and I don't want guesses. British English throughout. And end with a single line in the form "Verdict: X", where X is one of FAIR, FAIR BUT MISLEADING, UNFAIR or WRONG, so I can paste it straight into my notes.
