I am the teaching assistant for an upper-level course on well-being, and I am grading a timed short-answer question. Please grade the response below and justify your grade.

**Question (20 marks, 40 minutes, 500 words):** Hedonists about well-being hold that pleasure is the only thing good for a person in itself. But pleasures seem to have nothing in common: the warmth of sun on one's shoulders, the absorption of working through a puzzle, the relief of a fever breaking. Explain the problem this heterogeneity poses for hedonism, and assess one strategy for answering it.

**Student's answer:** Hedonism needs pleasure to be a single kind, because its claim is a general one: it says that every pleasure is good for the person who feels it, and that only pleasures are. If nothing is shared by all the states we call pleasures, the general claim has no real content, since there is no one thing that is present in each case and good in each case. Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures was an attempt to answer this: he conceded that pleasures differ, but ranked them by the settled preferences of competent judges, so the unity of pleasure is restored by giving it a common measure. A more recent strategy denies that pleasure is a sensation at all. On Feldman's attitudinal hedonism, being pleased is a matter of having a pro-attitude toward a state of affairs — being pleased *that p* — and this has one and the same structure in every case, which is exactly what the heterogeneity objection says the phenomenal accounts cannot provide. But this simply converts hedonism into a desire-satisfaction theory, since being pleased that p is just wanting p to obtain; nothing distinctively hedonistic survives, and what was an objection to hedonism becomes an objection to some other view. A third option is to treat pleasure as a family-resemblance concept, held together by overlapping similarities and no common core. This is in my view the best answer: it concedes the premise of the objection and yet leaves hedonism intact, because a concept can be perfectly serviceable, and a theory perfectly true, without corresponding to a natural kind.
