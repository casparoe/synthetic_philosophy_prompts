For a reading group on Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" I built the following argument, and it now looks to me like it proves too much: it seems to leave the anti-aggregator with either no view at all or the very view he was arguing against.

Setup, as I read Taurek: one supply of a drug, enough either for David, who is your friend, or for five strangers, each of whom needs a full dose to survive. He holds two things about situations of this shape — that where none of the six is a friend you should toss a coin, so that each has an equal chance, and that where one of them is your friend you may simply save your friend, no coin required.

1. What an agent ought to do is determined by the weight of the reasons bearing on the choice.
2. The weight of a reason is fixed by how much the relevant outcome matters to the person whose reason it is, and by nothing else.
3. Each stranger's death would be a loss to that person alone; nobody stands to lose five times over, so no reason in the case carries five times the weight of David's reason not to die.
4. Therefore the reasons favoring the five are not, taken together, weightier than the reason favoring David.
5. A coin toss is an instrument of fairness, and fairness has work to do only where claims of unequal weight must be reconciled.
6. By (4) the claims here are not unequal, so there is nothing for fairness to reconcile, so the coin toss is not required.
7. Taurek's permission to save the friend without a toss confirms (6): on his view, an agent-relative consideration can simply settle a choice among balanced claims.
8. But if friendship can settle it, so can the agent's own inclination, or indifference, or a dislike of boats; hence nothing requires saving anyone.
9. Alternatively, if the coin toss really is required, what requires it must be that each of the six claims counts equally in fixing the odds — that is aggregation of claims, performed probabilistically instead of by summation.
10. So Taurek's position either collapses into (8), which is no moral position at all, or into (9), which is just the view that the numbers count.

Where does this break? I would rather keep (3) and (4) than give them up, since they are the whole point of the paper, so I expect the pressure falls on (2), (5), or the slide from (7) to (8) — but I cannot tell whether any of those can be rejected without quietly letting aggregation back in. Please identify the failing step or steps, say why each looked respectable where it stood, and price the available exits, including whether there is a coherent position on which a randomizing device is required precisely because the first-order reasons are balanced rather than despite it. Note as well where my reconstruction is uncharitable or depends on a contested reading of what Taurek is committed to, and be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled among people who work on this problem and which are genuinely contested — don't hand me one side of a live dispute dressed as the consensus.
