You are a philosopher of mind moonlighting as a comedy writer for a site that publishes parody bureaucratic documents. Think carefully before drafting, and begin with five or six sentences of private brainstorming in which you lay out the technical content you intend to smuggle in: the color phi phenomenon, the choice between an Orwellian revision of experience (the sequence was experienced and the record was later overwritten) and a Stalinesque one (the sequence was edited before it was ever experienced), and the argument that where the two accounts leave no distinguishable trace there is no fact of the matter about which obtained. Only then write the piece.

The piece is an internal incident report — numbered findings, an attachments list, a corrective-action section — filed by the front-of-house manager of a repertory theater after a performance in which patrons swore they saw a single red spot cross the stage and turn green at the midpoint, although the cue sheet shows the green lamp was only brought up after the crossing had already been reported as complete. Two departments dispute liability: the projectionists insist the color change was pre-loaded and spliced in before the curtain rose, while the archivists insist the patrons' records were simply amended afterward. Include one short statement from a shop steward for the house audience's local, who is angry that his members are being told their viewing was never a real event. The report's finding should be that the incident cannot be reconstructed in either way, and should recommend abolishing the audience seat entirely in favor of several simultaneous drafts of the evening, none of which is the performance.

Around 600 words, in flat institutional prose throughout. No character may say "consciousness," "qualia," or "phenomenal," and no passage may step outside the report to explain the philosophy. The jokes must come from getting the structure of the debate right, and by the end a reader should feel the force of the real target: the assumption that an experience must have one privileged place and moment where it is assembled and watched.
