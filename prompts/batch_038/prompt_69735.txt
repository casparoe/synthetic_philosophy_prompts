**STIPULATED NOTATION (printed on the examination paper)**

Throughout the question, the following labels are used with fixed meanings:

- **(SI) Strong intellectualism:** for an action Φ, knowing how to Φ consists in knowing some proposition p (canonically, that w is a way for oneself to Φ).
- **(CP) Contemplation premise:** in order to employ one's knowledge that p, one must contemplate p.
- **(AP) Action premise:** if one Φs, then one employs one's knowledge-how to Φ.
- **(D) Dispositionism:** knowing how to Φ is a complex disposition to Φ in suitable circumstances.
- **(FA) Factivity:** knowledge that p entails that p is true.
- **(AE) Ability entailment:** knowing how to Φ entails being able to Φ.
- **(GC) Guidance condition:** S knows how to Φ only if, were S to Φ intentionally, S's knowing how to Φ would be among the states guiding S's Φ-ing.

**EXAMINATION QUESTION (as printed)**

(a) State the regress argument against (SI) as precisely as you can. Indicate which of (SI), (CP), and (AP) do the essential work, and explain why the conclusion drawn is that intelligent performance cannot be analysed in terms of the intelligent contemplation of propositions. [30 marks]

(b) The standard contemporary intellectualist reply, associated with Stanley and Williamson, rejects (CP) and holds that knowing how to Φ is knowledge that w is a way for oneself to Φ, entertained under a *practical mode of presentation*. Explain how this is supposed to block the regress, and assess whether it succeeds. In your assessment, say explicitly whether the reply leaves intellectualism distinguishable from (D). [30 marks]

(c) Two cases. *The Pianist:* a concert pianist has, through a degenerative condition, permanently lost the use of her hands; she can no longer play, but she can still describe the concerto, dictate fingerings, and correctly identify and correct errors in her students' performances. *The Archivist:* through documents, plates, and film, she has reconstructed in complete detail a restoration procedure she has never performed and, given her circumstances, cannot perform; she can nevertheless walk a restorer through it, step by step, without error.

Using (GC), discuss whether the Pianist and the Archivist know how to do the relevant things. Does the intellectualist reply from (b) help here? Say whether (FA) commits the intellectualist to (AE), and what the intellectualist should say about (GC). [40 marks]

*Conditions: 50 minutes, closed book. Brevity is not itself penalised.*

**STUDENT'S ANSWER**

Ryle's objection to the intellectualist legend is that it generates a vicious regress. On (SI), knowing how to Φ is just a matter of knowing some proposition, roughly that w is a way for one to Φ. But an operation is intelligent only if the agent has thought about the relevant proposition beforehand, and contemplating propositions is itself something that can be done more or less intelligently. So every intelligent act would require a prior intelligent act of contemplation, which would require a prior one in turn, and so on without end; no agent could ever break into the circle. Ryle concludes that intelligence cannot be defined in terms of intellect, and hence that knowing-how cannot be a species of knowing-that. The premises doing the work are (SI) and (CP); (AP) is needed only to connect the action with the knowledge that is supposed to explain it.

The intellectualist reply is that the regress depends on (CP), which no intellectualist need accept. What makes a state knowledge-how rather than inert knowledge-that is that the proposition is grasped under a practical mode of presentation. In effect the intellectualist here adopts a qualified dispositionism: knowledge-how is a standing disposition to behave appropriately in appropriate circumstances, and because a disposition can be manifested without being contemplated, the regress never gets started. The price is that intellectualism surrenders its distinctive claim, that knowing-how is a kind of knowing-that, but it saves the phenomena, and Ryle's argument is shown to bite only against a caricature of intellectualism rather than against the view itself.

The reply does less well with ability. If knowing how to Φ is a species of knowing that, it inherits the factivity of knowledge-that, and the truth of "w is a way for me to Φ" cannot be made true by a state I am incapable of acting on. Intellectualism is therefore committed to (AE). But the Pianist plainly still knows how to play the concerto: she can specify it, dictate it, and detect and correct errors in it. Since intellectualism must deny this, the cost is real, and it vindicates Ryle's insistence that knowing-how is bound up with what an agent can actually do.

The Archivist is harder. Since she has never performed the procedure and cannot, (GC) implies that her state could not guide her own doing of it, so (GC) entails that she does not know how. That result is too harsh, and the intuition that she does know how is strong; so the intellectualist should reject (GC). A better formulation restricts (GC) to agents who are able to Φ: where ability is absent, guidance is impossible, and (GC) simply falls silent, leaving the Archivist's knowledge-how intact. This preserves both cases, though, as noted above, the intellectualist still owes an explanation of why (FA) does not already require ability.
