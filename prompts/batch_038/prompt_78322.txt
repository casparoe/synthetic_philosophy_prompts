I've come to hold a particular view about hard choices, and I want it stress-tested. When two options are genuinely on a par — neither better nor worse nor equally good — the impasse is not a matter of vagueness in the comparative, nor of missing facts about the options, but of standards the agent has not yet willed. Commitment settles the ranking without bootstrapping a reason out of nowhere, because it completes a determination that was underdetermined rather than creating a new value fact. The view carries a signature: a true hard choice should be unresolvable by any further information about the options, resolvable by any sincere commitment, and the resulting ranking should survive sustained reflection.

What would count against this? Sort the candidates from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each what the world or the case would have to look like: cycles arising from the non-transitivity of the on-a-par relation and the choice-theoretic objections they invite; the small-improvement argument turned back against the parity it was meant to establish; a Broome-style vagueness account that explains everything my view explains; imprecise-equality views of the Parfit kind; the phenomenology of agent-regret and of dirty-hands residue. What does my view predict that these rivals don't, and are those predictions checkable at all, or only by reclassifying every apparently resolved case as one that was never a hard choice? Address that insulation worry head-on, including whether it is a genuine defect for a thesis of this kind or simply what a conceptual claim about the structure of comparison should look like. Say also which of these a defender of parity would herself concede as telling against the view, and which she would dismiss. I suspect I'm least worried by the cycle objections and most worried by the reclassification problem; tell me if I have that backwards.
