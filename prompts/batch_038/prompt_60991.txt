I am a senior at a public high school, and this spring I will defend my capstone paper before a panel of five drawn from our faculty and administration. I have fifteen minutes to present and roughly twenty minutes of questions, and I expect to lose the room in the questions. I would like a preparation sheet for that question period.

My thesis is that our deadline policy should be replaced by a self-binding commitment contract. Every student signs one at the start of the year specifying a fixed, automatic, published penalty for any late submission, and the administration commits in writing to imposing it without exception and without appeal. My argument is Parfitian. In the hitchhiker case, the stranded traveler is rescued only if the driver can see that he is the sort of person who will pay once they reach town, even though paying then is something he will have no reason to do; the rational move is to become someone for whom keeping that promise is not in question. I argue our school faces the same structure at the level of policy rather than character: discretion feels merciful in each individual case, but a policy whose exceptions are visible and predictable is worth nothing, because the point of a deadline is that it holds precisely at the moment when the student most wants it not to. The distinctive part of my proposal, and the part I most need defended, is that I do not eliminate compassion. I relocate it. The penalty is automatic and imposed by the school in its contracting role; a separately funded hardship grant, decided afterwards by a committee that never sees the enforcement question, compensates the student who had a genuine emergency. Mercy is moved downstream, past the point at which it could have been anticipated.

The panel consists of the assistant principal for discipline, who will care about precedent and about the week a student's parent dies; the school psychologist, who thinks in terms of autonomy, reactance, and whether external sanctions crowd out internal motivation; the AP Psychology teacher, who runs a behavioral economics unit, is the only one who will recognize commitment devices and Ulysses at the mast, and has read enough philosophy to raise Kavka's toxin puzzle against me; the government teacher who coaches our debate team and believes in individualized justice and due process; and a parent representative who practices contract law and will ask whether a minor can bind himself and whether a school can bind itself. Please do not assume they will meet me on Parfit's ground. Four of the five will translate my argument into something else and attack the translation, and I need to know what those translations will be.

Give me the eight objections I will actually receive, ordered by how much damage each could do, and phrase each one the way that particular panelist would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would. For each, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, followed by one sentence telling me where that reply is softest. Then tell me what I should concede in my opening so that I am not forced to concede it later, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive the vote, and the question I am privately hoping nobody asks. I have a candidate — that Parfit's traveler chooses his disposition whereas my students cannot decline the contract, which would turn rational irrationality into ordinary paternalism — but tell me if you think some other question is more dangerous.

For each objection, indicate how confident you are that this panel would actually raise it and how confident you are in the reply you have given, and close by naming the single point in the whole sheet about which you are least sure.
