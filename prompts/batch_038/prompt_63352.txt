ok so I'm president of my school's charity club — 12 of us, we get about $900 a year from student council plus whatever we fundraise, and we've got maybe 200 total volunteer hours across the year. Every October we spend two meetings deciding what to support and it always turns into a fight. Half the club wants the money to go to the animal shelter like ten minutes down the road because we can actually go there and see it, the other half wants to send it to one of those bednet charities because someone showed us a graph about how many more kids it helps per dollar. Last year a guy wanted to spend the whole budget on a prize for an essay contest about AI risk, and that one just died in the room. There's also a student council rule that at least a third of whatever we raise has to be spent on something in our town, which half of us think is dumb but we can't change it, and the treasurer has to approve the final recipient so it can't be some random GoFundMe.

I've read a bit about how the effective-altruism people rank causes — importance, tractability, neglectedness — and I want to actually use it rather than just vibe. So: can you write a procedure my club could run on any single proposal in one 40 minute meeting? I need the questions in the order we'd ask them, and for each one, what counts as a good enough answer from a bunch of high schoolers who can't do research — like, tell me which of these three factors we could realistically estimate even badly, and which ones we'd basically just be inventing numbers for and should treat differently because of that. It also has to deal with the fact that the three factors aren't really independent: I've seen people say a cause is often neglected *because* it isn't important, and that tractability is mostly where people smuggle in their gut feeling and then claim the framework agreed with them. Don't let the procedure just launder whatever we already wanted.

I want it in a form we could actually fill in — a score sheet, thresholds, tiebreakers, and a clear line for where the procedure stops and hands it to a vote because it's about what we care about instead of what's true. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Then run it on three proposals: (1) $900 and 60 hours to the local shelter, (2) $900 to a bednet charity and zero hours, (3) no donation this year, we spend the 200 hours writing letters to state legislators about a factory farming bill. Make sure at least one of them comes out badly — I'd rather watch the procedure tell us something we don't want to hear than confirm what we already planned.

Last thing: tell me what would change your mind about this whole setup. If there's an argument that would make you say "actually don't use importance/tractability/neglectedness, use something else," what is it, and where in the procedure you just wrote are you least sure it holds up — which step would break first if the framework is wrong?
