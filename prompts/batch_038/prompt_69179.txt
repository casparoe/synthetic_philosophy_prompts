Nobody in my social ontology seminar can agree about the coffee pot, and I have been asked to prepare something for our final session that will either settle the disagreement or show why it cannot be settled. The case: for eleven years the graduate students in my department have left the last of the communal coffee in the pot rather than drinking it. Nobody can trace the practice to a decision, a posted notice, or a meeting; new students pick it up within a term by watching and are never told about it; and when someone drinks the pot dry the reaction is a shrug, although the person who brews the replacement pot is reliably the one who took the last cup. Half the seminar says this is a convention in Lewis's sense. A quarter says it cannot be, because nobody in the population could have articulated the alternative that was passed over. The rest say the category is beside the point — that what makes a regularity a norm rather than a habit is what befalls someone who departs from it — and that the coffee pot therefore sits on one continuum with queueing, with the rules of a game, and with grammar.

What I want is a decision tree that routes a described regularity in a group's behavior to a diagnosis of what sort of thing it is. Please build it to the following specifications.

1. Between ten and twelve decision nodes, and between six and eight leaves.
2. Every node must be a question about a described situation that a reader can answer without knowing which leaf the answer leads to. I want questions of this shape: has anyone in the group ever encountered a workable alternative, and could they describe it; is a newcomer corrected, and by whom; what actually happens to someone who departs from the pattern; could an outside observer have written down the regularity without interviewing anyone; would the pattern survive if everyone stopped believing that everyone else followed it. Do not place nodes that ask directly about beliefs or preferences in the abstract.
3. Order the nodes so that those answerable from observable behavior come before those that require attributing mental states. Where the tree is forced to break that order, say so and explain why.
4. The leaves must include at least: a Lewisian convention proper, with common knowledge and a genuinely arbitrary alternative; a pattern sustained by correlated behavior with no shared representation of any alternative, of the kind an evolutionary signaling model generates; a descriptive norm in Bicchieri's sense, resting on empirical expectations alone; a norm resting on expectations of approval and disapproval and backed by sanctions; a constitutive rule that creates a status or a possibility that would not otherwise exist; and plain habit or custom, carrying no normativity at all. Where a leaf has a standard name in the literature, use it; where nobody actually defends the position, label it unnamed.
5. After the tree, list the unoccupied cells of the space — full common knowledge with no arbitrary alternative, sanctions with no coordination problem, a constitutive rule nobody in the group can articulate, and whatever else falls out of your node structure — and say for each whether nobody occupies it because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored.
6. Use these four cases as the material your nodes sort, and report the route each takes through the tree. (a) The coffee pot above. (b) Grain merchants along a river all measure with the same wooden scoop; each learned the measure from two or three partners upstream or downstream, no merchant has ever traveled the length of the route, and no one has any idea whether the practice holds at the far end — yet a stranger carrying a nonstandard scoop is spotted and refused within a single transaction. (c) In an online game players end matches by typing two letters, including against opponents they will never meet again; everyone knows the practice, everyone knows other strings could do the job, and they type it even in matches whose outcome both sides already know; omitting it is read as an insult rather than as nothing at all. (d) In a climbing club, a rope tied off with a particular knot at the gear locker counts as retired and may not be climbed on; the rope is physically identical either way, and a member who can neither tie nor recognize the knot is not merely ignorant of a habit but is excluded from an activity the knot makes possible.
7. For each leaf, state what the position predicts a departure from the pattern will produce in the group — indifference, irritation, sanction, or a change in what the actor is then counted as having done. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate in practice, and say what further question would separate them if there is one.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
