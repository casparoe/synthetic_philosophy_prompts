I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar — 22 students, one 110-minute block, week 9 — and they'll have already read the usual things: Mayr and Dobzhansky on the biological species concept, de Queiroz on the general lineage concept, Ereshefsky's pluralism, and something on Ghiselin and Hull's species-as-individuals thesis. Two of them are biology majors who will happily dominate any conversation the moment data comes up.

What I want to run is a mock statutory listing committee. The class is the committee, it has one sitting to decide whether to protect one lineage or three, and it has to produce a single signed list at the end. Not a summary of the species problem — an actual decison, made under pressure, by people holding incompatible criteria.

The case is invented on purpose, so nobody can look up the answer and the biology majors can't win by having read the paper. Here it is.

Made-up genus, call it Torrentotriton (I invented the name; if it collides with something real, ignore that). A stream-breeding salamander in a fictional range, the Sierra Verano. Four populations: North Ridge (NR), Middle Valley (MV), South Ridge (SR), and a coastal Lowland isolate (LW).

- NR and MV meet in a contact zone about 2 km wide, sampled for 30 years. Lots of F1s, a handful of apparent backcrosses, hybrids consistently rarer than the parental types. One lab crossing trial found hybrid fertility around 60% against 90% in parental crosses.
- SR is cut off from MV by 40 km of dry intermontane valley; no contact, no chance of one soon. mtDNA puts SR nested inside MV, which makes MV paraphyletic. Microsatellites put SR in its own cluster with no intermediates. Three of 22 scored morphological characters are fixed differences. Courtship sequence differs by one step. For calibration: in a neighboring genus in the same range, sympatric sister species pairs differ by an average of four fixed characters.
- LW is separated from MV by a river gorge. 7% COI divergence from MV — much deeper than NR/MV (2%) or MV/SR (1%). But it's morphologically cryptic: zero fixed differences across the 22 characters. It breeds in autumn after the first rains rather than in spring. And there's one specimen from 1974, taken at a low river crossing, that a grad student thinks might be a hybrid; nobody can settle it because the vial label came off.
- Stakes: a highway bypass crosses LW's only known breeding seeps and the permit issues automatically unless LW is listed within 90 days. If SR gets split off as its own species, the petition has to be refiled under a new name, because the 1911 type came from MV, and that costs about two years. A local NGO is lobbying to lump everything into one listed species, since a single wide-ranging name is easier to defend and NR on its own is far too abundant to qualify.

Halfway through, hand out a sealed envelope: a RADseq study in press showing LW's nuclear genome is about as close to MV's as NR's is, so the 7% mitochondrial divergence looks like one ancient capture event — and showing that the autumn breeding phenology stays fixed after two generations of common-garden rearing. I want the twist to break some roles' conclusions and leave others completely untouched, and I want that asymmetry to be the thing the debrief turns on.

Give me: the one-page student briefing document built out of that data, with the ambiguities left in rather than tidied; eight role cards, at least five of which are committee scientists handed a decision criterion written in the flat register of a regulation and NOT told which concept or which author they're holding — I want them to notice for themselves partway in; a chair who has to sign one list; a counsel who only cares whether the decision survives judicial review; and one member who thinks the whole question is metaphysically malformed and still has to vote. Tell me how to write that last card so it doesn't just license someone to be annoying for two hours.

Then the decision rule and the record-of-decision form the chair fills in — show me what the final list looks like under plain majority versus a rule requiring a positive finding under at least two criteria, and say which you'd pick and why. Minute-by-minute timeline including when the envelope goes out. A debrief with the questions in order, the moment the concept names get revealed, and a way to bring Ghiselin and Hull in so students feel that taking species-as-individuals seriously changes the shape of the committee's question (where are this lineage's boundaries in space and time) rather than answering it. Also I want them to argue about whether the NGO's lumping preference is corruption or legitimate.

Plus: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, including the biology-major problem and the whole class retreating into "it's all just a convention," and what I do about each. An honest paragraph on which feature of the debate the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand — I suspect it's going to make species look like whatever committees decide, and I don't want to teach that. And what I cut if I only get 75 minutes.

Everything has to fit on four printed pages total, our dept printer is held together with tape. And don't send me a reading list or references — whatever the students need has to be in the handouts.
