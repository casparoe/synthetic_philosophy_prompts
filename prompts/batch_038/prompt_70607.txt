In a seminar I have been running a variant of the Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster case that Dennett uses against qualia. In my version, both experts have kept daily journals for thirty years, ranking the same ten blends in a vocabulary fixed at the outset, so that drift in their reports can be tracked. Chase's rankings stay exactly where they were while his stated preference inverts; Sanborn's rankings collapse into indiscriminability while his preference stays put. Neither is lying, and neither has any motive to reshape his history.

My colleague's reading: the journals are only further reports, and therefore cannot adjudicate whether what changed was the taste itself or the subject's response to it — which is precisely the lesson, since it shows the distinction does no introspectible work. My reading: the journals settle part of the matter after all. They separate the case in which the discriminatory apparatus changed from the case in which it did not, and if that apparatus is all there is to speak of, the question has been answered rather than dissolved.

Give me your credence that my colleague is right — that a case enriched this far with behavioral and reportorial detail still leaves it genuinely indeterminate whether the taste itself changed or only the response to it. Then decompose the number: how much rides on whether the taste itself, as opposed to the response to it, has determinate application conditions even in principle; how much on whether first-person accessibility is part of what the term qualia is meant to carry; how much on whether journals of this kind add genuine discriminating power or merely more evidence of the same kind; and any further term you think belongs in the factorization. Give a rough probability and a sentence of justification for each, show how they combine, and identify the term to which my own credence — currently about 0.3 — is most sensitive, together with what would have to be true to move it.
