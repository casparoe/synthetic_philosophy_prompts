I never went to university, and I make my living buying and reselling discontinued industrial equipment, so I hope you will forgive anything crude in the way I put this. Over the past year I have been reading about how large a stake to commit to a favorable gamble, and in particular about the rule that says you should choose your fraction by maximizing the long-run growth rate of your capital rather than its expected value. What persuaded me was the claim that this is not a matter of temperament at all. Expected value, the argument goes, averages across outcomes that would happen to many different copies of you living in parallel, while the growth rate averages across what actually happens to the one real you, week after week; and because capital compounds rather than accumulates, only the second kind of average describes an actual life. That seemed to me like correct arithmetic rather than an opinion about risk, and I have been trying to use it.

A concrete situation has come up that has unsettled me, and I cannot say precisely why. A competitor is retiring. He will sell me his remaining block of a certain obsolete part, but only as a sealed lot, and my read of the market is that I have one chance in three of placing it with the buyer I have in mind at four times what I pay, and two chances in three of finding that the parts are scrap and recovering nothing of what I committed. Putting those numbers into the growth-rate calculation tells me to commit about eleven percent of my capital, and tells me that the growth I earn for doing so is on the order of one and three-quarters percent per deal, which is barely distinguishable from doing nothing. My brother, who thinks in more conventional terms, says I am being incoherent in both directions at once: the expected value of the deal is plainly positive and increases steadily with the amount committed, so either I commit everything or I am not really following the arithmetic I claim to be following; and, on the other hand, a one-in-three chance of losing the entire sum is not something a man with a mortgage and two employees should entertain at any size whatsoever.

Here is the difficulty I cannot resolve on my own. This offer will not repeat. It is a single transaction with a single retiring man, and afterward the parts are gone and he is gone, and nothing like it will come my way again. So what, exactly, is the eleven percent the growth rate of? There is no run of weeks over which any rate could be measured. The way I have managed to keep hold of my answer is by telling myself that this deal is really the first of an infinite series of similar deals, so that the long run exists after all — even though I know perfectly well that it does not, and that the series is something I invented in order to keep the rule usable.

I would like to know whether that move is legitimate or whether it is a way of hiding a problem. If the growth-rate rule is genuinely nothing but arithmetic, as I took it to be, then it ought to have something to say about a single non-repeatable transaction, and I want to know what it says and why the answer is not simply the same eleven percent. And if it has nothing to say about such a transaction, then I want to know what I was in fact relying on when I chose eleven percent, because it certainly did not feel as though I was expressing a personal taste for risk — and yet it now seems to me that I must have been expressing something of the sort, or something else equally contestable, without noticing that I was doing it.
