I have recently taken over a small working group at my university's ethics centre — four people, all trained in philosophy, no empirical resources and no fieldwork budget — whose remit is nominally "the epistemology of moral advice." We have three years and, realistically, the capacity to do two or three substantive things well rather than six mediocre ones. Separately, and I mention this only because it has made the topic difficult for me to keep at arm's length: over the past year I made two significant decisions, one about leaving a position and one about ending contact with a member of my family, very largely on the say-so of a single friend whose judgment I trusted. I have since learned that this friend had a considerable undisclosed personal stake in both outcomes. I do not think either decision was wrong, and I am not looking for reassurance about that. What I cannot shake is the question of what, if anything, went wrong in the way I arrived at them — whether the defect lies in having been misled, in not having deliberated, in having trusted the wrong person, or in something subtler about the standing I now have to treat those judgments as my own.

For the group's agenda I have drafted six candidate questions. Please treat them as raw material to be assessed, reshaped, merged, or discarded, not as a menu to be dutifully commented on.

1. **Derivative standing.** Can testimony transmit not only a moral belief but the standing to hold someone to it — to resent, to blame, to demand an explanation, to accept or refuse an apology? If I learn from a third party that someone disparaged me in my absence, is my resentment appropriate, or can I at best note dispassionately that resentment would be fitting? Does the answer differ between a victim and a bystander, and if standing is in some measure non-transferable, is that because of the epistemic route by which the belief arrived, the absence of a felt response, or the absence of the relationship in which the demand would have meaning?

2. **The meta-deference regress.** If relying on another's moral judgment is objectionable in the way the standard deference puzzle suggests, then relying on another's judgment about whether to rely on moral judgment should be objectionable on the same grounds, on pain of arbitrariness. Where does that chain terminate? Does any termination point yield a substantive conclusion, or does the regress indicate that the puzzle's real target is not deference but something else — a demand for moral autonomy, or a particular conception of what it is to have a view at all?

3. **Manufactured understanding.** It is often held that testimony can transmit true belief and perhaps knowledge, but not understanding, and that understanding is what underwrites both moral worth and the flexibility to handle novel cases. What would a case look like in which someone applies moral concepts reliably and flexibly, explains them well to others, and corrects their own errors — and yet has never engaged in first-order moral deliberation about the matter? If such a case is genuinely possible, does the knowledge/understanding distinction do any real work, or is it tracking the effort expended rather than the capacity possessed?

4. **Correlated corruption.** Much of the discussion implicitly assumes a single testifier, or a moral community in rough agreement. What is the correct normative picture when the moral testimony you receive is internally conflicting, when the lines of conflict track your own social position, and when each source testifies that the others are either mistaken or corrupt? Is there any non-question-begging way for the recipient to adjudicate? If not, should the individual-agent framing of the debate be replaced by a structural one, and what would such a replacement have to explain that the individual framing explains well? I suspect this is simultaneously the most important and the least tractable question on my list, and I want to know whether you agree.

5. **Deference as a life-shaped defect.** Can the assessment of a single act of moral deference and the assessment of a sustained pattern of deference across a life come apart — each act defensible, the pattern damning, or vice versa? If they can, which of the two has the debate actually been about, and does the objection that attaches to character rather than to acts have any purchase that the act-level objection lacks?

6. **The testifier's side.** Attention falls overwhelmingly on the recipient. What about the obligations of the person who speaks — to disclose one's stakes, to decline to advise when badly positioned, to resist being installed as an authority, to be legible about the basis of one's own judgment? Is there a distinctive wrong in offering moral testimony, and would the existence of such a wrong change the diagnosis of what went wrong for the recipient? This is the question my own situation most directly raises, which makes me distrust my ranking of it.

Please write as a senior epistemologist who has worked for some years on testimony, disagreement, and the metaphysics of moral judgment, and who is willing to be blunt about which of these will not repay three years of attention. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming pass before you commit to an ordering: note any question I have missed that plainly deserves a slot, identify any of mine that are the same question in different clothes or that dissolve once stated precisely, and say where a case I have offered is doing more work than I realize. Then give me a defended ordering of what the group should work on, and be explicit about the criteria you are trading off — how much other work depends on the question's answer; how tractable it is with purely conceptual and argumentative tools; how *stable* a good answer is likely to be, meaning whether it survives whichever way the disputes about moral realism, the nature of understanding, and metaethics generally turn out; and how much the answer would change what someone in my position should actually do. Rank accordingly rather than treating these criteria as independent boxes to tick, and tell me where they conflict. Flag anything you consider overrated and explain why; I would much rather have two questions with real weight than six with padded ones. If you think one of my candidates has already been substantially worked over, say so and identify the part of it that remains genuinely unexploited — I do not want the group spending a year rediscovering an argument. Finally, tell me what would move your ranking: what sort of result, or what change in the group's composition or remit, would make you reorder things. Please do not include a bibliography or a list of authors to consult, and do not attribute particular views to particular people; describe positions rather than citing them, since what I want from you is the shape of the problems and not the state of the literature.
