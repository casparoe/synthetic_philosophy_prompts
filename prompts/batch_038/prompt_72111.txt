So, quick context: I'm a junior and this year I've got a seat on my university's Campus Dialogue Fund panel — small pot of money, student orgs apply, six of us read the applications and vote. I'm also taking a seminar in moral psychology this term, which is probably why this one application has been rattling around in my head all week instead of getting written up. I'm supposed to circulate an opinion piece to the panel before we vote and I want it to actually be good rather than just vibes. Can you draft it for me?

The application: a student org wants about $4,000 to run four structured conversations this spring about the campus fight over divestment. Their method is the part that matters. Before each event, attendees fill out a short online survey that is basically a knockoff of the moral foundations questionnaire — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation. Then the organizers prepare two versions of each side's opening statement: one leaning on care and fairness, one leaning on loyalty, authority and purity. Each attendee is quietly handed the version matched to their own survey answers. Nobody is told this happened. The handout doesn't mention tailoring, and when people ask, facilitators say everyone got the same packet. Their pilot (38 people, two events last fall) found that attendees who got the matched handout rated the opposing speaker as noticeably more reasonable and were more likely to say they'd shifted their view somewhat, compared to a control group that got a neutral summary of both sides. They also offer to post both handouts side by side on their website after each event.

Their justification in the proposal is its basically Haidt: if people's moral intuitions run on different foundations, then making your case in a foundation the other person doesn't weight isn't a neutral choice, it's just failing to communicate — so tailoring the frame is what turns two monologues into an actual conversation. They insist it can't be manipulation because nothing is added or hidden: same facts, same claims, same argument structure, only which foundation the emphasis lands on.

Two panelists have already dug in and I have to address both.

- One wants to fund it, and wants the memo to go further: she says the whole exercise shows moral foundations theory is right that there's no single correct set of moral concerns, that the left has the narrower palate, and that a dialogue fund should stop pretending otherwise.
- The other wants to reject it. Two reasons: (a) quietly sorting individuals into frames based on a psychometric profile is manipulation regardless of what's in the packet, and (b) if tailored framing can make a true claim more persuasive it can make a false one more persuasive too, and nobody on this panel is going to be checking which framing got stapled to which statistic. He also thinks the premise is wobbly — that liberals score low on the binding foundations partly as an artifact of how the items are worded, and that you can get liberals to endorse loyalty/authority/purity items if you rephrase them.

Our fund's charter says we may only support activities that are "open to all students and transparent in their methods." Nobody on the panel agrees on what that second clause means and honestly I think it's doing most of the work in this case.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. The bottom line has to be clear — fund it, reject it, or fund it with conditions — and if it's conditions, make them conditions we could actually enforce and the org could plausibly accept, with a timeline (we vote Wednesday, their first event is five weeks out). Take the strongest version of the loosing side seriously instead of strawmanning it. And I specifically want it to deal with two things that keep coming up in our email thread: whether "we didn't change the content, only the framing" is a real distinction or a dodge, and whether a descriptive claim about how moral psychology works can carry a normative claim about what we should say to people — that's the move both the org and the first panelist are making, just in opposite directions. No citations or reading list please, it's a short document for a student panel and just needs to stand on its own reasoning. Around 800 words is plenty.
