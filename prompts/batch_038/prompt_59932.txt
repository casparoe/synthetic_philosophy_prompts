I'm a software engineer on a platform team. I have no formal philosophy background beyond what I've read on my own, and I'm writing a design document that has turned into a problem I don't know how to settle.

Our system collects structured votes from a review panel of three engineers on a handful of factual findings about a production incident — things like whether the failing build had actually been deployed, whether the alerting gap predated the change, whether the runbook was followed — and then derives a single recorded decision from those findings using a fixed policy rule implemented in code. The rule is a Boolean function of the findings. Today the service takes the majority verdict on each finding across the three reviewers and then applies the rule to that majority set. A colleague pointed out that this can record a decision a majority of the panel would have voted against had we asked them to vote on the decision itself, and can even record a decision whose supporting set of findings no individual reviewer actually endorsed. I have since learned this pattern has names in the literature, but the document has to stand on its own worked example, because colleagues on this team will try to break it.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specification, one I can drop into the design doc as the exhibit.

1. Three reviewers, one incident, no more than five findings, and a decision rule that is not a plain conjunction of all the findings — it must contain at least one negation or at least one disjunction, so that nobody can wave it away as the textbook version they already know. All three reviewers must accept the same rule; the disagreement has to be purely about the findings, never about the policy.
2. The setting must be realistic and mundane: an internal engineering process. No courts, no science fiction, no stylized life-and-death abstraction. One or two paragraphs of scenario is plenty; the substance should be in the ballots.
3. Give the complete ballot table — each reviewer's verdict on every finding plus the decision that reviewer's own findings imply under the rule — and then compute, showing the arithmetic rather than asserting it: the majority verdict on each finding and the decision the rule derives from it; the decision a majority of the reviewers reach on their own; and confirmation that the two disagree and that the majority finding-set is held by no individual reviewer. If any of those three conditions fails, the case fails and I'd rather you say so than fudge it.
4. Test two fixes people will propose in review, inside this same profile rather than in the abstract: requiring unanimity or some supermajority on each finding before it counts, and re-sequencing the vote so the panel decides the decision first and records findings afterward. For each, say whether it restores agreement here, and if it does not, name the new problem that appears instead — in particular, whether some reviewer can now obtain their preferred decision by misreporting a finding they believe to be false, and if so, which reviewer and which finding.
5. Two of our requirements are in tension and I need to know whether they can both be met. The system must log the findings it relied on, because an internal audit team reads that log and a bare decision is useless to them. Separately, our process requires that the recorded decision match what a majority of the panel would have said if asked directly. Are those jointly satisfiable for an automated rule applied uniformly across hundreds of incidents a month, or does your case show they are not? If they are not, which should give way, and why is that a principled choice rather than an arbitrary one?
6. Finish with the diagnosis: which single feature of your construction is doing the work — the shape of the rule, the odd panel size, the absence of any reviewer holding the majority set, something else — what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed, and how a defender of our current implementation would attack the case. Include whether letting the three reviewers see one another's ballots and argue before voting would dissolve the problem, and what an asynchronous, geographically distributed panel loses if we permit that.

The point of the exhibit is to persuade people who currently think either that aggregating findings is self-evidently right or that we should simply vote on the final decision, so a case that only generates the divergence by stipulating a rule no one would ever write into a policy engine does not serve the purpose. Please don't give me a reading list or citations. If there is a general result that bounds how much of this can be fixed by choosing a cleverer aggregation rule, name it in a sentence or two and tell me what it rules out.
