Bongard problems — the hundred puzzles appended to Mikhail Bongard's 1967 book on pattern recognition, each consisting of twelve line drawings, six on the left and six on the right — ask the solver to state the single concept that separates the two sets. Before I commit myself to any account of how that is done (search over an explicit hypothesis space, abstraction over similarity, program induction, a language model prompted with a textual rendering of the figures), I want the specification rather than the candidates.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of concept induction from a handful of positive and negative instances — Bongard problems as the standing case — would have to meet. Fix the purpose as follows: the theory must both explain how a human solver converges on an answer within a minute or two, with no feedback and no second attempt, and be specific enough to serve as a design brief for a system that handles previously unseen problems without per-problem tuning. If you think those two purposes pull the criteria in different directions, say so, and say which you have privileged wherever they conflict.

For each requirement, give the phenomenon or consideration that motivates it. The list should cover, at a minimum: the gap between a rule that separates the twelve panels and the rule the designer intended, and what therefore makes an answer correct rather than merely consistent; the treatment of incidental variation in the figures, which solvers dismiss as irrelevant but which any statistical account must handle without being told to; the asymmetry whereby a solution is naturally stated in one direction rather than as its complement; the way solvers shift levels of abstraction after a promising low-level rule fails; the fact that differently worded solutions are judged to be the same answer; and the absence of any fixed vocabulary that could be assumed in advance without begging the question. Since the figures cannot be reproduced here, illustrate each point with sketches of your own devising — describe, in words, a few imaginary left-hand and right-hand panels together with the rule that separates them — rather than attempting to recall published problems.

Then separate the non-negotiable requirements from those that are in truth the commitments of one research programme dressed as neutral criteria; I suspect the demand for an enumerable hypothesis space belongs to the second category, but argue it either way. Where some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, demonstrate this and state which requirement must give way — that is the part I most want to see, since such an impossibility would be a finding about the problems themselves rather than about any particular theory. Close by naming the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of otherwise plausible accounts, and explain why. A scored comparison of existing approaches is optional, should be brief, and should come last; the weight belongs on the criteria. Please write in British English.
