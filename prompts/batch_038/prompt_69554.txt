Retired seismologist here — forty years of inverse problems, mostly crustal structure from surface-wave data — and since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy of physics and sitting in on a group that meets Tuesdays in the library basement. Last week we did the hole argument and two members, who I'll call Ravi and Dana, went at each other for the better part of an hour. I honestly couldnt tell who had the better of it and I don't want to just side with whoever talked longer. Here's the exchange as best I remember it, roughly in order:

1. Ravi opened by saying the whole thing is a notational trick. Two diffeomorphic models agree on every number anybody could ever measure, so they're the same world described twice. He said every gauge theory has this — two vector potentials that differ by a gradient describe the same physics, and nobody thinks Maxwell's equations are indeterministic — so at most the hole argument shows Einstein spent three years fretting over bookkeeping.

2. Dana said the analogy hides the important part. In electromagnetism the gauge-related potentials are related at the very same spacetime point; in GR the diffeomorphism moves the fields around relative to the manifold points, so at a given point the two solutions assign genuinely different metrics, and you can arrange the difference to sit entirely inside a vacuum region to the future of some spacelike slice. Both models then match all the data on the slice and disagree about the hole. That isn't automatically one world twice — it's one world twice only if manifold points have no identity apart from the fields living on them, which is exactly what the substantivalist denies. Hence the dilemma: give up substantivalism or live with indeterminism.

3. Ravi said fine, but she can't have that identity. There's no way to pick out a manifold point except as the place where these fields take these values, and both models say the same about that. He said Einstein's own answer was that coordinates carry no physical content, only coincidences — a needle against a dial, two worldlines crossing — so the second horn never gets off the ground, because the "different futures" are the same future.

4. Dana agreed that's the right answer but said notice what it costs. If an event has no identity except through the metric and the matter fields, then spacetime isn't a substance, it's a way of talking about relations among events, and that's a claim about what the world is made of rather than a rule for tidying notation. She said calling that trivial is like calling the fix for the ultraviolet catastrophe a bookkeeping change.

5. Ravi came back with two things. First, that old Kretschmann point — any theory at all can be written in generally covariant form, so covariance can't be a physical principle, and therefore the formal machinery Dana is leaning on can't carry metaphysical weight either; you can always bolt bare manifold points onto a theory as invisible decoration, and dropping an unobservable decoration when the theory says it's redundant is ordinary physics, not a discovery. Second, if diffeomorphism invariance really forces relationalism, Dana owes some job for the manifold in the equations, and it has none.

6. Dana said Kretschmann cuts against Ravi. Sure, anything can be made formally covariant, which is why the content isn't in covariance but in the fact that GR's symmetry is a genuine local gauge redundancy with consequences: constraints, the contracted Bianchi identities, and above all that you can't build a local diffeomorphism-invariant observable — only relational ones, the reading of one clock where two worldlines cross, proper distances between events, holonomies around loops. GR has observables; they just aren't the value of a field at a point. That's what the hole argument taught, and it's why the problem of observables is still a live headache in canonical quantum gravity rather than a quibble about notation.

7. Ravi said then there's nothing left between them: if the two models agree on every invariant, they've stated the same physics, and "spacetime is not a substance" is just a preference in vocabulary.

8. Dana said no, the positions differ about what happens when you change the theory — put in a fixed background metric and the redundancy disappears and points get their identity back, so the claim is about which theories are on the table, not about which words you like.

Then time ran out.

Could you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each one? I don't want a final score by itself — I want to know which particular claims hold and which don't, including whether Ravi's electromagnetic analogy is as bad as Dana says it is, whether the first half of move 5 is really answered by move 6, and whether move 8 is a genuine reply to move 7 or a dodge. Then tell me what both of them missed that would have sharpened or settled it.

One selfish extra. My instinct from forty years of inversion is that this is non-uniqueness wearing a costume: you never get a unique model of the crust from finite data either, and what we did was decide which differences counted as real and which were artifacts of the parameterization. I don't know whether that template helps here or is exactly the wrong one. If it breaks down, say precisely where it breaks, because that's the bit of the arguement I'd have gotten wrong.
