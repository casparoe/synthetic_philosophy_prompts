I retired three years ago after thirty-four in a neuro lab — most of it on cephalopods, a stretch before that on spinal preparations. Since then I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time, slowly, and I've got as far as Descartes on animals (the Discourse, the Passions, bits of the correspondence) plus some commentary. So forgive an amateur's framing.

Here's the thing that won't leave me alone. Every account I've come across treats the beast-machine as a dead end and an embarassment — the thing you mention to show how far we've come. But sitting with it, it looks to me like Descartes built a framework for exactly the phenomena I spent my working life on. An octopus arm severed an hour ago still tastes things, still reaches and grasps. A cat with the forebrain taken out still steps on a moving belt and adjusts when you change the belt speed. A strip of gut in a dish still runs a coordinated peristaltic wave with nothing coming from the head end. In each case you get flexible, apparently purposive, situation-sensitive behaviour out of tissue nobody would call a unified subject of experience. And when someone in my field asked "so is the arm choosing?", the honest answer was that we didn't know and that the question might not be well posed.

As I read the Discourse, his test for telling an automaton from a thinking thing isn't cleverness at all — it's whether one thing can cope with any circumstance it lands in, plus speech. That's a test about unity and generality, not about how impressive the behaviour looks. Which is why I don't think the usual refutation (look how clever they are!) actually touches him.

So: make the case for it. Not a strawman — the strongest version you can build. The position I want argued is that Descartes' automaton doctrine is a live resource for thinking about distributed cognition rather than a relic. Specifically that (a) there's no contradiction in a machine whose parts negotiate with each other, whose behaviour adapts to genuinely novel situations, and which nonetheless has no single seat of awareness anywhere in it, and (b) the feeling that such behaviour must be accompanied by felt experience somewhere is a prejudice carried over from our own case, not a conclusion with grounds behind it.

What I'd like out of you:

- the argument laid out as premises and conclusions wherever you can manage it, so I can see which step is doing the real work;
- what you have to throw out of Descartes to make it stand up. I assume the pineal gland and the clean two-substance split go — but tell me wether the argument survives without them or whether you're quietly keeping some of the machinery;
- where that leaves the animal as a whole. If the arms are machines and the central brain is a machine, does the doctrine give the octopus no mind, many minds, or a mind of a sort Descartes wouldn't have recognised? Pick one and defend it;
- the two best objections faced head-on: that flexible, novel, situation-appropriate behaviour is precisely the mark of a mind, so I'm defining the mark away; and that this is just behaviourism wearing a seventeenth-century coat;
- and say plainly where you think the argument is weakest — not where you'd defend it best.

British English, please. And no reading list or references at the end: I've got more books than years left and I'd rather have your reasoning than your bibliography. Conversational is fine, I'm not marking a thesis.
