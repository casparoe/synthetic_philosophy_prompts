Here's the question. It was worth 20 points and students had about 25 minutes:

Consider the following case. A subject, Dev, has a visual state V caused by a very brief, dim presentation of a red disc. Dev also has an extra neural pathway carrying information from V straight to a higher-order state H — no inference, no observation of Dev's own behaviour or circumstances, no third-party evidence, just a hardwired feed-forward link. But the pathway is crossed: H's content is that Dev is in a state of hearing a middle C. Nothing else in Dev's brain represents the red disc at a higher order, and Dev makes no report.

(a) On Rosenthal's actualist higher-order thought theory, what is it like to be Dev during this episode? Explain your reasoning.

(b) Is the case a counterexample to the theory? In your answer, say what work Rosenthal's requirement that the higher-order thought arise non-inferentially and non-observationally does here.

(c) How would Carruthers's dispositionalist version handle the same case? Be specific about where the two views come apart, if they do.

Here's the student's answer:

---

(a) For Rosenthal, a mental state is conscious not in virtue of any intrinsic property it has, but in virtue of the subject having a higher-order thought about it. So during the episode Dev is having a conscious experience of hearing a middle C, even though no auditory state is present and even though the visual state V is not conscious at all, since nothing is directed at it from a higher order. Dev would be surprised by the tone. This looks like the theory working as intended: experience depends on the content of the higher-order representation, and here that content says tone.

(b) I don't think it's a genuine counterexample, because of the way Rosenthal says the higher-order thought has to be produced. He insists it can't rest on inference or on observation — you can't make your anger conscious by noticing that you're clenching your fists and reasoning that you must be angry, because then the anger isn't conscious in the relevant way. Rosenthal calls this the transitivity principle. The point of the constraint is that the higher-order thought gets its content directly from the first-order state, so H's content has to match V's content. A crossed pathway is just what the constraint excludes; if we stipulate one anyway, we've described something the theory says can't happen, and an impossible case can't refute anything. So the misrepresentation worry is defused at the source. What's left over is only the intuition that consciousness can't be that disconnected from what's actually going on in the head.

(c) Carruther's version is dispositionalist: on his account a first-order state is conscious if it's available to the mindreading faculty, or disposed to cause a higher-order thought, rather than if it actually causes one. So he doesn't need any occurrent H, and V is disposed to be higher-order represented, which makes the dim red disc what Dev is conscious of. On this account Dev consciously sees a red disc and has no auditory experience whatsoever. So the two theories really do diverge about which of Dev's states is conscious. That's an argument for dispositionalism, since the actualist theory forces us to say someone can be having a conscious experience with nothing at all going on in the corresponding modality.
