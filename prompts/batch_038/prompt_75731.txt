I help run a Thursday-night letterpress shop that doubles as a neighborhood makerspace, and once a month we do an "ideas night": 15 to 20 people, 90 minutes, wine and pretzels, no lecture. The crowd is mostly not academic — a couple of retired teachers, some printmakers, a nurse, two high schoolers who come for the snacks — so anything I do has to be something they can *do* rather than read about. This month I want to run the old puzzle about the thing whose parts get replaced one at a time, but built around our actual 1913 platen press, because I think it hits completely differently when the machine in question is standing in the middle of the room and people have ink on their hands from it. Hard constraint: nobody hears the phrase "Ship of Theseus" until the last five minutes, and I don't want to say one word about identity or persistence up front either.

Here's the shape I have in mind, though you should push back on any of it that won't work. Six tables of three, each table acting as a heritage certification board. Before any cases appear, each table writes its own one-sentence standard for when the press is still the press, folds it into an envelope, and it stays sealed until the debrief. Then a deck of about a dozen event cards lands one at a time, and after each one every table has to rule and pay up. I want the opening cards to be our real maintenance history — three replacement gears over the decades, a rebuilt flywheel, a frame casting swapped out after it cracked in '94, the bed re-poured in 2011 — because those literally happened and nobody will smell a thought experiment yet. Then escalate: an apprentice who hoards every removed part in a seperate crate and eventually assembles a working press out of all of them; a modern machine that prints the same newsletter with the same fonts and the same Thursday crew; a fire and an exact rebuild-to-spec; twenty years of gradual conversion until every part is printed or fabricated in-house; a decade where the original sits in storage while the crate-press does all the actual work; and one card that turns entirely on which noun you're tracking — the press, the machine, the shop, the newsletter. Tokens decide the outcome: you lose credibility chips when a ruling contradicts your sealed standard or your own earlier rulings, you gain them when it follows, and the final pot determines which claimant gets the plaque and the insurance money. The trap I want built in is that only one claimant can be certified, so a standard that certifies both — or neither — forfeits the whole pot. Poker chips or bottle caps, no real money involved.

What I need from you: the full card text for each of the dozen events, the token economics with actual numbers so I can sanity-check that a table can't just coast, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, any roles or materials beyond cards and chips, and then the debrief. In the debrief I want each sealed standard read aloud and matched to the position it turns out to be — that's the moment the names get introduced, not before — plus your read on which card reliably splits the room and how to referee a table where two people are each perfectly consistent with their own standard and neither can be shown to be wrong. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do in the moment. And be blunt about which feature of the puzzle a votes-and-tokens format can't reproduce: my worry is that making it a board decision quietly teaches that identity is something we administratively settle rather than something there's a fact about, which may be smuggling in an answer I don't want to hand them. One last thing — two of our regulars actually run that press twice a week and are pretty attached to it, so give me a closing line that doesn't land as "so your press was never real."
