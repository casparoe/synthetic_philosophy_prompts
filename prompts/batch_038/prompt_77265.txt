So, slightly unusual favour. I'm building a neighbourhood comms app for a city council pilot here in the UK, and next Thursday I pitch the design to a panel of three: a political theorist who does deliberative democracy and writes in a broadly Habermasian vein about public spheres; a computational social scientist who runs big-N platform studies and cares about what you can actually measure; and a former regional newspaper editor who now runs a local journalism trust.

The app deliberately strips out most of what counts as engagement. No bylines (posts go out under a pseudonym even the author can't see attached anywhere). No counts — not followers, not likes, not views, not 'how many people read this'. No comment threads or reply chains, and no way to share a post onward. No archive and no search: a post is gone once you've read it, and you can't find out what anyone else made of it. Roughly speaking it's a private conversation between you and a piece of writing, and then nothing.

My pitch is that this isn't austerity for its own sake. I'm going to say it comes out of Kierkegaard's line on the press — that a mass readership manufactures something called 'the public', a phantom abstraction that isn't any actual person, and that once that thing exists people can swap actually existing and taking responsibility for a position for merely having opinions, endlessly, alongside everybody else; that a lot of what looks like discussion is really chatter, in which nobody is answerable for anything because everyone is just talking; and that real communication has to land on one single individual who appropriates it for themselves rather than on an aggregated crowd. So my claim is that a feed built the conventional way reproduces that phantom even at parish-council scale, and the only honest way to build the thing is to make the public technically impossible.

Give me the Q&A prep for that room. The objections these three will actually raise — in their own vocabulary, not a strawman I can knock down and not the ones a design crowd would ask — about eight of them, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the pitch. For each: how they'd phrase it, roughly how long my reply can be, what I should concede straight away, what I should hold onto, and where my reply is soft. Then flag the single question I'm praying nobody asks, and the load-bearing assumption in my own argument that's most likely to snap — I suspect it's the step where I make a 19th-century theological point about inwardness do work for a planning application, but tell me if the real weak spot is somewhere else.

Don't pad it with quotes or page numbers from Kierkegaard; I'm not doing that bit and neither should the answer.

Sections in this order, British English spelling throughout, about 1,100 words total:

**Bottom line** — three sentences on whether the pitch survives that panel.
**Who's actually in the room** — two or three lines per panellist: what they care about and what they'll smell blood on.
**The objections** — the main list, ranked by damage, each as a tight block (their phrasing / reply / concede / hold / soft spot).
**The one I'm hoping nobody asks** — and a sentence on why I can't fully answer it.
**Where my argument snaps** — the load-bearing assumption, ~80 words, plus the honest fallback if it goes.
