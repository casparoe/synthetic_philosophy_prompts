I am facilitating a half-day workshop for an interprofessional audience: internal medicine residents, psychiatric nurses, a public health epidemiologist, a hospital chaplain, and two philosophers of medicine. In rehearsal I ran a show-of-hands question, and almost everyone said that whether a condition counts as a disease is a biological matter settled independently of what anyone thinks about it. Yet when I moved to particular cases, the same people's verdicts tracked social disapproval almost perfectly. I want an instrument that exposes that gap without lecturing anyone about it.

Please write a diagnostic instrument, titled "Which account of disease do you actually hold?", in American English. The requirements are as follows.

**1. Lay out the logical space first.** Before any questions, give me the axes along which accounts of disease genuinely come apart. I want four to six axes, and they must be cross-cutting rather than points on a single naturalism-versus-normativism dial: a respondent should be able to sit high on one axis and low on another without contradiction. Two axes I definitely want. First, whether the account is about the bodily or mental condition itself, about what the person experiences, or about the status their community confers on them — no single question may blur those three. Second, what fixes the reference class against which typical and atypical are measured: species, sex, age cohort, local environment, historical period, or the built environment. State each axis's poles in one sentence apiece.

**2. Populate the space.** For every combination of positions on the axes that corresponds to a view actually defended, name it and give a single clause saying what it claims. For combinations that are empty, say so and diagnose the emptiness in one line: incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not attribute a view to any person unless you are confident they hold it; "unnamed" is better than a guess.

**3. Then the instrument: ten to twelve questions.** Each must be a concrete case rather than an abstraction, with three answer options (four at most), and answerable by someone with no philosophy training in under a minute. Hard constraints on question design:

- No question may ask, in any form, whether some condition is or is not a disease. My audience has heard the standard verdicts on the famous cases and can recite them, so a bare extensional question measures recall rather than commitment. Every question must instead be counterfactual or comparative: what would have to change for the verdict to change, whether the verdict survives a change in attitudes, whether two communities can both be right, what happens to the classification if the environment is rebuilt.
- At least three questions must be capable of receiving the same first-order verdict from two respondents who then diverge on the rest of the question. That divergence is the point.
- No question may name a theory, a philosopher, or a term of art. Furthermore, these words are banned outright from both questions and answer options: natural, normal, normative, value, values, objective, subjective, dysfunction, social construct. Where one of them seems unavoidable, rewrite the question in terms of what happens in the person's body or in the person's life.

**4. Cases I want included, one per question:** a condition that a previous generation of physicians classified as a disease and that was dropped when the surrounding attitudes changed, framed so the question is not whether the old classification was wrong but what the pattern of revision shows; a condition that is statistically typical of the population in which it occurs; an inherited variant that protected against a lethal infection in the environment where it arose and that produces no measurable impairment in the carrier's own life; a condition that limits a person only because of how their world is built and that would limit no one in a differently built world; a condition that measurably shortens life and narrows what the person can do, and that the person nonetheless treasures and does not want removed; a condition whose classification is the only thing standing between a family and a year of paid leave; the same affliction in a grapevine or a dairy cow as in a person, and whether one account gives the same verdict in both; a period of grief after a spouse's death, together with the practice of withholding a diagnosis from the recently bereaved; and one case in which two communities disagree about a condition, where the question is whether they can both be right about it.

**5. Scoring key.** Map answer patterns onto the named positions from step 2, giving a primary position for each coherent pattern. Where a pattern is internally unstable, do not average it into a "hybrid view": identify the specific pair of answers that cannot both be held, explain in one sentence why they cannot, and state the further question the respondent would have to answer to resolve the tension. This is the part I care about most. The instrument should tell a resident that their answers to Q3 and Q7 pull in opposite directions, not that they are a moderate.

**6. Limits.** Close by listing the pairs of positions my instrument cannot separate however the questions are phrased, and for each say why the disagreement is unreachable from inside the case-verdict format. Do not offer additional questions as a remedy.

Practical constraints: the whole thing must be runnable by one facilitator in roughly forty minutes, so keep the questions tight. Write in American English. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no footnote apparatus — participants should never be asked to recognize a name.
