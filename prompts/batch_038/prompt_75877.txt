I work on post-training for reasoning models, and roughly every six months my group has the same argument about process supervision versus outcome supervision. The argument has been running across the field for years, the empirical literature keeps growing, and nobody appears to move. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself — not a survey of results and not a verdict on which side is right.

Let me lay out the two positions as they are actually argued in the room, since the diagnosis has to engage these rather than a strawman.

The process camp says: outcome supervision is the wrong signal for anything we care about. A model can reach a correct final answer by luck, by retrieval of a memorized solution, or by a chain containing a serious error that happens to cancel; rewarding only the terminal outcome reinforces exactly those trajectories, and at scale this shows up as reward hacking, unfaithful reasoning, and brittle generalization. Scoring intermediate steps puts the reward where the failure actually occurs, gives denser credit assignment, and yields a signal a human can audit line by line — which is the property you want if the eventual deployment is high-stakes.

The outcome camp says: the notion of a correct intermediate step is not a feature of the world but an artifact of a labeler's expectations. Human step labels encode one preferred style of solution, penalize unconventional but valid routes, and are prohibitively expensive; process reward models trained on them then hand out confident scores to steps that look right and are wrong, and they are gameable by fluent, well-formatted nonsense. What we actually want is the result. Any intermediate standard is a proxy for it, so it is better to estimate that proxy from outcomes than to import a human's aesthetics about method.

Here is the part I find most telling. Both camps cite the same empirical record and read it in opposite directions. One side points to the result that step-level human labels substantially outperformed outcome supervision when training a verifier on hard competition mathematics. The other points to the comparison in which pure outcome-based supervision reached similar final-answer error rates with far less label supervision, and in which process supervision became necessary only when the metric changed — from final-answer accuracy to the correctness of the reasoning in solutions that got the final answer right. Each side treats the other's headline number as either a confirmation or an irrelevance, and no experiment seems able to settle anything.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than adopt: the impasse is largely an artifact of three distinct contrasts being collapsed under one pair of terms. (i) Where reward is placed along the trajectory — dense versus terminal. (ii) What property is being evaluated — the justification for a step versus the result it produces. (iii) Who does the evaluating — a human reading the trace versus an automatic procedure that scores a step by rolling forward from it and measuring how often the final answer comes out right. On the third axis the distinction arguably collapses outright: a step score defined as the probability of reaching a correct final answer from that step is simply the value function of the outcome reward evaluated at an intermediate state, so what is really being disputed there is an estimation strategy, not a philosophy of supervision. If that is right, much of the argument is two groups answering different questions — one about how to allocate a fixed labeling budget, one about what property of a system we should hold it responsible for — under a single heading.

Diagnose this properly. Which parts of the disagreement are merely verbal, turning on the words process and outcome; which are substantive empirical questions, and what would settle them; which turn on a hidden dispute about the target quantity (final-answer accuracy, faithfulness of the reasoning trace, robustness under distribution shift, resistance to hacking); and which rest on normative commitments neither side argues for — for instance, whether a reward signal must be auditable by a human, or whether a labeler's conception of a good step is a legitimate input to training at all. Say explicitly where my three-axis hypothesis fails, and whether a real, non-verbal core of disagreement survives it. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, an experimental design, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or a reasoned argument that progress should not be expected.

Please write in American English, argue from the structure of the positions rather than from the literature, and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have read the relevant papers and they are not what is in dispute. Aim for roughly 1,200 words.
