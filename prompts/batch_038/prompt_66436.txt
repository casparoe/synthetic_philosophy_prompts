I run a Wednesday-night reading group out of the back room of our town library and we're finishing the Meditations next week — eight of us, mostly retired people plus two nurses who work nights, and the last session is supposed to be a bit of a party. We print a one-page broadside for each meeting and I want this one to carry a poem instead of a summary. So write it as someone who does both things seriously: a working poet who's also spent real time on early modern philosophy, so the verse has to hold up as a poem and not as a lecture chopped into line breaks. Think carefully before you start, and give me two or three sentences of scratch work first, sorting the three readings our group kept circling. One, the inference reading, where the ergo does honest logical work — our complaint was that it needs a smuggled general premise about thinkers existing, which is somehow more doubtful than the conclusion its supposed to support. Two, the performance reading, roughly Hintikka's, where there's no inference at all: the doubting is an act that makes its own denial impossible, and existence in the conclusion means something closer to can't-consistently-be-denied than is-out-there-in-the-world. Three, the deflationary one, Lichtenberg's point that we ought to say it thinks the way we say it lightnings, so all that's been established is that thinking is going on, with no warrant for a continuing thinker underneath it, let alone a substance with a past and a memory.

Now the poem, and this is the part I actually care about. Around 20 to 26 lines, no set rhyme, but here's the hard constraint: the first-person singular is banned from the entire body of it. No I, no me, no my or mine, no myself — it has to run wholly in the impersonal idiom, so doubting happens, wondering happens, a hand gets noticed on a cold table, and there's simply no slot in the grammar for an owner anywhere. Then the final line, and only the final line, is allowed to break the rule and use a first-person singular pronoun. The constraint is meant to do the arguing: the body should make the Lichtenbergian reading feel like the honest default and the inference reading feel like cheating, while that one line at the end marks whatever survives — and it should stay genuinely ambiguous whether the survivor is a substantial self or just the grammatical register where the thought gets written down. Please dont resolve it, and don't name Descartes or any of the philosophers inside the poem.

After the poem, add a short note of maybe five to eight sentences saying which of the three readings it dramatizes, where it takes liberties or leans on a contested interpretation of them, how confident you are that it's faithful to what the cogito actually licenses, and which single line or move you're least sure of and why. No reading list, no citations, no bibliography — I'm not asking for scholarship here, just the verse and that note.
