I am marking scripts for the midterm of a third-year philosophy of mind course of roughly ninety candidates. The paper was set by the course convenor; my mark will be moderated against a second marker who has read only the question and the script, so my comments must justify the mark on their face rather than appeal to what was said in lectures. Candidates had been assigned the section of Douglas Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979) in which the careenium is introduced, and had had a term of lectures on supervenience, mental causation, and Kim's exclusion argument. The rubric printed on the paper told candidates that part (a) would be marked for accuracy of exposition and part (b) for the quality of the argument.

The question, worth 20 of the paper's 100 marks, read as follows:

> Hofstadter asks us to imagine a careenium: a sealed chamber containing an immense number of tiny hard spheres, which he calls simmballs, that bounce off one another and off the walls according to simple deterministic laws. Given the initial position and velocity of every simmball, the entire subsequent history of the chamber is fixed. When sufficiently many simmballs cluster together, the cluster may be regarded as a symbol; clusters collide, merge, split, and cause further clusters to form. Thus at the level of the simmballs everything that happens is a collision governed by physics, while at the level of the symbols one symbol may be said to cause another.
>
> (a) State the objection that this picture is intended to answer, and explain how the picture is supposed to answer it. (8 marks)
>
> (b) Is the answer successful? In particular, does the careenium establish that symbol-level descriptions pick out genuine causes, or only that such descriptions are useful? Justify your position. (12 marks)

The question was drafted to be answerable from its own statement of the thought experiment, so please assess part (a) against that statement rather than against further details of the book.

One candidate's script is reproduced below in full.

> The careenium is Hofstadter's answer to the objection that symbols cannot be causes. The objection runs as follows. Everything that happens in the chamber happens because simmballs collide, and those collisions are fixed by the laws together with the initial positions and velocities, so anyone who knew the physics could derive the whole future of the careenium without ever mentioning a symbol. When we say that one symbol caused another, then, we are only producing a manageable summary of a very large number of collisions: the real causes are all at the bottom, and talk of symbols is bookkeeping that adds nothing to them. This is exactly the worry that mental states are epiphenomenal. If the physics of the brain is complete, then whatever a belief does is already being done by neurons, and to say that the belief caused the action is at best a manner of speaking.
>
> Hofstadter's reply is that the objection proves too much. The symbol level is not a summary we adopt for convenience, because no one could actually carry the summary out: with an immense number of simmballs colliding, no computation available to us could take the initial conditions and return the configuration of the clumps a minute later. The only way to say anything at all about what will happen in the chamber is to notice which clumps are near which, and to expect that a collision between two of them will produce a third. So the symbol-level description is not eliminated by the physical one; it is the description that does the work, and the physical description is the one that cannot be used. That is what it means for symbols to push one another around: the clump really does move other simmballs, and the clump's being a symbol is nothing over and above its being that clump.
>
> This, I think, disposes of the objection. Because a symbol just is a pattern of simmballs, when the symbol pushes other simmballs around the pattern is literally exerting force, and there is therefore genuine downward causation in the careenium: the higher level reaches down and makes the lower level do things it would not otherwise have done. Kim's exclusion argument is defeated, since it assumes that if the physical cause is sufficient the mental cause must be a rival to it; but here the symbol cause and the simmball cause are one and the same event described twice, so there is nothing to exclude and no overdetermination to worry about. The same holds of the brain. A belief is a pattern in neural activity, no one could predict a person's behaviour from the firing of individual neurons, and so beliefs are genuine causes of action. And once it is clear that the pattern level is where the causation lives, it is also clear why a clump means what it does: meaning is just a matter of one pattern reliably setting off others, so the careenium gives us intentionality as well as efficacy.
