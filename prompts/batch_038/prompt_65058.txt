My flatmate and I lost most of a Saturday to this and by the end neither of us could say what we believed, so I'm putting it to you cold. He is a realist about universals of the sparse variety: a red billiard ball and a ripe tomato are both red because they instantiate one and the same universal, and which universals there are is a substantive matter about the world rather than a shadow cast by our predicates — most predicates, on his view, cut no joint at all. I lean the other way: I'd rather explain the whole business in terms of resemblance among particulars and stay quiet about what exists over and above them. What I set out to do was build him a reductio out of materials he already accepts, and it worked far too well. As far as either of us can tell it demolishes his position, demolishes mine, and then demolishes the assumption that there was ever a fact of the matter to disagree about. I suspect a step is rotten but I can't isolate it, and I've talked myself in and out of three different diagnoses since.

Here is the argument, with the objects fixed once and for all: let a be the red ball, b the green one, and c a walrus.

1. For any predicate P, a resembles b in respect of P if and only if a and b are alike with respect to P — that is, either both are P or neither is.
2. For any two objects whatsoever, there are infinitely many predicates in respect of which they are alike. Trivially, everything is such that the ball is red, and everything is self-identical; and from any one such predicate infinitely many more can be generated by disjoining it with predicates that neither object satisfies. Likewise there are infinitely many predicates in respect of which they differ.
3. If resemblance is to explain why some things fall under the same predicate rather than another, it must do so as a relation between the things themselves. A resemblance that holds only relative to a respect we have selected explains nothing, since selecting the respect is already doing the work that the resemblance was supposed to do.
4. So a resembles b, without qualification, just in case there is some respect in which a and b are alike.
5. By (2) and (4), then, a resembles b, a resembles c, and b resembles c.
6. Moreover they resemble one another in the same way, since in each case the resemblance consists in the very same thing: agreement in some respect. There is no further fact that could make a's resemblance to b a different sort of resemblance from a's resemblance to c.
7. The universal that a and b share — redness — is, by (1), just an agreement in respect of a predicate. But a and c also agree in respect of predicates, indeed in infinitely many. Nothing in the account so far makes redness a better candidate for a universal than being such that the ball is red, or than being non-green.
8. Therefore either there is a single universal instantiated by everything, in which case instantiation explains nothing, or the choice among candidate universals is made by something other than resemblance and agreement.
9. The realist says the choice is made by which agreements are genuine property-sharing; the resemblance nominalist says it is made by which resemblances cluster into a class. By (8) neither has said anything: each has merely re-registered the distinction between the good predicates and the bad ones.
10. So there is no fact of the matter about which agreements constitute a kind. Redness does not carve reality any better than walrus-or-ball-hood, and the dispute between realism and nominalism is a dispute about nothing.
11. But there is a fact of the matter. The ball is red and the walrus is not, and any two red things plainly have something in common that the ball and the walrus lack. My flatmate and I do not merely differ in bookkeeping.
12. So the argument cannot be sound — yet I have gone back through it many times and every step still looks true to me, including the ones I most want to deny.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Tell me which step or steps you reject, and — this is the part I keep failing at — why each of them looked perfectly acceptable when I wrote it down, since I was not trying to trick myself. I should say where my loyalties lie, though I don't know if they can be honored: I would give up almost anything to keep (11), and I would also like to keep (1), because (1) seems to me to be nothing more than an unpacking of what "resembles" means and I don't want to be told that I never understood the word. Then price the repairs. If the bad step is dropped or tightened, what does the sparse realist have to say in order not to look like he is simply christening his favorite predicates, and what does the resemblance nominalist have to buy in order for his classes to be more than arbitrary — is there some third option, a theory of tropes, or an appeal to a primitive privileging of certain predicates over others, and what does that primitive cost? Finally, tell me whether the argument can be repaired rather than abandoned: I have a suspicion that some near-cousin of it survives, aimed at anyone who insists that all predicates are on an ontological par, and I'd like to know what that cousin looks like and who it actually threatens.
