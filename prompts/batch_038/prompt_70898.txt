For thirty-eight years I was an experimental physiologist, most of that time in a laboratory that sat awkwardly between the basic sciences and a teaching hospital. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy properly for the first time, and I have become absorbed by a question my own trade never formulated, though it measured its subject matter daily: what exactly makes pain bad? I have read enough now to see that there are at least three rival answers on offer — that the badness lies in the felt character of the experience itself, in a kind of intrinsic unpleasantness; that it lies in what the experience disposes the sufferer to do, the aversion and the struggle to make it stop; and that pain is a perception of bodily damage as damaging, so that its badness is a matter of what it tells you. I confess I cannot yet tell these positions apart cleanly. What has struck me with some force is that the practices I spent my career inside do not merely fail to settle the question: they settle it, in different corners of the same building, in incompatible ways, and never say so.

Let me describe the practice as I know it, and then put my question.

First, on the wards, pain is recorded as a single number from zero to ten, elicited by asking the patient, and governed by a working rule to the effect that pain is whatever the sufferer says it is and is present whenever the sufferer says it is. That number is treated as a vital sign, charted beside pulse, temperature and blood pressure, used to titrate analgesic doses, and, since the late nineteen-nineties, used as an institutional performance measure against which departments are audited.

Second, in the research literature emanating from the very same institutions, one routinely encounters protocols that ask the subject for two separate ratings of the same stimulus — one for intensity, one for unpleasantness — as though these could come apart and as though it mattered which one moves.

Third, the standard word-list questionnaire, whose descriptor groups are scored separately into sensory, affective and evaluative categories, so that a drug or a lesion can be reported as having reduced the affective score while leaving the sensory score untouched. Words like punishing, cruel and sickening are tallied in a different column from words like throbbing, shooting and cramping.

Fourth, for patients who cannot report — the newborn, the demented, and in my own former trade the laboratory animal — a quite different instrument is used: an observational scale that scores facial action units, posture, guarding, activity, consolability, and occasionally heart rate and corticosteroids. Nobody asks; the number is inferred from what the body does. The grimace scale for mice and the behavioural scales for neonates are, in practice, treated as measures of pain in the same sense as the verbal zero-to-ten.

Fifth, chronic pain services increasingly do not try to lower the number at all. They treat a patient reporting seven out of ten and aim at acceptance, at valued activity, at reducing the struggle, measuring success by what the patient does while still in pain.

Sixth, there is the phenomenon that started me on this: pain asymbolia. As I understand the reports — a small and, I gather, contested literature, mostly following insular damage — such patients say they feel pain, localise it, discriminate sharp from dull, and call it pain; but they are unmoved by it, do not withdraw, do not ask for relief, and may consent to its repetition. I have also read that until the nineteen-eighties it was ordinary practice to operate on newborns with muscle paralysis and little or no analgesia, partly on the ground that their pathways were too immature and partly on the ground that they would not remember it.

I should like you to reconstruct the account of pain, and of its badness, that each of these six procedures takes for granted. Which of the three positions I listed is strictly required by a given procedure, and which merely sits comfortably with it? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that the same number would be licensed by theories that disagree about what the number measures? I am particularly exercised by the fourth item: we take the word of the patient who can speak and the grimace of the patient who cannot, and we call both pain scores. Is that a single implicit theory applied to two sources of evidence, or two theories sharing a vocabulary? And what would have to change — in what we elicit, what we titrate to, what counts as a successful treatment, what we do about the stoic post-operative patient who reports two out of ten, and what we do about the asymbolic patient who reports eight and is content — if we replaced the assumption built into the vital-sign practice with the view that pain's badness consists in its aversive grip rather than in its felt intensity? I am not after a survey of the literature, and I have no need of references; I want the reasoning laid out, and I should like to be told plainly where my own description of the practice has smuggled in an answer.
