Been down a rabbit hole on punishment theory this week and my mate forwarded me a piece from some philosophy mag trying to sum up where Dennett and Caruso land on whether anyone ever deserves to be punished. It doesn't name the author or cite anything, so I can't check it against the source, and I can't tell how much of it is load-bearing and how much is confident-sounding decoration. This is the passage that did the work for me:

'Strip out the metaphysics and the disagreement is simple. Both men reject the libertarian picture: a self that stands outside the causal chain and could have done otherwise in some ultimate sense is an illusion, and Dennett has said as much. Where they part company is prisons. Dennett is a compatibilist — determinism threatens nothing, because being responsible just amounts to being the sort of creature whose behaviour can be steered by reasons, and punishment steers it. So he keeps desert: the offender deserves punishment, and that is why we punish him. Caruso, assuming determinism as he does, goes the other way. If nothing is ultimately up to us, nobody deserves anything, and what is left is a public health model: we detain the dangerous the way we quarantine carriers of an infectious disease, and for Caruso blame itself drops out of the picture, since the offender is no more a proper target of resentment than a typhoid carrier is. The difficulty is that the disease analogy cuts against him. We quarantine carriers for exactly as long as they remain a risk, which for a serious violent offender can mean a very long time indeed, so the supposedly humane alternative may end up harsher than the retributivism it was meant to replace. And in practice the two look like the same policy in different vocabulary: Dennett keeps the word desert and Caruso throws it away, but both want dangerous people locked up for as long as necessary, so the argument is really about which words we prefer.'

I came away thinking Dennett is the sensible one and Caruso is doing something sneaky with the disease analogy — which is suspiciously close to what the piece seems to want me to think, so that's probably a reason to distrust my reaction.
