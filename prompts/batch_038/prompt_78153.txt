I am drafting a novel in which a decision theorist, Dr Ilse Marren, has spent her career arguing that civilisations which can never exchange a signal may nonetheless trade, each doing what the other values on the strength of nothing but mutual predictability. I have fixed her recorded rulings in my notes, and I need the next one to follow from them rather than from my convenience as an author. Her rulings so far:

1. Faced with a one-shot dilemma against an exact copy of herself, unreachable by any signal, she holds that cooperation is compulsory rather than merely permitted.
2. Faced with a civilisation that can model her in fine detail but which she cannot model at all — she has only hearsay of its reasoning — she cooperates. Asymmetric access does not, for her, sever the correlation.
3. Offered astronomical stakes on a partner whose decision procedure was drawn at random from an immense space, with some vanishing chance of matching hers, she declines, recording the reason as a refusal to wager on the shape of a stranger's arithmetic.
4. She cooperated with a civilisation whose reasoning she could reconstruct only in outline, where neither side could simulate the other, on the ground that both would converge on the same obvious division of the gains. In the margin she notes that what is correlated here is not code but the geometry of the problem.
5. She treats her present decision as binding upon later versions of herself who will be tempted otherwise.

The scene I cannot yet write: a partner, call it P, arrives with a correlation to her verified beyond any in the list save the copy, and offers gains that dwarf everything she has previously traded for. The price is that she abandon, mid-performance, a bargain already struck with a Q of exactly the case-4 type — weaker correlation, no verification, a claim resting on convergence alone. P's arithmetic plainly dominates Q's. And P knows about Q: P is in effect buying her willingness to break faith with partners of Q's kind.

Predict her ruling. Set out the candidate principles her first five verdicts admit — pairwise correlation, some population-level account of what makes an agent a credible partner at all, and any others you think fit — and say what each implies in this scene. Then commit: what does she do, and why do you prefer that principle to its rivals? Finally, name the further case I might write in which two of those principles come apart most cleanly, and state what my choice in the P scene would oblige her to stand for either way.
