I've been circling constitutional precommitment for a while — the Ulysses-and-the-mast picture Elster made current, plus its macro-policy descendant in the rules-versus-discretion argument for independent central banks. What I want now is a worked case, not the theory recital.

The case is Britain's Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 and its eleven-year life. The skeleton, so you can spend words on analysis rather than reconstruction: for centuries the timing of a general election was effectively a prerogative power exercised on the prime minister's advice, giving the incumbent a well-known advantage in choosing the moment. The Act replaced that with a statutory default date — the first Thursday in May of the fifth year — and allowed an early election by only two routes: a no-confidence motion in the government not reversed within fourteen days, or a motion for an early election carried by two-thirds of all seats in the Commons, vacant seats included. It was introduced by the Liberal Democrat deputy prime minister as part of the 2010 coalition bargain. In 2017 the prime minister got her early election because the opposition supplied the two-thirds. In 2019 the prime minister wanted one desperately and put the motion three times; the opposition refused to supply the supermajority on each occasion. The government then passed an ordinary one-off statute on a simple majority providing for an election on 12 December 2019 — it neither amended nor repealed the Act, it simply legislated around it, and notably fixed the polling date in statute rather than leaving it to the executive. In between, the government had prorogued Parliament for an exceptionally long stretch, and the Supreme Court held that prorogation unlawful. The Act also obliged the prime minister to arrange a review of its own operation in 2020, empowered to recommend repeal or amendment. Both main parties fought the 2019 election pledged to repeal it; it was repealed in March 2022 by a statute reviving the prerogative power and purporting to put its exercise beyond judicial review.

With that on the table:

1. Map the theory onto this carefully. Who is Ulysses, what is the siren song, who is the crew? The natural mapping — the Parliament of 2011 binding its successors against opportunistic election timing — looks to me as if it breaks in at least two places, and I want you to say where.

2. In 2019 the device was defeated not by repeal but by substitution: the outcome it forbade was achieved by a route it did not anticipate, at a much lower legislative cost, while the rule itself stood untouched. Take seriously a fourfold distinction I've been toying with — a precommitment that is revoked, one that is circumvented, one that is idle because the future self's preferences never diverge from the rule (arguably 2017), and one that genuinely holds. Does the standard apparatus have room for these categories, or does it assume a bound/unbound binary? Which category does this Act fall into across its whole life?

3. A constraint liftable at a known, modest price is not removing an option from the choice set so much as taxing it. If precommitment works by price rather than foreclosure, the Act did bind in a weaker sense in 2019: it forced three public defeats, burned parliamentary time, made the government accept a date set in open legislation, and pushed repeal into a manifesto. Is that real binding, and what does the answer do to Ulysses contracts generally? Note the cost: a price-based account seems to abandon exactly what made the metaphor attractive — the point of the ropes was that the bound man cannot act on the temptation even at the height of wanting to.

4. The identity problem. A personal Ulysses contract assumes the man on the beach and the man in the water are one agent, so the earlier self's authority needs no defense beyond prudence. Here the binding legislature and the bound legislature are differently composed, and the settled doctrine is that no Parliament can bind its successors. Whose weakness of will was this guarding against? Is there a coherent collective self available, or does the episode show that constitutional precommitment is really something else — a coordination device, a shifted burden of public justification — wearing the vocabulary of self-control?

5. The crew. Enforcement in the myth is delegated to others under standing orders. In 2019 enforcement appears to migrate to the courts, and in 2022 the constraint's authors attempt to dismiss the enforcer. What does the theory make of a Ulysses who legislates that no one may adjudicate whether he is bound? And under a rule of recognition whose ultimate clause is that the sovereign legislature may always change its mind, does this episode count as an anomaly for precommitment theory, a confirmation of a properly deflated version of it, or a situation the theory never contemplated?

No references or reading list; argue from what you know. Where the theory is silent, say that it is silent rather than pressing it into service. And if a factual detail that matters to your argument is one you're unsure of, flag it and argue conditionally instead of papering over the gap.
