My thesis. Everyone in ML treats Turing's 1950 child-programme idea as a cute anticipaton of RLHF: rather than trying to program the adult mind directly, build something childlike and then put it through a course of education using rewards and punishments. I think that reading is wrong in a way that matters, because that stretch of the paper describes two nested loops and the field has only built one of them.

Loop 1 — a teacher applies reward and punishment to a machine it does not need to understand. The teacher operates entirely at the behavioural interface and doesn't care how the structure underneath got there.

Loop 2 — the designer searches over candidate *child* structures, and here's the part I think everyone skips: Turing frames this with the genetical/evolutionary analogy, where what selection acts on is the initial structure's educability rather than the trained behaviour it eventually produces. He also likens the child's mind to a blank notebook with rather little mechanism in it, which I read as the same point from the other direction — most of the content comes from the education, so what you're actually designing is small, and its whole job is to be teachable.

So the claim: modern practice collapses loop 2 into loop 1. We pick architectures, data mixtures, and hyperparameters on the basis of post-training benchmark performance, which is exactly the measure loop 2 is supposed to be independent of. That's not a historical quibble, it's a live cost — it's a good part of why there's no principled account of which starting structures make the education process converge at all, and why "it trains" and "it's the right structure" have quietly become the same sentence. And it implies a research program nobody's run: search over child structures under selection criteria that are deliberately not final performance (sample efficiency, sensitivity to reward misspecification, how much the teacher has to know, etc.).
