I have worked in vendor management at a mid-sized regional insurer for eleven years, and most of that time has been spent reading other people's contracts. Last quarter we had a dispute with the supplier of our underwriting analysts' decision-support platform. An analyst had two licensed facts in front of him — the internal rule that any policy with two or more claims in a rolling twelve-month period is to be flagged for review, and the record showing that Policy 4471 had two claims in a rolling twelve-month period — and he wrote down the flag himself. The vendor's account team told us that step was out of scope. Their position, so far as I could reconstruct it across three calls, was that being given the inputs and being given the rule does not entitle a customer to produce the output: entitlement to combine them is a distinct feature, sold separately, and an analyst who performs that combination without it is using the platform in an unlicensed manner. I wrote back asking which clause entitled them to move from "you hold the inputs" and "you hold the rule" to "you do not hold the output," and their counsel replied that this was governed by the general construction clause, which we would need to accept by executing an addendum. It took me some time to see why that answer can never terminate. Whatever document confers the entitlement to take a step must itself be accepted before it binds us, and accepting it is itself a step — from what we accepted to what now binds us — which would call for a further document entitling us to treat our acceptance as acceptance. I believe a Victorian mathematician made this point in a short dialogue involving a tortoise; I have never managed to track it down, and I do not want the piece below to refer to it.

What I would like is a parody of the Master Services Agreement for that product — call the vendor Aporia Decision Systems and the platform something plausibly bland — drafted as though it has already been amended nine times, complete with a residual cross-reference or two to clauses that no longer exist. Exactly five numbered clauses, each with its own heading, roughly 130 to 150 words apiece, about 700 words in all, in the register of real corporate drafting: defined terms capitalized on first use, internal cross-references, a signature block folded into the final clause. The five clauses should trace the escalation I have described. The first should define the disputed step as a licensable feature and distinguish it carefully from the mere possession of the inputs and the rule; one of the middle clauses should introduce the addendum mechanism by which entitlements to combine are granted and by which each granted entitlement generates a further one; the fourth should attempt to stop the proliferation by incorporating all present and future addenda by reference and deeming continued use to constitute assent; and the fifth should make visible — without anyone saying so — that the closure has not worked. The Policy 4471 derivation should appear as a defined term or as a reference to an exhibit that is expressly not attached.

Two things matter more than the rest. First, the drafters must not be made to look foolish: each of the five clauses should be a move a careful, competent lawyer would genuinely make, so that the absurdity arises from their composition rather than from anyone's ineptitude — if the joke turns out to be that lawyers are dim, the piece has failed. Second, no winking and no explanation. Please avoid the words logic, valid, validity, inference, premise, deduction, regress, and paradox altogether; do not name Carroll, Achilles, or any reptile; and do not add a prefatory note, a recital, a drafting comment, or an afterword telling the reader what the agreement is doing. A reader who has never heard of the old dialogue should still enjoy it as a EULA, and a reader who has should recognize it in the architecture of the clauses. Output only the five clauses, in order, with no introduction and no conclusion.
