Six months ago I left a perception lab to direct the assessment program in a hospital's disorders-of-consciousness service, and last week the question I had been dodging came up in a family conference. A patient shows a reliable but very slow cortical response to a command to imagine playing tennis — roughly eight hundred milliseconds later than the latency in healthy controls, and without any overt behavior. Her sister asked me whether there is a moment at which her brother becomes aware of the command, and, if there is, why we should think that moment is delayed rather than merely its registration. I said something to the effect that his brain might settle on a content after the fact without there ever having been a first moment of experience to revise. I could see that I had committed myself to something I cannot currently defend, and the two philosophers on our ethics panel told me, unhelpfully, that I had simply picked a side in a thirty-year-old argument. So I want to know which side, exactly, and what I would have to stop saying.

Please build me a decision tree that starts from working commitments about the timing and unity of experience and ends at a named position in the debate over whether consciousness involves a single place-and-time at which contents are settled and made available. Audience: my panel, which includes clinicians and ethicists who have never read the literature and two philosophers who will nitpick everything, so the tree has to stand on its own.

Constraints on the branch nodes. I want six to eight of them, each phrased as a concrete case a reader could run in a lab, observe in a patient, or check by introspection — never as an abstraction about the nature of mind. No node may use the vocabulary of the positions it is sorting between: the words theater, draft, workspace, global, higher-order, specious present, and illusion are banned from the nodes themselves, since otherwise readers will sort themselves by which label they like. For each node, state the case in at most three sentences, give two or three answers, and then, underneath, name the assumption each answer encodes — that annotation is for the philosophers and can use technical language.

The tree must at some point separate all of the following, in whatever order you think is most diagnostic:

1. Whether there is a fact of the matter about the instant at which a given content becomes something the subject experiences, as opposed to only a fact about which content is available at a later instant.
2. Whether the timing and content of a subject's report is evidence about the timing and content of the experience, or is itself just one more process that may or may not line up with the experience.
3. Whether "the content was fixed before it was ever experienced" and "the content was experienced and then the record of it was altered" are two genuinely different histories or only two descriptions of one history that no experiment could tell apart. I want a node here, because my whole answer to the sister turned on it.
4. Whether what a subject has at a moment is one unified content or a set of partly conflicting contents of which some win and some lose without any of them ever having been jointly presented.
5. Whether my own vivid sense that experience is happening right now is privileged data, or is itself a kind of report and therefore inherits whatever problems node 2 raises.
6. Whether this is a dispute that better instruments could settle, a dispute about how we choose to describe a settled set of facts, or both at once — and if you think part of it is merely verbal, the node should force the reader to say which part.

Distribute the following cases across the nodes, using each at the point where it does the most sorting work: the two alternating lights of different colors that observers report as a single moving spot changing color mid-flight; the taps on the forearm that are felt as a smoothly hopping rabbit in the gaps where nothing was touched; a briefly flashed shape made invisible by a mask that follows it, with a forced-choice probe showing better-than-chance performance; the finding that people date the onset of their own intention to a moment later than the neural activity that precedes the movement; a word shown for thirty milliseconds followed immediately by a probe question about it; and, since it is my daily material, the delayed command-following response in a patient who cannot speak.

For the leaves, I want eight to twelve of them. Each leaf gets: its standard name if one exists in the literature, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it; one sentence saying what it implies about whether there is a moment of onset; and one sentence saying what a clinician who holds it is entitled to tell the sister in my scenario. The leaves must include at least the following, plus whatever else your nodes generate: a view on which a finished content is presented at a place and a time; the view on which there are only parallel processes of editing and re-editing with no such presentation; a view on which experience requires a representation of the representation; a view on which the question of when the content was settled has no determinate answer because it is fixed only by which probe eventually comes along; views that treat experience as coming in overlapping extended chunks rather than instants, including both the chunk-is-directly-experienced version and the version on which the just-past is only retained by a momentary present; and a deflationary view on which the phenomenon the tree appears to be sorting does not exist as described.

Then do three things with the space the tree opens up. First, list the combinations of node answers that no live position occupies, and for each say briefly whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and say what extra question would separate them or why none exists. Third, run my own answers through it. My answers, in the order I would give them if asked cold: I think there is a determinate moment at which a content becomes experienced; I think a subject's report can misdescribe when that moment was; I think the difference between fixing a content beforehand and rewriting the record afterward is real and could in principle be measured; I think what the patient has at a moment is one content rather than a field of competitors; I take my own sense of the present moment at face value; and I think the dispute is empirical. If those answers do not correspond to a single path through your tree, tell me which one of them I should give up, and what it costs me to give it up — I suspect at least two of them are in tension and I would rather find out from the tree than from the philosophers.

Format: numbered nodes in tree order with their branches indented beneath them, leaves clearly marked at the end of their paths, the assumption annotations set apart in italics or a sub-line so clinicians can skip them. No reference list, no citations, and no suggestions for further reading — I cannot chase anything down and neither can half my panel. End with a single final line in the form "Verdict: <name of the leaf my answers land on, or 'no consistent path'>".
