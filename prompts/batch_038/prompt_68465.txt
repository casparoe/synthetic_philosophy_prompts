I manage a twelve-acre apple orchard in a river valley, and for the last several springs I have been trying to be more disciplined about the one decision that actually costs me money in April: whether to run the frost machines overnight. Every evening I check a commercial forecasting service that publishes a percentage chance of the air at my elevation dropping below the critical temperature before sunrise. I understand the figure comes from a great many simulated runs of the weather, averaged together, and I have no way of inspecting how they do it. Alongside their number I have three things of my own: a barometer and a thermometer mounted at the top of the north block, a long run of handwritten records of what actually happened on nights following particular sorts of days, and — I will say this plainly, since pretending otherwise would make the question dishonest — an occasional strong feeling on certain evenings that frost is coming, which I can partly trace to the way the light goes off and the stillness that settles after sunset, and partly cannot trace to anything at all.

The difficulty is that these sources disagree, and they disagree in a way I do not know how to weigh. My records suggest that on nights when the service publishes a figure around one in five, frost in fact arrives in my valley roughly one night in two, because cold air drains and pools here in a manner it does not at their nearest reporting point. If I act on their number as given, I lose wood; if I act on my tally, I am acting on perhaps thirty-odd genuinely comparable nights, all of them falling within the eleven years since I began subscribing to the service, and they revised their method four years ago, so I cannot even be sure the tally describes the number I am looking at tonight rather than a number that no longer exists. Worse, I suspect I remember the killing nights more vividly than the mild ones. And then there is the feeling. Some evenings it arrives before I look at the forecast, and on those evenings I have noticed that I find a low figure much easier to dismiss and a high one much easier to accept, which makes me fear the feeling is not independent information about the weather but only my mood dressing itself up as evidence. I cannot rule out, though, that it is my body registering something about the air that no model of theirs has any way of seeing. Two people I respect have given me opposite counsel: the county agent told me to use their figure exactly as published, on the grounds that a well-built number already contains more than I could ever assemble by hand, while a neighbor who farms forty minutes up the ridge says a percentage means nothing unless it is the number I would take a bet at, and that the bet I would actually take on my own land is a different one.

What I would like is help deciding which of these should govern tonight's choice, and in what proportion. I have come to think the three are not the same kind of thing at all — one is a claim about how often an event happens, one is a claim about how often another claim has turned out right, and one is not a claim about frequencies in any sense — and I suspect that this difference in kind matters more than the difference in their reliability, though I cannot make that suspicion precise. So: is there a principled way of settling which of my sources is entitled to override a published figure and which must always defer to it? Does my tally license me to substitute my own one-in-two, or only to nudge theirs upward, and if only to nudge it, what fixes the size of the nudge — the number of comparable nights, the fact that the method behind the figure changed mid-record, or something else? And how should I treat the feeling, given that I cannot tell whether it precedes my reading of the forecast or is manufactured by it? If it helps, rank the three by the weight you think each should carry on a night like this one, and say what would reorder them: more years of records, learning how the service actually computes its figure, discovering that my barometer has been drifting for two seasons, or finding out that the feeling reliably arrives before rather than after I look at the screen.
