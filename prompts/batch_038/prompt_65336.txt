I help put together a training day for people who sit on a regional "enhancement review" panel in a public hospital system — they rule on requests for stuff the system doesn't obviously owe you, and roughly half of them have never heard of the therapy/enhancement debate and the other half have very firm intuitions they can't articulate. My sister-in-law is on the panel and she keeps saying "I'm not against enhancement, I just think it should be for medical reasons," and I could not for the life of me tell her what that sentence commits her to. So I want to build the panel a sorting quiz: "which position on enhancement do you actually hold?" Ten to twelve questions, each one a concrete case with two to four answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. Nothing should ask about labels and nothing shoud use loaded words like "natural," "playing God," or "cheating" — the panel members shouldn't be able to tell which leaf an answer leads to, because the whole point is to sort them by what they say about cases rather than what they call themselves. And I want the instrument, not the literature: names can appear in passing in the key, but no reading list, no bibliography.

Cases I need in there, add others as you see fit: a stimulant prescribed to a kid with no diagnosis who wants to get into a selective music conservatory; growth hormone for a boy in the first percentile for height who is otherwise healthy; a cochlear implant for a deaf child whose parents are Deaf and don't think their daughter is missing anything; a drug that makes four hours of sleep fully restorative — treatment for the sleep-deprived or enhancement for everyone else?; a vaccine that as a side effect leaves people with above-average baseline immunity; a pill that lifts someone whose mood is statistically normal but persistently low; reconstructive surgery after a mastectomy sitting next to cosmetic augmentation of the same size; an exoskeleton for a warehouse picker versus for a soldier; and a drug that makes people measurably more cooperative and honest, which I suspect blows up whatever tree you build, because it targets a moral capacity rather than a health capacity.

For the tree itself: the first split should be about what does the sorting work — whether the relevant baseline is species-typical function, the aim of whoever is intervening, the social baseline for taking part in normal life, expected change in welfare, or nothing at all. Then the leaves, at minimum: the normal-function view, the welfarist account, the social-constructivist/positional view where enhancements are arms races for positional goods, the view that the distinction is real but drawn by purpose rather than by effect, the eliminativist or deflationary view, the giftedness/species-ethics objection that doesn't care about function but about who designed whom, the capability-approach view, and the pro-enhancement position on which we can have a duty to enhance. Attach the usual label or a standard defender's name where one exists, and where a cell is genuinely unoccupied say so and diagnose why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. I'd bet at least one combination nobody defends is sitting right there in your space, like someone who thinks the distinction matters morally but denies there's any fact of the matter about normal function. Three more things: one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment, i.e. the case it handles worst; flag any pair of leaves my quiz can't separate, and either hand me a question that would separate them or explain why no case could; and build in one consistency trap — the same intervention described twice, once as restoring a loss and once as exceeding the norm — with a note in the key for what to say to someone who answers it inconsistently, since I want to be able to tell them "you're holding two views at once" instead of just pinning a label on them.
