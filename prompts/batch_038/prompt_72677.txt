For thirty-four years I was an experimental petrologist, and a large part of my working life consisted of building, breaking and mending instruments. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with rather more seriousness than my family thinks advisable. I write to you, however, in a practical capacity.

I sit on the register panel of a small charitable trust that lists historic scientific instruments in this part of the country. When a listed instrument is altered, rebuilt, lost, dismantled or replaced, its owner or custodian writes to us, and we rule on one of three things: the entry survives, the entry lapses, or a new entry is required. In eleven years we have issued some forty such rulings, and we have never written down the principle behind them. The six that bear on the matter now before us:

1. The 1858 refractor at the county observatory. Over a century of maintenance every component was renewed in rotation — tube, clock drive, eyepieces, and finally the object glass itself in 1974 — always one at a time, never out of service for more than a fortnight. Entry retained.
2. A surveying theodolite of 1811, gutted in a fire and reduced to its signed brass limb and its original telescope; rebuilt around those surviving parts with new work by the maker's successor firm; never used in the field again, but displayed continuously in the same survey office. Entry retained.
3. A marine chronometer of 1902 whose entire movement was replaced in a single operation in 1968, the original box, bowl and gimbals being kept; it remained in daily service aboard the same training vessel until 1984. Entry retained.
4. A sextant lost overboard in 1911 and replaced within the month by an identical instrument from the same workshop, same pattern, next serial number, catalogued under the same entry and used continuously for fifty years. The substitution came to light only in 1998. We held that the entry lapsed in 1911 and that the substituted instrument had never been on the register.
5. A barograph of 1880 that stood dismantled in crates in a damp loft from 1921 to 1994, was then reassembled with some nine-tenths of its original parts, and was set working again in a different building thirty miles away. Entry retained.
6. In 2009 a donor offered us both a dismantled original and a working reproduction made to the original drawings by the same firm in the 1930s. We accepted both objects under two new numbers and expressly declined to say which, if either, answered to the older entry, on the ground that that entry had already lapsed when the instrument passed out of our custody. The question was never reached.

The case now before us is the first that has defeated us. A longcase regulator of 1793 stood in the corridor of the old observatory from 1795 onward. In 1940 its movement was removed for safekeeping to a bank vault and the empty case left in the corridor. In 1947 the observatory workshop fitted the case with a new movement to the original specification, and that clock has run in the corridor ever since, wound daily by the same staff, carried in every catalogue under the same number, and described on our own label by the words of the 1793 entry — an instrument standing in the corridor and regulating the other clocks. Meanwhile the original movement lay in the vault until 2022, when we withdrew it, cleaned it, gave it a new dial and a new period-style case from a local cabinetmaker, and set it running in our study collection. Both clocks now keep good time; both are in our custody. The county, which owns the observatory building, asks which of them is the registered instrument of 1793, and whether the entry should be retained for one of them, split into two, or declared lapsed.

I should like the following, and I should like it treated as I would treat a data set rather than as an occasion for exegesis.

Which of our six rulings is the closest analog to the corridor clock, which to the vault clock, and why the others are less close? I notice that rulings 2 and 3 seem to point in opposite directions here, and I want to know whether that is an illusion.

What principles are consistent with all six verdicts, and which are ruled out by the record, and by which ruling? I suspect that more than one survives, and that they have never had to be separated because in every case we have actually decided there was only one candidate before us.

What should we decide? Please commit: name the clock, or tell us the entry is dead, and say what principle we would be adopting for the next such case in doing so. Take seriously the possibility that neither clock is the registered instrument and the possibility that both are, and tell me what would have to be true for either of those to be more than an evasion of the question.

Does the wording of the 1793 entry carry any weight at all, or does it merely record what was true of the clock when it was written?

Finally, specify one further case that a claimant might plausibly bring us within a few years and that would separate whichever two of the surviving principles your recommendation leaves alive — a case, that is, on which they would give different answers, so that we could find out which one we actually hold.

I am told there is a standard name for puzzles of this shape and a large literature attached to it. Please do not send me any of it, and no references: a panel that must issue a written decision by the end of the month cannot use a survey. What I do want is a definite answer with its reasons attached, not a balanced menu from which I am to choose for myself. And since I spent my career separating what an experiment shows from what it merely fails to exclude, I ask one further thing: mark clearly the places, if there are any, where our record licenses no inference at all and you are making a choice rather than reading one off.
