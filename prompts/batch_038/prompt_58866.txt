A friend and I argued for most of yesterday evening about whether the Löbian obstacle is a genuine constraint on the design of agents that must evaluate and then defer to their own successors, or whether it is a philosopher's artifact. As I understand the obstacle, a theory capable of reasoning about its own proofs cannot establish in general that its proofs are reliable without already having established the particular conclusions those proofs deliver; self-trust is therefore not something that can be had wholesale, in advance, and applied case by case. Her position was that this bites only if one insists the parent agent demonstrate its successor's reliability deductively, and that a working system would instead attach a high degree of confidence to its own reasoning and update that confidence on evidence about its own performance. Mine was that this substitution of confidence for proof does not escape the circularity but conceals it, and that there are designs in which the agent's evidence about its reliability is manufactured by the very faculty whose reliability is in question, leaving the confidence internally impeccable and externally worthless. I did not convince her, and I am not certain my examples were good enough to convince me.

The design I want to examine is this. Call it the self-audit loop. A planning agent reasons within a fixed background theory and, before committing to an irreversible action, generates a batch of candidate judgments about the action's consequences. It then re-examines a randomly selected subset of those judgments using a second pass of the same procedure — different search order, different decomposition of the problem, but the same underlying inference rules and the same representation of the world. The proportion of the subset on which the two passes agree is recorded as the agent's estimate of its reliability for judgments of that kind, and that estimate is what licenses the action. There is no external oracle, no human reviewer, and no feedback from the environment: agreement is the only signal available. The claim on its behalf is that, because agreement is measured on a fresh sample each cycle, the estimate is statistically honest, and the agent therefore enjoys precisely the self-trust a proof-theoretic account would demand without being required to prove anything.

Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises for this design — one concrete case, worked through in enough detail that I could place it in front of my friend and have her recognize it as a genuine difficulty rather than a restatement of my general worry. I have three requirements.

First, the case must be specific: identify a kind of judgment the agent makes, describe the structure of the error the two passes share, and explain why the agreement rate nonetheless comes out high.

Second, it must be a case in which auditing more does not help. Her obvious reply is that the loop is merely a sampling procedure, and that any sampling procedure can be improved by drawing a better sample or by diversifying the second pass; I want an example in which enlarging the audited subset, or running additional passes that differ only in surface heuristics, monotonically increases the agent's confidence in a judgment that is false.

Third, and this is the part I care most about, the example must make contact with the original obstacle rather than reducing to a familiar complaint about unrepresentative samples or about unreliable inputs. I want an account of why the agent cannot formulate the hypothesis that its auditor shares its blind spots without thereby presupposing something it is in no position to establish, and I want that account to make clear what survives of the deductive worry once the machinery has been made probabilistic. If your view is that some residual self-trust remains available, say so and mark its scope; I would rather be handed a definite limit than a rhetorical victory.
