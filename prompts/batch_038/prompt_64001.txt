So, quick context, because I think it matters for what I'm asking. I spent about 38 years as a physical chemist, most of it fitting models to noisy spectroscopy data, which means a big chunk of my working life was spent arguing with colleagues about priors. In my lab, if you and I came out with different numbers, the normal move was to rerun the whole analysis using the other person's prior and see whether the answer actually moved. If it moved, we'd argue about the data. If it didn't, we'd say so and go get coffee. Nobody ever thought the disagreement itself was evidence that one of us was defective.

Retired two years ago, started reading philosophy and bits of economics for fun, and I've now run into this thing called the common prior assumption — Aumann's result that if two people share a prior and it's common knowledge what each of them now believes, then those beliefs can't differ, so any leftover disagreement has to come from a difference in information, or from somebody not updating properly. I find it genuinely interesting and also, to my eye, slightly deranged, and I want to see if the funny version makes that clearer than an essay would.

Can you write me a satirical piece, maybe 700 words, in the form of the paperwork from an inpatient clinic that treats lingering disagreement as a medical condition? Structured like something out of a hospital forms binder: intake questionnaire with checkboxes, an attending's progress note, a differential diagnosis with made-up codes, a short consultation note from another clinician, and a discharge summary with a signature line. Deadpan institutional voice throughout, never winking at the reader.

The stuff I'd like actually in there, because I think it's where the jokes live:

- The clinic's mission statement or motto should encode the assumption itself — something to the effect that differences in belief are only ever differences in information, so a disagreement that survives pooling the evidence is by definition a symptom and not an opinion. Nobody at the clinic treats this as a policy choice.
- The workup should be technically right. They screen first for whether the patient and the other party really did have different evidence; if so, that's the benign, physiological kind, and the patient gets reassured and sent home. What makes a case pathological is that the two parties' current beliefs were common knowledge between them and still differed. Then the attending should reason, formally and completely straight-faced, that this is impossible, so there must be a charting error somewhere, and the chart gets amended until the numbers agree.
- Differential diagnosis as separate conditions: failure to conditionalize (double-counting the same evidence, or ignoring evidence you were handed), withholding private information, refusing to treat the other person's stated belief as evidence about the world, and the one the attending is most worried about — the patient insisting their prior is theirs.
- A section where "sensitivity analysis" is listed as a self-harm behavior and a relapse trigger: patient caught rerunning the same argument with a roommate's prior substituted for their own, to see if the conclusion held. Note the contraindications.
- A referral note on a patient who wants to place a bet with his brother about something they've both studied. The attending should conclude that if their priors were really shared and the evidence pooled, no bet both of them would accept could exist, so the desire to bet is itself the finding. Do not explain that reasoning to the reader, just chart it.
- A short consult from an old-school personalist clinician — invent her — who argues that priors aren't beliefs at all, they're dispositions, and you can't diagnose a disease of a disposition. The attending's reply should be bureaucratic and dismissive, and should inadvertently reveal that the clinic's own intake form asks every patient to declare a prior before they've been given any evidence about the case. Nobody at the clinic notices that this is a problem.
- A notice about the common-knowledge ward, where patients are held until their posteriors converge, including one who has been in there for six weeks because he and his roommate each know that the other knows that the other knows, and the discharge criterion keeps failing for a reason the nursing staff can't articulate.

Two things I care about most. First, be fair: exaggerate something the assumption really does — it rules out genuinely personal probabilities, it does a lot of load-bearing work in models of trading and bargaining and speculation, it turns a modeling convenience into a claim about people — rather than inventing a strawman, because I want the people who know this literature to laugh rather than wince, and I want the ones who don't to still get a coherent story about a very strange hospital. Second, no citations, no bibliography, no real paper titles and no real names anywhere in the piece — invent the clinicians, the codes, the institution. And please don't tack on a paragraph at the end explaining what the satire means. I'll know.
