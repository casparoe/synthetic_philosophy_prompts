I'm drafting a chapter on takeoff speeds and I keep coming back to Flowers for Algernon, which I know sounds like a stretch. Charlie's curve is documented report by report, there's a crossover point where he starts beating Algernon at the maze, he blows past the people who made him, and then he works out from Algernon's collapse that his own gain is going to revert. So he's simultaneously the exploding intelligence and the safety researcher studying it, with no standing at all with the people actually running the experiment. My colleague calls this the algernon-gordon objection to hard takeoff: capabilities installed from outside a system's own optimization loop don't compound, and they decay. Is that anything more than relabeling the no-recursive-self-improvement point? Where does the book back it up and where does it fall apart — the operation is a one shot thing done to him, so is there any recursion in there to speak of? And how much weight should the fine grained inside view of the progress report carry for what a takeoff feels like from the inside, which is exactly what the timelines people never get at. Also, tell me if the framing itself is objectionable. Casting Charlie as a proto-superintelligence seems to do to him precisely what Nemur does, which is treat him as a specimen. I'd rather have that argued than assumed.
