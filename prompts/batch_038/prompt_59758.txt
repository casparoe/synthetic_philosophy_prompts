I am a doctoral candidate in a Sanskrit philosophy program, and for the past year I have kept a register of how my adviser rules on disputed knowledge claims in our Nyāya reading group. He is a systematic thinker who resists multiplying the list of pramāṇas, and I have tried to record his verdicts in the idiom he uses rather than in mine. I would like you to predict his ruling on the case I have to present next week, and to be explicit about the reasoning that gets you there.

The register so far:

1. Fire on the hill inferred from smoke: inference, no dispute.
2. The man who is told in the village that a gavaya is like a cow, goes into the forest, sees the animal, and thereby learns what the word picks out: comparison (upamāna), and neither testimony nor inference, because the instrumental cause here is a cognition of similarity yoked to the memory of a description.
3. The cognition that there is no pot on the bare floor: he refuses a separate means of non-apprehension and treats this as an inference from the floor as perceived.
4. Devadatta is not at home, is heavy, and does not eat by day, so he must eat at night: he refuses postulation (arthāpatti) as a distinct means and reduces the case to inference.
5. Recognition — this is the very man I saw yesterday: not a distinct means; it is perception plus memory, and whoever makes it a further faculty is counting the contents of cognitions rather than their causes.
6. One's own pleasure: inner perception (mānasa-pratyakṣa), not a private faculty of its own.
7. An elder who has himself walked the road reports that it is passable: the hearer knows, by testimony.
8. The same elder honestly and carefully repeats what a walker told him about a road he has not walked, and the report is true: the hearer still knows. He said that the second mouth does not spoil a report so long as it is a mouth that understands.
9. A fluent reciter who repeats a passage he neither understands nor accepts, the passage being true: the hearer does not know. He said that in such a case the words carry nothing.
10. A child who has never been told the meaning of a word but has watched adults use it consistently in the presence of the thing, and then applies it correctly: knowledge, and by testimony — the adults' usage is a declaration even though no one was addressing the child.

Two standing remarks of his bear on what follows. First, he holds that a means is counted distinct only when it has an instrumental cause of its own, which is why he defends his school's four against the Mīmāṃsakas' six. Second, he has remarked that his school's insistence on an author for the Veda, against those who call it authorless, exists precisely so that there is a testifier whose competence can be assessed; and he holds that the truth of a cognition is not self-guaranteeing but is ascertained, when it is ascertained, by further means — while denying that an untested true cognition is for that reason not knowledge.

The new case: a manuscript is found in a monastery giving a precise description of the rites performed at a particular shrine. Its author died two centuries ago, and the manuscript was lost until now, so there is no chain of copyists and transmitters to inspect; nothing stands between the author and the reader but the paper. Nothing else is known about the author from any source, and he cannot be cross-examined. Stipulate that he was in fact honest, that he attended the rites himself, and that his description is accurate. Stipulate also that the reader has no independent evidence of any of this, and can point only to the internal coherence and specificity of the text. Does the reader know, and if so by which means?

What I want from you:

(i) Your best prediction of his verdict — whether the reader knows and by which pramāṇa — with a rough degree of confidence, together with the single entry in the register that is the closest analog and an account of where that analogy strains.

(ii) The candidate principles that would accommodate entries 7 through 10 while remaining consistent with 1 through 6. Do not stop at the first principle that occurs to you: at least three should survive the register. For each, say which entries it fits naturally and which it must strain or reinterpret, and say which entry or entries verdict 10 is doing the most work to accommodate. Consider, among other axes, whether the operative constraint concerns the instrumental cause of the hearer's cognition, the competence of the person from whom the words come, or the history of the content those words carry.

(iii) What each surviving principle predicts about the manuscript case, and your reasons for preferring one over the others, drawing on his two standing remarks. Address explicitly whether his stated rationale for requiring an author of the Veda cuts for or against admitting testimony from a text whose author cannot be assessed, since in both situations the testifier is out of reach.

(iv) One further case, describable in the same idiom rather than as a modernization, that would discriminate between the two principles you take most seriously; state how each would rule on it and why.

(v) If the register genuinely underdetermines the manuscript case, what he would be committing himself to with each possible ruling: which further claims he could no longer hold, and whether any of his reductions in entries 3 through 5 would come under pressure.

Finally, tell me which way I should argue the case if I want the system he ends up with to be the most defensible one available to him, and what that recommendation would cost him elsewhere in the register. I am not asking for citations or for the texts in which these positions are debated: work from the framework as you know it and reason from the register.
