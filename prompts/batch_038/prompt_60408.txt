For several years I have run a graduate seminar on higher-order evidence, and I have come to think the literature is less a debate that has been settled than one that has stopped generating movement. The positions are all on the table and all are well understood by everyone in the room. There is the camp that treats higher-order evidence as genuinely defeating: if you are told by someone you trust that your reasoning has been compromised — that you were given a drug that quietly deranges arithmetic, that you have been awake for thirty hours — you ought to lower confidence in conclusions your first-order evidence still supports, and Christensen's version of this is candid about how strange the consequences look. There are the level-splitters, Coates and Lasonen-Aarnio most prominently, who hold that a person can be fully rational in each of two attitudes — confidence that p, and the belief that her confidence that p was produced badly — even though the pair is flatly incoherent, and that enkratic requirements therefore fail in general. There is Worsnip's proposal that what looks like a contradiction is really a conflict between two kinds of assessment, evidence-responsiveness and inter-level coherence, so that a single state can be both structurally irrational and justified without anyone having said anything false. And there is Horowitz's Non-Akrasia Constraint, on which ideally rational agents never find themselves in the akratic situation at all, which relocates the whole dispute to the question of what norms govern non-ideal agents and whether the relevant standard is maximal rationality or rationality relative to the evidence one happens to have.

The seminar has spent two years on a case of my own construction, and I mention it because its fate seems diagnostic in itself. Marta is a proof-checker of unusual reliability. A colleague who has watched her work for a decade, and whom she trusts completely, tells her that she has a blind spot: whenever a proof contains a step that is valid only in the finite case, she fails to notice it, and she has failed to notice it repeatedly. The colleague cannot say which proofs were affected and kept no record. Marta re-examines her past acceptances and finds nothing amiss in any of them; there is no particular judgment she can single out as suspect, and no amount of further reflection would let her single one out, since the flaw is by hypothesis invisible from inside. The higher-order evidence here is not misleading — it may well be true — but it is unactionable at the level of individual judgments, and there is no other level at which it could be acted on, because the colleague's report is the only route to it. Every camp absorbs this case without strain and delivers its own verdict: the defeat camp distributes doubt across the whole class, the level-splitters say Marta may rationally retain each confidence while believing the faculty unreliable, the two-norms camp says both descriptions are correct and non-competing, and the idealization camp says the case concerns a non-ideal agent and asks which norms apply to her. Nothing anyone says surprises anyone else. What strikes me is that each side also accuses the others of equivocating on rational — of assessing a state in isolation where the others assess an agent's management of herself, or of demanding joint satisfiability where the others never demanded it — and those accusations look to me perfectly symmetric, which is itself an odd feature for a disagreement to have.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a survey and not a verdict on who is right. Take these hypotheses seriously and tell me which, if any, is correct: that the dispute is verbal in a specific sense, the camps answering different questions under one word — what an attitude's status is given a total evidence package, versus what an agent's overall self-management merits; that it is substantive but about the formal properties of the norms rather than about the cases, turning on whether rational requirements must agglomerate, whether a requirement that is in principle unactionable is a requirement at all, and whether ought-implies-can reasoning imported from the theory of obligation has any business constraining epistemic norms; that it is downstream of an unexamined choice about how higher-order evidence is to be represented — as evidence for a proposition about one's own past performance, as evidence about what one's evidence supports, or as evidence about a standing disposition — with each camp's intuitions tracking its implicit representation, which is my own working hypothesis; that it conceals an empirical question about whether self-directed reliability information of Marta's kind can be used by a real agent at all; or that the question as posed is ill-posed and the stalemate is a symptom rather than a difficulty. Argue the diagnosis from what each camp actually claims and is committed to, separate the genuinely substantive disagreements from the cross-talk, and tell me explicitly whether the symmetry of the equivocation accusations is evidence for any of these hypotheses. Then say what would count as progress: a distinction, a proof — perhaps an impossibility result showing that no revision rule for higher-order evidence can satisfy both a level-coherence condition and an evidentialist one, or a fixed-point result about self-referential reliability information — a reformulation, or a change of subject; and if your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, explain why the impasse is stable rather than merely slow. Finally, and bluntly: could any new case, however carefully built, discriminate between these camps, given that none of ours has? Please characterize the positions by their content and use names only where they genuinely help; I am not looking for a bibliography.
