For a seminar on religion and public justification I have built an argument that I now suspect proves too much, and I cannot locate where the excess comes from. Begin with a stipulation: call a reason *auditable* when any citizen, whatever else she believes, can check by an ordinary route whether it supports a proposed law — where "ordinary" means a route that does not require her first to adopt the worldview from which the reason comes.

(1) Coercive law is legitimate only if it is supported by reasons that are auditable, in this sense, for each of the citizens it binds.
(2) A citizen who does not share a religious faith cannot check premises drawn from it without adopting it: the texts, the tradition, and the experiences that warrant those premises are available only from inside.
(3) So religious premises are not auditable, and by (1) they cannot render a law legitimate.
(4) But (2) generalizes. Secular moral realists offer premises warranted by intuitions the sceptic does not share; Rawlsians offer premises about justice warranted by a method the holder of a comprehensive doctrine must first accept; and legislators routinely offer premises warranted by expert modelling no ordinary citizen can actually check. In each case, verifying the reason requires standing inside the framework that produces it.
(5) So none of these is auditable either, and by (1) none can render a law legitimate.
(6) Yet a state obviously must be able to legislate on the strength of expert consensus about, say, epidemiology or climate, and a liberalism that forbids this is one no one could live under.
(7) Therefore (1) is false, and there is no principled basis for excluding religious reasons while admitting everything else.

I want to keep (6), and I would like to keep the conclusion of (3) if I can. Something in the passage from (4) to (7) feels like a cheat, but I cannot tell whether it is a cheat or simply the premise I was reaching for all along. Which step should I reject? Explain why it looked unobjectionable as I wrote it down, and if the repair is to revise (1) or the stipulation, say what the revised version still manages to rule out — and whether it rules out enough to deserve the name of a constraint.
