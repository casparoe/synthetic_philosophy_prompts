I'm writing a piece on why economists and philosophers can't agree about the social discount rate, and I want to hand readers a decision tree that sorts them onto a position rather than a label. Build it from the shared setup — a climate policy imposes costs on people alive now in exchange for damages avoided a century from now — and run it through eight to ten branching nodes ending in these leaves: pure time preference as an ethical defect that should be set at or near zero; positive pure time preference justified by the chance that there will be no one left to receive the future's benefits; uncertainty about future growth that makes the rate fall as the horizon lengthens; the descriptive view that we should simply read the rate off observed market returns; and the view that the whole calculation is a political decision dressed up as arithmetic. Add any leaf you think the logical space requires and I've omitted.

My readers are smart but have never taken welfare economics, so every node must be answerable from a described case, with no formulas and no Greek letters, and no node may simply ask which of the positions above the reader prefers. Useful candidates include whether the sheer distance of the future counts against it once we've already granted that future people will be better off, and whether a measure worth adopting over a fifty-year horizon can lose its justification over two hundred.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and mark any combination of answers the space permits but nobody defends, with one line on whether that region is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it. Please write in American English.
