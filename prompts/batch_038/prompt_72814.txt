I'm on the grants committee of a mid-sized foundation that funds programs to get more young women into engineering and computing. Two years ago the board adopted a standing rule that our money can only remove barriers — it can't be spent changing what people want. The rule came out of a fight about a career-counseling program we were told was steering girls away from nursing and toward tech.

Now there's a proposal that runs head on into that rule. It's a two-country trial of a school outreach program: same curriculum delivered in Norway and in Turkey, measuring women's enrollment in engineering four years out. The curriculum's main lever is messaging — presenting engineering as socially useful, team-based, and adjacent to caring work — because the stuff our staff dug up keeps finding that gendered career choices actually get stronger in more gender-equal countries, not weaker. The PI's argument is that in a place like Norway, where the legal and financial barriers are mostly gone, messaging is the only lever left, and a foundation that refuses to touch preferences has written off exactly the countries where the gap is most stubborn.

Half the committee says the rule is clear and we should decline. The other half says the rule was aimed at counselors steering individual kids, not at a randomized trial whose whole point is to find out whether steering works at all.

We vote on the 14th. Draft the memo I'll circulate ahead of that: a recommendation (fund, decline, or fund with conditions specific enough to actually write into a grant agreement), the reasoning for it, and a serious engagement with the strongest case for whichever way you don't go. I'd also like it to take a position on what the pattern itself implies — if choices really are more gendered where equality is higher, does that make them freer choices or just better-hidden influences — since that decides whether our rule is protecting anything at all. And tell me what we'd need to see in the trial design to know the rule isn't just being evaded under a nicer name. American English, please, and short enough that a busy board reads it in ten minutes.
