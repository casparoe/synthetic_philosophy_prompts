I am a student in my penultimate year of secondary school, and I convene a self-run reading group of eighteen pupils, aged sixteen to eighteen, which has spent the year on the prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation. Between us we have covered the two computer tournaments and the popular account of them, the properties a robust strategy is said to need (nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear), the folk theorem as our faculty adviser presented it, the backward-induction argument that a known final round unravels cooperation, and a handful of animal-behaviour cases offered as evidence of reciprocity. I am now writing the paper for our end-of-year assessment. My fellow students are comfortable with expected-value arithmetic and with conditional probability at the level of a tree diagram; none has taken calculus, and none should be asked for a formal proof.

I want one essay question, to be answered in sixty minutes and marked out of twenty-five, built on a variant of the iterated dilemma that I have designed myself and that I am fairly confident appears nowhere in the material we have read. Here is the variant, which I have been calling the audit round.

Two players meet indefinitely. After each round there is a further round with probability w = 0.9, and this is common knowledge. Stage payoffs are the usual ones: temptation 5, reward 3, punishment 1, sucker 0. Monitoring is imperfect and private: at the start of every round after the first, each player separately receives a report of the other player's action in the previous round. Each report is correct with probability 0.95 and reversed with probability 0.05, independently across rounds and across players, so the two may be working from incompatible records. Before choosing, a player may pay an audit cost of 1 point, deducted from that round's payoff, to be told with certainty what the other player actually did in the previous round. Audit decisions are simultaneous, private, and unobservable: the partner never learns whether you audited, and no record of audits survives into later rounds. The first round has no history, and no audit is available in it.

Three things I suspect make this variant interesting, though I may well be wrong about all three, and I would rather you tell me so than write the question around a mistake:

- The value of an audit seems to depend on how surprising the report is, and therefore on the player's prior expectations about the partner, not merely on the error rate; a report of defection from someone you take to be a cooperator is likelier to be a lie than the same report from someone you already distrust.
- The auditor pays the whole cost, while the benefit of avoiding a retaliatory spiral is shared by both players, so audits should be systematically under-bought relative to what the pair would jointly want.
- Because audits are invisible, they cannot function as costly signals of good faith, which is exactly what a visible audit would be able to do.

Assess each of these claims against the specification as I have written it. Say plainly which survive, which need qualification, and which are false, and build the question around whatever survives. If some feature of my rules makes one of the parts trivial, incoherent, or unsolvable without mathematics my students do not have, say so first and propose the smallest repair that keeps the idea intact.

The question itself should have three parts: one asking under what conditions a player should buy the audit; one asking why the audit decision is a collective-action problem in its own right and what that predicts about how often audits will be used; and one asking how, given only the sequence of choices both players made and not the reports they received, an observer could tell whether a collapse into mutual defection was driven by temptation or by misperception, and what each diagnosis would imply about whether more auditing would have helped.

Please deliver four things:

1. The question exactly as it will appear on the paper, restating the rules in full, since students will not see my description above. The paper is closed-book, so no part may require naming a researcher, citing a study, or recalling any fact that is not contained in the question itself.
2. A model answer of about 800 words, pitched at the mathematical level I described.
3. A marking scheme totalling twenty-five marks, distributed across the three parts, that names the specific moves a top answer must make rather than describing it in general terms. For each must-hit point, give the marks it carries and a single line naming the characteristic way students get it wrong.
4. A short paragraph saying what an answer that competently recites the tournament lore would look like, at which mark it would cap out, and why it cannot go higher.
