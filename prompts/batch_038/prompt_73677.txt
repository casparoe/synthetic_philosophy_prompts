I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. For the past year I've run a monthly, self-organized discussion group at my company for colleagues who want to think about how organizations allocate decision rights. This month's topic is federalism and subsidiarity, which I arrived at through arguments about platform teams versus autonomous product teams and then found the political version. Sixteen participants — mostly engineers, plus two product managers and a designer — sixty minutes, a conference room with a whiteboard and a printer. Nobody has studied political theory and nobody will do reading beforehand.

I want an activity, not a talk. Specifically, a game in which the participants run into the problem of allocating decision authority across levels, and discover by playing both why "decide at the lowest level that can handle it" feels compelling and why it never tells them where the line goes. The word subsidiarity should not be spoken until the debrief.

Structural requirements I'd like the design to meet:

- Three or four decision-making units of deliberately unequal size, so that pooling them into a single majority body has predictable winners and losers.
- Two public goods, at least one of which produces spillovers across unit boundaries, and unit-level costs that are private information: each unit knows its own cost structure, while the center sees only what units report or how they vote.
- At least three rounds that change the allocation of authority — decentralized, then centralized, then some negotiated arrangement the players write themselves — with authority transferred in round two not recoverable in round three without a supermajority.
- Scoring in points only. No money, no candy. Points, plus one non-material stake that the losing unit would genuinely dislike.

Please give me roles and materials, exact rules for each round, the point tables, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto specific features of the idea. Two things matter especially. First, treat the payoff scheme like code: include a worked playthrough with real numbers so I can verify that the intended experience is what the arithmetic actually produces, that no unit has a dominant strategy of always centralize or never centralize, and tell me which parameters to tune if the group solves it too fast. Second, at some point a participant should lose local control over something they care about, and their complaint should sound like a grievance about legitimacy — we had no say — rather than about a mispriced externality. If your design cannot reliably produce that complaint, say so plainly, because that distinction is what I most want the group to feel, and I'd rather know the game only supports the efficiency reading than find out in the room.

In the debrief, note where the mechanics correspond to real federations, but keep this at the level of institutional patterns rather than legal specifics; I don't want anyone reciting article numbers or dates. Also address the problem of who gets to decide who decides, which round three throws open and never settles. If that problem has an established name, give it, but don't make the activity depend on knowing the name.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the phase that runs long, the table that optimizes the scoreboard instead of playing, the person who asks whether this is just a tragedy of the commons with extra steps — and how to handle each. And be frank about which feature of subsidiarity the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand.
