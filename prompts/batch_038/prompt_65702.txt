I am preparing a session for our hospital's ethics consultation service, and the resident educators have settled on an image I find myself unable to leave alone. The image is this: the body is an orchestra, and the brain is its conductor. When the conductor collapses, the players do not instantly fall silent — the strings go on bowing, the percussion keeps a pulse — but there is no longer a performance, only a set of simultaneous soundings that nothing is holding together. The ventilator, on this telling, is something like a metronome left ticking on the podium: it maintains a tempo, and a tempo is not a symphony. Hence, we are assured, the patient on the bed is not a living human being whose brain has been destroyed; the living human being, the integrated whole, ceased at the moment the baton fell.

The image is doing at least three distinct pieces of work, and I want each audited separately rather than the whole thing either blessed or dismissed. First, it is turned against those who would locate the death of the person in the cortex alone: on the orchestral picture the brainstem is no mere stagehand but part of the conducting, since it is what keeps the sections in time with one another. Second, it is turned against families who insist that a warm chest that rises, an incision that closes, an infection that is resisted, and — in the longer-term cases the literature reports — a body that goes on growing, cannot be a corpse. Third, and least explicitly, it is what permits a team to proceed to organ recovery without the sense that anyone has been killed: the death is already in the past tense.

What I should like is a proper audit, not an impression. Begin with the mapping set out explicitly — a table is welcome — assigning each element of the orchestra its counterpart: conductor, sections, the individual players, the score, the music, the hall, the audience, the metronome, the tuning before the downbeat. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the intended uses from those that are merely decorative, and identify what features of the source the picture quietly imports that the target may lack: unity imposed from above rather than achieved from within; parts that remain fully alive and unimpaired when the whole is said to have perished; a unity that consists in a performance rather than in a substance; a conductor who is external to the ensemble and could in principle be replaced, or his part played from a recording.

Next, put the analogy to the discriminations it is supposed to license. Can it tell whole-brain death apart from the persistent vegetative state? Can it tell the irreversible loss of integrative function from loss that is merely unmanifest at the moment of testing, as under anaesthesia, profound hypothermia, or drug intoxication? And a further question that two of my colleagues have answered in opposite directions while agreeing on the analogy itself: taken at face value, with every player still alive and still playing, does the picture support the conclusion it is invoked for, or the conclusion that a distributed and locally self-sustaining whole has survived the loss of what was only its modulator? Tell me whose deployment is the illicit one, if either is, or whether the analogy is simply silent on the matter and both are smuggling their premises in from elsewhere.

Finish with a verdict on each of the three uses — which, if any, survive once the disanalogies are on the table — and then propose a better analogy, if there is one, and subject your own proposal to the same tests you have just applied. I should particularly like your view on whether any analogy in this neighbourhood can be neutral with respect to the substantive question that decides the case: what it is that makes an organism one thing rather than a collection of things.
