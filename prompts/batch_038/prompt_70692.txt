For the past nine years I have been the conservator responsible for the fabric and fittings of a working parish church, and last winter I lost a dispute that I find myself relitigating almost weekly. Our tenor bell — eighteenth-century in origin, but itself recast in 1888 from the metal of a cracked predecessor — developed a fracture. The bellfounder's recommendation was to melt it down and recast: roughly sixty per cent of the recovered metal went back into a new tenor for the same tower, hung in the same frame, bearing the same inscription and the same donor's name; the remaining forty per cent was cast into a set of twelve handbells, sold to fund the work. Meanwhile the second bell in the tower is a 1990s replacement, cast elsewhere from entirely new metal, which nonetheless carries the medieval dedication and rings in the same slot in the peal. Within a month I had three bodies telling me incompatible things about which object the parish's historic bell now is: the listing authority, whose criteria are material and which downgraded the entry; the diocesan liturgy committee, whose criteria are functional and which insists nothing of consequence changed; and the insurer's valuers, who require one object, one number, and no double-counting against the same schedule. I ended up signing a provenance certificate I do not believe. That experience is the reason I teach this material, and I want my students to arrive at the philosophy through the same vertigo rather than through a lecture about it.

The setting: a single 90-minute session with sixteen first-year students on an MA in heritage conservation. None of them has studied philosophy; all of them have strong, mostly unexamined intuitions about authenticity, and all of them will shortly be writing provenance statements for a living. What I want them to feel is the cluster of problems around material constitution — the lump and the statue it constitutes, identity as sortal-dependent, the argument from differing modal properties, and the conventionalist reply that there is no fact of the matter beyond our stipulations. They should not hear any of that vocabulary until the debrief.

Please design the activity in full. I would like it to use my case, or a close analogue of your own devising, with these features built into the rules rather than announced:

- Participants are sorted into the three bodies above — listing authority, diocesan committee, insurer's valuers — each with its own written remit, its own admissible evidence, and its own required output.
- Each body issues a signed certificate naming the bell that is the historic original, and the certificates are then read aloud against one another. There must be a genuine scarcity mechanism, using tokens or a paper budget rather than money, such that inconsistent certificates cost something the participants care about.
- Partway through, a revelation arrives that changes the case: I want you to write it. Consider, for instance, that some fraction of the metal was lost as dross in the furnace so that no surviving object contains all of it, or that the 1888 recast means the participants have been arguing about the wrong ancestor. Choose what will do the most damage to the positions they have already committed to.
- The facilitator keeps a silent ledger recording, for every verdict, the criterion the participant actually invoked. The debrief should turn on reading that ledger back to the room and showing them the pairs of commitments they cannot jointly hold.

Please also give me: a materials list, printable certificate text, the remits, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact debrief sequence that maps specific moments in the room onto specific moves in the philosophy, and a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to steer it back.

Two further things I want you to be frank about. First: whether what your activity actually stages is synchronic coincidence — two objects in the same place at the same time — or diachronic persistence through replacement of parts, since my case is arguably the latter masquerading as the former; if your design can only deliver one, say which, and say what I would have to add to get the other. Second: which feature of the target view the activity structurally cannot reproduce, and that I as facilitator will have to supply by assertion.

Tell me how confident you are that the intended conflict will actually surface in the room rather than being talked away by a confident student in minute twenty, and single out the one element of your design you are least sure about.

End your response with a single line of exactly the form "Rigged toward: X", where X is one of: constitution without identity; four-dimensionalism/perdurance; sortal relativity of identity; conventionalism about identity; mereological nihilism; diachronic persistence only (no synchronic coincidence staged).
