I'm a staff engineer on the team that owns the refusal and safety policy for a consumer AI assistant; my background is distributed systems, so please forgive whatever I get wrong in framing this. For two quarters we've been arguing about how much friction the assistant should insert when a user asks for something that looks self-defeating: drafting an incendiary email at two in the morning, step-by-step help adjusting a medication schedule, a home wiring job where the user asserts the breaker is off without giving any reason to think they verified it. I proposed a lightweight second-look interstitial for a narrow slice of those cases, plus a factual correction when a request rests on a premise the assistant has good reason to believe is false. A senior colleague kills both proposals with a Latin tort maxim, *volenti non fit injuria* — to one who consents, no wrong is done — which he encountered somewhere and now deploys at every design review. His argument, so far as I can follow it: the user is an adult, they typed the request themselves, they know what they are asking for, and the moment we add a warning or a check-in we are substituting the company's judgment for theirs, which is precisely what separates paternalism from mere care. I looked the maxim up just enough to see that it is a doctrine about consent as a defense in negligence cases, which does not resemble our situation at all, but I could not get past the feeling that it is doing more work in his mouth than it can bear, and I lack the vocabulary to say exactly where it slips.

So: what are the distinct, precise claims that one maxim could be making? I would like them separated rather than blended — for instance, whether consent is supposed to render an act permissible or only to waive the consenting party's claim afterward; whether the thesis is about law, about morality, or about both; whose wrong is in question, the consenting party's or a third party's; what conditions must hold before anything counts as consent at all, such as knowledge of the relevant facts, competence, absence of coercion or compulsion, specificity to this act rather than blanket agreement, and contemporaneity with the act; and whether consenting to a risk is the same thing as consenting to the harm that materializes out of it. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive thesis that could be argued for, or simply false — and, this being the part I actually need, whether that reading licenses his conclusion that the assistant should honor any explicit request from an adult user without interstitials or unsolicited correction.

Two features of our case strike me as breaking the maxim, though I cannot articulate why. First, the consent in question is one line of text typed into a box, addressed to a system whose defaults, tone, and willingness to push back were all chosen by my employer rather than by the user, and it arrives bundled with a terms-of-service agreement nobody reads; does that satisfy whatever standard the maxim presupposes, and does the answer change if the user is distressed, sleep-deprived, factually mistaken, or acting under a compulsion? Second, several of the requests we handle do not stop at the user — assisting them imposes costs on people who consented to nothing. I would also like to know what to concede: is there a defensible version of the maxim with genuine force against over-refusal, so that I am not attacking a strawman of my colleague? Please lay the readings out as a numbered list, each with a verdict and a one-line flag for whether it supports his use of the phrase, and close with a short paragraph identifying which reading he is really relying on and what I should say at the design review. No citations or reading lists, since I have no way to chase them down; I just want the analysis.
