So. I've got a problem and it's a weird one, and I want you to actually think it through rather than tell me to journal more or trust myself or whatever.

I'm a novelist, four books in, and a magazine has commisioned a long essay about how I work — the process piece, you know the genre. Easy money, I thought, because I've kept a work journal for something like fifteen years. Thousands of pages of me explaining to myself what I'm doing and why.

Except when I went back to mine it, its a mess of self-contradiction. Three examples:

- Before each book I write an entry confidently laying out what the book is going to be about. I have never once written the book I announced. And every time, a month in, there's a new entry explaining that actually this was the plan all along and the earlier version was just scaffolding. I believe that when I write it. I probably believed it last week too.
- In interviews over the years I've given at least four different accounts of why I wrote my second novel. Each one felt completely honest when I gave it. They don't line up. A reporter with a free afternoon could stitch them together and make me look like a liar, and my only defense would be that I meant every word.
- My wife says that when I announce I'm "stuck on structure," what's actually happening is that I'm avoiding one particular scene. She's right. Every time. And I cannot feel the avoiding happening. What I feel is a genuine, vivid, entirely intellectual frustration about shape and sequence.

Here's what stops this from being a simple case of *you're making it up as you go, so give up*. Some of my reports on myself seem to work. When I read a page and it instantly feels wrong — not a reasoned judgment, a gut thing, I couldn't tell you why — I flag it, and my editor almost always ends up agreeing, sometimes after an argument. And when I say "I'm anxious," that feels like the one thing I can't be wrong about. Except last spring I was cheerful and easygoing for ten days while my mother was in hospital, and I told everyone, and myself, that work was going great. My sister said I was terrified. She was right, and I found out she was right only afterwards, from my own behavior.

So which is it? Instant feel about a sentence: trustworthy. Instant feel about my own reasons: garbage. Instant feel about my own emotions: sometimes the most reliable thing I have and sometimes the least. From the inside all three look like the same kind of act. All three are immediate, none of them involve working anything out, and all three come with full confidence — and I've noticed my confidence is useless as a signal, because the reports I'm proudest of are the ones my wife or my editor can puncture in a sentence.

What I want: tell me whether there's a principled line running through these cases, or whether I ought to treat every report I make about myself as equally suspect. And then the practical part, because there's a deadline — can I write this essay at all? If the only instrument I have for checking how I feel about something is feeling it again, I'm going in a circle, so what do I actually do: mine the journals, or throw them out and build the piece from the drafts and their dates and what other people say happened? Is there a way to write honestly about a process you can't watch yourself having? And a smaller question underneath all of it — is there any real difference between inventing an explanation after the fact and the ordinary thing everyone does when they say why they did something, or am I just doing what everyone does, only with better receipts?

American English please, the magazine's in New York. And no reading list — I want the argument, not the bibliography.
