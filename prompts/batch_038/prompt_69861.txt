I teach political philosophy and I have a side gig that's eating my life. For about six years I've chaired a standing review committee for our state commission on public decision-making. The setup: whenever an agency or a court administrator wants to change how a group reaches a verdict — jury size, voting thresholds, licensing boards, parole panels, citizen assemblies, whatever — the proposal comes to us and we write a two-page recommendation on whether the change is defensible *on accuracy grounds*, i.e. whether the new arrangement is likelier to get it right than the old one. We get six or eight a year and I sign every one. My committee is me, two grad students (one logic, one ethics, neither does math), and a staff attorney. Ninety minutes per proposal, no access to data about our own jurisdiction's panels, and the commission votes on our recommendation without reading the appendix. So whatever we produce has to be runnable by non-specialists, fast.

Almost everything that lands on our desk gets argued for or against with Condorcet's jury theorem — usually badly, usually by someone who read about it in a newspaper column. "More people = more likely to be right, so don't shrink the jury." "Unanimity is just a high threshold and the theorem says high thresholds are more reliable, so keep it." "A hundred assembly members is obviously better than seven." I want to stop this.

So, acting as an expert on formal epistemology and collective decision theory who has also spent real time explaining this to non-experts, please build me a decision procedure — something my committee can literally work down a page — for evaluating a proposed change to a group decision rule on the basis of the jury theorem and its limits. Think carefully before writing anything. Start with a paragraph of brainstorming: list every way the theorem's conditions can fail in a real jury or board, deliberately including failure modes that *look like improvements from the inside*. Then tell me which of those a small staff with no data access and ninety minutes can actually detect, and cut the undetectable ones out of the procedure, saying out loud that you cut them and what we lose by cutting them.

Then the procedure itself: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what each answer licenses, and what a red flag looks like in a proposal document. For each step, say which condition of the theorem it's testing. Be explicit about:

- which distinctions absolutely have to survive being dumbed down for my grad students and which I'm allowed to drop. Off the top of my head I think we need to keep (a) the difference between jurors not talking to each other and jurors being independent in the technical sense, and (b) the difference between "this rule is reliable in advance" and "given that this particular panel voted this way, the verdict is probably right." Tell me if I'm wrong about either one, or if something else is more load-bearing.
- where the procedure stops flat with "not licensed — the theorem gives you nothing here," and where it hands the question up to the legislature because it's about values rather than accuracy. We are not permitted to set the acceptable ratio of wrongful convictions to wrongful acquittals; that's above our pay grade and it needs to be visibly above our pay grade in the document.
- what the procedure cannot do at all, phrased so plainly I can paste it into a footnote.

Then run it on three proposals actually in front of us this term, showing your work step by step:

1. Shrink criminal trial juries from twelve to eight, and replace unanimity with a requirement of at least seven of the eight.
2. A citizen assembly of a hundred randomly selected residents, all of whom receive the same two-hundred-page expert briefing packet and the same three days of testimony, voting yes/no on whether a water treatment plant should go at site A or site B.
3. The one I'll hate: letting jurors discuss the evidence as a group during the trial, in the judge's presence, instead of being instructed to withhold judgment until the close of evidence. I've testified for this twice and I still think it's right — the group catches things individuals miss, and it stops people from locking in early. I want the procedure to be able to tell me I'm overconfident, and I want it told in the terms I'd have to repeat to the commission.

If for any of the three the honest verdict is "we can't tell from here," say that, and say what evidence would settle it and who'd have to collect it.

Keep it to something I could hand out on paper. Bold the step headings. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I don't care where a claim comes from, I care that it holds up and that my grad students can apply it without me in the room. And if you're occassionally unsure about a step, mark it as a judgment call rather than dressing it up as procedure; half my job is knowing which parts of our recommendations are really just me having a view.
