I am in my final year at a public high school in the American Midwest, and I serve as president of our Civics and Government Society, which meets for fifty-five minutes each Thursday with roughly twenty members between the ages of fourteen and eighteen. This year our student council election had three serious candidates, and the winner was the candidate a clear majority of us would have ranked second-to-last; people have been arguing ever since about whether we chose badly or voted badly. Our faculty adviser has given me one session — fifty-five minutes, the same twenty students, no preparation on their part — to settle that argument properly, and I want to run a game rather than give a talk.

Please design the complete activity. I want my members to manipulate an election themselves, profit from it, and understand only afterwards that no rule we could have picked would have stopped them. Specifically:

- Give me a fixed deck of role cards dealing out private preferences over exactly three options, with the number of each card specified, plus the resulting preference profile in a table that only I see. The options should be things my members genuinely care about — a spring trip, a fund-raiser, a service project, your choice — and the payoff scheme must attach to the outcome rather than to honesty, without using money.
- The same deck must produce a voter with a strict incentive to misreport under plurality, under instant-runoff, and under Borda count. Show your arithmetic for each case: the sincere ballot, the strategic ballot, the change in outcome. If no single profile can do all three, use as few profiles as possible and say which rule needed its own.
- The voter with the incentive must not be told to use it. Build the information structure so that misreporting is simply their own best play, and tell me what to do in the likely event that nobody misreports in the first round — without my saying the word "lie."
- Include one further round using an escape hatch, such as an approval-style ballot or a lottery, arranged so that the members notice on their own which assumption the new rule gives up, and why giving it up is not the same as being safe from manipulation.
- Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, the exact text of the ballot cards, and instructions for who counts and how to get counting done in under three minutes.
- Then the debrief: a sequence of questions leading the room to state in its own words an informal version of the impossibility result together with every one of its hypotheses — a single winner, at least three options, deterministic, every option winnable for some profile, no dictator — and to match each escape hatch to the hypothesis it abandons. Withhold the name of the theorem and of the people who proved it until the very end, and keep every question answerable from what the members just did rather than from anything they were told.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — counting errors, the reluctant manipulator, someone blurting the answer, the round where a second voter copies the first and cancels the gain, time overrunning — and how to handle each.
- Finally, be frank about which features of the result the room cannot produce. My members will see that manipulation pays under the rules we tried; they will not see that it is unavoidable for every non-dictatorial rule. Tell me what I must supply in words to cover that gap, and what I should not claim on the strength of a game.

No lecture beforehand, no formulas on the board beyond a preference table, and no bibliography — I need something I can run on Thursday, not something to read.
