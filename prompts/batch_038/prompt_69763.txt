Been marking modal logic essays for a week and I've realized my own intuitions about which worlds there are are a mess, so I'd rather argue with you than with a colleague who will just say "read Lewis again." Set-up: grant Lewisian modal realism for the sake of the exercise — worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated, maximal; the plurality is as plenitudinous as it can be. I'm not asking you to relitigate the incredulous stare or to tell me modal realism is too expensive. I want to know what principle governs which worlds are in the plurality and which aren't, because my verdicts on the following clearly don't come from one principle.

1. Pebble. A world exactly like ours in every intrinsic and relational respect except that one pebble on a beach in Normandy sits two centimeters further to the left. In. I feel the gratuitousness of it but I can't find any non-arbitrary stopping point, so in.

2. Talking donkeys. In. Also in: a world where donkeys talk and are the only rational animals and nothing else about our world's history differs. Also in: a world containing infinitely many donkeys, all talking, none of them related.

3. Red/green ball. Out. There is no world at which one and the same ball is wholly red and wholly green all over at the same moment. And I want it to be out for a principled reason, not because my imagination ran out — otherwise the pebble world should worry me too.

4. XYZ world. In. There's a world where the clear, drinkable, lake-filling, rain-falling stuff isn't H2O, and where that stuff plays the water role perfectly. And I'm perfectly happy to say that at that world, "water" as used by its inhabitants doesn't denote H2O, so the world doesn't contradict the necessity of water's composition. Fine. Easy.

5. Hesperus. But there is no world at which Hesperus is not Phosphorus. Out, flatly. Notice that 4 and 5 are supposed to be the same shape of case — a posteriori necessity, reference pinned down by how things are — and yet I happily go along with the Lewisian treatment of 4 and refuse it in 5, where it would tell me that at some world "Hesperus" as used by its inhabitants picks out something other than Phosphorus. This is the pair I can't reconcile and I suspect it's fatal to the whole list. Either find me a real disanalogy between them or tell me one of the two verdicts goes.

6. Twins. Alba and Nera, two sisters who are exact intrinsic duplicates. A world just like ours except that their careers, names, memories and roles are swapped around — who did what, who is called which. I say that isn't another world. There is one such world, described twice. Anti-haecceitism, firmly held, and I like it because it's what stops the pebble case from exploding into an uncountable proliferation of role-assignments.

7. Me. But I also say, with a straight face, that I might never have existed, and that that's a genuine possibility about concrete reality rather than a piece of indexical bookkeeping. And I say it's false that I could have been one of my students. So in 6 I deflate which-is-which into nothing, and in 7 I want my own me-ness to carry substantive modal weight in one direction and no weight in the other, and I can't see how the same machinery does both.

8. Isolation and information. Nothing at any world can causally touch anything at ours. Firm. But when I came to believe that donkeys might have talked I take it I learned something — acquired information about a chunk of concrete reality I have no causal commerce with, not merely unpacked my own concepts. I want both, and I don't want to be pushed into ersatz worlds as the price of keeping the second one.

9. The solipsist. "There is only one world" is false, and false for a good reason. But everything about the plurality is necessary, so it couldn't have been true, and I don't want to say the solipsist is talking nonsense. I want there to be something they got wrong. If no world could make them right, I'm not sure what that is.

Here's my tentative principle, offered for you to break: a world is in the plurality iff it is a maximal way things could have been, where "could have been" is fixed by recombination of the parts of the things there actually are, minus any two candidate worlds that differ only over which individual haecceity accompanies which qualitative role. As far as I can tell that gets me 1, 2, 3 and 6, gets me 4 and 5 in exactly the same way (which is the problem), and doesn't touch 7, 8 or 9 at all.

So what I want: one principle, or two or three competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a grad student and watch them find counterexamples. For each candidate, say which clause is motivated by which of my cases, and then run it on cases I haven't listed — pick a few of your own, though here are three I'd like answers to: is there a world containing a set of all sets; is there a world that is a proper part of another world; is there a world at which the plurality is smaller than the plurality is. And be honest about the cost: which of 1 through 9 does each candidate have to break, and don't dress the breakage up. Please don't hand me a clause-per-verdict machine that saves everything by being unmotivated — if the right answer is that a lean recombination-plus-anti-haecceitism principle forces me to give up 5 and the second half of 7, say so and say why that's a good trade.

Commit, too. Rank your candidates, tell me which one you'd bet on, roughly how confident you are, and where in the ranking you're least sure of yourself. And if you think some of this list isn't a list of verdicts about worlds at all — that 5 and 7, say, are confused, or are really verdicts about sentences, or about what I'd accept as an explanation — say that plainly rather than politely manufacturing a principle to fit them. I would much rather hear that my cases are bad than read a smooth reconstruction of bad cases.
