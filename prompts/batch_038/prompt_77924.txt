At a reading group on the foundations of mathematics, a visiting speaker argued that the independence of the continuum hypothesis ought to trouble us no more than the independence of Euclid's fifth postulate ever troubled geometers. The pitch ran roughly as follows: for two millennia geometers kept trying to derive the parallel postulate from the remaining axioms, convinced that its truth was settled and only the proof was missing; when the proof turned out to be unavailable, no one concluded that geometry had failed. Instead, mathematics uncovered a family of equally legitimate geometries and grew richer for it. The continuum hypothesis, the speaker claimed, stands in exactly that position: ZFC is silent, and the silence is a permanent structural feature of the axiom system rather than a defect. So we should stop hunting for new axioms that decide the question, embrace a pluralism of set-theoretic frameworks, and treat the question of how many reals there are as meaningful only relative to a choice of axioms — just as the question whether a triangle's angles sum to two right angles is meaningful only relative to a choice of geometry.

Please audit the analogy rather than arguing the thesis directly. In particular:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly, in a table if that helps. What in the set-theoretic case plays the role of the fifth postulate, of the plane, of the centuries of failed proofs by reductio, of the alternative models, of the further question about which geometry the physical world obeys, and of the enrichment that followed?

2. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the speaker's use from those that are merely decorative. Is the analogy being drawn at the level of proof theory, where the two cases look nearly identical because both are relative-consistency constructions, or at the level of subject matter and truth, where they may look nothing alike? Does the verdict on the analogy shift depending on which level you fix on?

3. Identify what the analogy quietly imports from geometry that set theory may lack — an external tribunal that eventually adjudicated between rival geometries, intuitive pictures available for each alternative, widespread agreement that the original subject matter was underdetermined — and what it drops from set theory that might matter: a determinate intended model, an iterative conception of set that seems to speak to the question, and any grounds for thinking the choice among frameworks is indifferent rather than better or worse judged by fruitfulness or by what it settles elsewhere in mathematics.

4. Say whether the pluralist conclusion survives. Note that the fifth postulate's story ended not in indifference but in better axiomatizations and in a substantive discovery about space. Does that arc, followed carefully, support the recommendation to stop looking for new axioms, or undercut it? And does it matter that in the geometric case the loser was eventually identified from outside mathematics, whereas no comparable arbiter is in view here?

5. If the analogy misleads, offer a better one — drawn, say, from undecidable statements in arithmetic, from the way group theory tolerates independent sentences without anxiety, or from somewhere else entirely — or argue that no analogy of this kind is available and sketch what a non-analogical statement of the situation would look like instead.
