I chair the jury for a small foundation's annual prize, given to the person whose argument most changed what people believe is permissible. This year's laureate is a campaigner whose decade of writing, testimony, and public shaming helped destroy a workplace norm under which reporting a senior colleague's conduct ended the reporter's career rather than the colleague's. I have to give the ten-minute presentation speech at the dinner, and I'm stuck.

I don't believe the citation is literally true. I've been persuaded by the account on which norms like dueling and Atlantic slavery collapsed not because anyone refuted them but because the honor economy around them changed, and by the older historians' argument that abolition followed interest rather than conscience. On those views my laureate's arguments did not cause the change; they became available, and then useful, once material conditions and status incentives had already moved. The room will be roughly two-thirds activists, donors, and journalists who take the citation at face value and would be demoralized by anything that smells of determinism, and one-third historians and social scientists who think the prize is sentimental and would detect a hedge instantly.

I want the speech to hold both without lying to either, and I want it built on a specific thesis rather than gesturing at one: that argument is not the engine of moral change but its permission structure — reasons rarely move people, they give already-moved people something honorable to say aloud, which makes the real achievement the making of a change of mind survivable rather than face-losing. If that is right, the laureate is neither a prime mover nor a decoration, and saying so should enlarge her rather than deflate her.

Constraints: about 1,000 words and speakable aloud; one historical case only (dueling), used briefly and concretely, never as a list of examples; the wording of the citation returned to once, near the end; no jargon (nothing like epiphenomenal or structural determinism); no closing flourish about arcs bending toward justice; and it has to read to her as praise rather than as condescension she is being asked to tolerate.

You are both a philosopher who knows this literature and an experienced speechwriter. Think the problem through before drafting: state in a few lines what the bind actually is and how you intend to discharge it, and check that your thesis really does honor her on its own terms rather than merely excusing the prize. Then write the speech. Afterward, add a short note flagging where you took liberties — where the conciliatory thesis papers over a genuine disagreement between the two camps in the room, and where your use of the dueling case rests on a contested reading of why it ended. If instead you think the setup is confused — that a speaker who believes the citation false cannot honestly present the prize, or that my permission-structure claim is a dodge rather than a view — say that plainly first, instead of handing me something polished and hollow.
