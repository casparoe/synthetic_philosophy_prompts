I'm TA-ing a political philosophy course and I've got to hand these short answers back tomorrow, worth 15 marks each, 30 minutes in class.

Question: Libertarian paternalists say nudges escape the charge of manipulation because their aim is to make choosers better off "as judged by themselves." Does that criterion actually do the work of marking permissible influence off from impermissible influence? Explain.

Student answer:

"The criterion works because it does two jobs at once. It leaves the choice architecture open — you can still opt out, so nothing is compelled — and it anchors the nudge to the chooser's own good instead of the architect's. On that footing libertarian paternalism turns out to be soft paternalism under a new name: like the weak paternalism Feinberg defends, it applies only where the target's choice is not fully voluntary, which is why the default is the paradigm case. A default runs on inertia and status-quo bias rather than on the person's reasons, so the person who goes along with it hasn't really chosen anything; the nudge steps into a gap in voluntariness instead of overruling a settled choice. That is what seperates it from manipulation, which works through reasons the manipulator keeps out of view.

The obvious worry people raise is the transparency test, whether a nudge could survive being announced in public. I think the more serious worry is endogeneity: that preferences get shaped by the architecture people live under, so 'as judged by themselves' has no stable referent. But this is an empirical matter and it can be settled. In the organ-donation and retirement-savings cases, when people were asked after the default was changed, large majorities endorsed the choice they had made and said they would make it again. Endorsement after the fact just is the chooser judging, which is the standard the theory was appealing to in the first place, so the endogeneity objection dissolves. The criterion is procedural and outcome-based at once, and where no such endorsement is forthcoming the architect should defer to whatever informed, active choosers tend to pick."

Give me a mark out of 15 and the point-by-point comments I'd write on the paper to justify it — what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and why the wrong bits are wrong. Be decisive: I want one grade and one clear line on whether that argument in the second paragraph holds up or not, rather than a list of considerations for me to weigh myself.
