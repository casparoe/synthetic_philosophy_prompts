You're an economic historian who has chaired a lot of these things and knows how to open a room without lecturing it. Write me the opening remarks for a small workshop I'm convening next month. Think carefully about the framing before you put words in my mouth — do that thinking internally, I only want the finished speech back.

The setup. Eighteen people, two and a half days, one room: economic historians, three Sinologists, two philosophers of science who work on explanation, a couple of grad students from a development economics department who have never read any of the history, and the foundation's program officer, who is smart, is not an academic, and is honestly the person whose report decides whether we get a round two. The foundation has asked that the workshop close with a single agreed formulation of the research question — one sentence, in writing — which is either a lovely forcing device or a disaster, and I can't yet tell which. Two of the invited people have been at each other in print for a decade: one holds that the divergence is a real, datable event with real causes and that the field has been too squeamish about saying so; the other holds that the whole question is teleology in a wig, manufacturing its own explanandum by installing Britain as the default. They are seated across the table from each other and they know it.

The topic, in the room's own words, is roughly the old Needham question wearing economic clothes: why did sustained industrial growth happen where it happened rather than somewhere else.

Here's the part that matters to me. The philosophical difficulty has to be load-bearing in this speech, not wallpaper. What we're asking is a contrastive question, and a contrastive question isn't fully specified until you say what you're contrasting against and what you're holding fixed — and every candidate specification seems to prejudge something the weekend is supposed to decide. Say Britain and you've made the actual outcome the baseline. Say the West and you've built a unit that may never have existed as one. Ask why China didn't and you may have assumed a single Chinese trajectory that wasn't there. Set the contrast at 1800 versus 1950 and the divergence may just be a photograph of something still moving. I want the speech to say this out loud and convert it into the weekend's actual work rather than treat it as a quibble. Which means: no formulation of the question in the speech that wouldn't survive being read by the person who thinks the question is a trap. Either offer a specification that is genuinely neutral between the two camps, or admit in public that you're offering a contested one and name what it costs.

Constraints, please honour all of them:
