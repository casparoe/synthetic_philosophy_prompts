I'm a TA for an upper-level seminar on liberty, and one of my students has circulated a term-paper argument that I can't make sense of but also can't break. He's a negative libertarian through and through, and his target is the republican conception — freedom as non-domination, the one Pettit developed and advertised as a third concept of liberty alongside Berlin's negative and positive pair. His claim is that there is no third concept, and he insists this is a gift to the republicans rather than a refutation: on his view they should simply accept that they are negative libertarians who measure freedom over a longer horizon and across a wider range of possible worlds. Two other students have quoted the argument approvingly, which makes me suspect I'm the one who's confused. Here is the chain, cleaned up from his prose into numbered steps:

(1) Republican freedom is non-domination: the absence of subjection to another agent's capacity to interfere, on an arbitrary basis, in some of your choices. (2) For an agent to have a capacity to do X is for that agent to be disposed to do X in the appropriate circumstances. (3) So being dominated is being in circumstances such that another agent would interfere arbitrarily in your choices were those circumstances to arise. (4) Circumstances in which another agent's will settles which options are open to me are circumstances in which my options are constrained, whether or not the constraint is ever enforced, in exactly the way that a locked door constrains me whether or not I ever turn the handle. (5) Negative freedom is the absence of constraints deliberately imposed by other agents on the options open to me, and a constraint counts as a constraint whether or not anyone acts on it. (6) Therefore domination is negative unfreedom, and non-domination is negative freedom. (7) Anything further the republican invokes — control, civic status, the ability to meet another's eyes without deference or fear — is either just one more constraint, already counted at (5), or a good other than freedom, such as dignity or self-respect, that has been mislabeled. (8) So there are two concepts of liberty, not three, and the intuitions the republican treats as decisive are intuitions about non-liberty goods.

I'd like a diagnosis rather than a survey, and no citations, reading lists, or attributions — I know the neighborhood and I want the analysis. Specifically: which step fails, and why did it look innocuous enough to pass in a seminar? Then price the repairs. If I reject (2), what do I owe about capacities that are never exercised and about dispositions that can be masked or finked, and does the republican gain anything from that metaphysical work? If I reject (4), what is it about the locked door that makes the analogy mislead, and how do I explain the republican's flagship case — the slave of a master who is kind, unobservant, and never once interferes — without saying that the slave's actual option set is somehow narrower than the free person's, which is just false? If instead I accept (6) and take my student's offer, what has the republican lost, and is a longer temporal horizon genuinely enough to recover it, or does the collapse leave her unable to say why a self-binding, law-abiding, thoroughly benevolent despot is any less free-making than a constitutional assembly? Finally, work through a case of my own, because I think it separates the two readings better than the standard examples do. A firm owns the only road, the only water main, the only clinic, and the only significant employer in a town of nine thousand; its charter permits it to revoke any resident's access to all of these for any reason or none; its board has adopted a standing resolution never to invoke that power and has not done so in forty years; and residents may sue the firm in a court whose judges the firm appoints, a court that has ruled against the firm in roughly a third of cases. Variant A: the standing resolution is revocable by a bare majority of the board at any ordinary meeting. Variant B: the charter is amended so that revocation requires a two-thirds vote of adult residents, but the firm retains a veto over whether the amending procedure can itself be amended. Tell me which variant is dominated and which is merely constrained, whether a negative libertarian who has accepted (6) reaches the same verdicts on both, and — if he does — whether that concedes my student's point or instead exposes a place where the two accounts finally come apart. For what it's worth, I'm most attached to (1) and to the locked-door comparison in (4); the paper's author is most attached to (7). Say plainly whether either attachment can be honored.
