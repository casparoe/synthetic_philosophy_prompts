I'm taking a social epistemology / behavioral econ seminar this term and for my final project I'm coding a dataset of roughly 200 situations where a bunch of people converged on the same choice really fast — analyst upgrades on a stock, crowdfunding campaigns going vertical, a subreddit collectively diagnosing someone, half a subfield switching to the same assay in one year, that kind of thing. Each row gets a label for *why* the herd happened, not just a note that it did. The trouble is that basically every explanation in the literature predicts the exact same visible thing, namely later movers doing what earlier movers did, and I keep catching myself assigning labels based on how the story sounds rather than on anything in the data.

So: write me a decision tree I can run down a row of the spreadsheet, where every branch question keys off a feature I could actually check from the outside — whether later actors can see the individual prior choices or only the running total, whether the payoff to copying is public or private, whether the actors are evaluated on absolute or relative performance, whether the crowd ever gets feedback on whether it was right, whether dissenting carries a social cost that's visible to me, whether the action is reversible, how long the whole thing took, whether the early movers had any special access. If a question can't be phrased that way, either rewrite it or drop it and say you dropped it. No asking me to know what's in anyone's head, and no "do the agents have common knowledge of rationality" unless you also tell me what that would look like as a column.

Leaves I need, at minimum: a classic Bayesian informational cascade of the Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch shape where the third person's private signal gets swamped by the public history; a case where there's a genuine payoff externality so following is just correct even if you already know everything; reputational herding à la Scharfstein and Stein, where sharing the blame for a failure matters more than being right; plain normative conformity where people have their own evidence and are overriding it to be accepted; and pluralistic ignorance / preference falsification where everyone privately disagrees and each wrongly infers from the others' silence that they agree. If you think there are more live mechanisms — coordination and convention feel like they might deserve their own leaf, and I don't know where "everyone's just using the same salient source" goes — add them and say why.

Three extra things:

1. Be blunt about observational equivalence. My suspicion is that several of your leaves produce literally the same trace and are only separable if I grant an extra assumption, so group the ones that collapse into the same observable pattern into equivalence classes, and for each tie tell me what data would break it. Where it can't be broken even in principle, say so instead of leaving a fake branch.

Where a cell comes out empty (say: agents copy the crowd, have private evidence pointing the other way, and end up better off for it), tell me whether that's incoherent, unappealing, or just unexplored and worth a look.
3. Tell me which leaf I'm most likely to reach for wrongly by default, and what in the row would give me away.

Keep it shallow enough that one row takes me about five minutes, and flag any pair of leaves your tree just can't separate rather than papering over it. Also, no citations needed — I've got the readings, what I'm missing is the sorting instrument.
