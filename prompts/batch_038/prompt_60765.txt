I teach a philosophy-of-psychiatry seminar: sixteen students, all philosophy majors with no clinical training, one eighty-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes Hacking's looping effects something the students undergo rather than something they are told about. Two constraints on the setup: no participant may be assigned a real psychiatric label, so the category has to be invented on the spot, and nobody should be told in advance what the category is supposed to pick out. What I need is a design in which the loop closes in both directions — the labeled students' behavior changes under the description, and, much harder, the class's own criteria for applying the description migrate in response to the behavior they observe, so that by the final round the category means something it did not mean in the first. Give me roles, materials, a timeline with minute marks, and an eligibility or scoring structure carrying genuinely small stakes, so that something in the game actually turns on being in or out of the category. Explain how the design keeps a closed loop distinguishable from an ordinary expectancy or stereotype-threat effect, which is what exercises like this usually end up demonstrating by accident. Close with a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto the interactive/indifferent kinds distinction; a candid list of what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to recover mid-session; and the structural feature of looping effects that a single classroom hour cannot reproduce, which I will have to supply by argument instead.
