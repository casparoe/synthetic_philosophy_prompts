Next Thursday I give a 30-minute paper at our center's bioethics seminar with about twenty minutes of Q&A, and I'm nervous because the room is not a philosophy room. It's mostly clinicians — geriatricians, two palliative care nurses, a neuropsychologist who does capacity evaluations — plus two health lawyers who do guardianship and advance-directive work, and four philosophers, two of whom work on personal identity and are pretty serious Parfitians who will not let me fudge. Nobody in the room does social ontology or contract theory. They can't get the paper in advance, so everything has to land from the mouth.

My thesis: promissory obligation is graded by psychological continuity. A promise binds whoever has to keep it in proportion to how much of the promiser-who-made-it is actually carried forward in them, and the reason advance directives for late-stage dementia feel like a metaphysical scandal is just that they make visible something true of all promising, which we normally hide by pretending people don't change that much between thirty and fifty. So it isn't a dementia exception bolted onto an ordinary theory — it's what promising already is. My positive argument is roughly three steps: (1) making a promise works by handing the promisee a standing to hold you to your word; (2) that standing can only be held against whoever the original promiser's commitments are carried forward into; (3) since that carrying-forward comes in degrees rather than all-or-nothing, the standing, and therefore the obligation, comes in degrees too. I use this to reject two views I think are both wrong — the practice view, where the obligation's strength comes from the social institution and so doesn't depend on the particular person at all, and the reliance view, where it tracks what the promisee reasonably built thier plans on and so doesn't depend on the promiser's identity either. And I explicitly want to say the law's bright-line capacity test is a crude instrument that gets the cases wrong in both directions, which is why I expect to be in trouble.
