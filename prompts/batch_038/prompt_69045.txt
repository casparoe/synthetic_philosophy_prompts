I am teaching a graduate seminar in early modern moral and political thought, and our final session is devoted to Pascal on justice and force — the fragment in which he observes that justice without force is powerless and force without justice tyrannical, that the first is always contradicted because there will always be wrongdoers and the second always accused, that the two must therefore be brought together, and that, justice having proved unable to lend itself force, force has simply announced that it is justice. My students have now spent three weeks inside the fragments and are beginning to treat this one as a slogan, and usually as a relativist slogan, which is precisely what it is not. I would like to close the session by reading aloud a short poem that shows them what the argument is actually doing, and I would rather the poem carry the point than any paraphrase of mine, since I am told I paraphrase at length.

What I have in mind is a sonnet in loose iambic pentameter, fourteen lines, with an octave in enclosed rhymes (abbaabba) whose two halves are set out in deliberately mirrored syntax: the first quatrain giving justice without force, the second inverting its word order for force without justice, so that the chiasmus in Pascal's thought becomes a chiasmus in the lines. The turn at line nine should be the word therefore itself — the moment the argument promises a union. What the sestet must then perform is the failure of that promise, and I want the failure to be structural rather than merely rhetorical: force belongs to the order of bodies and acts only on bodies, whereas justice belongs to the order of minds and wills, so force cannot cross over and make anything just; all it can do is take the name. If the sestet can be rhymed so as to withhold a closing couplet (cdecde, perhaps), leaving the ear denied exactly the resolution the ninth line advertised, so much the better.

Two further requests. First, the last lines should descend from the abstraction to the machinery that keeps it unnoticed — the magistrate's furred robe and the guards at his door, the physician's square cap and mules, the fact that custom is treated as the whole ground of equity simply because it is received, and that we obey the laws not because we take them to be just but because they are laws. Pascal's view of all this is that the imagination is moved by costume rather than argument, which is why the substitution is never examined; the poem should let the reader feel that scenery doing its work. Second, the poem must read as diagnosis and not as endorsement: it should not collapse into a shrug about might making right, nor resolve into piety, nor offer the reader any comfort Pascal himself would have refused. If the closing line can turn on word order — the strong pronounced just rather than the just made strong — I will consider the exercise accomplished. Accuracy matters to me more than ornament here; someone who knows the Pensées well should find nothing in the poem that Pascal would have disowned, and someone who has never read him should still find it a good poem.
