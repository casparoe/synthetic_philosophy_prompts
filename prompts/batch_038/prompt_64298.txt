Hey, so I'm the instructor of record for a 300-level seminar on classical utilitarianism and I'm writing the take-home final this week, which is due from me on Friday. We spent two weeks on Bentham's sexual-offences manuscripts — the stuff eventually published under the title "Offences Against One's Self," long after his death — and the problem is that half the class skimmed it and the other half will just go straight to a summary online. So I need a question that can't be answered by reciting the manuscript's biography or the decriminalization argument as everybody already knows it.

Format: 72-hour take-home, one essay question, 1,500 words, worth 25 marks and it's the whole final. Please write it in American English, and make it look like something I could hand out as-is.

Here's what I want the question to do. Give the students a reconstruction of Bentham's argument *inside the question itself*, so nobody needs to remember the text or hunt up quotes. Something along these lines: (i) punishment is only justified where it prevents a mischief greater than the mischief the punishment itself does; (ii) mischief comes in a first-order kind, borne by assignable individuals, and a second-order kind — alarm and danger spreading out through the community; (iii) in consensual private sexual conduct there is no first-order mischief, since no assignable person is made worse off against their will; (iv) what's left is the pain of disgusted onlookers, which is the work of antipathy rather than a ground for punishment, plus the pain of ruined reputation, which the law itself largely manufactures by stamping the conduct with infamy; hence (v) repeal. You can phrase the premises better than I just did and merge or add to them, but keep them self-contained and paraphrased. No verbatim quotations from Bentham anywhere, in the question or in the model answer — I don't want anyone quoting from memory.

Then the crux. I want two stipulated cases built into the question:

Case A — the offence is a dead letter: still on the books, universally known to be universally loathed, not prosecuted in living memory. Essentially all the pain in the community is being delivered by gossip, ostracism and ruined prospects, i.e. by the popular sanction rather than the political one. Does repeal still do any utilitarian work here, and which premise is carrying the weight?

Case B — the mirror image: the law is vigorously enforced, but the population genuinely doesn't care; the disgust is being produced by the trial and the gallows, not by the conduct.

The question should push them on whether Bentham's discounting of onlooker-disgust is a premise doing independent work or just a restatement of his conclusion, and what he'd have to add to keep it from looking like a smuggled non-utilitarian constraint. It should also make them say whether the second-order mischief doctrine actually cuts in his favor here or against him — a disgust-mobilized majority looks like exactly the kind of diffuse alarm that only criminalization quiets. And it should end on a verdict: is the claim that Bentham anticipated the harm principle defensible, and if so at what cost to the calculus. Tell them explicitly that they don't need to cite anything and shouldn't drag Mill in except to say what Bentham can't borrow from him.

Deliverables I need from you:

1. The question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including the reconstruction and the two cases.
2. A model answer of around 800 words that actually reaches a verdict instead of hedging at the end.
3. A marking scheme out of 25, with five or six named moves, the marks for each, and a one-line note on how students typically botch each one.
4. A seperate short list of specific errors that should cost marks. I already know what they'll do: confuse the ascetic principle with the principle of sympathy and antipathy, declare Bentham a rule utilitarian by fiat, treat the popular and political sanctions as interchangeable, and write tyranny of the majority as if that were an argument rather than a slogan.

For the marking scheme, make the A/B boundary concrete. Something like: a competent but derivative answer reports the objection; a first-class answer says which premise the objection targets and whether Bentham can revise that premise rather than abandon it, and states what he loses by revising it. Also flag what should count as evidence that a student has just reproduced a summary — I want to be able to point at it when someone disputes their grade.

Thanks so much, I'm way behind on this.
