**Essay question:** Explain what makes an experience transformative on Paul's account, and why she thinks neither testimony nor imagination supplies what is missing. What exactly does she conclude about the rationality of transformative decisions, and is that conclusion as strong as her premises warrant?

**Student's answer:**

Paul's argument is Jackson's knowledge argument applied to decision theory. Mary knows every physical fact about colour vision but has never seen red, and Paul's point is the same one: there are facts about an experience that no formal or functional description captures, and expected utility theory, like physics, leaves them out.

A transformative experience, for Paul, is one that generates facts of this kind, and it has two features. It is epistemically transformative, meaning you cannot know what it is like to have it except by having it. And it is personally transformative, meaning it changes who you are — your core preferences and your sense of what matters. Her central example is deciding whether to have a child; she also develops the case of deciding whether to become a vampire, where the other vampires assure you it will be wonderful, and she mentions smaller cases such as tasting a fruit you have never tried.

Testimony and imagination do not close the gap because they can give you only a description. People can tell you about the exhaustion and the joy, and you can picture yourself holding a baby, but neither route delivers the phenomenal character itself. Since the phenomenal character is what makes the outcome worth what it is worth to you, you cannot know the value of the outcome in advance.

This breaks expected utility reasoning in two places. First, we cannot assign probabilities to the outcomes, because life as a parent is not something we have any evidence about. Second, we cannot assign values either, because the person who will have the outcome is not the person choosing: after a personally transformative experience my preferences are different, so the utilities I would enter now are not the utilities that will apply then.

Paul's conclusion is that such decisions are irrational. Rational choice means maximizing expected utility, no such calculation is available, and so choosing to have a child cannot be a rational choice. She thinks it follows that we should avoid transformative decisions where we can, and that where we cannot, we should defer to the testimony of people who have already made the transition, since that is the closest thing to evidence we have. The argument fails at this last step: many people make life-changing decisions well and appear to do so for good reasons, so the conclusion is far too sceptical. The central claim, though — that we cannot know in advance what a transformative experience will be like — seems to me true and very hard to answer.
