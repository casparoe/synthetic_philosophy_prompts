I'm into the second year of my PhD and I've ended up in the slightly awkward position of having one supervisor in philosophy and one in a behavioural-economics department, and they do not get on about conventions. Both of them are perfectly friendly in person, but every time the reading group touches Lewis they end up talking past each other and I can't work out whether there's actually anything being disputed. The philosophy side treats *Convention* as doing a piece of conceptual work: a convention is a regularity in a population's behaviour in recurrent situations which is common knowledge, which people follow because they prefer general conformity conditional on general conformity by the others, and for which some alternative regularity would have served just as well. That account then gets picked apart from several directions at once — Gilbert saying the conditional-preference structure misses the joint commitment, Marmor saying Lewis can handle coordinative conventions but not constitutive or deep ones (what makes a bishop move diagonally is not obviously a solution to a pre-existing coordination problem), Millikan dropping the knowledge and expectation requirements altogether because the whole thing is too intellectualist and plenty of regularities get followed by people with no beliefs about them whatsoever. The modelling side basically shrugs at all of that. They point out that Lewis's own examples got formalised into sender–receiver signalling games, that evolutionary dynamics can generate those equilibria in a population of agents who believe nothing and represent nothing, and that Bicchieri-style work turns the conditional-preference idea into something you can actually measure with surveys and vignettes about empirical versus normative expectations. From where they sit, either the formal work vindicates Lewis and the armchair refinements are noise, or Lewis is decoration on a result that does all the work by itself.

The case I keep coming back to, and the one that made the two supervisors genuinely annoyed with each other last term, is escalators. The London Underground convention is stand on the right, walk on the left. Then TfL ran a trial at Holborn, which has very long escalators where almost nobody walks up anyway, and found that getting people to stand on both sides cut congestion by something like thirty per cent; they followed it up with a longer pilot in 2016 trying to make the change stick with signage and jokey messages designed with help from behavioural scientists at LSE. My recollection, and please push back on it if you think I've got the details wrong, is that the regularity snapped straight back once the signs came down. Now here's the bit that matters for the disagreement. On the Lewisian account the regularity is supposed to solve a coordination problem, and the agents are supposed to prefer general conformity conditional on general conformity — but the throughput evidence suggests a strictly better alternative regularity was sitting right there, and people still stand right, and if you stand on the left at Holborn you get tutted at and told to move along. So which is it? Camp A says I've mis-described the case: the coordination problem isn't maximising throughput, it's about safety and about fair access to the walking lane, or else it's a deep convention sitting under a surface one, and anyway the tutting shows I've confused a convention with a norm. Camp B says it's a bog-standard equilibrium-selection-plus-enforcement phenomenon, that whether anyone has the relevant second-order beliefs is an empirical question you settle with experiments rather than intuitions, and that the philosophy is a story told after the fact.

So what I want is a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on who's right about escalators. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, in that 'convention' is being used to pick out a natural kind for the modellers and an ordinary-language category for the philosophers? Are the two camps answering different questions under one heading — one about what it takes to justify or make sense of a regularity, one about how to predict and change it? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, such that whether common knowledge and conditional preferences are doing anything real is just a matter of what the psychology turns out to be, and the armchair objections are unfalsifiable by construction? Or is it bedrock: a disagreement about what a theory of the social world is for, which neither side can argue for without begging the question? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and quite possibly wrong, is that it's a change-of-question situation and that both camps keep scoring points against claims nobody actually makes — the modellers think the philosophers are predicting behaviour, and the philosophers think the modellers are offering an analysis, and neither is. Tell me if that's too glib. I'd also like you to be honest about whether the thing is ill-posed or merely hard, and about whether anything — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a decision to stop using the word — could actually move it. Please work from the positions as I've laid them out rather than doing a general survey of the literature on conventions, and do use the escalator case as a test of whatever diagnosis you land on, since that's the concrete thing my supervisors were actually fighting about.
