Next month my city will unveil a sculpture in the park along the river. It is cast from the reduction gearbox of the tidal barrage we spent nine years and some four hundred million dollars building before it was cancelled with about a third of it finished. I directed the project from its second year until the cancellation, and I have been asked to speak for roughly ten minutes at the unveiling.

The room will contain three groups who do not share a view of what happened, and the speech has to be true for all three. At the front will be perhaps forty of the engineers, welders, and site supervisors who built the thing; many of them have not worked steadily since. A delegation of economists and decision scientists is in town for a conference on cost overruns and has been invited to treat the unveiling as a field excursion; they have told the parks department, in good spirits, that they intend to run a live dollar auction at the reception afterward — a twenty-dollar bill, the game in which the runner-up pays his bid and receives nothing — and to invite our former staff to play. Behind them will be the ordinary civic crowd: the mayor's office, local press, schoolchildren, people who remember the barrage as a scandal and people who remember it as the last occasion on which this city attempted anything at all.

The fact I need the address built around is not the one the delegation expects. By our fifth year the case for finishing had collapsed: a cheaper means of achieving the same output had appeared, and our own projections said the barrage would never return what it cost. We went on for two more years regardless. When I have tried to account for that, the standard sunk-cost story has not fit, because what we were buying in years six and seven was not the barrage. It was the fact that cancellation meant dismissing a workforce whose skills were specific to this site and this employer, and nobody in my position wished to be the person who did it. That may be the most defensible thing I ever did, and it may be the reason four hundred million became four hundred million and then considerably more; I have not settled which, and the speech must not pretend that I have.

Write the address. It must be speakable in about ten minutes. It must not deploy the vocabulary of behavioral economics at the people who lived it, and it must not be so homespun that the delegation hears nothing new. It must say aloud, to their faces, whether those final two years were a failure of nerve or a failure of arithmetic, without resolving the question by flattery. It must handle the auction at the reception in a way that leaves the room warned and amused rather than ambushed. And it must end on the sculpture rather than on the project: what a gearbox accomplishes as a monument that a report of inquiry could not.
