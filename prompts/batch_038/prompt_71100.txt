Question 3 (25 marks). A well-known argument in the theory of rational choice holds that an agent whose strict preference ordering over three options is cyclic can be led, by a suitable sequence of offered trades, to pay a small premium at each step and finish holding the option they started with and less money than they began with.
(a) Set out the sequence of trades, and explain step by step why each individual exchange looks rational to the agent at the moment it is offered. [10]
(b) What does the argument establish about cyclic preferences, and what does it fail to establish? [8]
(c) State one reply available to someone who denies that cyclic preferences are irrational, and assess how well it works. [7]

Student's answer. (a) Suppose Priya owns a used camera body, C. A dealer offers to swap it for a different body, D, if she pays one dollar. As far as anything Priya cares about goes — image quality, weight, the feel of the dials — she cannot distinguish C from D, so she is indifferent between them, and since the swap costs her nothing she minds losing, she takes it. The dealer then offers the same deal between D and a third body, E, again for one dollar, and again she cannot tell them apart, so she takes it. Finally the dealer offers her C back for one dollar; she cannot tell C from E either, so she takes that too. She ends where she started, holding C, three dollars poorer. Each step looks rational on its own because at each step she gets something she does not mind having in exchange for something she does not mind giving up, and the dollar is small enough that it never feels like the point of the transaction. The trouble only appears when the steps are added up, which is why the dealer can run the whole cycle again the next day, and the day after, and drain her a dollar at a time even though she never once made a choice she would call a mistake. (b) This is the diachronic Dutch book argument applied to preferences rather than to degrees of belief. In both cases what gets exploited is a failure of coherence that shows up only across time: no single decision is bad, but the sequence guarantees a loss, and the loss is guaranteed by the agent's own standards rather than by bad luck. What the argument shows is that cyclic preferences are self-defeating, in that an agent who has them is guaranteed to end up worse off by their own lights than an agent who does not, at least where someone is around to make the offers. What it does not show is that anyone actually has cyclic preferences, or that Priya is confused about the facts: she holds no false beliefs about the cameras, she is simply badly organised. So the conclusion is normative, about how preferences ought to be structured, and not a psychological prediction; and it is not an argument that the agent is irrational at any particular moment, since by construction every moment looked fine to her. (c) The obvious reply is that Priya can see the whole cycle coming. If she knows the dealer will offer E and then offer C back, she knows that accepting the first trade leads to a net loss, so she refuses at the first step and the pump never gets started. I think this reply is decisive: the argument only has force against a short-sighted agent, and short-sightedness is a separate defect from intransitivity, so the pump does not indict cyclic preferences at all. A second reply is that a sensible agent insists on some minimum margin of improvement before paying to swap; but that just changes the subject, since it replaces the cyclic ordering with a non-cyclic one, so it refuses to play rather than defending intransitivity.
