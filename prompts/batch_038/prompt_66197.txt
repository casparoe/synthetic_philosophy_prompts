I am preparing a short written statement of my own position for a jurisprudence seminar in which the convention is that each participant's view is then attacked, so I would like a consistency audit before I hand it in. The topic is the legal enforcement of morality — the Hart–Devlin debate and its modern extensions into commodification questions. Here is what I intend to say I believe.

1. Devlin's central premise is false. A society's continued existence is not tied to any particular shared morality about sex or the body; Britain decriminalized the conduct he warned about and did not disintegrate. I also accept Hart's charge that Devlin slides between two senses of "society" — the organized political community and one of its moral traditions — and that this equivocation does most of the work in the argument from self-preservation.

2. Nevertheless, the settled revulsion of the majority is a legitimate and, in my view, indispensable input into what the criminal law should prohibit — not merely a symptom of harm that the liberal must then go and locate somewhere else, but a reason in its own right. I don't think this makes me a moralist: when most people find a transaction repugnant they are usually, however inarticulately, registering that somebody in it is being degraded. In any case Devlin's reasonable man is a better guide to the content of the law than a philosopher's critical morality, and a legislature that overrides him is not thereby being more moral.

3. Harm to others is a necessary condition of justified criminalization but not a sufficient one. Even conduct that genuinely harms can fail the test where the prohibition would be widely flouted, would drive the conduct underground, or would arm the police with discretion whose exercise does more damage than the conduct itself.

4. I reject Feinberg's offense principle. That other people are disgusted, shocked, or distressed by what consenting adults do — where nothing sets back anyone's interests — is never a ground for prohibition. I keep the ordinary public indecency offenses, but I cash out their justification as a captive-audience harm, not as offense.

5. Legal moralism, meaning the claim that there are wrongs the law may prohibit although nothing is harmed and nobody's interests are set back, is false.

6. My case verdicts: paying a surrogate mother should remain prohibited, and the altruistic model is the right one; paying a living kidney donor should remain prohibited; but paying for sex between consenting adults should be decriminalized, and I think the Nordic model that punishes only the buyer is wrong for much the same reasons full criminalization is.

That is the package, and I want to know whether it hangs together. I am not asking you to adjudicate between Hart and Devlin, to summarize the debate, or to tell me what the better view is; I have a view and I want to know what it commits me to. Please separate the genuine conflicts from the ones that only look like conflicts, since I suspect I have several of the latter and would rather not spend the seminar defending against them. I don't know where the trouble is and I'm not telling you which item I'd be most willing to give up — that's part of what I want from you.

Please structure your answer under exactly these headings, in this order:

- **Verdict** (about 100 words). Say plainly whether the package is jointly tenable, and name the single most serious problem in it.
- **Genuine tensions, ranked** (roughly 600–800 words). The real conflicts, worst first. For each: which items clash, why the clash is substantive rather than merely verbal, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. Do not present them as equally bad.
