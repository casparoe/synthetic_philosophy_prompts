I'm in a philosophy of law reading group that has spent about six weeks on free will and punishment, mostly working through Dennett and Caruso's dialogue Just Deserts plus Pereboom's quarantine material, and I've come away frustrated in a way I can't quite articulate. Here's the thing that bothers me: on the surface this looks like a maximal disagreement -- one guy thinks competent adults are genuinely deserving of blame and punishment in a way that licenses treating them differently from a broken machine, the other thinks basic desert is a myth and that criminal justice should look more like infectious disease control -- and yet when you actually list what each of them endorses, the overlap is enormous. Neither thinks suffering is intrinsically good or that anyone should be punished for its own sake. Both think the American system is grotesquely harsh. Both want proportionality constraints on sanctions, both emphasize rehabilitation and prevention, both oppose the death penalty. And they still wrote an entire book shouting at each other. Our group has been going in circles trying to say what the residue actually is.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? A few hypotheses I keep cycling through, none of which feel complete. (1) It's verbal or functional: the fight is over what the word desert is doing -- a putative name for a real property of persons vs. a piece of social technology that earns its keep by regulating behavior -- and neither of them can win because they're using it as different kinds of tool. (2) They're answering different questions under one heading: Dennett asking what the practice of holding responsible is for and who is eligible to be enrolled in it, Caruso asking whether anyone is blameworthy in the narrow sense that would justify inflicting harm. (3) There's a buried empirical question doing the work -- what actually happens to public attitudes and to sentencing policy if you withdraw desert-talk -- with each side assuming a different answer and neither treating it as something to go check. (4) Both are, underneath, revisionists in Vargas's sense, and the real dispute is about which threads of ordinary responsibility talk to keep and which to cut, which is a methodological disagreement dressed up as a metaphysical one. My own favourite candidate, which the group thinks is too cute: the substantive residue is about where the ceiling on punishment comes from. Desert functions as a cap -- you may punish only up to what is deserved -- and a purely forward-looking model has no internal cap at all, so Caruso has to import one from the quarantine analogy, least restrictive alternative and all that. The question then becomes whether that imported constraint is doing genuinely independent work or is just desert smuggled back in with the metaphysics scraped off.

One thing we tried, which maybe tells you something: we hunted for cases where the two views give different verdicts, and the only ones we could find were edge cases -- the person who is dangerous but has done nothing wrong, and the person who did something terrible decades ago and is now no threat to anyone. In both, each camp had a ready move (quarantine is about threat, not wrongdoing; desert tracks competence and the point of the practice, not risk). Which makes me wonder whether the whole standoff is a fight about the edges being sold as a fight about the core. So: diagnose it. Which of the above, if any, is right, and what's the evidence from what each of them actually argues rather than from their labels? Is Caruso's epistemic point -- that we can never know enough about the causal history of an act to justify desert-based punishment -- actually fatal to the metaphysical debate, i.e. if it works, do we even need to settle anything about determinism, and shouldn't Dennett be glad to take it? What would count as progress here: a distinction, a decision procedure for when we know enough, a set of policy cases where the models genuinely diverge, an experiment on free-will beliefs and punitiveness (the results there seem messy and don't clearly favor either side, which is itself maybe part of the problem), or a change of question entirely? And tell me honestly whether this is one of those disputes where the impasse is the rational response, because the two of them are partly arguing about political risk rather than truth. No references or reading list needed, I just want the diagnosis.
