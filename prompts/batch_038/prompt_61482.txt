In 700 words: (i) reconstruct Hume's account of what actually happens to a kingdom that has lost four-fifths of its specie, and why he thinks the loss cannot persist; (ii) say what Hume would make of the proposed law and why; (iii) explain his claim that an increase in the quantity of money does good only during the interval before prices rise, and show why that claim does not undercut (i); (iv) say where interest rates fit into any of this.*

Here's the script:

Hume's monetary theory is best read as a rejection of mercantilism on its own terms: he accepts that money matters to a state, but denies that the quantity of it a state holds determines how wealthy that state is.

(i) Money for Hume is not one of the subjects of commerce but the instrument men have agreed on to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another; he compares it not to a wheel of trade but to the oil that makes the wheels turn more smoothly. Considered within a single kingdom, the quantity is therefore of no consequence, since prices are always proportioned to the plenty of money — a crown in Henry VII's time did the same work that a pound does now. What stops this being a tautology is the thought experiment Hume proposes. Suppose four-fifths of the money in Britain were annihilated in one night. The price of all labour and commodities would sink in proportion, British goods would become cheaper than any foreign rival could match, and foreign demand would consequently rise; the lost money would flow back until Britain reached the level of her neighbours, at which point the kingdom is full and the further inflow stops. The argument runs in reverse too: multiply the money fivefold overnight and prices become so exorbitant that no neighbour can afford to buy from us, while their goods become so comparatively cheap that they come in regardless of any law we could pass, and our money flows out again. The distribution of specie across Europe is self-correcting, like water finding its level.

(ii) Against that background the proposed felony is worse than useless. Hume's general point about prohibitions of this kind is that they act directly contrary to their intention: they raise the exchange against the country and produce a still greater exportation, by pushing the bullion trade underground and adding the smuggler's premium to the cost of remitting funds. Money cannot be held in a kingdom that has lost its people and its industry, and cannot be lost by one that has kept them. The legislature is trying to bail out a vessel whose level is set elsewhere. The only sensible policy is the indirect one: preserve industry, and the money will take care of itself.

(iii) The interval argument is Hume's concession to the mercantilist intuition. An augmentation of gold and silver, he says, has influence only in the progress towards the change: while the new money is still working its way through the ranks of the economy — the merchant who first receives it, the manufacturers he sets to work, the farmers who supply them — it excites industry, because wages and prices have not yet caught up with it. Employment rises ahead of the price level, and that gap is the whole of the benefit. Once prices have settled to the new abundance, the augmentation has no influence at all. This does not undercut (i), because (i) is a claim about the endpoint and (iii) is a claim about the path: the flow mechanism tells you where the money ends up, the interval argument tells you what it does on the way. In modern terms Hume is a quantity theorist who allows short-run non-neutrality.

(iv) Interest is where the two arguments meet. Hume is famous for denying that low interest is caused by plenty of money — Batavia and Jamaica lend at ten per cent and Portugal at six, though those places abound more in gold and silver than London or Amsterdam; low interest comes instead from a small demand for borrowing, great riches to supply it, and small profits from commerce, that is, from the advance of industry. But the same channel runs inside the adjustment mechanism: when specie drains out of a kingdom the reduced stock of loanable funds pushes the rate of interest up, borrowing and investment are checked, the demand for imports falls, and the money returns. Interest is thus both an index of commercial maturity and one of the levers by which the balance restores itself. Which is also why Hume thinks a state should still want a full treasury where it can get one: whatever the long-run neutrality, a continual inflow keeps the interval open, and the interval is where all the stimulation of industry lives. The mercantilists were right about the end and wrong only about the means.
