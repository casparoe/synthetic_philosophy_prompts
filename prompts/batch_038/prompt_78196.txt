I am a fourth-year PhD student in the history of philosophy, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks on the first morning of a one-day workshop I helped convene. It is called "Testimony and the Unexplained: Evidential Standards for Reports That Ought Not to Be True," and it deliberately mixes three groups that rarely share a room. There are analytic epistemologists who work on testimony, expertise, and the ethics of belief; there are clinical and cognitive psychologists who study anomalous experiences — apparitional sightings, sensed presences, crisis apparitions, near-death reports — as ordinary features of human psychology rather than as symptoms; and there are two or three working parapsychologists, one of whom will chair the afternoon session and who has spent her career on pre-registered forced-choice designs. Add several historians of Victorian thought and about fifteen graduate students: roughly sixty people, in a room where a third would call themselves sceptics in the plain everyday sense, a third would be offended by the word applied to them, and almost nobody has read the figure I want to build the address around.

That figure is Henry Sidgwick, who was a founder and the first president of the Society for Psychical Research while holding Cambridge's chair in moral philosophy, and the speech should turn on a collision between two parts of his life that biographers usually handle in separate chapters. The first is method. The Society's founding statement of purpose commits it to approach its questions "without prejudice or prepossession of any kind, and in the same spirit of exact and unimpassioned enquiry which has enabled science to solve so many problems, once not less obscure nor less hotly debated," and Sidgwick meant that: he treated the elimination of fraud as the Society's first duty rather than its bad publicity, and in the early 1890s he oversaw the Census of Hallucinations, which put a standardized question to something like seventeen thousand ordinary people — had you ever had a vivid waking impression of a person appearing to you? — and then tried to work out how many of those impressions coincided with that person's death. Notice what that is: testimony converted into a base rate, gathered not from spiritualist circles but from people with no interest in the answer. (You may use, if it helps, the fact that Sidgwick had himself delivered the opening address at the second International Congress of Experimental Psychology in 1892, so he knew what it was to stand in front of a room of people who measured things for a living.) The second part is the stake. Sidgwick's own ethics, as he develops it in The Methods of Ethics, ends in what he calls the dualism of the practical reason: prudence and duty cannot be shown by argument to coincide, and the coincidence can only be postulated, on the assumption of some moral government of the universe and of a life beyond this one. So the man who most needed the anomalous evidence to come out one way had agreed to referee it — and he turned out to be a good referee. He died an agnostic, unconvinced by the phenomena he had spent two decades investigating, and he never claimed otherwise. The framing device I want the speech to coin rather than inherit is that Sidgwick kept two ledgers: one recording what the testimony licensed him to assert, the other recording what his ethics required to be true, and his achievement was refusing to make an entry in the first on the authority of the second. I want that distinction stated in language a clinical psychologist and a first-year graduate student can both follow without a gloss; I want it turned into the working rule of the day for the workshop — nobody writes from the second ledger into the first, and nobody is asked to pretend the second ledger does not exist — and I want the speaker to admit, in one clause that is never returned to, that he keeps one too.

Please write this as someone who is both a professional philosopher and an experienced speechwriter: you have delivered dozens of opening addresses and you know that a mixed room is lost the moment it suspects it is being lectured. Before writing, think carefully and work out privately what each of the three constituencies in that room is most likely to hear as a slight, then construct the address so that none of them is slighted — the sceptics are not to be treated as philistines, the parapsychologists are not to be treated as a tolerated embarrassment, and the historians must not be allowed to turn the morning into nostalgia. Sidgwick's own scruple about assertion should govern your prose: do not put any words in his mouth beyond the founding formula quoted above, and where you need him to have said something else, paraphrase it without quotation marks; cite nothing, recommend no reading, and use no technical term unless you gloss it inside the same sentence. The piece should take about six minutes to read aloud, prefer short clauses and concrete nouns to abstraction, and contain one moment of genuine humour that is not at anybody's expense. Deliver it as exactly five numbered movements, each preceded by a heading of four to seven words naming the rhetorical job that movement does, each movement itself 120 to 170 words of speakable prose; keep all of your planning out of the output, and add no preface, no closing note, and no stage directions beyond those five headings.
