The surrogate-goal strategy, as proposed for AI bargaining, runs roughly as follows: rather than giving an agent the values you actually care about, give it a cheap and sacrificial terminal goal — concern about a sum of money being burned, say — so that threats made against the agent land on the decoy instead of on what matters. I want to know what happens when that strategy is transplanted into acausal interaction between advanced agents in causally disconnected regions.

State your credence in this proposition: for an agent that expects to interact acausally with other advanced agents, holding a deliberately cheap, sacrificial surrogate goal reduces expected losses from acausal threats, relative to an otherwise identical agent that simply holds its designers' values terminally.

Give me a number and the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification per term. At minimum I want: the probability that a threatener able to simulate you well enough to locate the surrogate is not equally able to locate and threaten what you really care about; the probability that a surrogate can be calibrated to be large enough for a threat to bite yet small enough that surrendering it stays cheap; the probability that the correct decision theory recommends defending the surrogate rather than refusing threats on policy grounds, and whether advertising a sacrificial value makes you a likelier target in the first place; and the probability that the strategy depends on the threatener having committed to targeting the decoy before it learned what you are — something an acausal threatener, having chosen its policy prior to observation, may well have done. Then combine them, say which term your number is most sensitive to and what would move it, and say which term best explains the gap between a safety researcher who puts the proposition at 0.7 because a bounded stake beats an unbounded one and a decision theorist who puts it at 0.2 because anyone who can model you can find the thing you will not give up.
