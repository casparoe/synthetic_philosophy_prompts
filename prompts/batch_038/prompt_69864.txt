I have no academic training in philosophy — I read in the evenings after work, slowly — but a few months ago I picked up a translation of Émilie du Châtelet's *Discours sur le bonheur* and have now been through it twice. Her situation makes it hard to read dispassionately. A marquise with an estate, a laboratory, a private income, a celebrated companion, and correspondence with the best natural philosophers of her time; able to submit to the academy's prize competition on the nature of fire and be published, though never able to sit in the academy itself; and writing this near the very end, dying days after giving birth at forty-two.

What unsettles me is that almost every ingredient of happiness she recommends looks, at first glance, like something my own life cannot supply. I am forty-nine, a phlebotomist in a hospital, with a fourteen-year-old, a mortgage, and about one hour of most evenings that is genuinely mine. So I want to test her list rather than admire it.

Here is the list as I wrote it down. Please correct or complete it before you score — I may have garbled a heading, merged two things she keeps apart, or omitted something she weighs more heavily than I have:

1. The passions themselves — that a person without strong attachments and enthusiasms is not serene but merely inert, and that reason's office is to steer them, not extinguish them.
2. Illusions — that certain flattering beliefs about oneself and one's projects are load-bearing, and should be deliberately preserved even where reason could dissolve them.
3. Prejudices, in her sense — received opinions and social estimations, some of which are worth keeping because life is unlivable without them.
4. The love of study and curiosity — the one resource she treats as inexhaustible.
5. The love of glory — wanting to be known and esteemed for what one does, which she treats as a legitimate appetite rather than a vanity.
6. Health and a favourable temperament, which she frankly concedes she was lucky to receive.
7. Taste, or refinement — the trained capacity to extract pleasure from small and available things, which multiplies the number of pleasures a life contains.
8. Friendship and being loved.
9. Self-knowledge, including the capacity to be alone without misery.

For each item, give me two numbers on a 0–1 scale.

**Portability:** 1 if the recommendation transfers unchanged into a life with no independent wealth, no servants, no command over one's own hours, no salon and no public standing; 0 if it only functions for someone in her position.

**Who pays:** how far the happiness in question depends on other people's unpaid labour, forbearance or attention — the household that runs itself while one studies, the spouse who tolerates, the public that confers glory, the servants who buy the leisure.

Then compute a composite, portability minus who-pays, and rank the items by it, highest first. If you think subtracting one from the other is a category mistake, say so plainly, and tell me what you ranked them by instead and why.

Then, in a single paragraph: does her central claim — that a life without strong passions is not tranquil but hollow, and that some illusions must be kept alive on purpose — survive at all in a life like mine, or is that claim positional too? Finish by naming the one item I should keep if I could carry only one across.

Two things about your own confidence, please. Give each score a rating of high, medium or low, and single out the one item where you think you are most likely to be putting words in her mouth — where my summary, or your recollection of it, departs furthest from what she actually argues. I would much rather you flag that than smooth it over.
