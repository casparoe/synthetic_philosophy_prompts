My friend and I argued for three hours last night about Friedman's instrumentalism in the methodology of positive economics, and by the end he had produced an argument against which I could find nothing to say, though I remain convinced its conclusion is false. I would like you to locate the break.

First, the target position, as I understand it, so that we are reasoning about the same thing. Friedman holds that the question to ask about a theory's assumptions is not whether they are descriptively realistic but whether they generate sufficiently accurate predictions about the class of phenomena the theory is intended to explain; that simplicity and fruitfulness, rather than literal truth, are the operative virtues of assumptions; that descriptive accuracy matters only instrumentally, as a means to or an indicator of predictive success; and, more provocatively, that the hypotheses which turn out to be most significant tend to be those whose assumptions depart most boldly from observed detail. Critics have labelled this feature of the view the F-twist. My friend's argument is that the twist can be performed deliberately rather than suffered, and that once performed it disposes of every realist objection.

Here is the argument as he laid it out.

(1) The assumptions of any theory are to be assessed solely by whether they yield sufficiently accurate predictions within the theory's intended domain; descriptive realism is not an independent criterion of assessment, and matters only insofar as it is a means to, or a reliable indicator of, such predictive success.

(2) A methodological doctrine of the kind expressed in (1) is itself a theory: it consists of assumptions about how inquiry works — about what scientists do, about what makes theories succeed, about which criteria track success — advanced in order to guide the acceptance of theories.

(3) Therefore the assumptions of (1) are themselves to be assessed solely by whether (1) leads its users to accept predictively successful theories; whether (1) accurately describes scientific practice, or the actual relation between unrealistic assumptions and predictive success, is not an independent criterion of its assessment.

(4) The standard realist objection to (1) is an objection from descriptiveness: it says that (1) is false about what successful theories are like, that working scientists do test their assumptions, that prediction cannot be cleanly distinguished from the accommodation of data already in hand, and so on.

(5) By (3), that objection is inadmissible in the assessment of (1), since it applies to (1) a criterion whose applicability is exactly what (1) denies.

(6) What remains is the admissible test, and (1) passes it: the theories accepted under (1) — the price-theoretic and monetary work it was written to license — have an impressive predictive record, whereas the methodologies recommended by (1)'s critics have no comparably successful body of accepted theory behind them.

(7) Therefore (1) is not merely beyond the reach of its critics; it is vindicated by the only test it can coherently be held to, and the realist objection to Friedman defeats itself.

His closing move was a generalization, and it is what troubles me most. He said that any doctrine D which (a) presents itself as a theory about how to assess theories and (b) applies to itself the criterion it applies to others is thereby immune to any objection drawn from the criterion it rejects, and that this immunity is a virtue rather than a defect; for the alternative, subjecting a criterion of assessment to some further criterion of assessment, either regresses or appeals to the very sort of consideration the doctrine exists to exclude. Self-exemption, on his account, is the only available stopping point.

Tell me which step you reject, and what rejecting it costs me. If your diagnosis is that the argument is valid and that the real question is which premise is least expensive to surrender, say so plainly and rank the candidates. So that you know my sympathies, though I do not want them to settle anything: I find (1) attractive as first-order methodology and I am unwilling to abandon (2), since it strikes me as evasive to deny that a methodology makes claims about the world; I would rather lose (6) than (1).

I would also like one exchange from the evening examined directly. Late on, I put him a parallel. An astrologer maintains that astrology is to be judged only by predictions made in astrology's own terms of art; that the astrologer's methodological doctrine is itself a theory about how to judge theories; that it is therefore to be judged only by whether it leads its practitioners to accept charts that work out. My friend's reply was that the parallel fails because astrology does not pass the test in (6). I suspect he answered a different question from the one I asked, but I cannot show it. Either demonstrate that the parallel does damage to a specific step of the argument, or identify the disanalogy that is doing the work — and then say whether that work can be done by the instrumentalist, or whether it is only available to the realist he was trying to refute.
