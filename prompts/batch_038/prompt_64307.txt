I am a novelist, and the hinge of my book is the first Bollingen Prize: in 1949 a jury of the Library of Congress's Fellows in American Letters awarded a thousand-dollar prize, funded by the Bollingen Foundation, to Ezra Pound for the Pisan Cantos while he was confined at St. Elizabeths Hospital, under indictment for treason over his wartime broadcasts and never to be tried. One juror, Karl Shapiro, voted against the choice because of Pound's anti-Semitism; the press and then Congress erupted, the Library was pressed to disassociate itself, and the prize went to Yale. I want this episode analyzed through the standing debate over whether a work's immorality bears on its artistic value: what ethicism and its variants say about moral defects counting as aesthetic defects, what moderate autonomism says about bracketing the artist's character, and what the contextualist insistence that a work's meaning is fixed by the circumstances of its making does to Pound's Cantos specifically. No names, dates, or scholarship required — I want the positions and their reasoning.

Then run the episode through a distinction I have been developing, which I suspect that debate lacks, and which I call conferral. An aesthetic verdict is propositional: the book is or is not good. A prize is not propositional but performative — it does not assert beauty so much as attach an institution's name to a person and thereby alter that person's standing. The state had already declared Pound unfit to be judged; the jury then restored him to the company of those whose words count. On conferral, the live question is not whether the jurors judged the poems correctly but whether an institution may let an aesthetic judgment do the work of a pardon.

So: map the episode onto the standard positions and say which features of it each explains and which it misdescribes; then show what the argument looks like once conferral is added — whether it dissolves the quarrel, since both sides may have agreed about the poems and disagreed about the institution, or merely relocates it. Finally, and most importantly, tell me where conferral fails: where this was genuinely a fight about the merits of a book and nothing else, and where my distinction flatters me instead of doing work. I can use the failure far better than the confirmation, so do not soften the last part for the sake of the first two.
