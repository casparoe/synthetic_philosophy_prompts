I write literary fiction. No philosophy training, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise — I've skimmed enough of the Sleeping Beauty debate to know there's a halfer camp (Lewis, roughly: her credence stays at 1/2 because waking up tells her nothing new she didn't already have) and a thirder camp (Elga, roughly: it drops to 1/3 because from where she's sitting the three possible awakenings look identical and two of them are in the tails world), plus a few people who think both sides are wrong in a way I couldn't follow, something about self-locating belief and centered propositions instead of ordinary belief. That's the extent of what I've got. I am, on the other hand, completely fluent in the vocabulary of craft — it's my day job.

Here's the thing. My new book turns out to be structurally the Sleeping Beauty problem, and I didn't realize it until draft three. A woman signs up for a sleep study in a rented clinic above a laundromat. A coin gets flipped; heads, they wake her once, on the Monday; tails, they wake her Monday and again Tuesday, and between the two they give her something that erases the Monday entirely. Every time she wakes it's the same room, same chair, same minibar humming, and she's being asked a question she has no memory of being asked. She's the sole narrator, first person, present tense, no framing device — no scientist playing back tapes, no epilogue that reorders things. The reader recieves exactly what she gets and nothing more.

What I'm stuck on isn't the plot, it's the sentence level. I have conventions for a gap between what the character knows and what the reader knows (dramatic irony), for a gap between the narrator's register and the character's (free indirect style), for a narrator who withholds or lies. What I don't have is any convention for a narrator who is uncertain about where she is in her own book. When she thinks this is the first time they've asked me, there's no craft term I know that tells me whether that's a mistake, a limitation, or just true-as-of-here.

So: restate the halfer/thirder disagreement, and the self-locating-belief machinery both sides are leaning on, as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually live in — point of view, narrative distance, scene versus summary, what a reader is licensed to infer at a given position in the text, unreliable narration, the difference between the character's evidence and the text's evidence. Use my words. Only reach for the formal ones when you're explicitly pointing at something my vocabulary can't hold.

Then give me the residue report, and be honest, because the residue is the part I want:

- which claims land cleanly, meaning there's something in craft that just *is* that claim;
- which ones you had to bend, and what got bent in the bending;
- which ones have no counterpart at all. Is credence one of them? Can a betting disposition even be expressed in a framework where belief is a property of a voice on a page rather than of an agent in a world? I genuinely don't know, and I suspect the answer is going to be uncomfortable.

And then the judgment call, which is what I actually care about: does the residue point at a real hole in my toolkit — some structural inability to talk about a narrator's uncertainty about her own position — or does it suggest the philosophers are fighting over something less substantive than it looks, two answers to two different questions that only sound like one question? If it's the second, tell me which question each side is really answering, and tell me whether a novelist who can't state that difference loses anything when she writes the book anyway.

One rule: don't smuggle. If you catch yourself translating "the thirder is right" into "the reader knows something the character doesn't," stop and flag that you've just assumed a thing about who the reader is and what they're allowed to know. I'd rather have a clunkier translation I can trust than a smooth one that quietly converts my intuitions into premises.

No reading list at the end, please — I won't read it, and I'd rather you spent those words on the residue.
