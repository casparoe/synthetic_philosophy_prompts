Our AI safety reading group just hit a chapter arguing that intransitive preferences are irrational because a cycle A > B > C > A lets someone walk you around it: start you off holding A, charge you a small premium to swap to B, then to C, then back to A, and you end up where you started with less. The author leans on that hard -- an intransitive agent is basically a money pump, a machine that drains itself -- and uses it to claim transitivity is a non-negotiable design constraint, because an agent built without it can be bled of arbitary amounts of whatever it values. One of us objects that the pump only runs if the agent is myopic and can't see the cycle coming, so what the machine picture really indicts is a lack of planning, not the shape of the preferences; someone else says that's beside the point since anything we actually deploy will be myopic. Can you audit the thing for me: what plays the machine, the coin slot, the three options and the walk back to the start; which disanalogies genuinely threaten the design-constraint conclusion and which are just noise (transaction costs, foresight, precommitment, the premiums having to be paid in something the agent cares about at the moment it pays them); what the machine picture quietly assumes about an agent being a chooser who faces swaps one at a time and never a sequence; and whether the constraint survives. If there's a better analogy for what intransitivity actually costs you, give me that instead.
