I'm giving the three-minute opening remarks tomorrow at a workshop that's half consciousness researchers and half working film editors and colorists — it's in a cinematheque's screening room, and the second half were sold it as being about how cuts and color timing *feel*, not about neural latency, so I have to keep both groups in the room without letting either one feel it's their workshop. The spine of the thing is color phi: two lights of different colors flashed in sequence get seen as one light that changes color somewhere in the middle of its apparent motion, and the argument is whether that mid-flight change is Stalinesque (the show was already doctored before it reached anybody) or Orwellian (the record got rewritten afterwards and the experience was never like that). Two of the people most responsible for keeping that vocabulary alive are sitting in the front row and haven't spoken to each other in a decade, so the remarks can't referee it or come down on a side. Make the structure of the speech do the work rather than the content: plant something in the first minute (a word, a detail about the room, a small claim about what we're all about to see) whose sense is revised by something in the last minute, so that the audience's own memory of the opening has to be edited in flight while they're sitting there — and let that, not any argument, be the thing the speech is actually about. It should end by handing over to the first talk without resolving anything.
