Next week I record a forty-minute episode of my formal epistemology podcast with a philosopher who defends radical probabilism in an unusually uncompromising form. My listeners are mostly graduate students, plus a substantial minority of outsiders who have met Bayes' theorem but nothing beyond it, so the question set needs to open gently and then go technical rather than the reverse.

His position, as I read it, has four parts: no experience ever renders a proposition certain for a subject, so ordinary conditionalization describes an idealization that is never actually instantiated; all perceptual learning is probability kinematics, in which experience shifts one's probabilities over a partition while leaving one's conditional probabilities on the members of that partition untouched; the partition and the new weights on it are fixed by the phenomenology of the experience itself rather than by any proposition the subject could report; and there is no standard of correctness for a response to evidence independent of diachronic coherence — coherence is the whole of the norm, not a constraint on a norm that lives somewhere else. He has recently been arguing that this package is the only one that blocks bootstrapping arguments about the reliability of perception.

Please draft nine questions, ordered as an episode should run, with the first two accessible to the outsiders and the rest aimed at the technical cruxes. For each question give three things: the question as I would read it aloud; a one- or two-line note on what he is most likely to say in reply; and a follow-up keyed to that specific reply rather than to the question, whose purpose is to catch the answer he will actually give instead of repeating the first question more loudly. Flag separately the two or three questions on which he is most likely to deliver a polished, rehearsed response that does not engage the difficulty, and supply a shorter re-ask for each.

I want at least one question on each of the following:

- The provenance of the numbers. His account requires experience to deliver exact new weights on each member of a partition while delivering nothing propositional. Where do the weights come from, and what does it mean to say that phenomenology fixes them rather than merely causing them?
- Rigidity, and the cases in which it fails. Holding conditional probabilities fixed amounts to assuming that the evidential import of the experience is stable; yet the situations that matter are precisely those in which the subject simultaneously learns that the lighting is poor, that he has been drugged, or that he is exhausted. What is his rule for those cases, and does possessing such a rule undercut the claim that kinematics is more basic than conditionalization?
- Order. Two successive kinematic updates do not in general commute, whereas conditionalizing on two items of hard evidence is indifferent to the order in which they arrive. Should we conclude that kinematics is not an updating rule at all but only a constraint on which pairs of probability functions count as coherent?
- The minimum-distance characterization. There is a classic result to the effect that his rule is the unique update minimizing relative entropy subject to the constraints on the partition. Is that a vindication of the rule or a redescription of it, and what becomes of the rule when the constraints do not take the form of a partition?
- Zeros, and the ban on certainty. Under rigidity, a hypothesis whose conditional probability is zero on every member of the partition can never acquire positive probability. Does his view oblige him to rule zero probabilities out of the algebra altogether, and if not, how does he accommodate the fact that some learning seems to consist precisely in coming to entertain a possibility one had not entertained?
- The enlargement rejoinder. A classical Bayesian will say that nothing prevents her from adding a variable for the character of the subject's experience and conditionalizing on the proposition that he is having that very experience: if that proposition is certain when he has it, kinematics is redundant, and if it is not certain, he has not explained the source of his weights. How does he meet the dilemma, and can he say what his view forbids that the enriched conditionalizer's does not?
- Bootstrapping. Does probability kinematics actually block the easy-knowledge style argument, or merely slow it down? If a long run of small kinematic shifts can accumulate into near-certainty that one's vision is reliable without any independent check, is that bootstrapping, and if it is not, why not?
- Deference. If a trusted expert reports her probability for a hypothesis rather than for any observation, what should he do? Adopting a reported marginal is not an update on a partition; it is also unclear what becomes of his own conditional probabilities, or how he is to proceed when two experts report figures that are not jointly reachable by any single kinematic move.
- The normative question. If coherence is the whole of the norm and no response to evidence is uniquely correct, in what sense does experience teach him anything, and what survives of the thought that two agents who begin far apart and receive the same experiences might converge?

Close with one question designed to make him name a concrete case in which his view and a classical conditionalizer's issue in different advice — a bet to accept or refuse, an experiment to run, a clinical judgment — and tell me in advance whether he is likely to have such a case or whether the question will end in a restatement of the view. Leave out anything about his influences, his training, or how he came to philosophy, and do not ask me to supply references; everything you write should stand on its own.
