Write an essay on whether the frame problem is one problem or two, and reach a verdict. My working hunch, which you should treat as a hypothesis to test rather than as a premise: the original technical puzzle — writing down enough axioms to say what persists when an action occurs — was substantially defused by nonmonotonic formalisms such as circumscription, fluent occlusion, and successor-state axioms, whereas the difficulty Fodor had in mind when he argued that cognitive processes are isotropic, so that in principle any belief may bear on any other, is not touched by them at all. The substantive question is what kind of problem the second one is. Take seriously the proposal that isotropy is only fatal if you assume a reasoner must be able to survey all of its relevance relations at once, and that if relevance is instead a matter of spending scarce computation on the inferences with the highest expected epistemic value, then holism describes the shape of that value landscape rather than an impossibility result. Then weigh the obvious regress — the metareasoning that allocates the computation must itself decide which computations are worth doing — and the stronger claim that nothing short of a non-representational, situationally embedded engagement with the environment escapes it. Say plainly whether Fodor's generalization is legitimate, and whether, if it is, it indicts computationalism as such or only one particular way of budgeting inference. A conditional verdict is acceptable provided the conditions are stated exactly; a both-sides shrug is not. No references needed — I will add them myself.
