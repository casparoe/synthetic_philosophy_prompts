My uncle is retiring after fourty years running a small machining shop, and I've been asked to give the speech at his retirement dinner. Half the room worked for him, some of them for three decades. He was genuinely good to people — above-market pay, he quietly covered tuition for workers' kids, and he never once told anyone how to do their job. But he could also have fired any of them between coffee and lunch, for any reason or no reason, and everyone knew it; no contracts, no union, no say in how the place was run. The year the orders dried up he kept everybody on the payroll and cut his own draw to nothing — that's the story they'll all be telling, and it's also the clearest proof that their livelihoods hung entirely on one man's mood.

So: write the speech, about four minutes, warm, funny where it can be. The hard constraint is that it can't say or imply that he gave those people their freedom — not because he was cruel, but because that isnt the kind of freedom one person is ever in a position to grant as a favor. It should sound like love rather than a grievance aired at a party, and it has to work for old machinists who felt the dependence in their bones without ever reading a word of political philosophy, and for my cousin who wrote her dissertation on republican liberty and will be checking whether I got it right.
