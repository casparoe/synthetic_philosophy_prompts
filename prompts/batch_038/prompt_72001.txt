Our Thursday epistemology reading group has, over the past term, drifted into something closer to a forecasting exercise than a seminar. We were supposed to be working through the literature on self-knowledge, and early on one member argued for a principle he said went by the initials KK — roughly, that if you know something, you thereby know that you know it, or are at any rate always in a position to find out that you do. Another member, Priya, said that was obviously false, and when pressed to say what exactly was false about it she declined to offer a formulation, insisting she could only answer case by case. So we have spent eight weeks feeding her cases, always in the same fixed wording: given everything the subject has to go on, is she in a position to know, by reflection alone and right now, that she knows that p? Her answers, in order. First, a ward nurse who picks out which of her patients is about to deteriorate hours before the monitors flag anything, who is right nearly every time, who cannot say what she goes on, who calls it a hunch and who is emphatic that she is only guessing: the nurse does know which patients are deteriorating, Priya says, but she is not in a position to know that she knows, and asked what the nurse is missing Priya would say only that the nurse has nothing to go on. Second, a splitting headache: you know you have it, and you know that you know it, immediately, with no need for evidence about yourself or for any track record. Third, a column of figures you have added twice, once in the morning and once in the afternoon, sober, rested and unhurried, with the same total both times: you know the total and you know that you know it. Fourth, a radiologist who spots a small lesion and can describe the features that show it, but who was told ten minutes earlier by a colleague — falsely, though neither of them realises it — that the imaging software has been scrambling contrast all morning; she weighs the warning, dismisses it, and stands by her reading. Priya says the radiologist knows what the scan shows and is nonetheless not in a position to know that she knows: the warning, false as it is, has taken that away from her. Fifth, the one that unsettled all of us: between six and seven on a cold morning you gradually stop feeling cold, the change being far too slight to notice from one minute to the next. At six you feel cold and know that you feel cold; at seven you do not feel cold; she will not name a minute at which anything changes; and — this she will not retreat from — there are minutes in which you still feel cold and yet no longer know that you feel cold, the knowledge, as she put it, going before the feeling. Sixth, a man who has had far too much to drink, who looks down the road, who believes it is empty, and whose belief is in fact correct: he does not know the road is empty, because his looking is not up to it tonight, and he does not know that he fails to know either.

The case we send her on Thursday is this. A sonographer performs a routine scan and correctly identifies an abnormality; she can say exactly what she is looking at and why it matters, she is well rested, she has no doubts, and nothing in the past hour has unsettled her. But there sits in the departmental shared inbox — one she has never opened and that no colleague has mentioned to her — a maintenance notice recording that her machine has an intermittent display fault which reverses left and right on roughly one scan in twenty, that the fault cannot be detected from the images themselves, and that nobody has yet found a way to predict which scans it will affect. Her scan this morning happened to be one of the unaffected ones. Is she, on our fixed formulation, in a position to know that she knows what she is looking at?

What I want is not a verdict on the sonographer in the abstract, since I have no independent idea what the right answer is; I want to know what Priya will say and what she must be running to say it. So: predict her answer with a rough degree of confidence. Then reconstruct at least three candidate rules that would reproduce all six of her answers — stated precisely enough that I could check each against the record myself — and say what each implies for the sonographer. I expect some obvious candidates are already dead, and I would rather be shown than told: I assume a rule on which knowledge always puts you in a position to know that you know cannot survive her fourth answer, and that a rule on which second-order knowledge requires the grounds of the first-order belief to be articulable cannot survive it either, but I want that checked rather than granted, and I want to know which of her answers does the killing in each case. Then say which surviving rule you would bet on and what in the record earns it that place, attending in particular to the fact that the radiologist and the sonographer look like deliberate inverses of one another and to whether a single rule can treat them the way she has. Flag wherever the six answers come closest to outright inconsistency: my own suspicion is that the headache and the cold morning cannot both stand, but I cannot see what she could surrender to make them cohere, and I also cannot see how her first-order verdicts, which look externalist to me, sit with second-order verdicts that look internalist — tell me whether that combination is stable or whether one of the six answers is doing covert work to hold it together. Finally, if she answers the sonographer case the way you expect, name the case we should send the following week, the one that would separate whatever two rules remain tied at that point, and say what each of those two would say about it.
