So. I've been reading the situationism stuff — the argument that character traits aren't real because experiments keep finding that tiny details of the setting flip whether people help: whether they're in a hurry, whether they just found a coin in a phone booth, that kind of thing. And there's a pattern in how the back-and-forth goes that I don't have a word for, and it's driving me nuts becuse it shows up on both sides.

The pattern: the claim in play is a tendency, not a guarantee. Honest people tell the truth more often than dishonest people; that's the whole content. So when someone produces a single case where the tendency didn't show up, one side says that's not a refutation at all — one case is noise, you have to average over a bunch of occasions before anything is visible. Which seems right to me. But then the same person turns around and says: fine, so the trait only pays off across dozens of occasions — except what virtue ethics actually needs is for the honest person to be honest in the one case that matters, when they're being tested and there is no second trial, and there the average is worthless. And the first point just gets dropped. It got them out of the counterexample, and then it stopped being relevant.

Mirror image, same shape: a fan of the experiments says this one study shows a trivial detail changed behavior, therefore the trait isn't doing the work — a single result treated as decisive. Then it comes out that the effect didn't replicate, or that a correlation around .3 is actually normal-to-good for this kind of thing, and suddenly the reply is that no single study could ever establish a trait either, you need the whole pattern. Same switch, other direction.

What I want to know: does this have a name? Not the general area. I know there's a standing fight about how much evidence an experiment can carry, I know "cherry-picking," I know people say "moving the goalposts." It isn't exactly either of those, because each of the two standards being invoked is legitimate on its own — averaging really is how you evaluate a tendency, and sometimes a single case really does carry the weight (I'd take one trial of a medicine saving one life seriously). The thing I can't name is the switch between them, which somehow always lands on whichever standard helps whoever is making it, and never gets announced as a switch — it's delivered as if that standard had been the obvious one all along.

Don't stop at the label, the carving is the part I care about: what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, where its edges sit.
