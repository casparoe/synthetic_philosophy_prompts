I maintain a service whose job is to decide which other services to believe. Trust propagates along a chain — a source counts as credible if something we already trust vouches for it — and we multiply confidence at each hop, which is why we cap chain length: past three or four hops an endorsement carries no more weight than a coin flip. Reading about testimony in epistemology (I have no philosophy training), I find the Humean position, that believing a speaker requires positive non-testimonial reasons, and the Reidian position, that it is justified by default absent defeaters, trading the same two objections — bootstrapping against the first, gullibility against the second — for decades, with nobody moving.

My hypothesis is that both sides treat justification as a property of one belief held by one person at one moment, whereas the phenomenon they are describing is a property of a bookkeeping system spread across many agents and many times. In our system trust is not a reason anyone has; it is a balance that gets spent, and that a source can forfeit the right to replenish. Is that a fair diagnosis, or does it miss a disagreement that is genuinely substantive — about default entitlement, say, or about an empirical question as to how independent purportedly corroborating sources really are in a heavily derivative information environment? Tell me what kind of disagreement this is, what question each side is actually answering, and what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all. Argue for the diagnosis rather than surveying positions; I am not looking for references or a reading list.
