I run a tiny philosophy podcast — maybe two hundred listeners, mostly my department — and next month's guest is a philosopher who's just published a book on sexual offences and the criminal law that's explicitly Benthamite in method. I want eight to ten questions, and I don't want any of them to be the kind she could answer by giving a general defence of utilitarianism or a tour of what Bentham happened to think. Her book's core is something she calls the four-channel test: any case for legally prohibiting some consensual sexual practice has to run through one of four channels — demonstrable harm to one of the parties, a defect in consent, second-order mischief, meaning alarm and danger spilling onto people who aren't party to the act, or bare antipathy — and the fourth isn't a channel at all, it's just the admission that the argument ran out. Three of her commitments are worth building questions around. First, she holds that disgust has no representational content about wrongness whatsoever, that it's a contamination monitor drafted into ethics, so appealing to it in law is a category mistake rather than a miscalibration; she's extremely impatient with people who say disgust is merely unreliable. Second, she argues that widespread antipathy is itself a punishment, administered socially without the magistrate, and that when the law ratifies it the prohibition is harmful over and above whatever the penalty does. Third, she concedes that the alarm-and-danger channel is the only real battleground and thinks most opponents of decriminalisation have quietly migrated into it.

Two of the questions should press the worry I cant shake, which is that the third channel is a back door for the fourth: if the distress of onlookers counts as alarm, then antipathy gets back in under a respectable name and the test can't exclude the thing it was built to exclude. I want a question that makes her say what turns an alarm-claim into a legitimate one rather than relabeled antipathy, plus the follow-up for the answer she'll most likely give — I expect she'll say alarm has to attach to the non-party's own interests, so the follow-up should test that against a parent who says the sexual environment their kids grow up in is one of their interests. And I'd like a short sequence on whether anything could survive her test and actually surprise her. She tends to answer hard cases by shipping them off to social science, "that's an empirical question," so build the sequence so the empirical answer is granted in full and she has to say what she'd do with it: suppose general adoption of some practice measurably lowered birth rates, and did so not by displacing other sexual activity but because the people who took it up had fewer children on net. Which channel does that land in, does it justify prohibition, and if not, which channel just told us no?

Round the rest out with: whether the four-channel test is supposed to govern non-sexual conduct too, and if not what's special about sex; how her category-error claim about disgust sits with her own view that prejudice can be argued out of people through publicity, since a state with no representational content doesnt look like the sort of thing that responds to reasons; whether consent does all the work she needs in channel two, given money, authority and long dependency; and what she makes of Bentham's own hedging — as far as I can tell, and correct me if I'm wrong, he was willing to let opinion-shaping and indirect legislation do work the criminal law shouldn't, which sits oddly beside her claim that socially administered antipathy is already a punishment in its own right. No what-drew-you-to-this-topic material. And for every question give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not a generic one.
