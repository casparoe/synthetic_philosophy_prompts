I'm writing a feature for a general-interest science magazine about the small group of researchers who want to design provably safe self-improving agents, and I'd like the piece to end on a forward-looking note: what is actually still open. The Löbian obstacle sits at the center of the story — the result that an agent which gates its own decisions on proving things cannot coherently trust its own reasoning, because a proof system able to establish its own soundness would prove everything. Around it I can identify four live directions: tiling agents whose successors run deliberately weaker proof systems; reflective oracles that let a system query its own future decisions; replacing proof with probabilistic or bounded-resource self-reasoning; and giving up on self-verification entirely in favor of oversight from outside the agent. Which of these deserves the most column inches, and which should I present as settled or as a dead end? Please defend the ordering rather than merely produce it, and state your criteria explicitly — how much settling the question would change what anyone builds, whether paper-and-pencil progress is still available, and how many other problems in the area hang off the answer. I'd also like your read on one objection in particular. A researcher told me the whole framing is a category mistake: anything that will actually be deployed reasons with trained neural networks rather than first-order proofs, so Löb's theorem constrains nothing that will ever exist, and this literature is solving a problem no one has. Give me the strongest version of that objection and the strongest reply to it, and tell me whether it is serious enough that I should restructure the piece around it instead of around the technical agenda.
