I'm trying to pin down what you should demand of a decision theory that claims to handle Keynesian uncertainty — uncertainty proper, not risk — before I burn another month on candidates. Write me the spec: what such a theory has to deliver to count as adequate, with the case or consideration motivating each item. Some things I already suspect belong on it, treat these as a starting point and not a cap: it can't just launder "we have no idea" into a number and then run expected utility on it; it has to make room for Keynes's point that how much evidence bears on a hypothesis is a separate question from which way it points; it has to explain why sitting on cash rather than betting is rational and not a failure of nerve when the odds can't be computed; and it has to cope with the beauty-contest problem — that adopting a rule, and everyone knowing you've adopted it, changes the very thing the rule was forecasting.

Then tell me which of these are genuinely non-negotiable and which are one camps prejudices wearing a lab coat. Is "the theory has to say what to actually do" a requirement, or something a Keynesian is entitled to refuse? And the bit I most want: is some subset of the list jointly unsatisfiable? My hunch is that wanting objective probability relations (Treatise Keynes) and wanting irreducible unquantifiable uncertainty (General Theory Keynes) can't both be delivered, so anyone after both is chasing a contradiction — land somewhere on that and defend it, don't hand me a menu. Close with the single desideratum you think eliminates the most existing proposals.
