I'm in a reading group on climate ethics and last week we burned two hours going in circles on the inefficacy problem, and now I've been volunteered to present something next week that actually pins down where the disagreement lives. I think the published debate is way to tidy — it usually comes down to "your act has a 1-in-N chance of triggering the harm, so your expected contribution is harm/N, done." So I'd like to give the group a dialogue that runs the argument through a case where that reply doesn't just fire automatically.

Could you write one, somewhere around 1500–2000 words, between two smart people who disagree about whether a particular individual has a decisive reason to cut back? Here's the case — adjust the numbers if you like but keep the structure. A regional grid operator keeps three old coal units in must-run reserve. Cheap renewables cover base load; the coal units only get dispatched when total demand crosses a forecast threshold, and they come on in whole 40 MW blocks for a minimum six-hour run, so a block-run is 240,000 kWh. Each block-run puts out a fixed quantity of SO2 that measurably worsens asthma in one town downwind, call it 300 extra ER visits over the season. A single household's extra 2 kWh is roughly a hundred-thousandth of one block-run. The twist that makes this not the usual case: the threshold isn't fixed. The operator sets tomorrow's dispatch threshold from a forecast that is continuously updated on yesterday's observed aggregate demand, so a reduction in demand doesn't merely fail to trigger anything — it lowers the predicted peak and therefore lowers the level at which the coal block gets called. The system re-equilibrates, and the objector's claim is that this re-equilibration eats an individual's effect faster than the individual can produce it.

Characters: Maya, who thinks the individual act makes no morally relevant difference and stresses that this is a point about the *shape* of the causation rather than the effect just being small; and Devon, who thinks a reason to cut back survives all of that anyway. Both at full strength, no strawmen, and neither one should get to just say the same thing louder. They need to respond to what the other actually said, including conceding sub-points where concession is the right move.

A few things I specifically want in there:
- About two-thirds of the way in they should agree to stipulate every empirical fact on the table — block size, the threshold feedback, the probabilities, all of it — and then discover they still disagree, and try to work out what the residue is. I want them to consider at least three candidates: whether expected-harm reasoning over a tiny probability of a big harm can generate a reason at all, whether the right unit of assessment is the act or a policy/disposition, and whether "your act makes no difference" is even the kind of claim that gets you to "you have no reason to refrain" without a further premise nobody has stated.
- The "but imagine everyone did that" line should not appear in the first third, and when it does appear it should get properly attacked and then partly survive the attack.
- Each of them should make at least one move that isn't in the standard repertoire. For Maya, something about the fact that the feedback mechanism means individual restraint gets learned *into* the forecast, so the harm tracks a population's dispositions rather than anybody's acts — plus the uncomfortable consequence she has to swallow because of it. For Devon, something about how the number of block-runs and the threshold level come apart, so a case can be built that doesn't depend on triggering anything.
- It should end with no winner, but with the two of them jointly formulating the open question they've been circling, stated precisely enough that I could put it on a slide.

Don't cite anything and don't name-drop philosophers or papers; they should argue from the case, not from a literature, and I'll add references myself if I end up needing them. Fine to give them a setting — I pictured Maya's kitchen with the electric kettle going, which is either too cute or exactly right, your call.
