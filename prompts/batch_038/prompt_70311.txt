I have a vote next month on the board of a private endowment whose charter commits it to establishing a permanent human settlement beyond Earth within this century. This year's allocation is $500M, and the realistic options are two. Commit now: a crewed founding mission on a 2041 departure, small cohort, political institutions we help design in advance — our own analysts put the chance of a settlement that is both durable and not, in retrospect, a catastrophe (starved, authoritarian, permanently dependent on Earth) at perhaps 40 percent. Or hold: fund robotic precursors and life-support engineering for three decades, so that whoever goes, goes later and safer.

Two complications. A state consortium has announced a crewed program on a comparable timeline that we cannot stop, and our staff think the money would buy genuine leverage over crew selection, safety margins, and the founding charter; on this reading, waiting does not prevent a bad first colony, it merely withdraws us from the room where it is designed. And the charter requires the board to certify that any grant "will not bring into existence persons whose lives would be worse than never having been born." Counsel says the sentence cannot simply be struck, and I cannot see how it can be signed under either option. I have read enough of the non-identity literature to know that the colonists of a botched 2041 mission would owe their existence to our decision, and I still cannot accept the conclusion that their suffering is therefore nobody's harm.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the other trustees before the vote: about 900 words, with a definite recommendation (commit, hold, or a third course specified concretely enough to be actionable), the reasoning behind it, and the strongest available version of the case against rather than a strawman. Say what would change my mind between now and the vote, and be candid about which of the figures above are load-bearing and which are decoration. No references; this is an internal document.
