I am preparing a chapter for an interdisciplinary volume on the methodology of animal-mind research, and I have been reading Descartes on the beast-machine with an eye to how the argument has been transmitted rather than to whether it is true. In the process I have run into a pattern that recurs across centuries and that I cannot name, and I want to know whether that is a gap in my vocabulary or a gap in the literature. I am not after sources or a reading list, which I have no time to chase down; I am after the carving.

Here is the pattern, as concretely as I can state it.

A theorist holds, flatly and as part of a general metaphysics, that an entire class of beings has no mental life — no thought, no feeling, no inner episode of any kind. The denial is argued for at the level of doctrine, from considerations about what thought requires. But when the same theorist turns to explaining particular pieces of behaviour — a dog that comes when called, an animal that avoids a place where it was once hurt, a horse that answers a signal from across a field, a bird that greets its owner — the explanation offered is written in the vocabulary of the very thing just denied. The animal notices, remembers, expects, is alarmed, wants to please, has learned that the sign no longer means what it meant. The theorist does not mark this talk as loose, as shorthand, as a manner of speaking, or as a useful fiction. There is no hedge, no avowal that the mentalistic description is not meant literally, no note that it is a convenience adopted for exposition. The mentalistic account is offered as the actual explanation of the case; the eliminative doctrine is offered as the actual account of what there is. Both registers are explicit, both are earnest, and they are simply never brought into contact with one another.

Descartes is my principal instance. The published doctrine is that beasts are automata, argued from the absence of language and of general reason; yet across the correspondence and the more physiological writings he concedes that animals can be trained, that they recognise their keepers, that they retain traces of past experience, that they signal to their own kind, and he is willing to entertain a bird taught to produce a greeting while insisting that this demonstrates nothing about thought. Commentators then split. One camp says the concessions are so extensive that the automaton thesis survives only as a slogan; the other says every concession is mechanical and the thesis stands intact. What strikes me is that neither camp has a term for the structural feature that makes the dispute so hard to settle — the fact that the disagreement is not about what was said, since both strands are said, but about whether the two strands were ever meant to be read against each other.

The pattern is not period-bound. In the experimental literature of the mid-twentieth century one finds papers whose theory section insists that the organism has no states worth calling mental, and whose results and discussion sections are full of what the rat expects, hesitates over, and has discovered about the apparatus. More recently, one finds write-ups of machine systems that deny any understanding in the framing and then analyse the system throughout in terms of what it takes the input to be, what it is trying to do, and where it gets confused. In each case the eliminative claim and the intentional explanation are published together, by the same author, without anyone flagging the switch.

I have looked under headings that seem nearby, and none of them fits, though I may be looking in the wrong places. It is not a performative contradiction: nothing the theorist does is ruled out by the doctrine, since asserting is not what the doctrine denies to animals. It is not fictionalism or instrumentalism, because the whole point is that the mentalistic talk is never presented as pretence — there is no avowed gap between literal content and intended content, which is what makes those views stable. It is not esoteric writing, because neither book is private: the doctrine and the case-by-case explanations appear in the same published work, or in letters that were meant to circulate. And it is not special pleading or an ad hoc rescue, since the intentional vocabulary is not deployed to fend off an objection; it is the default explanatory style, used even when nothing is at stake.

So: is there an established concept, in philosophy of mind, in the history of philosophy, in philosophy of science, or in the methodological literature on animal cognition, for this configuration — a flat eliminative doctrine coexisting, in one author and without any marker of pretence, with unflagged intentional explanation at the level of cases? If there is, state it precisely and tell me exactly where my description overshoots or undershoots it: which of the features I have listed the concept does not require, which relevant features I have omitted, and which of my near neighbours it is most often confused with. If there is not, coin a term and define it so that it excludes each near-miss — the theorist who does avow a pretence, the theorist whose doctrine is merely under-specified, the theorist who contradicts himself within a single argument — and give me two or three boundary cases where it is genuinely unclear whether the term applies. Either way, tell me whether the concept, once fixed, is neutral between the two readings of Descartes, or whether possessing it forces one of them.

Finally, please say what would change your verdict: what consideration would lead you to conclude that one of the established concepts does cover this after all, or that I have run together two phenomena that ought to be separated. And indicate where you think your own carving is most likely to fail — the case that would show the definition you settle on is too broad, or too narrow.
