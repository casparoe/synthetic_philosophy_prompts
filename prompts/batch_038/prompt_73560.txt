I sat in on a city council session about a pilot program that would let judges sentence some offenders — first-time, low risk of reoffending, victim not asking for punishment — to a track with no jail time: restitution, a restorative conference, community service, and supervision that ends once the person is clearly not a danger. Two panelists got into it and I honestly couldnt tell who was landing punches.

Nadia, for the track: nobody ever basically deserves anything. What you do comes out of a history and a nervous system you didn't pick, so the only things that can justify locking someone up are protecting people, rehabilitating them, and — within tight limits — deterring others. If a guy is no risk and the victim doesn't want him gone, the extra years are just harm with no function.

Owen, against: fine as far as it goes, but it drops the one thing the offender is owed *as an agent*. Holding someone responsible isn't a technique for managing risk, it's the only way to address a person at all. And her view has no floor: if somebody is dangerous but hasn't done anything yet, the only question left is whether detention pays.

Nadia: mine has a floor, it's just a different one — you may only impose the least harmful means to safety, proportionate to the actual danger, and the burden is always on whoever wants to restrict. Yours has no ceiling: once you've decided he deserves it, the amount is set by how bad the act was rather than by how much good the harm does. That's how you get twenty years for a theft.

Owen: then your pilot is desert in disguise. You keep the restitution, keep the conference, keep the service requirement — none of that protects anybody or fixes anybody. It's there because he owes it. You just don't want to say the word.

Nadia: it isn't owing, it's repair. It fixes the damage and rebuilds trust, and if some cheaper, gentler set of measures did that as well I'd switch to them tomorrow. Could you say the same about the jail time you're defending?

Owen said he could — that a person who is never called on to answer for himself has been treated as less than a person.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step, and flag anywhere one of them is just changing the subject? I especially want to know whether the desert-in-disguise charge lands, whether the no-floor-on-prevention charge lands, or whether both are overblown. And tell me how confident you are in each verdict, plus which single point you're least sure about.
