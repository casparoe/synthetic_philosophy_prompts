"The hole argument is a theorem, first noticed by Einstein himself in 1913, showing that general relativity cannot be deterministic. Pick any bounded region of spacetime — call it the hole — and suppose there's no matter in it. Because Einstein's equations are generally covariant, you're free to relabel the coordinates inside the hole however you like while leaving the labels outside alone, and the reshuffled field is still a perfectly good solution of the same equations. If coordinates are mere labels nothing has changed; but a substantivalist holds that the points of the manifold are real individuals over and above whatever fields live on them, so for her the reshuffled solution says something genuinely different, namely that the metric is distributed over the points differently. Same physical set-up going in, different metric at every point of the hole coming out — so substantivalism plus general relativity buys you radical future indeterminism, plus facts about the world that no observation could ever reveal, since nothing you measure could tell you which of the infinitely many solutions is the actual one. Earman and Norton pressed this argument in the 1980s and concluded that spacetime points don't exist; that's now the consensus reading, and it's why physicists happily say the metric is just one more dynamical field with no fixed stage underneath it. The familiar reply — that diffeomorphic models represent one and the same physical situation — is of course available, but notice that it begs the question, because it assumes from the start the thing general covariance already told us, that coordinate labels carry no physical content. Either way the moral is the same: any generally covariant theory of gravity is indeterministic, and the only honest way out is to give up general covariance."
