I work on pre-deployment evaluations for an assistant system, and in a recent go/no-go review the sentence "it only has to happen once" was used twice, apparently to do two different jobs, and both times it ended the discussion. The first time, a reviewer said it about a measured failure rate of roughly two in a thousand rollouts on an adversarial probe set, arguing that no measured rate, however low, could justify shipping. The second time it was said about a single observed instance of the system fabricating a source in an internal demo, to argue that our whole account of why that behavior occurs must be wrong. I suspect these are different claims wearing one sentence, and that at least one of the uses does not follow.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that sentence might be asserting — about the magnitude of the loss a single event can produce, about what happens to the chance of at least one event as the number of deployments multiplies, about what one observation does to a general explanation, and about the stance an engineer ought to take toward residual risk? For each reading, I want to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false; what assumptions it silently needs (independence of failures, a per-event probability that stays fixed as scale grows, irreversibility of the harm); and whether it would actually support refusing to ship at a two-in-a-thousand rate. Please close by identifying which reading each of the two uses in my meeting was relying on and whether the inference went through.
