I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar: 14 students, one 75-minute meeting. Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about whether running the right computation could ever suffice for moral status, and he had a move I could not answer — that if the correspondence between physical states and program states is permissive enough, a lump of granite implements every finite-state computation there is, so the claim that a system implements a mind turns out to be a fact about the correspondence we chose rather than about the system itself.

Design an in-class activity that puts my students in both of our positions. They should begin by dividing a fixed, scarce pool of moral standing among a docket of concrete systems (a deployed chatbot, a climate model, a rock, a high-fidelity neural emulation — choose the docket), with an assigned advocate for each, and only later discover that the criterion they settled on either grants standing to something plainly absurd or requires a distinction they cannot draw without already presupposing the answer. Do not let the words implementation, mapping, or triviality surface until the debrief.

Give me the setup and materials, the roles and rounds with timings, and a debrief that ties specific moments in the room to the underlying distinctions. Build in at least one system that plausibly supports two incompatible claimants at once, and make the group dispose of it. Finally, tell me what will predictably break the first time I run this, and which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know to supply it myself.
