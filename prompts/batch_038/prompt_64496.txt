I run a jurisprudence reading group at a small law school and for two terms now we've had the same fight every other week about the Hart–Devlin debate, and its gone nowhere. One of us (me, mostly) is a Hartian: the law needs a harm-based limit, and majority disgust is just prejudice wearing a badge. The other is a fairly sophisticated Devlin sympathizer who insists that Hart's position is itself a substantive moral view smuggled in as a neutral criterion, and that you can't run a legal system without committing to some picture of what a decent shared life looks like. We each think the other is attacking a straw man, which is the classic symptom of a debate that's partly verbal and nobody has bothered to say which part. So I'd like you to act as a philosopher of law who has worked on legal moralism and also has some grip on the empirical social science of social cohesion, and draft the adversarial-collaboration statement the two of us should have produced instead of shouting. Start with a page or so of brainstorming before you draft anything: map where the two positions genuinely touch, where they're just using "society" or "harm" in seperate senses, and which disagreements could in principle be settled by evidence rather than by more exegesis.

The material I want engaged is the weedy stuff, not the textbook two-paragraph summary. Both parties should be pinned to the specific theses Hart identified in Devlin: the disintegration thesis (a society's shared morality is what holds it together, so a change in that morality threatens the society's existence, on roughly the model of treason) and the conservative thesis (a society may enforce a morality simply because it is that society's morality). I want the statement to say which of those a contemporary moralist actually needs, whether the conservative thesis is a seperate claim or just question-begging — Dworkin pressed something like this in his Yale Law Journal reply — and whether the disintegration thesis is even the sort of thing that could be confirmed or refuted. Then, as the shared test case, don't use the homosexuality question that started the whole business after the Wolfenden Report; both sides have their lines memorized on that one and it's politically exhausted. Use R v Brown instead, the House of Lords decision of 1993 on a group of men convicted for consensual sadomasochistic acts carried out in private over a number of years; the majority was 3–2, with Lord Mustill and Lord Slynn dissenting, and the headnote frames the holding as resting on public policy requiring that "society be protected by criminal sanctions against a cult of violence" along with the danger of serious injury and of young men being proselytised and corrupted. It's ideal for us because the injuries weren't permanent, everything was consensual and private, and the majority still appealed to something like the character of the society as a whole. Have each side say what the case reveals about its own view and about the other's, and have each write down in advance a prediction about a well-designed study — say a longitudinal comparison of jurisdictions that decriminalized consensual adult conduct against ones that didn't, tracking whatever proxies for cohesion you think are defensible — plus what result would count against them. Work in two further cruxes I suspect we've both been dishonest about. First, whether "harm" can do the work at all: if harm is allowed to include damage to the participants' characters, to third parties' sense of what's acceptable, and to the background conditions of trust, does the liberal position just collapse into the moralist one, or is there a principled stopping point? Second, the perfectionist move — if I'm remembering Raz right, a harm principle that rests on its own substantive view of the good life is already a species of perfectionism, so the Hartian owes an account of why her picture of the good is neutral and the moralist's isn't. Let the parties say whether that asymmetry is real or a tu quoque, and where you can have them sign shared claims: I'd guess enforcement has costs and needs proportionality, the criminal law is a blunt instrument, Devlin's own prudential cautions about gradual change and respect for privacy are mostly about implementation rather than principle, and Hart himself permits paternalism that Mill wouldn't have allowed.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table with columns for the issue, the moralist's formulation in wording she'd accept, the liberal's formulation in wording he'd accept, whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive (and if verbal, which word is doing the work), and the test or argument both would accept as bearing on it with each side's recorded prediction. Finish with a short section on what would actually move each of them and what stays unresolved. Roughly 1,800 words, and please don't give me a bibliography, citations, or page numbers — you won't have net access and I'd much rather you flag which historical details you're unsure about than quietly invent them.
