I am writing a paper for my school's independent-study seminar, and there is a pattern in the art market that I cannot resolve. A panel painted mostly by Rembrandt's workshop assistants, with only a few strokes from the master's own hand, sells as a Rembrandt, for a vast sum. A panel painted entirely by a later hand in the same style — one that experts could not tell apart from the first by looking at it — sells as a forgery, for a small fraction of the price, if it sells as art at all. What conception of a work does this practice presuppose: that the work is the arrangement of pigment on the surface, or the history of how the pigment got there, or something about achievement and whose achievement it was? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, and say whether rival theories could license the same prices. Then tell me how the theory survives two cases that seem to cut against it: a heavily restored old master, most of whose visible surface is modern paint added by conservators, still sells as the master's work, while a forgery made entirely of modern paint does not; and an exposed forgery sometimes begins to command a price of its own once its maker becomes famous. Finally, if the market abandoned the theory you reconstruct and judged works by appearance alone, what would museums and auction houses have to do differently?
