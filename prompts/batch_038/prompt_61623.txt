Can you write me a short story, 900 words at the very most, about dispositions — specifically the difference between a fink and a mask?

Here's the setup I want you to work from. In some city everything load-bearing is made of a smart ceramic, and there's a certification office that stamps artifacts fragile or non-fragile without ever stress-testing them, because a real test either destroys the thing or costs more than the thing is worth. They certify off the microstructure instead. Our narrator is one of those certifiers. Two artifacts come across her desk that are structurally identical, layer for layer. Both would shatter if struck — except that one has an internal repair mechanism wired to switch off its fragility the instant anything is about to hit it, so by the time the blow lands it simply isnt fragile anymore; the other keeps its fragility throughout, but sits inside a damping field that eats the impact, so the shattering never gets to happen. Neither has ever broken. Neither ever will. And the office still has to hand down different stamps, and the story should turn on theres being no possible test that tells the two apart — the only difference lives in what would happen in a world where the repair mechanism, or the field, wasn't there.

Two things I want in it. First, don't lecture. Let the characters fight about it, and let one of them make a genuinely strong case that the distinction is empty: same stamp for both, and if a property never shows up anywhere in the world, in any test or any consequence, maybe it isnt a property at all. Make that case good enough that the narrator cant fully answer it. Second, let the narrator get tangled up over whether the second artifact is really masked at all, or whether the field just means the artifact never gets struck in the first place — in which case it's not a manifestation being blocked but a stimulus that never arrives, and maybe the whole mask/fink line is an artifact of how sloppily the office writes down what "being struck" means. That worry should matter to her for reasons that are hers and not the reader's; tie it to something she stands to lose if the stamps get appealed.

End on something. Don't explain the ending.

Then after the story, a note of 150 words or less: where the piece takes liberties with how finks and masks actually work, which contested account of dispositions you leaned on to get the ending you wanted, and what would change your mind — which argument, if it's right, would make you write the ending the other way around.
