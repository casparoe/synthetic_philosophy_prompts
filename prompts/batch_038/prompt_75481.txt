"Arrow's theorem says that once you have three or more options, there's no way to combine individual preference rankings into a collective ranking without giving up something you wanted. Three of the conditions are hard to argue with. If everyone prefers A to B, society has to prefer A to B. The collective ranking of A versus B can't flip just because some candidate C enters or leaves the race — that's independence of irrelevant alternatives, and it's formally the same thing as saying no spoiler can ever change the outcome. And the rule has to work for any possible combination of individual preferences, no matter how weird. Arrow proved that the only rules satisfying all of that are dictatorships: one voter's preferences always determine the result. So a fair voting system isn't a policy preference, it's a mathematical impossibility, which is also why every real election with a strong third-party candidate goes wrong — the spoiler effect is just Arrow's theorem showing up in the wild. The escape hatch is that Arrow was working with ranked ballots, which throw away information about how much you prefer one candidate to another. Approval voting and score voting ask you to rate candidates instead of ranking them, so they sit outside the theorem entirely, which tells you the impossibility was an artifact of a bad modeling choice rather than a deep truth about democracy. And it's the same reason economists in the mid twentieth century gave up on adding up different people's happiness: unless you appoint a dictator, you can't do the sum. Utilitarians have never had a good answer to Arrow."
