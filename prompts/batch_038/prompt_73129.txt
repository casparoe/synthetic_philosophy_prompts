I run the works-in-progress series in my department, and next month's session is with a visitor who defends what she calls generalized etiological teleosemantics. The view: proper functions are fixed by any history of differential retention and reproduction — natural selection is the paradigm case but not the only one — so engineered artifacts, lab-evolved circuits, and reinforcement-trained networks have genuine proper functions, and therefore genuine representational content, where the content of a state is the condition whose obtaining the state's consumer system was retained for detecting. She's emphatic that this content is original rather than derived: a trained network doesn't just inherit the intentionality of its designers, because the retention history that fixes its function is its own, not thiers.

I don't want questions that just let her restate all that — everyone in the room will have read the pre-circulated paper. Press where the etiological tradition always gets pressed, and assume she has good answers ready. Specifically: (1) Swampman — if the relevant history is any retention history at all, what does she say about a duplicate that pops into existence with no history whatsoever, and does her answer cost her the claim that content is original rather than borrowed from designers? (2) the disjunction problem — a fly-detector fires at BB pellets too, and the stock etiological reply is that only the fly condition explains why the detector was retained; does that reply survive when the retention history is a few months of gradient descent instead of a few million generations, and what fixes the granularity of the content it yeilds (flies? small dark moving things? edibles?). (3) success conditions versus truth conditions — how does a mechanism whose function is defined by what benefits its consumers end up with states that can be false, especially about things that make no difference to success? (4) novel content — representations of quarks, of the far future, of counterfactual situations the retention history never touched. (5) what would count against the view — she answers nearly every objection by saying it smuggles in an unargued intuition that aboutness has to be intrinsic, so I want a final question that can't be met with that move.

So: exactly five numbered questions, in that order, each paired with the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Keep each entry to a few sentences, and give me just the questions — no preamble, no framing for the audience, no suggested readings.
