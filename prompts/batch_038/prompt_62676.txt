Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend — a veterinarian who moonlights as an amateur philosopher of mind, and the only person I know who will defend a seventeenth-century position on its merits rather than its public relations — about the slogan he keeps reaching for whenever animal consciousness comes up: 'animals are mere machines'. He used it to settle two very different questions in a single stroke. First, he said the slogan means Descartes held that a dog being kicked, a rat screaming when its tail is pinched, or a chicken flapping away from a fox involves no felt experience whatsoever, only hydraulics and animal spirits; second, and more aggressively, he said that once you accept the slogan, behavioral evidence simply drops out of the picture — operant conditioning data, preference tests, the whole mirror-self-recognition literature, comparative work on play and consolation behavior — because on the Cartesian view none of that could ever be evidence of anything mental, in animals or, he seemed willing to add, in principle anywhere. I do not think the two moves are licensed by the same claim, and I suspect he is treating one sentence as if it were five or six, some of which are trivial consequences of his physics and at least one of which he probably never asserted.

What I want is the menu, not a summary of the beast-machine doctrine. Take that slogan apart into the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — for instance, a metaphysical claim about what kinds of substance an animal is made of and whether anything immaterial is involved; an explanatory claim about whether every motion of an animal body admits of a complete mechanical account; a phenomenological claim about the presence or absence of felt qualities; a cognitive claim about the absence of judgment, belief, or propositional attitudes; a claim specifically about language and about the capacity to adapt one's behavior to genuinely novel circumstances rather than to a fixed repertoire; and, separately, whatever moral standing claim he thinks follows. For each reading, tell me how demanding it is, whether it is something Descartes's own commitments elsewhere force on him, whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply not a view he held — and, where you know he hedged, say exactly where the hedge sits. I am thinking in particular of the late correspondence with Henry More, in which he seems to withhold only the kind of sensation that depends on thought while granting the kind that depends on a bodily organ; of the letters to the Marquess of Newcastle, where he allows that dogs, horses and monkeys can be trained and appear to display fear, hope and joy, and where he seems willing to credit animals with a bodily memory located in the brain; and of the discussion in the Discourse where he says a machine built in the shape of a monkey would be indistinguishable from a real monkey, while a machine built in the shape of a human being could be detected by two tests that are themselves behavioral. Please tell me whether he is being consistent across those, or whether one of them is really doing all the work while the slogan hides the strain.

Then turn the screws on the second move. If Descartes's own reason for withholding thought from animals is an inference from the absence of speech and general adaptability, is it not an inference from behavior to mind — and does that not make behavioral evidence exactly the right kind of evidence, just pointing the other way? I would like you to say which of the readings, if any, could support the claim that no amount of comparative behavioral data is relevant to whether an animal has a mind, whether that reading is one Descartes himself can hold without undermining his argument against animal thought, and which reading he would most plausibly have invoked instead. Finally, a smaller question I could not resolve over the argument: is 'beast-machine' even a phrase of his, or a later tag attached to him by critics and historians, and does it matter for any of the readings above which of those it is? I would like the whole thing as a numbered list of candidate readings with a verdict on each, in language plain enough that I could read it aloud at our next dinner without sounding like I am giving a lecture.
