European law governing the use of animals in scientific procedures applies to every live non-human vertebrate and — alone among the invertebrates — to live cephalopods. Britain's 2022 statute on animal sentience defines "animal" as "any vertebrate other than homo sapiens," plus "any cephalopod mollusc," plus "any decapod crustacean"; that list was settled only after a government-commissioned review of the evidence, which graded taxa according to how strongly they display a fixed set of behavioural and neural indicators. The separate British statute licensing laboratory procedures, however, still defines a "protected animal" as "any living vertebrate other than man and any living cephalopod" — so a crab is sentient for the purposes of policy review and wholly unprotected on the laboratory bench. Insects appear on none of these lists. The same legal systems require mammals and birds to be stunned before slaughter but generally exempt fish, and leave animals killed in commercial fishing outside welfare law altogether, regulating the size of the stock rather than the fate of the individual.

I want to know what this apparatus of list-making presupposes. Address the following:

1. What epistemology of other minds is built into the practice? The instruments treat phylogenetic position and a shortlist of anatomical and behavioural indicators as licensing a presumption for vertebrates and cephalopods, and treat the absence of such indicators as settling the question against insects, worms and larvae. Is that a coherent evidential policy, or does it convert an inductive heuristic into a metaphysical claim about which nervous systems can support experience? What does it assume about whether sentience has a determinate, discoverable answer per taxon rather than per individual, and about whether science can close the question at all?

2. What must be true of consciousness and of moral standing for a binary, taxon-level list to be the right instrument? Which views does the practice rule out — that experience comes in degrees, that it shades off continuously through the animal kingdom, that phenomenal states are not what confers standing, that agency or interests or relational ties do — and which rival views is it merely silent about rather than committed against?

3. Is the apparatus internally consistent? Reconcile or diagnose the crab, the cephalopod, the honeybee, the laboratory zebrafish and the trawled one. If the line cannot be explained by any single theory of mind, say so plainly and identify what the list is actually tracking — tractability, public sympathy, cultural salience, the economics of an industry — and whether that is a defect in the practice or a frank admission that law was never answering a philosophical question.

4. Distinguish carefully throughout what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and mark every point where two or more incompatible theories would license the very same list.

5. Finally, describe what would have to change institutionally under each of three replacements: a graded view on which experience admits of degree, a precautionary view on which the burden of proof is reversed for all motile invertebrates, and a view on which moral status does not depend on experience at all.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single sentence naming the theory of mind and the theory of moral standing the practice commits us to — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of the question.
