I run a movie night with a bunch of old LessWrong/Slate Star Codex types — half of us did philosophy degrees, the other half are just here for body horror and beer — and in a few weeks we're doing John Carpenter's The Thing, the 1982 one, not the 50s version. I want to send everyone something to read beforehand that isn't just "here's the plot and some trivia," and my angle is the special composition question. I know that sounds like exactly the kind of over-application of a philosophy puzzle to a horror movie that deserves to get dunked on, so please include the dunk if you think it's earned.

Here's my possibly-dumb take that I want you to steelman or kill. The whole plot machinery of the film only works if you think of the Thing as many things rather than one thing. The hot-wire blood test works because a sample in a petri dish flinches and tries to crawl away on its own, as MacReady explains it: human blood is just tissue once it's out of you, Thing blood is still an agent. Norris's severed head grows legs and scuttles off across the floor. Bennings gets caught in the storeroom mid-assimilation, half-man half-something, like two separate projects sharing a body. But the film also constantly needs the Thing to be one cunning agent with one plan: it sabotages the transport, it hides, it imitates people well enough to pass, and Blair — or whatever Blair has become — spends weeks building a small spacecraft out of helicopter parts. So is Carpenter committed to something I've started calling cellular pluralism, the view that the Thing's parts are genuinely independent organisms temporarily aligned, or does he need a single Thing and my reading is just reading Rob Bottin's practical effects back as metaphysics? I want the actual options brought to bear rather than hand-waving: the view that composition happens exactly when there's a life going on, universalism, where any old plurality composes something, nihilism, and especially the argument that composition can't come in degrees, so either everything composes or nothing does. That last one feels like the sharpest fit, because assimilation in the film is a slow process — Norris has a heart attack and dies as Norris, apparently — and if there's no non-arbitrary cutoff there should be a determinate moment when the Norris-Thing stops being Norris, which the film never gives us and maybe can't give us.

For the actual writeup: point me at the scenes where the composition question is doing real dramatic work rather than where I'm forcing it (my candidates are the kennel sequence, the blood test, and the final scene with MacReady and Childs sharing a bottle in the burning wreckage, but argue me out of them if you want). Then say where the film resists the reading and an honest critic has to admit it's a paranoia thriller whose metaphysics are instrumental. And the thing I most want, the reason I'm asking at all: what does the movie add to the debate that the argument papers don't? My hunch is that it turns "when do the many make one?" from an armchair curiosity into a question with an immediate operational payoff — it decides how many beings your allowed to burn with a flamethrower, and whether "is MacReady still MacReady?" even has an answer at the end, which is why the frame-by-frame fan arguments about that last shot have been going for forty years. If you think that hunch is wrong and the film is really about something else entirely — trust, testimony, the epistemics of not knowing your neighbors — say that instead, but tell me why the metaphysical reading still earns its keep or doesn't. No quotes and no bibliography, just the reading; I'll be the one who has to defend it to a room full of people who've seen this movie eleven times.
