ok so — I'm on the board of a small nonprofit that runs two assisted-living houses in central Ohio, fourteen residents each. My mom has moderate dementia and lives in one of them. I disclosed that when I joined and I'm putting it up front here because I dont fully trust my own judgment on this one. For context the board is five volunteers and three of us have a parent currently in one of the houses, so everyone is sort of arguing about their own mother a little without saying so.

The board votes in two weeks and I've been asked to write the memo that goes in the packet. A vendor wants us to put an AI companion tablet in ever resident's room. It talks to them, remembers the grandkids' names, prompts about meds, plays music from their teens, and — this is the part that's been sitting weird with me — listens all day and sends the family a weekly report flagging changes in mood, speech patterns, and possible cognitive decline. The pitch deck says it will free up staff for meaningful human connection. It costs roughly what two part-time aide positions cost us per house.

The board is split 3-2 and each side thinks the other is being gross. The pro side says loneliness is measurably shortening these people's lives and we are not in a financial position to be precious about it. The con side says care is a relationship between persons, that a device which reports your mother's Tuesday afternoon to her daughter is surveilance with a friendly voice attached, and that adopting it is also a message to a workforce that's mostly women and mostly underpaid that what they do is substitutable.

Then our one member who's an actual retired nurse said the thing nobody wants to say out loud: the freed-up hours won't get reallocated, because next year's budget already assumes we cut them. So the real choice isn't the tablet versus good staffing, it's the tablet versus worse staffing.

And one more thing I don't know what to do with. They loaned us two units over the summer. One resident, 91, sharp as anything, talks to hers like it's a person and gets visibly distressed when the battery dies and it goes quiet. Her daughter wants it removed. The resident wants it kept.

So: draft the memo. I want a recommendation the board can actually vote on — adopt, reject, or adopt under conditions — and if it's conditions, give me ones we could monitor and enforce, not aspirations. Reason it in the order a tired volunteer board member needs, and give the losing side its best case in its own terms rather than a strawman; if I'm wrong about this I'd rather find out from you than from the vote. And somewhere in there, say plainly what the decision commits us to believing about what care is and who or what can give it, because that's the thing the board keeps circling without naming.

About 900–1000 words. Please don't hand me a seminar — no bibliography, no name-dropping theorists, use whatever ideas you need but write like a person trying to get five volunteers to a decision. And don't split the difference just to be safe. If the honest answer is no, say no.
