I'm putting together next week's seminar on moral relativism for my second-years — eighteen of them, mostly philosophy with a handful of joint honours students from politics — and I've hit a wall. Every case I can reach for is one they've met a dozen times already: the anthropologist and the Inuit elder, the young graduate who takes a job in a country where paying off inspectors is just how the port works, foot-binding, the missionary. My TA keeps proposing the same one and by minute five the room has switched off. What I want instead is a case where the relativist and the objectivist actually come apart on something somebody has to decide, and where the usual groan — that relativism is inert, that it just collapses into 'be nice and don't interfere' — can't dissolve it. My own hunch is that the way in is to drop the two-cultures framing altogether and make the competing frameworks belong to one person at two different times, because then nobody can appeal to cultural distance or to a live-and-let-live truce. So build me that case to order.

Here's the shape I'm after. One woman, now in her sixties, is the last surviving trustee of a small grant-making foundation, and the founding deed — which she herself drafted when she was twenty-seven — contains an allocation rule that is still binding and that only she can now vary. This month two applications are in front of her and the rule picks out one of them, and she no longer thinks the rule is right. Constraints, and these matter: realistic, no sci-fi machinery, no memory loss, no time travel, no magical access to other minds; the scenario itself in a single paragraph; there has to be a second person in the room, a junior trustee or the like, who asks her the meta-question directly, something along the lines of whether the old rule was wrong all along or was right then and is wrong now, so that the semantic issue can't be dodged. Two further constraints do most of the work, so please respect them: her change of mind must not be explained by any new non-moral information — she hasn't found out that either programme works better than she used to think, nobody has given her better data — and it must not be explained by self-interest or by any obvious corrosion of character, since she stands to gain nothing personally. If either escape hatch is open, the objectivist wins the case for free and I've learned nothing. I want the change to be the kind we'd ordinarily describe as her coming to see a consideration as morally relevant that she previously didn't register at all, though you should choose the content rather than borrow mine. And don't stipulate the verdicts: if your paragraph simply asserts that the old rule was true-relative-to-her-framework-then, you've smuggled in the answer, and the whole point of the exercise is to test whether relativism makes any practical difference, so the verdicts have to be generated by the two views rather than written into the setup.

Then do the demonstration. Tell me what a framework relativist of the familiar sort — the view on which an 'ought' is true only relative to a framework the agent is actually party to — says about each of three questions: whether the twenty-seven-year-old's original judgement was true, what she should do about this month's applications, and what she should say to the junior trustee. Then the same three from the objectivist. At least one of those questions has to get genuinely different answers from the two views, and different in a way that isn't merely verbal; if the only divergence is about which sentences come out true in a technical sense, say so plainly and tell me the design has failed rather than dressing it up. After that, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the verdicts if you take it out — for instance if she could vary the deed at no cost to anyone, or if nobody ever asked her the meta-question. Finally, hand the microphone to whichever view comes out worse and let it attack the case properly; I'd expect the objection to be something like 'this isn't moral disagreement between frameworks at all, it's one person's prudence across time, so you've changed the subject' — meet that or concede it. British English throughout, please, and no formatting of any kind: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose the way you'd write me a letter. And be straight with me about how confident you are in the claims you're making, and single out the one point in the whole thing you're least sure of — my guess is it'll be whether the relativist can really say anything interesting in reply to the junior trustee, but I may be wrong about that.
