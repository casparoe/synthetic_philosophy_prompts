I am in the fourth year of a philosophy of mind program, and next month I am giving a talk in a seminar I co-run with a postdoc in a neurorehabilitation lab. The talk is supposed to take a stand rather than survey the field, so I need a number I can actually defend under questioning. The proposition I have settled on, stated as precisely as I can manage, is this:

(P) In a human being who has undergone a complete callosotomy — the corpus callosum sectioned, and, where the surgical protocol also severed the anterior commissure and the commissure of the fornix — there are, during ordinary unstructured waking life and not only during lateralized laboratory testing, two subjects of phenomenal experience rather than one.

What I want is your credence in (P), decomposed rather than asserted. My own rough breakdown, which you should feel free to reject, replace, or reorder: (i) the probability that the right way to frame unity is the maximal one, on which all of a subject's concurrent experiences must be co-conscious within a single phenomenal field, as opposed to a graded or access-based notion on which (P) simply lacks a determinate binary truth value; (ii) conditional on that framing being correct, the probability that the classic lateralized findings — the naming failures, the left hand's apparently autonomous behavior, the divided visual field work — track phenomenal disunity rather than merely cognitive or informational disunity between systems that nonetheless remain within one field; (iii) the probability that the integration surviving sectioning is too thin to sustain a single field, where I have in mind shared arousal and brainstem modulation, the bilateral representation of most of the visual world outside the monocular crescent, ipsilateral control of proximal and axial musculature, cross-cueing between the hands, and the left hemisphere's well-attested tendency to confabulate a coherent narrative over behavior it did not initiate; and (iv) the probability that "how many subjects are there" is well posed at all for a single organism, rather than indeterminate in something like the way counting questions go indeterminate elsewhere. Give each term a number, one or two lines of justification, and then show me how you combine them into a final credence in (P); if you think a term is missing or that two of mine collapse into one, say so and restructure.

Part of what prompts this is a proposal from my postdoc collaborator that I find seductive and would like an honest assessment of. The design: recruit a complete callosotomy patient and, over several months, train a bimanual task in which the two hands are placed on separate reward schedules that gradually become mutually exclusive, with feedback arranged so that neither hand's movements need be lateralized in order to be available to both hemisphere systems. If, after training, the hands come to pursue genuinely conflicting goals — one hand systematically sabotaging the other, and each continuing to adapt to the other's sabotage — he thinks we would have evidence for two subjects that does not depend on tachistoscopic presentation at all. My suspicion is that this conflates two systems with independent reinforcement histories with two centers of experience, but I cannot yet say where the inference fails, or how much success it would need in order to move me. So tell me: which of your terms would this kind of evidence bear on, how much would a clear positive result shift each of them, and what would a clean negative result show — that (P) is false, or only that the protocol does not probe it?

Two further requests. First, the two people I argue with most are far apart: one puts her credence in (P) at roughly 0.85, the other at roughly 0.2, and both claim to accept the same empirical literature. Tell me which single term in your decomposition most likely accounts for that gap, and what the two of them would have to settle before any new experimental result could count for anything. Second, give me a separate number for the probability that, twenty-five years from now, the majority position among philosophers working on consciousness is that complete callosotomy patients are two subjects of experience, and say briefly which of the earlier terms does most of the work in that projection. Please also flag any of my anatomical or methodological background assumptions above that you think are simply mistaken, and adjust the numbers accordingly. I am not looking for references — I have the literature and will supply it myself.
