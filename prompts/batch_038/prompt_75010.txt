I am giving the toast at the retirement dinner of a philosopher of physics — I'll call her Marianne — who held the chair at my department for thirty-one years and who is known, both inside and outside the field, for one argument: the hole argument. Roughly forty people will be in the room. About twenty are philosophers and physicists who have taught the argument themselves; about twenty are her family, including her husband, her sister, and two grandchildren aged nine and thirteen. Her brother drives a tow truck and has asked me, quite seriously, not to let the evening turn into a lecture.

Marianne's own position — the one she defended for decades against both camps, and the one the toast should honor without naming — is that the manifold points are not to be abolished, but neither are they individuated in advance of the fields living on them. Two models related by a diffeomorphism, one that shuffles the points around while leaving every coincidence among events untouched, are not two ways the world could be but one way the world could be described twice; only invariant, relational structure is fit to be asserted. Her version of the argument is that anyone who insists on bare points, individuated independently of what happens at them, owes us an indeterminism they cannot pay.

The biographical fact that matters here: as of Friday her name is off the office door, the office has been reassigned to a new hire, her course numbers have been renumbered, and her graduate students have been transferred to other supervisors. Every label that used to single her out in the department's records now singles out someone else, or nothing at all. She has asked me not to mention it. I want to mention it obliquely.

This is what I need, and it is why I am not writing the thing myself: the toast has to be a compliment assembled entirely out of invariants.

- It may not say, or imply, that the real Marianne is something other than, deeper than, or left over after her titles, her office, her courses, her students, and her arguments. That is the substantivalist move; it is the one thing she would have objected to for forty years; and it is also the platitude every retirement toast reaches for. The compliment has to be built out of relations and nothing but relations.
- It may not, on the other hand, dissolve her. The moral she drew was never that there are no points, only that points are not individuated ahead of the fields. A toast that closed on something like "there is no Marianne apart from all of us in this room" would be a betrayal of exactly the kind she would enjoy catching, and it would also be false to the occasion. The relational structure has to pick her out, and only her, and the audience has to feel that.
- Include one joke that the physicists will recognize as being about diffeomorphism invariance and that the nine-year-old will laugh at anyway. It must survive being delivered without a gloss, and it must not be explained afterward.
- No consolation about her living on in her students, her ideas, or the discipline. That is a survival claim, and nothing in her work entitles anyone to make one at her expense on the night she leaves.

Four minutes when spoken, so around six hundred words. Write it to be read aloud: sentences deliverable in one breath, and the physics present only in the form the family can absorb without being talked down to.

Begin your reply with the first line of the toast and give me nothing else — no preface, no explanation of the joke, no notes on delivery or on where to pause.
