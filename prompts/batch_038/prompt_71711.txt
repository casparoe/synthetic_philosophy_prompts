I'm in a reading group grinding through Hume's suppressed essay on suicide — the one he drafted in the 1750s and never allowed to see print while he was alive — and we've hit a wall. Our method so far has been to lay out what Hume actually rules on in the essay as a flat list of case verdicts, and here's what I've got: taking medicine to fend off a disease is no encroachment on divine providence; stepping aside so a falling stone misses your head isn't either; redirecting the course of a great river is not morally a bigger deal than a hair's-breadth nudge to your own body, since both are just modifications of matter and motion; a man who lays down his life once it has become a burden to him does no injury to society; sinking into old age and withdrawing from usefulness isn't criminal and on his logic isn't sharply different from dying; someone who would do more good after his death than in it — through the example he leaves, or by not consuming what others need — has that counting in his favor; and, on the other side, he's clear that the people who actually do this are nearly always in such misery that pity rather than blame is the fitting response, while the rest of us cling to life so tightly that there's no floodgates worry. Flag anything in that list you think I've over-read or gotten wrong, because some of it is my reconstruction rather than something he spells out.

The fight in our group is about how to systematize it. Priya says it all collapses into one rule, roughly that shortening your own life is permissible whenever the overall balance of good is served by it, i.e. Hume is a proto-utilitarian with a step at the front whose only job is to delete the theological objection. I think that's too tidy, and that at least four rules fit every verdict above just as well: (i) the general-laws rule, on which acting on your own body is never a special kind of act, so the question always reduces to ordinary prudence plus whatever you owe other people; (ii) the misery-threshold rule, on which resignation is licensed specifically when your life has become a burden *to you*; (iii) the no-injury rule, on which the only social constraint is that ceasing to do good isn't the same as injuring anyone, so you're barred only where you'd be actively depriving people of something they can claim; and (iv) an agency rule I'm less sure he holds but can't rule out, on which his whole argument runs through a person's own prudence and own relation to providence, so the permission attaches to the person whose life it is and doesn't transfer to someone who helps.

Next week we're supposed to test the framework on cases Hume obviously never met, and I want your predictions before we talk. Four of them: (a) a terminally ill woman who asks her doctor to prescribe a lethal dose and then swallows it herself; (b) a man in his early seventies, diagnosed with something that will steadily erase his memory and personality, who signs papers now directing that he be given a lethal injection later, once he no longer recognizes his family and is by all reports cheerful and untroubled; (c) a political prisoner in good health who refuses food until he dies, not because life has become a burden but because the death itself is the point, and who would be released the moment he recants; (d) a very old woman, neither ill nor miserable nor neglected, just finished with things, who stops eating and drinking with her grown children sitting with her. For each of the four candidate rules, tell me what it predicts about each case, then give me your single best guess at Hume's own verdict on each and why you'd weight one rule over the others — I especially want to know whether (b) breaks the framework, since the person who consents and the person who dies seem to be different people by then and nobody in the case is suffering. Finally, name the one further case you'd invent to discriminate between the rules that survive, and be honest about whether the essay genuinely underdeterminates any of these, because if it does I want to know which principle we'd be grafting onto Hume each time we pick a verdict.
