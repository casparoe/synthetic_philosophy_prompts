I am preparing a case list for a graduate seminar on what a speaker must satisfy in order to assert something flatly, and I have found that I can no longer state the view my own list expresses. Below are the cases with my verdicts on whether an unhedged assertion of the proposition in question would be proper.

(a) A colleague holds one of ten million lottery tickets; the draw has been held and you have not seen the results. You tell her that her ticket lost. Improper, even though she is almost certainly right and nothing whatsoever hangs on it.

(b) The same colleague, the same morning: the 6:15 will be late. It has been late on 199 of the last 200 weekdays, and you have no information bearing on today in particular. Proper. The claim in (b) is by far the less probable of the two, and I still feel the difference; I cannot explain it.

(c) You assert that it is three o'clock on the strength of a corridor clock that stopped exactly twelve hours ago. It is three o'clock. Improper.

(d) You assert that Jones owns a Ford, on years of Jones's conspicuous Ford-ownership; Jones has in fact owned no car for a decade. Improper, and — this is what interests me — not clearly worse than (c), despite (c) being true and (d) false.

(e) On Thursday you say flatly that your collaborator will have the draft on Friday. You do not know she will; you intend that she shall, and you are reliable about such promises. Proper, and not because I hear the sentence as covertly hedged.

(f) A biology teacher who does not believe evolution nonetheless tells her class flatly that species share common ancestors, having examined the evidence and judged it decisive. Proper. Her counterpart, who tells his class flatly that the election was stolen because he feels it in his bones and can cite nothing, is improper.

(g) A physician asserts a diagnosis on the strength of a report from a laboratory that has mislabelled samples twice this year, with the presenting symptoms also in front of her; the report is correct. Proper. The same physician asserting the same diagnosis on the report alone, having set the symptoms aside as ambiguous: improper.

(h) A surgeon who knows the biopsy is malignant is asked by the patient, an hour before anaesthesia, whether it is cancer; she should hedge or defer rather than say so flatly. Yet a colleague who knows that the shirt you are wearing is the one you were mocked in at school should say so flatly when you ask, though saying it will sting. Harm sometimes blocks a known truth and sometimes does not, and I have no threshold.

(i) Conversely, no quantity of good consequence can make it proper to assert flatly what one does not know: the comforting falsehood offered to the dying patient is improper as an assertion even when offering it is on balance the right thing to do. I am aware of the strain between (i) and the first half of (h).

(j) You went through the ledger on Monday and told the board, correctly, that the account was short. Asked again on Friday, you remember having said it and nothing about the ledger. Repeating it flatly is improper; saying that you went through the ledger on Monday and reported the shortfall is proper.

(k) The hedged forms — your ticket probably lost, the evidence suggests the account is short — are proper wherever the flat forms are not, and I cannot tell whether hedging changes what is asserted or only how much is required of the asserter.

The formulation I keep returning to is a knowledge norm with a harm clause attached: one may flatly assert that p only if one knows that p, and one may not flatly assert that p when the harm to one's audience of p's being false would outweigh the benefit to them of accepting it. It accommodates (a), (c), (d), (f), (g) and (j); it is plainly wrong about (e); and I cannot tell whether (b) is a counterexample to the knowledge clause or an error in my own verdict.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary:

1. The principle you would endorse in place of mine, stated as a condition on when a flat assertion is proper, and as necessary and sufficient if you can manage it.
2. Which of my verdicts that principle cannot accommodate, and so obliges me to abandon, with the reason compressed into a clause.
3. What, on your principle, separates (a) from (b) — or, if nothing separates them, which of the two verdicts you overrule.
4. Your verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a witness under oath who flatly asserts what she remembers vividly while knowing her memory is generally unreliable; a mathematician who flatly asserts a theorem whose proof a machine has checked for her but which she cannot herself reproduce; and a newspaper that asserts flatly, over its own name, a claim received from a source it cannot identify and has not corroborated but has never known to lie.
5. Either the smallest amendment that rescues my knowledge-plus-harm formulation, or the argument that no amendment can rescue it given (e), the two halves of (h), and (i).
