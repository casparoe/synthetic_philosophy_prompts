I've been reading LessWrong and the old SSC comment threads since roughly forever, and one recurring thing in my little corner of the internet is that someone will invoke Grice, everyone will agree that Grice is important, and nobody can say what's actually load-bearing about him. So a few of us started a reading group and we're doing the pragmatics material this term, and I've volunteered (been volunteered) to open each session with a joke about the assigned reading. This week is Grice on implicature and speaker meaning. My first attempt bombed, and it bombed for a diagnosable reason: it was a generic "philosophers overthink ordinary sentences" joke with the word implicature pasted on top. I'd definately like something better.

Here's the material I want the joke to actually be about, since the punchline should turn on the content rather than on the vibe. Grice's classic illustration is an academic writing a reference for a pupil who's a candidate for a philosophy job, where the letter says essentially only that the candidate's English is excellent and that he's been turning up to his tutorials regularly — the letter is damning precisely because the writer keeps to the literally true and lets the reader do the arithmetic. Relatedly, and this is the bit I most want you to use: Grice distinguishes several genuinely different ways a speaker can fail to observe a maxim. You can violate one covertly, to mislead. You can opt out. You can infringe it through ignorance or inarticulacy without intending anything by it. You can flout it blatantly and openly, in order to generate an implicature. And you can suspend one, for instance out of cultural deference. Flouting is the only one of those that's still cooperative — the hearer has to assume you're making sense and go hunting for what you meant. Conflating flouting with violating, or with infringing, is exactly the mistake that makes people who know this stuff wince, so please don't make it. Scalar implicature is fair game too, as is the cancellability point: conversational implicatures can be explicitly taken back without contradiction, and that retractability is the standard test for whether something was an implicature at all rather than part of what was said.

So — write me a joke about Gricean implicature, with a real setup and a real punchline rather than a one-line pun, that lands for someone who's read the chapter and doesn't work if you swap in any other philosopher's name. Setup ideas, take or leave them: the reference-letter scenario, a Gricean on a first date or at a job interview or explaining to a police officer why he answered the question literally, or a maxims-themed support group. Keep it short enough to say out loud to eight people before we start reading. If you've got two or three variants, rank them and tell me why the top one is the top one — I like knowing which mechanism is doing the work. And if a variant only really pays off with a gloss, give me the gloss in a sentence, but make sure the joke still functions without it.
