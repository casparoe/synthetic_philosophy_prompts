I sit on a regional health authority's standing priority-setting panel. The panel's remit is narrow: given a fixed budget, it recommends how to trade off the relief of suffering of different intensities. It does not treat the creation of pleasure as a benefit in its own right, and its record to date consists of six recommendations.

1. Mosquito-borne disease program: a proposal that would have spared roughly ten billion people a day of mild itching and low-grade fever, at the cost of cancelling a pilot that would have prevented fifty cases of prolonged torture. Recommended: fund the torture prevention.
2. Headcount escalation: a billion people each given a papercut, versus one person spared a minute of torture. Recommended: spare the torture, and the panel minuted that raising the billion to any larger finite figure would not change its answer.
3. Sub-threshold aggregation: a thousand people each given a papercut, versus one person spared a month of severe but bearable chronic pain. Recommended: accept the thousand papercuts.
4. Equal headcounts across a severity gap: one billion people each enduring a month of severe pain, versus the same one billion people each enduring a minute of torture-level pain. Recommended: spare the torture minute for everyone.
5. Personal cost: the panel endorsed an agent breaking her own hand to prevent one stranger's torture, but refused to endorse an agent crushing ten of her own fingers to spare a thousand strangers a papercut each.
6. Within-region trade: a shelter proposal covering two thousand people each spared six weeks of severe pain, versus a proposal covering forty people each spared one day of the very worst pain the panel has considered. Recommended: the two thousand.

The case now before us is a digital mental-health triage programme, three-year fixed budget, and we must fund one of two configurations.

Configuration S: a round-the-clock clinician-staffed crisis line with intensive follow-up, reaching about twelve thousand people per year in acute suicidal crisis or in intractable pain syndromes. Roughly six in ten receive substantial relief and four in ten partial or no relief; the relieved would otherwise have faced weeks to months at the top of our severity scale.

Configuration M: a low-intensity digital intervention reaching about four hundred thousand people per year with mild-to-moderate anxiety and low mood, delivering a modest but real improvement in most recipients, sustained for some months.

Two facts are settled and should be taken as given. Capacity in the crisis programme is capped, so mild cases turned away are not redirected to any other service: the forgone relief is genuinely lost. And the panel's own record does not determine where its threshold sits relative to acute suicidal crisis or intractable pain; its severity vocabulary is torture, severe pain, chronic pain, papercut, and those labels do not map onto our clinical categories.

What I want is a decision, not a survey.

First, state the rule or rules jointly consistent with all six precedents, formulated precisely enough to be falsified by a further case. Go beyond the obvious lexical-threshold reading: at least two further hypotheses fit, including one on which there is no threshold at all but only a very steep exchange rate, and at least one on which the panel is not comparing quantities of suffering at all. Rule out the ones you reject and say what in the record rules them out.

Second, say what each surviving rule recommends for our configuration, showing the arithmetic where the rule has any. I specifically want to know whether the steep-exchange-rate reading, which the panel's numbers have not yet excluded, tips toward M at the actual scale of twelve thousand against four hundred thousand, and at what headcount of mild cases it would tip if it does not.

Third, name the closest analog among the six precedents and say where that analogy breaks.

Fourth, if you judge the record genuinely underdetermining, recommend anyway and state explicitly which principle the panel would be adopting by doing so, and what it would have to disavow later to remain consistent. Flag any precedent you think was decided wrongly under the rule you favour.

Fifth, specify one further case, with exact numbers and severities, that would separate your favoured rule from its nearest rival, and state what each predicts about it.

Be committed throughout: take a definite position on the configuration, give your reasons, and mark clearly the points where the record forces a choice rather than a discovery. I do not want both-sides exposition or a list of options for me to choose between.

End your reply with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: [S or M] — [one sentence giving the rule you are attributing to the panel and the reason it decides this case].
