Spent three hours last night arguing with a friend about interpersonal utility comparisons. He's an economist, thinks the debate got settled in the 1930s and was then revived for no good reason; I think the ordinalist truce just swept the problem under a rug and left it there. Neither of us moved. Unfortunately I now have to teach it.

Grad seminar, 18 people, one 90-minute session. Mostly philosophy students plus two economists who will smugly agree with each other. Design a game that makes them run into the problem instead of hearing about it. No lecture up front, and dont let the phrase "interpersonal comparison" surface until the debrief.

Structurally, here's what I'm after: an early round whose rules force them to aggregate using nothing but rankings, and which predictably spits out an allocation that feels obscene to everyone in the room including the three who voted for it; a middle round where intensity reports become admissible and they discover those reports are cheap talk; and a late round where they choose before knowing which seat they'll occupy, so they hit the veil move on their own and then have to say what it had to assume in order to work.

Give me rules, materials (points or candy, no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief with an explicit map from what happened in the room to which position in the debate. Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time it's run and how to steer it, and which feature of the problem a game just cannot reproduce.

Be straight with me about rigging too: say which round is engineered to make which view look bad and whether that's a fair fight. And if you think my middle round is impossible to build without smuggling in a public currency that quietly solves the very thing it's supposed to dramatize, or that the feel-it-themselves approach is confused here, say that plainly rather than handing me something that pretends to work.
