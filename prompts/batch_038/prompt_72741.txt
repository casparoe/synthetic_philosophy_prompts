Write a fourteen-line sonnet about the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the worry that the pleasure of cold water on a hot day, of a proof finally clicking, of sex, of a migraine lifting, and of finding you are liked have no single felt quality in common, so the hedonist's one intrinsic good threatens to be a word rather than a thing. Let the form carry the argument. The octave should be a first-person inventory in which no two lines rhyme: each line a pleasure rendered concretely, with no abstractions and no naming of categories, and the speaker turning each one over to look for the shared thread and failing to find it. Loose iambic pentameter is welcome, but the rhyme constraints are not negotiable. At line nine, the search must change target rather than give up — pleasure stops being a quality to be discovered inside experiences and becomes a degree to which any experience whatsoever is more or less had, a dimension rather than a flavor. The sestet should accordingly rhyme tightly and interlockingly, and should take up exactly one end-word from the octave inside a new rhyme pair, so that the same item reappears under a measure. The closing couplet should say plainly what the reply buys — comparability, a single scale — and what it concedes: that we can no longer say what pleasure feels like, only how much of it there is. Do not use the words hedonism, heterogeneity, or phenomenology; a reader who knows the debate should be able to identify the objection and the reply from the poem alone, and a reader who does not should simply get a good poem about pleasure.
