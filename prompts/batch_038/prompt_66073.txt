I am preparing a short commentary on Kant's *Perpetual Peace* (1795) for a graduate reading group, and I have run into a difficulty in the third preliminary article that repeated re-reading has not resolved. Here is the passage in the English translation I am working from:

> 3. "Standing Armies (miles perpetuus) Shall in Time Be Totally Abolished";
>
> For they incessantly menace other states by their readiness to appear at all times prepared for war; they incite them to compete with each other in the number of armed men, and there is no limit to this. For this reason, the cost of peace finally becomes more oppressive than that of a short war, and consequently a standing army is itself a cause of offensive war waged in order to relieve the state of this burden. Add to this that to pay men to kill or to be killed seems to entail using them as mere machines and tools in the hand of another (the state), and this is hardly compatible with the rights of mankind in our own person. But the periodic and voluntary military exercises of citizens who thereby secure themselves and their country against foreign aggression are entirely different.
>
> The accumulation of treasure would have the same effect, for, of the three powers — the power of armies, of alliances, and of money — the third is perhaps the most dependable weapon. Such accumulation of treasure is regarded by other states as a threat of war, and if it were not for the difficulties in learning the amount, it would force the other state to make an early attack.

Three puzzles, which I suspect interlock.

First, the article seems to contain two very different kinds of reason for abolition: a strategic one (standing armies menace neighbours, drive an unlimited competition in armed men, and make peace more oppressive than a short war, so that they become themselves a cause of offensive war) and a moral one (paying men to kill or be killed treats them as mere machines and tools). I want to know whether the moral reason is meant to carry independent normative weight or only to reinforce the strategic one. Note that the strategic reason appears to concern a relation *between* states rather than any internal defect in a single state's army; if so, the obligation might be satisfiable by reciprocal disarmament rather than by unilateral abolition — in which case "in time" is doing considerably more work than it is usually given credit for. Is there anything in the wording that decides this?

Second, the moral reason is conspicuously hedged — "seems to entail," "hardly compatible with" — where the article itself is categorical. Two sub-questions. (a) Whose right is at issue? The phrase "in our own person" suggests that the soldier wrongs *himself* by allowing himself to be used as an instrument, which would make this a duty to oneself; but the sentence can equally be read as a claim about what the state may not do to its subjects. Do these readings diverge on whether the article could be satisfied by, say, voluntary long-service enlistment on generous terms? (b) Does the hedging indicate that Kant takes this second reason to be weaker than the first, or is it stylistic caution about a claim he holds strictly?

Third, the exception for "the periodic and voluntary military exercises of citizens" is hard to place. Kant marks at least four differences from the standing army: periodicity, voluntariness, the agents being citizens rather than paid men, and the purpose being self-defence rather than constant readiness for war. Which of these does the justificatory work? This matters because if the relevant difference is that the citizen-soldier is not a hired instrument, the article concerns the internal constitution of armed force; if it is that the force exists only defensively and not as a permanent threat, the article concerns the posture a state presents to others — and the accumulation-of-treasure paragraph then reads not as a digression but as a second application of the same principle.

Please argue from the passage itself rather than directing me to secondary literature, which I cannot consult this week. Where you think the English wording loads a question the argument does not actually settle, say so explicitly; and close by stating which of the competing readings above you judge best supported by the text, and why.
