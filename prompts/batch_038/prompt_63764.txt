The exam question was as follows:

> **Question 3 (40 marks).** Coherence arguments are said to show that an agent whose attitudes are rationally permissible can be represented as maximizing expected utility.
> (a) (10 marks) Explain what a coherence argument is. Identify the coherence conditions on which the two best-known versions rest, and say for each version what it takes as its starting data and what it derives.
> (b) (15 marks) Set out the structure of a representation theorem: what is assumed, what is proved, and what the conclusion licenses. State precisely in what sense the derived utility function is not unique.
> (c) (15 marks) It is sometimes objected that arguments of this kind are either trivial or question-begging. State the objection as clearly and charitably as you can, and assess it. Does it succeed? Does your assessment depend on whether the argument is read as a normative thesis or as a piece of interpretive methodology?

The student's answer ran to about three sides and read:

> (a) A coherence argument does not begin from introspection or self-report but from behaviour. There are two canonical routes, and they differ in the behaviour they start from.
>
> The betting route goes back to Ramsey and was sharpened by de Finetti. An agent's degree of belief in a proposition is identified with the quotient at which she is prepared both to accept and to offer a bet on it. If her quotients do not obey the probability calculus — for example, if she will pay 0.4 for a bet on P and 0.4 for a bet on not-P, spending 0.8 on a package that returns exactly 1 — then a bookmaker can combine such bets into a package that leaves her worse off no matter which state obtains. Coherence in the probabilistic sense is therefore not an additional requirement imposed on partial belief; it is what the ordinary requirement of avoiding guaranteed loss comes to when applied to partial belief.
>
> The preference route, associated with Savage and with von Neumann and Morgenstern, replaces betting quotients by a ranking of acts. The coherence conditions here are structural: the ranking must be complete and transitive, it must satisfy a continuity condition, and it must satisfy the sure-thing principle, according to which the ranking between two acts cannot depend on how they behave on any event on which they agree. The reasoning is the same as in the betting case: an agent whose ranking violates the sure-thing principle can be offered a package of bets that loses money whichever state turns out to obtain, so the condition is enforced by the same requirement of unexploitability.
>
> (b) A representation theorem is an existence-and-uniqueness result. One posits states, consequences, and acts from states to consequences, together with an ordering on the acts satisfying the axioms; one then proves that there is a probability function on the states and a utility function on the consequences such that the ordering ranks each act by its expected utility, and conversely that any ordering of that form satisfies the axioms. In Savage's version the probability function is not assumed at all: it is read off the preferences, which is what makes the result substantive rather than a piece of bookkeeping. In the von Neumann and Morgenstern version the probabilities are already built into the description of the options, since an option is a lottery over consequences with chances attached, and only the utility function is derived. In both cases the utility function is unique only up to multiplication by a positive constant; since a common rescaling leaves every comparison between expected values unchanged, nothing determinate about the intensity of the agent's desires is fixed by the theorem, and no interpersonal comparisons are licensed. The probability function, by contrast, is unique outright.
>
> (c) The objection is that the theorem proves too little to be interesting. Because it is an existence result, it says only that some pair of functions represents the ordering; and because the representation is far from unique, nothing follows about what the agent actually believes or wants. The worry generalizes: provided one is free enough in choosing the utility function, any ranking at all can be exhibited as expected utility maximization, so the axioms exclude nothing and cannot be violated in practice. This is the same difficulty Lewis ran into for radical interpretation, where too many assignments of belief and desire fit the behavioural evidence, so fit alone cannot select among them and further constraints have to be supplied from outside.
>
> Read normatively, however, the objection misfires. The claim is then not merely that the agent can be described as an expected utility maximizer but that an agent who departs from the axioms is exploitable or otherwise incoherent, and that is a substantive claim about her. On this reading the axioms are better understood as partly constitutive of having beliefs and desires at all, which is why the experimental evidence that people systematically violate independence, as in Allais's studies, is beside the point: it shows only that people are not ideally coherent, not that the coherence conditions are the wrong ones.
>
> The real soft spot is elsewhere. Both theorems take as given a partition of states and a space of consequences, and they say nothing about whether that partition is the right one. Savage attached a caveat to the effect that the apparatus is applicable only where the agent's own description of her situation is adequate to it, so that the framework does not cover the case in which the agent discovers that her state space was mis-specified in the first place. Since the choice of states and consequences does much of the substantive work, a coherence argument may be less constraining than its presentation suggests, even where it is not trivial.
