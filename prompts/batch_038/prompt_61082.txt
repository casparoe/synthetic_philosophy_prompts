I'm in a Thursday reading group that's been working through the Enlightenment antitheatrical material — we did Plato on the poets a few weeks back, and last week we finished Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert, the one arguing that Geneva shouldn't let a playhouse open. One of the guys, Devin, built an argument he claims shows the case eats itself, and he wrote it on the whiteboard like this:

(1) Substitution principle: when an institution reliably hands people a moral sentiment about suffering they can do nothing about, the sentiment gets spent inside the institution instead of issuing in action, so the institution weakens their disposition to act.
(2) That is exactly what Rousseau says the theater does — the spectator weeps for the misfortunes on stage, feels he's paid whatever he owed to humanity, and walks out into the street.
(3) So the theater weakens the disposition to act, and a small, industrious republic has a standing reason to keep one out.
(4) But (1) doesn't describe only the theater. It describes reading history, the newspaper's dispatches from a famine three thousand miles off, a friend telling you about her mother's illness, a sermon, and Rousseau's own books, which are engineered to make a solitary reader feel something about people he will never meet.
(5) It describes the open-air festivals he proposes as a replacement, too: a crowd in a square singing itself hoarse about the fatherland is sentiment that terminates in the crowd.
(6) So if (1) is true, every instrument Rousseau has for moving anybody — including the amusements he's willing to permit and the books he's writing to reform Europe — corrodes the disposition to act.
(7) He can't live with (6), therefore (1) is false.
(8) But (3) was generated by (1) together with a description of what a playhouse is. Pull (1) and you have no argument left from the nature of sentiment to the closing of theaters, just a grab bag of complaints about actors and about Paris.

Nobody in the room could beat it and I've been chewing on it for a week. Devin himself said the weak spot has to be (6), on the grounds that Rousseau would cheerfully accept that most civilized amusements corrode people — he's hardly shy about that — but that just relocates the problem, since he still owes an account of how his own writing does the work he wants it to do. My instinct is different and I cant get it into words: I keep wanting to say (1) is simply too broad, but every way I try to narrow it looks ad hoc, and a narrowing has to explain why the newspaper and the sentimental novel escape while the playhouse doesn't. So: which step would you actually reject, and why did that step look perfectly innocent when he wrote it down? Then price the repairs for me — what it costs to keep (1) and bite the bullet, what a narrowed (1) has to say (in your own words, not a slogan), and what it costs to drop (3) and concede the whole letter is Geneva prejudice dressed up as moral psychology. For what it's worth I'd rather give up almost any other step than (3); I think there's something real in the idea that a playhouse is a machine for converting pity into a thing you watch, but I have no argument for it, which is kind of the whole problem.
