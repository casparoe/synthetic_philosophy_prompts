So I've been going back over Hume's little essay on suicide — the one that only got published after he died — and I think the standard three-part summary everyone gives (impiety, duty to your neighbour, duty to yourself) hides a structural problem, and I want to understand the problem properly rather then just have my own hunch bounced back at me. I've seen this essay waved around in enough assisted-dying threads that the usual summary has become a kind of cached answer, so please don't give me that one.

Here's the hunch. The impiety section usually gets rendered as: God operates through general laws, we bend the course of nature constantly, so ending your own life is no more of an encroachment on providence than damming a river or dosing a fever — plus the bit where he says a man's life matters to the cosmos about as much as an oyster's does. Fine. But notice that neither half of that actually mentions the agent's own life. The general-laws point is about all created causes, and the insignificance point is about scale. So the argument looks like a completely general demolition of the premise 'some region of events is reserved to God and off-limits to us', which has nothing to do with suicide in particular. Which makes me ask how much weight that section is carrying. If the reserved-domain premise fails for everything, it was never the thing making suicide wrong in the first place, and the live question collapses entirely into the other two charges.

And the society charge is where I think the real conceptual action is, and where I keep getting stuck. Hume's move, as I read it, is that by quitting your life you put yourself beyond injuring anyone; at worst you merely stop conferring benefits, and ceasing to be useful isnt the same as doing harm. But that turns entirely on which baseline you measure harm against. On a counterfactual account — you harm someone when you leave them worse off than they otherwise would have been — everyone who'd have gotten years of your cooperation is clearly worse off than they otherwise would have been. On a doing/allowing account, nothing has been done to them at all. So the whole old arguement about whether suicide wrongs your fellows looks like it's secretly an argument about the metaphysics of harm, and nobody on either side says so out loud.

What I want is the conceptual machinery pulled apart, in order:

1. What is the structure of the impiety argument, really, and is it general in the way I claimed? If it is, does that make the essay weaker, or does it mean the actual target is a theological premise his opponents needed and which fails everywhere it's applied?
2. Why doesn't that same general argument make homicide acceptable on Hume's own account? I want the asymmetry named precisely — consent, the fact that harm and injustice do all the work over there, whose life is whose to dispose of, something else — and then a verdict on whether he's entitled to it.
3. Spell out the harm-baseline problem above and say which account of harm Hume's society argument requires. Then hit the part I can't resolve: his own moral theory has sentiments tracking usefulness and agreeableness, so shouldnt that make the fact 'you stopped being useful' morally visible rather than invisible? Is he the right philosopher to be leaning hard on a sharp doing/allowing line, or is he borrowing a distinction his system has no use for?
4. Is there a version of the fair-play objection — you've already taken the benefits of the cooperative scheme, so you can't unilaterally resign from it — that survives all of the above? Say where exactly it bites and what Hume could answer.

I'm explicitly not after a reading list, citations, or the story of why he suppressed the thing. Just the load-bearing walls: which parts of the essay are actually holding the conclusion up, and whether the walls in the impiety section are holding up anything at all.
