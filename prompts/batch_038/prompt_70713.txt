Six weeks ago a complainant in one of our cases wrote to my department asking why a racial slur had to appear on the public caption feed at all, given that the court had already suppressed the name of the man who had used it against her. She said that watching the word form on screen in real time had made her relive the attack, and that she could not see what purpose it served that the record did not. I was unable to give her a principled answer, because we do not have one. I run the transcription, captioning and interpreting service attached to our courts and to the national assembly: roughly thirty-five staff, most of them trained as shorthand writers or court reporters, none of them trained in linguistics or philosophy. The service takes several decisions of this kind every day, and they are not all of one shape — a witness repeating her attacker's word in open court; a legally admitted audio recording of two friends using a reclaimed term between themselves; a defence expert's report read into evidence containing a table of epithets and their glosses; a simultaneous interpreter who must find a counterpart in a language where the nearest word carries more force, or less, or targets a different group altogether; an archival recording being captioned for a commemorative broadcast. Our outputs also differ in status: the transcript is the official record and will be read on appeal, where any substitution can be attacked as inaccuracy, while the caption feed and the interpreted audio are public-facing and are routinely lifted by the press. Decisions must be made inside the working day, they are signed off by a team leader and then by a duty registrar for anything reaching the public feed, we cannot telephone counsel on each occasion, and inconsistent treatment as between two operators is itself a ground of complaint — so the reasoning has to be short enough to be applied under time pressure and to be written up in a paragraph a lay complainant can follow.

What I want is a decision procedure my operators can actually run, built out of what the philosophy of language has established about slurs, and I want it to be explicit about the machinery it is drawing on, because I suspect most of our errors come from applying half a theory. I did a philosophy of language course some twenty years ago and could tell you the phrase conventional implicature, but I could not now reconstruct why it matters here. The pieces I take to be relevant: the use/mention distinction, and the reasons offered for thinking it does not do the work people want from it; the claim that a slur's derogatory content is not at issue, so that it survives negation, embedding and quotation and cannot be withdrawn by the speaker's disavowal; the view that a slur smuggles in a presupposition which hearers quietly accommodate even when they reject what is said; the prohibitionist line associated with Anderson and Lepore, on which the word is toxic in every appearance whatever the speaker intends, and which taken seriously appears to condemn a verbatim transcript; the externalist line associated with Christopher Hom, on which the derogatory content is part of the literal meaning and is inherited from facts about the group; and the opposing pragmatic and expressivist arguments that what a slur does is express contempt, so that the speaker's stance is doing the real work. Tell me which of these the procedure adopts at each step, which distinctions must survive the simplification because a decision turns on them, which can safely be dropped for people who will never read the papers, and what the procedure loses by dropping them.

Then the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the default when no answer can be had — we frequently do not know whether a speaker belongs to the group, or whether a recording was made in private, or what an interpreter's counterpart word will do to a listener who is not in the room — and the points at which the question is one of values or of law and must be handed to a registrar or a judge rather than settled at the desk. Map it onto the rendering options we actually have: verbatim; a bracketed descriptor in the public feed with the word intact in the sealed record; a descriptor accompanied by an editorial note; and the situation where the record and the public feed pull in different directions, which I suspect is where most of our difficulty truly sits. Run it on at least four of the situations described above, and let one of them come out against me: I expect the complainant's case to require verbatim captioning, and if that is what the procedure yields I would rather be told so plainly than be handed a route around it. Close with a tie-break rule for when two operators reach different answers, an honest statement of what the procedure cannot decide, and a glossary of five or six terms for staff use, defined in plain language and without jargon. Please write the whole response in British English.
