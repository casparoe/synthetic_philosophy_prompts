For forty-one years I worked in a national metrology laboratory, much of that time concerned with instruments whose indication is produced by a chain of physical events that never passes through the quantity indicated: one calibrates the chain and trusts the correlation. Since retiring I have been attending a philosophy group that meets in the annex of our public library, and I have been working through introductory material on the metaphysics of mind on my own. Old habits die hard. When the group turned to the objection that epiphenomenalism cannot account for our own reports of experience — the paradox of phenomenal judgment, I believe it is called — I found myself asking not what the concept of a report amounts to but where the signal goes.

Two of the regulars argued the matter at length last Thursday, and I could not decide between them. Margit, a retired structural engineer, opened as follows. (1) In a structure, a member that carries no load can be removed and nothing changes; that is precisely how one establishes that it carries no load. Epiphenomenalism holds that experience carries no load, since every utterance we make about our inner lives is fully produced by the brain. The view therefore owes us an account of why those utterances are not merely made but true, and it cannot supply one; and since our talk would be identical were experience absent, nothing in the evidence speaks for its presence. Paul, a hospice chaplain, replied: (2) the premise she is leaning on — that a fact must stand upstream of a belief in the causal order for the belief to be about it, or warranted by it — is intolerably strong, as the long-standing difficulty about our knowledge of abstract objects makes plain; and the epiphenomenalist has a specific answer to the charge of coincidence, namely that psychophysical laws guarantee that where the neural state obtains the experience obtains, so the judgment is caused by the neural state and tracks the experience by law rather than by luck. (3) Margit said this relocates the difficulty rather than removing it, and offered a criterion of her own, which I shall call the screening test: a candidate fact is report-apt only if one can describe circumstances in which the subject's entire physical history is held fixed while that fact differs, and in which the report differs accordingly; if no such circumstances are describable even in principle, no report could ever discriminate the fact from its absence, and the fact is evidentially a spare member. Since the laws in question are strict, she said, the epiphenomenalist fails the test, and Paul's reply merely restates the failure as a merit. (4) Paul answered that the test, pressed consistently, condemns far too much: any fact metaphysically necessitated by the facts to which our reports are causally sensitive would be screened off, on his suggestion including the identity of water with H₂O; and on reductive physicalism the test is passed only because there is no distinct fact left to screen, so what Margit is really offering is an argument for an identity theory wearing the costume of a neutral criterion. He closed by saying that even a successful screening argument would bear on public evidence alone, not on the ground of the belief, which is acquaintance — a relation, he insisted, that no more requires causal contact than a mathematician's grasp of a proof requires the numbers to push on him.

Please adjudicate their exchange rather than survey the territory around it. I should like exactly six numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no suggestions for further reading. In items 1 through 4, rate moves (1) through (4) on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating. In item 5, say which of the two is more right overall and identify what both of them are missing, including whether Margit's screening test can be repaired and, if it can, at what cost. In item 6, state what would change your mind: which single argument or consideration would reverse your overall verdict, and where in items 1 to 5 your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
