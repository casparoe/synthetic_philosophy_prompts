I'm a postdoc in a visual cognition lab, and our review article on top-down influences has acquired an awkward philosophy-facing section that my PI has delegated to me because I did an MPhil before switching to psychology. The section is supposed to settle a methodological question that reviewers keep raising: by what standard do we decide whether a measured effect is a case of cognition genuinely altering perceptual experience, rather than cognition altering attention, or the subject's willingness to report one thing over another, or the way a post-perceptual judgment gets rendered in a rating task? I've assembled eight candidate criteria from both literatures and I want them ordered, not summarized:

1. **Semantic-coherence criterion** (the Pylyshyn-style requirement): the influence counts only if the change in the perceptual state is explicable as a function of the *content* of the cognitive state, so that the transition is describable in terms of what the subject believes, desires, or knows.
2. **Personal-level requirement**: the penetrating state must be one the subject could avow or reason with — a belief, concept, or piece of knowledge — rather than a subpersonal parameter setting.
3. **Stimulus-and-occasion constancy**: the physical input and the attentional allocation are held fixed, ideally with identical stimuli across conditions, while only the cognitive state varies.
4. **Locus-of-effect requirement**: the effect must land on the phenomenal character or content of the experience itself, which pushes the burden onto measures such as chromatic adjustment or matching rather than yes–no detection and rating scales, and onto analyses that separate sensitivity from response criterion.
5. **Overgeneralization-style control** (Firestone and Scholl's line of attack): the effect must fail to appear, or appear differently, in a matched control task that plausibly taps only judgment.
6. **Encapsulation-defeat criterion**: the effect must be shown not to reduce to attentional selection, perceptual learning or unitization, or expectation-driven criterion shifts — i.e., it must be demonstrated that an informationally encapsulated stage has been bypassed.
7. **Predictive-processing recast**: the effect is genuine if it can be modeled as cognition changing the priors, or the precision-weighting, governing sensory inference.
8. **Inert-intervention criterion** (this one is mine, and I want it judged as harshly as the others): an intervention on the subject's cognitive state that is otherwise inert — no change in measured attention, motivation, or instructed response strategy — yet reversibly flips the perceptual outcome, with reversal demonstrable within-subject.

Please score each criterion twice, once from 0 to 1 for discriminating power (how well it filters out effects that the standard battery of pitfalls — demand characteristics, criterion shift in the reporting task, memory and feedback across trials, attentional mediation, low-level stimulus artifacts — would explain away anyway) and once from 0 to 1 for theoretical neutrality (how little it presupposes the outcome, penalizing criteria that already assume a sharp perception/cognition boundary, or that experience has propositional content, or that introspective access to the locus of the effect is available). Then propose and justify your own weighting of those two dimensions rather than using mine, combine them into a single composite score per criterion, and give me the resulting tier list from most to least defensible. Alongside that, tell me which one criterion is most overrated in the current literature and which is most underrated, and give me a single number between 0 and 1 for the fraction of published "top-down effect" studies you'd expect to survive screening by your top-ranked criterion — with your reasoning for the number, since I have to defend it in the discussion.

Two things I'd like treated as part of the same exercise. First, apply your top-ranked criterion to three paradigm families and order them by evidential weight: chromatic adjustment of familiar object shapes toward achromaticity, where observers' gray point shifts in the direction opposite the object's typical color; ambiguous-figure and category-boundary paradigms in which a verbal label or an induced expectation changes where a boundary is reported; and matching tasks in which the lightness of a face or object is judged relative to a comparison patch after social or affective categorization. I want the ordering plus one sentence each on what would raise a paradigm family one rank. Second, flag the places where you're least confident in your own scores — in particular whether criteria 6 and 8 are really independent or whether one collapses into the other under scrutiny — and tell me which of my eight I should cut if the section has to lose two hundred words.
