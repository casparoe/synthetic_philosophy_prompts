I have spent more years than I would care to admit reading rationalist blogs and their comment threads, and one phrase there functions as a conversation-stopper: 'there is no such thing as an uninformative prior.'

It came up recently in a thread about estimating the failure probability of a first-of-its-kind engineering project: no base rates, no relevant historical analogues, one shot. One participant proposed starting from indifference over a bounded parameter and then updating on whatever evidence arrived. A second replied that Bertrand's chord problem settled this in the nineteenth century — any prior you write down encodes substantive assumptions, indifference merely hides them, and so the whole exercise is guesswork in formal dress; better, he said, to admit you are guessing.

I suspect that reply runs together several claims of very different strength, and that at most one of them is true. Could you take the slogan apart for me?

What I would like:

1. A menu of the distinct precise theses the sentence could express. For each, state it with its quantifiers made explicit — invariance under which transformations, over which sample spaces, and with 'informative' cashed out in terms of something definite: influence on the posterior at finite sample size, arbitrariness in the choice of partition, dependence on the parametrization, or failure to be uniquely determined by the structure of the problem.

2. A verdict on each reading: a platitude no Bayesian disputes, a substantive but defensible claim, or something false — or at any rate not supported by the considerations usually offered for it.

3. For each reading, whether it actually licenses the dismissal in my thread. In particular: does Bertrand's paradox, with its three individually defensible-looking answers of 1/2, 1/3 and 1/4, establish that indifference cannot determine a prior, or only that the question is ill-posed until the randomizing mechanism is specified? And what do invariance-based approaches — Jeffreys-style priors, transformation-group arguments, maximum entropy — do to the slogan: are they counterexamples to it, or merely more respectable ways of being informative?

4. Where the cube-factory puzzle fits: edges uniform between 0 and 1 cm, or volumes uniform, or surface areas uniform, each generating a different distribution for the others. Does it add anything to Bertrand's lesson, or is it the same lesson about reparametrization wearing a different hat?

5. Your best reconstruction of which reading the people who repeat the slogan actually mean, and whether they would accept your classification of it once it is stated precisely.

Please do not send me to the literature; I want the disambiguation itself rather than a reading list. What I need at the end is a short statement of the strongest version of the objection that survives your analysis, and what the person proposing the indifference-based estimate could legitimately say back.
