I run the editorial office of a mid-tier political theory / applied ethics journal — roughly 850 submissions a year, and I desk-reject something like half of them. The decisions actually get made by five associate editors, all grad students, who rotate out every two years, so each cohort starts from scratch and I end up re-explaining the same thing every fall. What I need help with isn't the quality judgements. It's that the reasons people give for a desk decision are a mess and I have no way to sort them. A board member tells an AE we should send a paper out because the author is eminent and will make trouble if we don't. A special-issue guest editor wants a friend's piece included. One AE argues we should reject a perfectly decent manuscript because we've already taken four on that topic this year and the reviewers are sick of it. Another wants to accept a weak submission because rejecting it would cost the author their funding. My rough habit has been to call the first batch "pressure reasons" and the second batch "paper reasons," but that clearly doesn't work — some of the pressure reasons feel legitimate (the funding one!) and some paper reasons are really about us rather than about the manuscript. At a conference bar last spring our outgoing editor told me a reason is a reason if it moves you, which was funny at the time and useless at 9am on a Tuesday.

I've read enough around the edges to know there's a whole literature on the wrong kind of reasons problem — the state-given versus object-given way of carving it up, fittingness, those thought experiments where a demon or an eccentric billionaire will do something awful or pay you a fortune unless you admire them. What I want is that turned into something my AEs can actually run: a procedure, in order, that takes about ten minutes per manuscript before they write the desk memo. For each step I want the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, what that answer licenses (reject / send out for review / escalate to me), and where the step is really a call about values that I should be making rather than a factual judgement. It has to work for people who've never read any of the philosophy, so no jargon unless you define it in a sentence. And it has to confront the thing I suspect breaks the naive version: a desk decision is an action, not an attitude, so I'm genuinely unsure whether a right-kind/wrong-kind filter is even the correct tool here or whether using it that way is a category mistake I should drop. Tell me which, and build the procedure accordingly. I also have two home-made tests I want you to keep, repair, or kill: the anonymity test (would this reason still count if the manuscript were fully anonymized and the author's identity erased?), and the flip test (would this same consideration count against a paper I already liked?).

Then run the procedure on three cases. One: a technically sound paper by a very senior author who has spent two years publicly trashing our journal in print and will certainly do it again if we desk-reject him. Two: a paper that's weak but not hopeless, by a grad student whose program requires a publication to keep her funding, and whose advisor sits on our board. Three: a genuinely strong paper on a topic we've already published three times this year, where a rival journal is about to launch a competing special issue, so there's a real strategic case for taking it anyway. I want at least one of these to come out the way I won't like — if the procedure only ever delivers comfortable verdicts I won't trust it. Be honest too about what it can't do: where an AE can game the steps to reach the verdict they already wanted, where two careful AEs will get different results from the same manuscript, and where the ten-minute budget just can't support the step you're asking for. Last thing, and it matters because this goes into a training packet: please seperate out for me which parts of the underlying theory are settled and which are live disputes among people who actually work on it, and don't hand me a contested account of reasons dressed up as the consensus view. If one of your steps only goes through on a contested reading, flag it right there.
