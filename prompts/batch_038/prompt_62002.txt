I teach a graduate seminar on the governance of emerging technologies — eighteen students drawn from philosophy, computer science, and international relations, one 100-minute session. I would like you to design an in-class simulation that makes the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax something the students *do* rather than something they hear described. The two ideas I want them to collide with are these. First, the alignment tax is positional: additional work that improves the trustworthiness of a system delays and degrades it only relative to rivals who decline to do that work, so a choice that reads as prudence in isolation reads as unilateral disarmament once a competitor is in the room — and conversely, if everyone pays, no one is taxed at all. Second, a race is not simply a prisoner's dilemma with extra steps, because the value of the prize is endogenous to the winner's own choices: a team that arrives first with an unvetted system can convert its victory into a worse outcome for itself as well as for everyone else.

Please supply: the number of teams and their roles; the full rules and the information structure, specifying exactly what each team can observe about the others' progress and at what point; a point-based payoff scheme that needs no real money, with the arithmetic worked out so I can run it from a whiteboard ledger; and a timeline that fits the 100 minutes, including at least one mid-game rule change whose effect on behaviour the students can see for themselves. I would like the structure to distinguish a round in which a single team's system is deployed decisively from a round in which every team's system is deployed independently and outcomes aggregate — and I would like the payoff table to reflect that the losing teams still receive some share of whatever the winner's system turns out to be worth.

Then: a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the two ideas above, ending by revealing the terminology and showing the students the gap between what they earned and what universal payment of the tax would have earned everyone. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including a team that refuses to engage or that colludes openly with a rival — and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the real competitive structure the simulation cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply verbally rather than let the game imply.
