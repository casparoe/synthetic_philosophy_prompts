I run a once-a-month thinking evening at our local library — about a dozen of us, twenty-somethings up to a couple in their seventies, nobody with any maths past school, me included. Day job I'm a shift planner at a bus depot, that's as technical as it gets. Next month I've volunteered to do the evening on that old string puzzle, the one where you start with MI and there's four rules and you're supposed to end up with MU but you never can. I've got 75 minutes, a whiteboard and a big pack of index cards.

Don't explain the puzzle to me, I know how it goes — I want you to plan how I should run the evening. What order the bits have to come in, and in particular whether I let them thrash about with the cards trying to make MU first or hand them the counting idea early. I tried this once before and it went badly: fourty minutes lost to arguing over whether you're allowed to double only part of the string, and then when I finally showed them the trick of counting the Is one bloke went "so it's a trick question then" and the air went out of the room. I don't want a repeat.

Here's what I think the actual point is, and tell me if I'm kidding myself: it isn't that you can't reach MU, it's that the answer only comes from asking something the rules never ask. You step outside and count a thing the system itself has no way of counting. My own version, from work, is the rota. Our scheduling software will cheerfully tell me every shift is legal and covered, and it cannot tell me the rota is grinding the same four drivers into the ground, because it doesn't track that. I only see it when I go off and count something it never counts. Should I open with that instead of the strings, or would it spoil them, or is it too close to the point to work as a warm-up? Give me your reasons either way.

Then give me the misconceptions in the order they'll actually turn up and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off. I know for a fact one regular will say this proves the human mind isn't a machine, and there's a retired accountant who will rules-lawyer everything I write on the board. Tell me what I should flatly refuse to get into at this level and announce that I'm refusing it, and whether saving the real Gödel business for the last five minutes — or leaving it out altogether — is the right call. And finish me off with one question I can put to the room at the end that'll show whether they've got the idea or just had a nice evening with some cards.
