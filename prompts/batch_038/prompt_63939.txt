Twice in three years, a field service bulletin of ours has been read aloud in an incident investigation, and both times the sentence under discussion was literally true. What was not true was what it implied.

I run technical communications at a mid-sized industrial robotics firm. We issue roughly fifteen one-page field service bulletins a month to service technicians and to customers' maintenance staff: a newly found hazard, a change to a procedure, the limits of a fix. Each draft gets about twenty minutes of my review, then sign-off from a regulatory-affairs colleague who has no linguistics training and no tolerance for theory. I came to this job after many years of reading rationalist blogs and forums, which left me with a fixed habit: I do not want principles, I want a procedure whose failure modes are stated in advance, and I want to be told which of its steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

Grice looks to me like exactly the right theory for our problem. Readers assume we are being cooperative and as informative as the exchange requires; so when we mention only the configurations we happened to test, they infer we tested every configuration that matters, and when we write that no unexpected motion has been observed, they infer there has been none rather than that nobody asked. The gap between what a sentence says and what it implicates is where our liability lives, and implicature is calculable, which means a reviewer ought to be able to calculate it. What Grice gives us is a diagnostic, not a workflow.

Please write the workflow: a numbered review procedure I can run in twenty minutes on a draft bulletin, usable by someone who has never encountered the cooperative principle. For each step I want the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the branching outcome — ship, revise, or escalate to me. Tell me which of the standard distinctions must survive the compression for a non-specialist and which you are discarding as too costly for the benefit (the flouting-versus-violating distinction, generalized versus particularized implicature, the maxims of relation and manner), and justify each discard. Include a triage rule separating implicatures that are actionable — a reader will act on them and could be hurt — from implicatures that are inert, because a procedure that flags everything gets ignored within a month. Assume I cannot look anything up: no reading list, no citations, nothing that depends on my fetching a source.

Three local constraints break the textbook version, and the procedure has to be built around them rather than patched with a disclaimer step at the end:

1. One sentence, heard once. Our bulletins are machine-translated, posted on shop-floor boards where a technician sees a single paragraph, and read aloud at shift meetings. Any implicature that depends on seeing the whole document, on English-specific word order or stress, or on the reader retaining earlier sentences is unavailable to us. Explain how the test "would a cooperative reader infer X?" changes under that context, and make the procedure's questions reflect the change.

2. The completeness assumption. We have trained our readers to treat a bulletin as our entire answer on its subject, so silence reliably means "there is nothing to say," and quantity implicatures fire even where we intend nothing by an omission. The procedure should key off the audience's prior rather than our intention — but tell me how a reviewer is supposed to estimate that prior in twenty minutes without a study, and say plainly how much of the estimate is guesswork.

3. Cancellation backfires. Our reflex has been to cancel: append "this is not to say that no failures have occurred," or "we have no information either way." In practice a cancellation reads as a hedge, and auditors and plaintiffs' counsel treat hedges as admissions, generating a second-order implicature that we are concealing something. So the procedure needs a rule for choosing among cancelling, stating the stronger thing outright, and deleting the sentence entirely. It also needs a stopping rule: I will not have reviewers iterating "the cancellation implicates X, cancel that, which implicates Y" indefinitely. Give me the recursion and a principled place to cut it off, and say explicitly whether the cut-off is a fact about language or a policy choice we are making and could make otherwise.

Then run the procedure on three drafts of ours and show the step-by-step trace, not just the outcome.

- Draft A: "The revised brake-hold routine has been validated on all Model 7 configurations. No unexpected motion has been observed in the field since firmware 4.2." Both sentences are true as we use the words internally: we validated the configurations present in our own lab, and we have observed nothing because we have not surveyed the field.
- Draft B: "Install the guard extension shown in Figure 3. The light curtain continues to provide its intended protective function." We know the curtain fails to detect a crouching operator in one cell geometry, and the draft never says which firmware versions the fix covers.
- Draft C: our regulatory-affairs colleague has asked us to change "the interlock must be replaced" to "we recommend that replacement of the interlock be considered."

For each, state what a cooperative reader of one sentence, heard once, would infer; whether that inference is something we believe; whether it is actionable; and what the revised text should say. At least one of the three should come out against my preferences — Draft B presumably requires us to state a known defect to customers in writing, and Draft C presumably requires me to overrule legal — and if the procedure instead clears either of them, say so and defend it rather than softening the result.

Close with the honest limits: the classes of case the procedure will get wrong, the steps where two competent reviewers following it will disagree, and what it cannot substitute for.

Verdict: A=…, B=…, C=…
