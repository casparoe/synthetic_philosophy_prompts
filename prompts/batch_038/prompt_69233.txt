I'm a novelist. Third book, about half drafted, due to my editor in the spring, and I've hit a problem I keep papering over instead of solving.

The book follows one object — a cello, made somewhere around 1790 — through a dozen-odd owners across 230 years, and it also follows three human characters across roughly forty years each. Nearly every chapter involves something that's been repaired, replaced, restored, revarnished, renamed, or just quietly worn into a different thing, and I have to decide sentence by sentence how to talk about it. Do I write as though the whole instrument is sitting right there on the page at every moment it appears? Or do I write it as a chain of versions, each of which was the cello-*then*? I'm told there's an actual philosophical fight about exactly this, something about objects being wholly present at each moment versus being stretched out through time in parts — one camp apparently thinks a thing is a four-dimensional worm, which sounds like an insult but I gather isn't. I haven't read any of it and I'm not going to before March. What I need is a working procedure I can run on a passage in five minutes with a coffee going cold next to me, not a seminar.

So build me that procedure. Specifically:

- The questions to ask about a thing in a scene, in the order I should ask them, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where a given question just terminates with "stop, you're done."
- Which of the distinctions in the debate actually change what I put on the page, and which ones I can drop without loss. Say plainly which ones you're dropping and what I'd be giving up. If there are two flavors of the stretched-out view and only one of them is any use to a fiction writer, tell me which and why.
- Where the procedure can't settle anything and it's my call. Mark those spots instead of hiding them behind a test that looks like it decides something.
- What it says about people, not just objects. Two of my three characters change a great deal; one does something at 55 that the 25-year-old version of her would have found unforgivable, and a third barely changes at all, which is its own problem. Does the procedure give me a way to write that, or does it just make me pick a side and stick to it?

Then run it on three things from the actual draft. There's alot of these, but these are the ones that hurt:

1. Chapter 6: the cello's top cracks in 1848 and a luthier replaces it. Chapter 19, set in 1962, has a character swear an oath on the same top that heard the war. I love that line. I suspect it's a problem.
2. A house in the second section that's been added onto so many times the original two rooms are now interior walls nobody goes into. A character keeps saying this is the house she grew up in, and I want her to be right and also to be a little wrong, in the same breath.
3. The one I'm afraid of: my frame device is a closing scene where the 1790 cello and the 2020 cello get described in a single sentence as one thing that has been waiting this whole time. If the procedure says that sentence is a lie, or that it only works on a reading I've been too sloppy to sustain for 300 pages, tell me straight. I'd rather find out now than in copyedit.

And tell me what the procedure can't do. I'll be using it as a drafting tool and then handing the results to a copyeditor who has never heard of any of this, so whatever it outputs has to be checkable against the text on the page without a theory in your head. Don't give me anything that requires me to know a term I haven't already used in this message, or if you must, define it in one line.

Long is fine. Vague is not.
