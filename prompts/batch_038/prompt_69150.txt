ok so here's something that's been bugging me about the fatalism / divine foreknowledge literature and I want someone to actually make the case, not just describe the positions back to me.

Setup. You know the move: God believed yesterday that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. That belief is in the past, God's beliefs are infallible, the past is fixed, and necessity is closed under entailment — so the sea battle is unavoidable. The Ockhamist reply is that God's past belief isn't a hard fact about yesterday but a soft one, becuase which belief it was depends on how the future turns out, so the fixedness of the past doesn't get any grip on it and the inference collapses. Adams, Plantinga, Zagzebski all work in this space.

Here's my worry and I want you to argue FOR it rather than just weigh it: the hard/soft distinction isn't doing any real work in that reply. It's a redescription of the conclusion. Consider a case with no minds in it at all. A lab in 2026 receives, over a channel everyone agrees transmits information backwards in time, a data file recording the outcome of an experiment scheduled for 2034. No belief, no intention, no agent whose mental state is about anything — just a pattern of bits sitting on a disk in the past. Now apply the standard gloss: a fact about the past is soft if it entails something about the future. That disk's having just that content entails what happens in 2034, so it's soft, so the Ockhamist has to say the fixedness of the past doesn't reach it either. But that seems exactly backwards. If anything, the mindless case is where "the past is already settled" has the strongest intuitive grip — there's nothing to reinterpret about a lump of matter — and the Ockhamist is committed to giving that up too. Which suggests the distinction was never tracking fixity. What its tracking is the direction of explanation: the future event explains the past record rather than the other way around, and that's a claim about explanation, not about whether something in the past is metaphysically nailed down.

So argue that, as hard as you can push it. I want the best version of the claim that the anti-fatalist should drop the hard/soft vocabulary entirely and put the weight on explanatory direction instead, and I want you to engage the obvious objection: in the backwards-transmission case the future record really does seem to make the 2034 outcome unavoidable in a way God's belief doesn't, since the receiver can try to bilk it and do the opposite of what the file says. So maybe the cases aren't parallel and the objection only shows the time-travel case is a bad example, not that the distinction is empty. Tell me why they are parallel, or tell me precisely where they aren't and whether the difference actually rescues the distinction or just relocates the problem.
