You are a philosopher of science specializing in Bayesian confirmation theory, with substantial experience advising working groups on methodology for post-hoc analysis. Please think carefully before writing; I want reasoning, not a survey.

**Context.** Our panel reviews modeling papers in fields where the relevant datasets are public and well-characterized long in advance — climate attribution, epidemiological modeling, high-energy physics analyses of archived collision data. Authors frequently propose a new model after all the pertinent measurements are in hand, and then note that the model reproduces those measurements. We must decide how much confirmatory credit such a model earns. The familiar textbook answer is unavailable to us: if the panelists' credence in the datum E is already 1, then P(T|E) = P(T), and E appears to do no evidential work at all — the difficulty raised by Glymour's problem of old evidence, whose canonical illustration is general relativity's recovery of the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion. We are not trying to solve that problem for its own sake. We are trying to know what would count as a solution good enough to be built into a decision procedure that several different analysts, with different evidence histories, must be able to apply to the same paper and get the same answer.

**Before drafting the list**, spend a short passage identifying what is actually up for grabs: which assumptions the standard impossibility result depends on (certainty of E, the static character of the confirmation measure, logical omniscience, the identification of the evidence base with a set of propositions the agent assigns credence 1), and which of those assumptions our use case can and cannot relax. That diagnosis should drive the requirements that follow.

**Then write the desiderata**: the numbered list of requirements any adequate account of confirmation-by-old-evidence must meet for our purpose. For each, give the motivating case or consideration, state the reasoning, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference particular to one camp (and if the latter, name the camp whose prejudice it is). Include, at minimum, requirements about: whether the account must assign degrees rather than a binary verdict; whether the agent's certainty in E may be disturbed; whether the resulting credences must remain coherent and immune to diachronic betting arguments; whether the account may appeal to counterfactual states the agent never occupied; and whether the account must be usable from the first-person perspective of an analyst who has only her current credal state to work with.

Three cases of my own should each be addressed by some requirement, and I want you to tell me which existing family of responses each one embarrasses:

1. *The young knower.* A doctoral student knows E perfectly well from textbooks and has credence 1 in it, but encounters T for the first time years later. An earlier analyst, with an identical current credence function, learned E only after formulating T. Should the two receive the same verdict? A requirement about whether the account may be sensitive to acquisition order, and if it may, what licenses the difference.

2. *Re-derivation.* The same old datum can be recovered twice over: T entails E under one set of auxiliary assumptions about instrument A, and entails it again under a different set concerning instrument B, where the two measurements are of the same quantity by the same collaboration. If each derivation is allowed to confer support, does the credit compound? A requirement forbidding multiplication by re-description, or explaining why compounding is sometimes correct.

3. *The sealed record.* An investigator runs the experiment, seals the output unread, builds T by fitting an entirely separate dataset, and only then opens the envelope to find E. Is this a prediction or an accommodation? The requirement should force an account to say whether what matters is the temporal order of access, the order relative to the agent's credence in T, or something non-psychological.

**Most wanted:** identify any subset of your requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and show why. If the tension is not outright inconsistency but a forced choice about which idealization to abandon, say precisely what must be given up and what the cost is. Then name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates, and say which camp it targets.

**Finally**, a brief scorecard — a table, no more than two lines per contender — assessing the main families of response: partial-credence updating of the Jeffrey type; the Garber-style strategy of relaxing logical omniscience and conditionalizing on the entailment relation itself; counterfactual removal of E from the evidence base; regulative constraints on priors requiring open-mindedness; and dissolutionist responses that reject one of the framing assumptions. Two lines each, no citations.
