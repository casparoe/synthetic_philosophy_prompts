I'm a senior software engineer on the evaluation team for a customer-facing support assistant, and I hold a seat on our model-behavior review panel, which owns the defect taxonomy used to grade sampled transcripts. I have no formal training in philosophy, so I'll describe what we observe operationally and ask you to sort out what it amounts to.

Roughly two percent of the 4,000 transcripts in our monthly sample contain a distinctive pattern. The assistant makes a flat factual assertion and then, in the same sentence or the next, disavows any view about it: "Your invoice failed validation because the currency field is empty, though I don't have a view on whether that's why it failed." Or: "The primary database is unreachable, but I'm not representing it as unreachable." A smaller set runs in the other direction: "I'm confident the retry will succeed, and it probably won't."

There are two camps on the panel. One wants a new check in the eval harness — I've drafted it as a regex-plus-classifier pass I'm calling the assertion-integrity lint — and wants these graded P2 defects and suppressed at inference by forcing the model either to commit or to stay silent. Their argument is that an assertion and a disavowal of that same assertion cannot both stand, so the output is internally broken, and customers reasonably read it as broken. The other camp argues these are the most calibrated things the model produces: it is an artifact trained not to overclaim, and suppressing the pattern just yields confident-sounding text we can no longer distinguish from genuine overconfidence. They would grade the whole class P3-cosmetic, or not at all.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's panel meeting. It needs to contain:

1. A recommendation — adopt the lint rule, reject it, or adopt it with a scope I can defend — including a severity classification and a concrete first rollout step.
2. An account of what is actually wrong with these outputs, if anything, and in particular whether the defect is a matter of logic or of something else. The two camps appear to be using the word contradiction in different senses, and the memo should say which sense does the work.
3. A test our labeling contractors can apply to separate the genuinely defective cases from lookalikes. Note that third-person and past-tense versions of the same surface form do not seem to provoke the same reaction — "the ticket was resolved, but nobody on the team thought it was resolved," or "I told the customer the cache was stale, but I didn't believe it was" — and I cannot articulate why the difference matters.
4. A fair statement of the strongest consideration on the side you do not endorse, together with what evidence from a future sample would change your recommendation.

If this pattern already has a standard name, give it, but do not build the memo around sources I have no way to check.
