Thirty-five years on a genus of moths, and now two years in a philosophy reading group, and I've hit an argument I can't get past. We're on the species problem: biological species concept, phylogenetic species concept, the line that species aren't classes at all but individuals — historical entities — and the pluralists who say there's no one right answer. Everyone in the room accepts the same dataset, because its mine. Genome-wide tree with strong support for a clade; two wing-pattern characters that diagnose it; but a wide contact zone where hybrids are common and apparently fully fertile, mitochondrial DNA crossing at around eight percent while the nuclear clustering stays sharp. Half the room says two species, half says one, eighteen months of it, nobody moves.

My hunch, offered only as a hunch: the data isn't the issue. The two halves are answering different questions with one word — one asking whether there's a lineage with its own trajectory, one asking whether there's an isolated gene pool, a third asking what sort of thing a species would have to be for the word to pick out anything real rather than a line we drew. Maybe the actual split is about explanation rather than ontology: one side wants species to be the units that make evolutionary generalizations come out true, the other wants them to be the things that speciate.

So is that right, or is there a hidden factual question in there that both sides would accept as settling it — some threshold of gene flow, or a fact about whether those clusters would cohere if the contact zone closed? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side says about my moths, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result about clustering, an experiment, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. I want your own diagnosis argued for, not a menu of the available ones.
