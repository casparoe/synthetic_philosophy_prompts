I'm convening a session of a graduate reading group on the knowledge-how debate, and I want to move the discussion past the two stock examples everyone reaches for — the champion who has lost the ability through injury, and the natural who performs beautifully without being able to say how. Instead I'd like you to build me a single thought experiment to specification, one that forces apart three positions that usually travel together. The target is this: a realistic, single-paragraph scenario about a person who is by any ordinary institutional standard the best coach of some demanding performance skill where she works — she can diagnose a flaw in a student's technique from across the room, prescribe the correction, and reliably produce measurable improvement — but who has never been able to perform the skill herself and does not perform it anywhere in the scenario. The paragraph must contain only public, behavioural facts: what she does, what she says, what her students do, what she would do if asked to demonstrate. It must say nothing about her inner representational life — no claims about whether she entertains propositions under a practical or a theoretical mode of presentation, no claims couched in any theorist's technical vocabulary about concepts, dispositions, or ways. That constraint matters because a colleague of mine insists that any case of this shape either smuggles in the verdict or quietly becomes a case about lost ability, and I want the theories to do the work rather than the stipulations. The point of the exercise is to find out whether the intellectualist analysis is committed to attributing knowing-how to someone who cannot perform — an attribution its own proponents are usually anxious to disown — so a case that pre-settles the answer by description is worthless to me.

After the scenario, run the analysis. Give the verdict each of three views delivers on whether she knows how to perform the skill, with reasoning drawn from inside each view rather than from intuition-mongering: a Rylean account on which knowing how is a matter of ability or complex disposition to perform, being careful to say whether your formulation needs the ability to be actually exercisable or only a standing multi-track disposition, since that choice may decide the verdict; a stripped-down intellectualist analysis on which knowing how to do something is just knowing, of some way of doing it, that it is a way for one to do it; and the refined intellectualist view that adds the requirement that the proposition be grasped under a practical mode of presentation. For the second and third, name explicitly what the candidate ways are in your case and argue whether they qualify as ways for her to do the thing, given her body, her training, and her circumstances. Then run all three views on the adjacent question of whether she knows how to teach the skill, and say whether any view returns an answer there that is independently embarrassing. Next, identify the single feature of the scenario that is doing the work — the one detail whose presence or absence determines whether the three views split or converge — and re-run the analysis with that feature removed, so I can see how much of the disagreement survives its removal. Finally, tell me how a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack the case: whether she would dispute the intuition, redescribe the scenario in her own terms and claim it was never a case of the kind I take it to be, or concede the verdict and explain why it costs her nothing.

Two further notes. Choose the skill for philosophical pressure rather than picturesqueness — opera coaching, dive judging, a piano technician who voices instruments, an acting coach, and similar gaps between judging and doing are all culturally familiar, but pick whichever makes the split sharpest and say in a sentence why you chose it. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose of roughly seven hundred words, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables, even though the analysis has many separate parts. Let the transitions carry the structure instead.
