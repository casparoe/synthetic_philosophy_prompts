I am a novelist, and I am three years into a book that has turned out to be, in its machinery, a biography: the life of an invented woman — a provincial surgeon practising in a mid-sized Central European city between the wars — assembled after her death by a colleague who loved her, once betrayed her professionally, and cannot be trusted with the material he has gathered. The book quotes documents, weighs testimony, and passes judgment. It is fiction and will be read as fiction, but I have come to think the traditions I am actually borrowing from are far older than the modern biography: Plutarch's ethical lives, and the saints' lives of late antiquity and the Middle Ages that took over his method and bent it toward edification.

I do not want scholarship, and I do not want a reading list: I have no library at hand and no use for citations, so please do not send me away to sources. What I want is the most important implications of those two traditions — and of the standing philosophical argument over whether anyone can learn anything moral from a life at all — for a set of concrete decisions I now have to make. Be as specific as you can about craft rather than about the history of ideas.

Selection. At the head of the Life of Alexander Plutarch announces that "it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives," and argues that the greatest battles and the capture of cities disclose almost nothing of virtue or vice, whereas some trifling act, a saying or a jest, often reveals character better than the largest events. What does a doctrine of that kind imply about which scenes such a book must contain and which it must refuse? Does it license me to invent the telling trifles, and if so on what terms?

Judgment. Plutarch's narrator is a magistrate who records faults alongside virtues; the hagiographer's is an advocate working toward an ending known from the first page. What stance is available to a narrator who is neither — a mourner and an accuser at once? Where should evaluation sit: in his sentences, in the arrangement of the material, or in the reader's own response? Is there a version of the book in which he never once says she was good and the reader nonetheless arrives at that verdict, and what would have to be true of the arrangement for that to work?

Comparison. Most of Plutarch's pairs close with a set-piece comparison, and at least one of his subjects appears to have been reshaped to serve as the counterpart the pairing required. I am weighing whether to give my surgeon a parallel — a rival, a sister, a second woman who made other choices in the same city. What does the comparative structure buy, and what does it do to the truth of the single life set beside it?

Exemplarity. Can a reader learn anything from a life that an argument could not have taught? Recent moral philosophy has pressed the claim that narrative attention to particulars is itself a form of ethical knowledge; the anti-narrativist reply is that lives do not have plots, and that imposing a beginning, middle and end falsifies rather than discloses. Give me the implications for a character who is meant to be attended to closely but is not meant to be admired, and for the transfer problem: whatever is admirable or damnable in her depends on circumstances she did not choose and my reader will never inhabit. And whether the book can borrow hagiography's apparatus — the fixed ending, the stock motifs, the reverent tone — while refusing hagiography's conclusion, giving a life that presents as saintly and is not.

Finally, and this matters more to me than courtesy: if you think the premise is confused, say so plainly before you answer anything else. It may be that Plutarch and the hagiographers were not doing remotely the same job and that yoking them is a category error I have picked up from my own reading; it may be that a novelist who wants her book to function as a moral example is asking fiction to do something its form cannot do; or it may be that all of this is simply beside the point for a book that will be read as invention. If you believe any of that, argue it against me first and then tell me what survives — I would far rather be contradicted than accommodated. Where your confidence in the historical material is thin, mark it as thin rather than smoothing it over.
