I am the faculty senate's appointee to a working group at a residential university of roughly four thousand students that is in the middle of replacing its old judicial board with a conferencing process loosely modeled on restorative practice. My own training is in the history of science, so I am out of my depth twice over, but I can follow the substantive disagreement that has come to dominate our meetings: two members, both of whom I regard as unusually sharp, disagree about which emotion the new process should be designed to elicit, and they have stopped pretending that this is a matter of implementation detail. One is a philosopher of the emotions, broadly Williamsian, who holds that guilt is the lesser response and that our attachment to it is an artifact of a peculiar historical institution built around blame, obligation, and something like a moral ledger. On her view shame is the deeper one: it concerns who a person is rather than a tally of deeds, it is inherently social, and it engages the thing that actually has to change, namely character. She concedes the familiar liberal worry — that shame is narcissistic, that it tips into withdrawal or rage, that it polices conformity by exposing people to a crowd — but denies that this is an argument for guilt, on the grounds that blame is no less a function of social power than esteem is, and that a process scrupulously designed to avoid shame usually ends up avoiding saying anything true about the person who caused the harm. The other member has run conferencing programs for twenty years and defends guilt on both conceptual and empirical grounds: guilt is directed at the deed and therefore at the victim's claim, it is proportionate to what was done rather than to the whole self, it motivates reparation rather than hiding, and the personality research on shame-proneness and guilt-proneness supports that asymmetry. He also presses the distributive objection that shame-based practices predictably land hardest on students who are already low-status. When a colleague at our last meeting invoked the old anthropological contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures, both of them dismissed it as a caricature, though for opposite reasons, and that exchange struck me as the most interesting thing that happened all semester.

Write a dialogue between these two, set at the point in our work where they have to settle a single concrete provision: whether the conference should include a public acknowledgment component, in which the responsible student describes what they did in the presence of the affected parties and a small circle of peers, or whether the proceedings should remain confidential and terminate in a negotiated agreement about repair. I would like the disagreement about the emotions to be fought out through that provision rather than around it. Both characters should be at the top of their game and neither should be made to look foolish; the shame advocate in particular must not be written as someone who wants to humiliate students, and the guilt advocate must not be written as a proceduralist with no theory of character. They should respond to what the other actually says, concede real points, and each should land at least one blow that the other cannot fully parry — and the one who cannot parry it should say so rather than change the subject.

Two further things I am hoping to see worked out. First, at some point one of them should challenge a presupposition the other is quietly relying on, namely that an institution can determine which emotion its members feel: perhaps the emotional economy is largely fixed by structural facts the working group does not control — the scale of the community, whether membership is voluntary and exitable, whether the audience persists after the event, whether the wrongdoer remains among the people they harmed — so that the real design question is which emotion the institution is going to produce anyway and badly, rather than which one it ought to produce. Second, by the end I want the two of them to have identified what kind of disagreement they are actually having: a clash of values, a bet on contested empirical psychology, a dispute about whether shame and guilt are two emotions or two objects of a single evaluative response, or some combination, with the burden of proof distributed differently than either assumed at the start. Do not let them reach a tidy synthesis or a joint statement; an acceptable ending is one in which they settle the provision for reasons that do not align with their principles, and both notice that fact. Aim for roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words of dialogue with minimal stage directions, and please do not turn it into a survey of the literature or append any references, bibliography, or list of sources — the names and findings mentioned above are all I need them to engage with, and they should argue with those rather than cite them approvingly.
