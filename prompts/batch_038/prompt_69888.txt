Retired four years ago after 34 years at a national weather and climate modelling office, most of it on convection parameterisation — the ugly business of writing formulas for clouds you can't resolve. Since then I've been reading philosophy, slowly and badly, with a dictionary next to me.

At our monthly get-together (ex-colleagues, mostly engineers and physical scientists, we meet at Bill's house) one of the regulars ends any conceptual discussion with a line he's been using for twenty years: that the philosophy of science is about as useful to a working scientist as ornithology is to a bird. He swears he got it from some famous mid-century physicist, and nobody has ever located the source. Last month he aimed it at me when I asked whether our old tuned model had ever actually been shown to be right, or only shown to be convenient. He maintains, seperate from that, that philosophy died somewhere around the time physics stopped needing it. He's a good friend and a much better scientist than I was.

Could you pull that bird sentence apart for me? I want the menu of precise claims it could be making, ordered from the true-and-boring to the radical, with a verdict on each: platitude nobody disputes, substantive and defensible, or false. Please stay on the parts people skate over — whose usefulness (one scientist at the bench, or the enterprise across centuries), useful for what (getting the answer, avoiding a wrong one, or knowing what you already assume), whether it's a claim about philosophy's truth or only its utility, and what the bird half is quietly conceding: ornithology is about birds and true of them, it just isn't needed by them.

Also tell me whether the self-refutation charge lands. Does the line smuggle in a philosophical claim, or is it possibly just an empirical generalisation about what helped people like me — in which case it isn't self-undermining at all but testable, and if testable, is it in fact supported by the history of my own trade?

Two things I don't want. No reading list or citations, I can't chase them and dont intend to. And no closing shrug about how it all depends which reading you prefer — I can assemble that myself. Take a position: say which version of the claim you think is actually true, whether the strong version deserves any of a working scientist's time, and give your reasons. If you think my own hunch is wrong — that the weak boring reading is the only true one and the rest is philosophers consoling themselves — argue against me, not around me. And mark the spots where you're less certain than you sound.

Be concrete about my case if you can. For a decade we ran two convection schemes that produced near-identical twenty-year statistics and told incompatible stories about what the atmosphere was doing, and we never agreed on what would settle which was right. Did that argument need a philosopher, or would one better satellite have finished it?
