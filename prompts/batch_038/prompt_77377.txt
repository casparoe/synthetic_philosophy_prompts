Something I keep coming across: rules that insist a human signs off on automated decisions. Welfare sanctions, credit refusals, content takedowns, flagged exam scripts — there's usually a step where a person looks at the machines output and either approves it or overturns it. But when that step gets audited, what's measured is mostly volume and turnaround, and as far as I can tell a reviewer who overturns a lot often ends up being the one who has to explain themselves, whereas one who approves everything never does. So the human is mandatory and their dissent is quietly costly.

What does this arrangement presuppose about what understanding is, and about who's got it? I keep being told the point is accountability, but I can't tell whether it assumes the reviewer actually understands why the system said what it said, or only that somebody has to be able to say they looked. Reconstruct the implicit theory: which view of understanding or of knowing-someone-else's-reasoning does the review step only make sense under, what rival views does it rule out without anyone saying so, and is the penalty on overturning consistent with the same institutions holding that human responsible for the decision? Also tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where two or three incompatible theories would license it just as happily.

Max about 600 words, British English please. And be straight with me about which bits of this are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on it — I don't want a contested position handed to me as the consensus.
