I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about the revisionist attack on the traditional doctrine that combatants on both sides of a war share the same moral standing to kill. The way that story usually gets told — in the literature and, I confess, in my draft — is that the revisionists make war harder: they withdraw the permission to fight, tell the soldier on the unjust side that he may not shoot, and leave the just soldier's list of legitimate targets shrinking. I suspect that framing is unfair, because the two views can come apart in the opposite direction as well, and I want a concrete case that shows it.

So: construct a thought experiment in which the traditional moral-equality-of-combatants view forbids a particular killing that a liability-based revisionist account permits — the reverse of the usual pedagogical direction.

The specifications are binding:

1. The scenario itself is a single paragraph, and it is a realistic contemporary war between two invented states. No science fiction, no weapons of mass destruction, no child soldiers, no prisoners, no strike delivered from ten thousand feet by someone who never sees the target: an identifiable human agent kills an identifiable human being, and the reader should be able to picture the room.

2. The target is a civilian whose contribution to the aggressor's war effort is real, effective, and hard to substitute within the relevant timeframe, but which does not consist in making or operating weapons. I want something unglamorous — logistical or informational, the kind of contribution that shows up on an organisational chart rather than a battlefield. Describe what he does in concrete, morally neutral terms: his shifts, the systems he maintains, what depends on him, what would happen if he stopped tomorrow.

3. Give the side contemplating the strike near-perfect intelligence. They know precisely what this man does, they know their own cause is just, they know his contribution matters, and they know nobody can replace him this month. I am stipulating this deliberately, because the most common defence of the traditional view is that soldiers cannot be asked to make liability judgements in the fog of war, and I want a case that survives that objection rather than sidestepping it.

4. Do not stipulate the verdicts. Words such as liable, immune, combatant, and direct participation must not appear in the scenario paragraph at all; they belong in the analysis. The two theories have to generate the split out of the described facts, or the case is a fraud.

Then the demonstration:

- State the verdict each theory delivers on this killing, and the reasoning that produces it, step by step, in prose a reader who has encountered neither theory can follow. Define terms on first use.
- Identify the single feature of the case doing the work — the one thing whose presence makes the two views peel apart.
- Turn two dials and report what happens to each verdict. First dial: put the man in uniform and hand him a rifle. Second dial: leave him a civilian but make his contribution trivially substitutable within a week. Say whether either variant collapses the case back into agreement, and what that tells us about where the real disagreement sits.
- Give the three strongest objections a defender of the traditional view would press — including the conventionalist one, that the rules of war are justified as an institutional scheme by what they save rather than by facts about who deserves to be harmed — and either answer them or concede them.
- Finally, test your own design against the sophisticated version of revisionism rather than a caricature of it. A careful liability theorist may refuse the permissive verdict here not because the man is innocent, but for second-order reasons: effectiveness, proportionality, the danger of normalising the practice. If that is what happens under my specifications, say so plainly and propose the minimal change that restores the split.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I will check the literature myself, and guessed attributions are worse than useless to me. Naming the two positions is fine and helpful. A list of who said what is not.

Close with a paragraph telling me where this case is most likely to fail: which consideration would change your mind about either verdict, which of your steps is the weakest link, and whether anywhere in the analysis you are leaning on a contested reading of one of the theories rather than a settled one. I would much rather publish a caveat you wrote than receive one from a reviewer.
