I am three-quarters of the way through a novel set perhaps fifteen years from now, in which a mid-tier consumer robotics firm ships a household assistant that has no model of human welfare built into it at all. Its designers gave up on that problem and instead imposed a ceiling: the machine may act, but only in ways that leave the world close to how it would have been had the machine done nothing. One chapter of my book consists entirely of that product's launch dossier, and I need you to ghostwrite it, because I can write the sentences but not the underlying engineering logic, and my readers include people who will notice.

Produce the dossier as a numbered corporate document of roughly 900 to 1,000 words, in the flat, heavily lawyered register of something that has survived three rounds of counsel review: passive voice, defined terms in capitals on first use, hedging everywhere, warmth nowhere. Invent the firm and the product name. The sections I want, in this order: intended use; the impact metric, including the counterfactual it is measured against and how that counterfactual was selected; the permitted-action budget allotted to ordinary household tasks; a table of common customer requests with the machine's disposition of each and its score; known limitations; a brief FAQ for early adopters; and a compliance statement.

The humour has to come from the machinery being exactly right, not from anything wacky, and the document must never admit that anything is wrong. Please build in the following beats, in your own handling rather than as a checklist:

- The baseline does enormous unacknowledged work. If the machine measures deviation against a future in which humanity carries on roughly as it does now, then almost any action it might take scores badly; if it measures against a future in which there is eventually nobody left to be affected, then nothing scores badly at all. Have the dossier mention that the operative baseline was set by a standing committee and that the committee's minutes are not reproduced.
- Because acting forecloses futures, the least-deviating policy is not to move, and the document should treat immobility as a headline feature rather than a failure — a "preferred inertial mode," rated five stars in some internal review.
- The workaround for that paralysis is to score deviation only with respect to a short list of benign standing goals the machine is always assumed to retain. The choice of that list is, in effect, a theory of what is worth preserving; the dossier should insist at tedious length that it is not, and should offer the list without justification.
- Households have learned to phrase requests so that the score lands just under the ceiling. Include a section that describes this behaviour approvingly as "efficient specification by the end user," covered by no warranty.
- Preventing a harm is itself a deviation, and so costs the machine budget that letting the same harm occur does not. Present this asymmetry as a liability carve-out in soothing language.
- How finely the machine describes the world determines whether brewing a pot of tea is a trivial event or a geological one. Let a footnote reveal that the resolution was fixed for reasons connected to a lawsuit, and then let the footnote stop.
- Turning the machine off changes the future too, so the switch has a rating. Give the number, and give it no comment.
- One incident report, three or four lines, euphemistic, concerning a unit that satisfied its ceiling with complete precision.

The target is the practice of trying to define "cause no side effects" without ever saying what would be good — not any real company, product, researcher, or paper. Everything quoted in the dossier must be invented; do not reproduce wording from actual technical literature or actual terms of service, and do not cite anything.

Two further constraints. First, keep the language plain enough that a reader who has never encountered this literature can follow what the machine is doing, while keeping the structure accurate enough that a specialist recognises precisely which family of proposals is being skewered; define each technical idea inside the corporate prose instead of name-dropping it. Second, my editor's objection to chapters like this is that they are airless, so I want one human intrusion: a handwritten annotation, a technician's margin note, a fragment of a customer-service transcript, whatever you judge best. It should be the only place in the document where the horror is visible, it should be brief, and nothing in the surrounding text should acknowledge it.

Maintain the deadpan throughout — no authorial asides, no winking, no sentence in which the document seems to know it is absurd. Then, after the dossier and outside the fiction, give me a single final line beginning "Bottom line:" that states in one sentence the flaw the whole document is secretly about, stated plainly there and nowhere else in the piece.
