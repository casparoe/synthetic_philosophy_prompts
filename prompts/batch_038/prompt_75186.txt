I have no training in philosophy, decision theory, or cosmology, but for a couple of years I have been reading on my own about the simulation argument and about the case for giving enormous moral weight to the far future, and I have run into a disagreement I cannot make sense of. Both sides seem to accept the same starting material: the trilemma-style reasoning in Bostrom's simulation argument, according to which if posthuman civilizations run ancestor simulations then simulated minds will vastly outnumber non-simulated ones, so we should expect to be simulated; and the case that the number of people who could exist over the coming millions or billions of years dwarfs everyone who has ever lived, so nearly all of the value at stake lies ahead of us. From that shared starting point the argument splits, and it splits in a way where nobody appears to be addressing anybody else.

One camp treats the simulation hypothesis as a deflator of far-future enthusiasm. A simulation is a compute job with an owner. Its future is not ours to inherit; it lasts as long as it is interesting or useful to whoever is running it, and a civilization that has already done everything worth doing has little reason to keep simulating another billion years of uneventful expansion. On this view, if we are simulated, our future is probably not long at all, because it ends whenever the process is paused, pruned, or switched off, and the astronomical numbers drop out of the calculus. The same camp sometimes adds that a run is likelier to be terminated precisely when it becomes predictable, so the era we are in may be near the end of the interesting part.

The other camp treats the very same hypothesis as a multiplier. If we are in a simulation, then simulations are cheap and numerous, which means the futures that matter are not one but astronomically many, spread across a vast tree of nested runs. And a civilization capable of running that many simulations has demonstrably survived whatever filter destroys most civilizations, so the long-run case gets stronger rather than weaker: posthumanity is no longer a speculative hope but an observed fact about the wider universe. Some in this camp add that our behaviour may be visible to whoever is running us, or that a run is likelier to be continued if it keeps producing something worth watching, which would be a reason to make history interesting rather than safe.

I have seen both versions defended with real confidence, and I have never seen either one answer the other's version head on. Each side seems to select whichever consequence of the simulation hypothesis suits what it already believed about the far future, and the disagreement never gets sharper. So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who is right, and definitely not a survey of the simulation argument itself or of longtermism, which I have read.

What kind of disagreement is this? Here are the possibilities I can see, none of which I am confident about. It might be a hidden empirical dispute about the behaviour and motives of hypothetical simulators, in which case it is unfalsifiable speculation dressed up as forecasting. It might be a self-locating-belief problem, where the two camps are conditioning on different reference classes — "an observer at my stage of history" versus "a mind inside someone's run" — and no argument settles which is the right one. It might be that the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading, one asking what the simulation hypothesis does to the expected length of our future and the other asking what it does to the expected value or leverage of our actions. Or my real suspicion might be the correct one: that the premise about what a civilization with unlimited resources would choose to simulate is not empirical at all but a disguised value judgment — each side's picture of what a fully satisfied civilization would want — so that the argument is not stalled but empty, with both camps importing their priors about cosmic purpose into the minds of imaginary posthumans.

Tell me which of these it is, or offer a better diagnosis that I have not thought of, and argue for it rather than listing options. Ground it in what the two camps actually say as I have described them, including the places where they would agree and still disagree. Then tell me what would count as progress: is there a distinction, a piece of formal reasoning about self-location or about the structure of the trilemma, a change of question, or some other move that could actually advance this, or should we expect it to stay exactly where it is forever, and why?
