I co-edit a small satirical broadside that our department prints every year for the interdisciplinary workshop on work and technology — two columns, a run of about two hundred copies, distributed mostly to people who would rather be at the hotel bar. This year we have room for exactly one long piece, and I would like it to be a parody of the annual letter to shareholders written by the chief executive of a fictional company that automates cognitive and warehouse labor; you may name the firm yourself, though please avoid Vantage Grind, which we used in 2023. The letter should run roughly nine hundred words and be followed by a five- to seven-item risk-factors appendix of the kind that gets buried at the back of a filing, for a total of about eleven hundred words, with section headings and the cheerfully grave register of real investor correspondence. The target is not any particular executive or company but a style of discourse: the confident, mission-flavored prose in which automation is announced as a gift to humanity rather than as a change in who does what for whom.

What I want the jokes to come from is the economics, not the vocabulary, so the letter has to be built on a single structural irony and then never break it. Our CEO has automated so thoroughly that, on Marx's own account of value, he should be destroying the source of his profit: surplus value arises only from the expenditure of living labor power, whereas machinery is constant capital that merely hands over its own value to the product piecemeal as it wears out, so a firm that keeps substituting machines for workers keeps raising the organic composition of its capital and should see its rate of profit fall. He nonetheless reports record margins, and he must explain the discrepancy away in the language of moats, unit economics, and macro headwinds, without ever appearing to notice that there is a theory in the neighborhood that predicts his numbers should look different. Every boast should be a Marxian category correctly translated into investor idiom: his mission of giving people their time back should quietly reproduce the claim that once labor in its direct form stops being the great source of wealth, labor time stops being the measure of wealth and free or disposable time becomes the measure instead — except that in his letter the disposable time arrives as a reduction in force; the model his company owns should be described as the accumulated knowledge of the species crystallized into a single depreciable asset, framed as a competitive advantage rather than as an awkward fact about who produced it; the workers who remain should be portrayed as tending, feeding, and being paced by the machines; and he should be pleased, in a passage about hiring, that a large pool of available applicants keeps compensation disciplined. In the appendix, please work in the point Marx makes about machinery in the chapter on modern industry — that a machine can lose value not because it has worn out but because the same work is now done by a cheaper or better one — along with a demand-side worry about the fact that the company's customers and the people it has displaced are substantially the same population, which the CEO will file under softness in end markets. Give him one passage, ideally near the end, in which he indignantly dismisses a rival firm's chief economist or a campus reading group for a crude and mechanical version of Marx, and let the sophisticated version he then attributes to them be, in every particular, his own business model.

Two craft requirements matter more than the rest. First, the piece must stay deadpan and internally consistent: the CEO never winks, never hedges, never uses the words irony or Marx about himself, and no sentence may step outside the letter to explain what is going on — a reader should be able to reconstruct the argument he is failing to see purely from what he boasts about. Second, the satire has to be fair as well as funny, so that someone who has actually read the machinery chapters finds the categories used correctly rather than as a string of slogans, and someone who has never read a page of Marx still enjoys it as a send-up of shareholder letters; if it comes out as a tract with a punchline, or if the CEO starts sounding like a Marxist who has been caught, we cannot run it. Please do not quote Marx or any other real text verbatim — paraphrase instead — and do not name real executives, companies, or works, or add footnotes, references, or a further-reading list; invented tickers, invented metrics, and invented board members are all welcome. Write the whole thing in American English.
