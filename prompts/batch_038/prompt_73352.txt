I'm a handling editor at a journal that publishes work on animal and human behavior, and my desk is full of papers offering evolutionary explanations — some say a trait is an adaptation shaped by selection, some say it's a byproduct of something else that was, some say it's drift or developmental constraint. My reviewers split into two camps: half reject any adaptation claim as an unfalsifiable just-so story, half reject any non-adaptive account as giving up too early, and I keep getting appeals both ways. I need one procedure I can run in about twenty minutes, before deciding whether to send a paper out, that a non-specialist can actually follow: the questions in order, what counts as a decent answer to each, which answers let me keep going and which mean desk reject, and which questions I should be passing to a reviewer or the stats editor instead of answering myself. Ground it in whatever philosophy of biology and behavioral ecology actually say about how you'd test these claims — what evidence could tell an adaptation from a byproduct from an accident — but keep it operational, not a lit review. Then run it on two submissions: a cross-cultural survey paper arguing a sex difference in mate preferences is an adaptation, and one arguing that religious ritual is a costly signal that was selected for even though the underlying belief is a byproduct of overactive agency detection. I expect it to come out against something I'd already made my mind up about — say so plainly. And separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's a live fight; don't hand me one camp's doctrine dressed up as standard method.
