**Question 3 (40 marks; advised length 1,000 words)**

Throughout this course we have used *coherence* in a stipulated narrow sense: an assignment of numbers to a set of propositions is coherent just in case there is no finite combination of bets on those propositions, each of which the agent would accept, whose net payoff is strictly negative in every possible state. Note that this definition speaks of assignments and of bets; it says nothing about belief as such.

With that stipulation in view, distinguish at least two different strategies that have been used to argue that a rational agent's degrees of belief must obey the axioms of probability, and say what each strategy does and does not establish. Address at least one of the following, and say which you are addressing:

(a) whether the conclusion of either strategy is a conclusion about belief at all;

(b) whether either strategy depends on the agent's dispositions towards money rather than towards utility;

(c) whether either strategy depends on the agent's expectations about her own future changes of opinion.

**Candidate's answer**

I address (b) and (c).

There are two families of argument. The first is the betting or Dutch book argument, which begins with Ramsey and is made precise by de Finetti. The idea is that a degree of belief is a disposition to accept bets: if you believe A to degree x, you will buy a bet paying S if A and nothing otherwise for any price up to xS. Given that identification, the axioms fall out of the definition of coherence above. If your assignment violates normalisation or additivity, a bookmaker can choose stakes so that each individual bet looks acceptable to you while the combination loses whatever happens; so an incoherent assignment is one carrying a sure loss with it, and coherence in the stipulated sense is exactly consistency with the axioms.

The second family is the representation theorem strategy. Von Neumann and Morgenstern show that if an agent's preferences over lotteries are complete, transitive, continuous and satisfy independence, then there is a utility function, unique up to positive linear transformation, and a probability function, such that she prefers the lottery with the greater expected utility. Savage's theorem is the same result with the sure-thing principle added: the agent's preferences are over acts rather than lotteries, and the sure-thing principle is what licenses the move from the consistency of those preferences to a probability over states. Jeffrey generalises further, dropping the requirement that some hypotheses have probability one, but the structure of the result is unchanged: coherent preference goes in, a probability and a utility come out, determined up to the usual transformations.

Both families are advertised as establishing that rational degrees of belief must be probabilities. They establish rather less, and for different reasons.

On (b): the Dutch book argument is often said to show only that incoherent agents lose money, which would make the normativity of the calculus depend on a contingent claim about the value of money and on utility's being linear in it. That objection misfires, though not for the reason usually given. It misfires because the payoffs in the definition of coherence are already in utility: the stakes are taken small enough that the agent's utility for them is linear, so linearity of utility in money was never a premise. What the objection does expose is that the argument needs the package principle. It is only the combination that loses, and an agent who evaluates each bet on its own, one at a time, and declines to consider them jointly, has not thereby been shown to hold inconsistent commitments. Since coherence is defined over finite combinations, the stipulation builds the package principle in, and such an agent rejects the definition rather than being caught by it.

On (c): the betting argument extends over time, and the extension is what supports conditionalisation. If an agent announces now that she will revise by some rule other than conditionalising, a bookmaker who sells her a bet now and another after the evidence arrives can guarantee a loss. He does not need to know in advance what she will learn: he can make the second bet conditional on whatever turns up, since her announced rule already fixes what she will pay for it. The same holds when she expects an experience she could not now articulate, since the second bet is settled by whatever she then takes the evidence to have been. So the diachronic argument is stronger than the synchronic one — the synchronic argument constrains the shape of a single opinion set, while the diachronic argument constrains the whole trajectory, whatever the world does. Jeffrey's probability kinematics is what coherence requires when the evidence does not arrive with certainty: the agent redistributes probability over a partition and holds the conditional probabilities on that partition rigid, and conditionalisation is the limiting case in which one element of the partition receives probability one. Any other way of changing one's mind leaves one open to the same book, so radical probabilism is not a relaxation of the coherence requirement but a restatement of it.

The representation strategy has a different limitation. It is an existence-and-uniqueness result about preferences, and it tells us nothing about belief unless we already accept that beliefs are the sort of thing that can be read off preferences at all. An agent with no betting dispositions, or one whose preferences concern only a handful of matters she happens to care about, is not incoherent in the stipulated sense; she simply falls outside the scope of the definition. That is the real cost of the stipulation: coherence is a property of assignments, so a theory built on it can say which assignments are ruled out but cannot say that anyone must have assignments. The most the two families jointly establish is that if an agent is to be represented as having degrees of belief, and if her dispositions are to survive the package principle, those degrees of belief must be probabilities.
