You are a philosopher of action with a working knowledge of the empirical literature on intertemporal choice. Approach what follows as an exercise in adjudicating a coding record, not as a general essay on weakness of will.

I help run a study in which participants submit first-person reports of occasions on which they took a smaller, sooner reward over a larger, later one. A coding panel then assigns each report to one of two categories: akrasia (genuine weakness of will), or not akrasia (something else — preference change, compulsion, revision for reasons, and so forth). The panel's six codes to date, with the reports in my own compressed paraphrase, are these. In every case the panel was unanimous.

1. A participant ate a second slice of cake at a party. At the moment of taking it she judged, all things considered, that she should not; she did not feel compelled; afterwards she disowned the desire that moved her. **Code: akrasia.**

2. A participant who had resolved at New Year to give up smoking relapsed in the third month. By then he had come to think the resolution over-strict; he reasoned that a stressful week plus the odd cigarette was harmless, and at the moment of lighting up he judged it fine. He does not repudiate the desire that moved him. **Code: not akrasia.**

3. A participant took out a savings product carrying a substantial penalty for early withdrawal, and two years later paid the penalty in order to withdraw and spend the money. She knew precisely why her earlier self had locked the money away, thought that self had mispredicted how much she would need it, and cited that misprediction as her reason for breaking the arrangement. She felt no conflict at the time and no regret afterwards. **Code: not akrasia.**

4. A participant watched an entire series instead of revising for an examination. At the time she judged, all things considered, that the viewing was worth more than the revision, since the examination was still a week away. Afterwards she reproached herself severely and said she should have known better. **Code: not akrasia.**

5. A participant in early recovery from alcohol dependence drank during a craving episode. He judged throughout that he should not, and wanted not to, but the episode felt, in his words, like something that happened to him rather than a decision; there was no window in which he experienced himself as able to refuse. **Code: not akrasia.**

6. A participant in our laboratory choice task took £130 available the following day rather than £200 in sixty days, having said a fortnight earlier that the £200 was the better deal. Even as she made the choice she thought taking the £130 was the wrong one; she felt torn; afterwards she wished she had waited. **Code: akrasia.**

The seventh case is coded tomorrow.

7. Same task. A participant chose between £130 available the following day and £200 in sixty days. Asked sixty days out, she said without hesitation that the £200 was better all things considered, wrote down her reasons (the implied rate of return; she had no present need of money), and predicted that she would still take it when the time came. A lock-in option that would have committed her was offered and declined, her stated reason being that she was confident she would follow through. When the £130 became immediately available, she took it. Her report at that moment: "The £130 genuinely seems better to me right now. I know I said the £200 was the better deal, and I suppose in some sense I still think that, but I don't feel torn about it." She offers no new consideration bearing on the choice: nothing about her circumstances, her needs, or the options has altered except that the smaller amount is now in hand. In debriefing she reports no regret, says she would choose the same way again, and does not disown the desire that moved her. The parameters our model fits to her earlier choices — a present-bias parameter of roughly 0.65 with a per-day long-run discount factor very close to 1 — predict exactly this reversal and no other reversal in her record. Unlike case 5, there is no craving, no compulsion, and she reports having felt free to decline at every moment.

Please work through the following in order.

(a) Reconstruct the rule, or rules, the panel might be running. Consider at least these four candidates, but do not treat the list as exhaustive, and say so if you think a better rule is available:

- **R1 (judgement):** akrasia if and only if, at the moment of action and while retaining ordinary control, the agent judges all things considered that she should do otherwise.
- **R2 (endorsement):** akrasia if and only if, at the moment of action and while retaining ordinary control, the desire that moves the agent conflicts with a standing value she reflectively endorses, and she repudiates that desire.
- **R3 (rational reversal):** not akrasia where the reversal is what the agent's own discounting predicts and her considered ranking at the moment of action favours the action; akrasia where her considered ranking at that moment still favours the alternative.
- **R4 (reason-revision):** a considered judgement formed at a reflective distance remains the agent's better judgement until she revises it for a reason; a reversal produced by nothing but the arrival of the reward is akrasia even if she feels unconflicted, provided she retained ordinary control.

For each rule you take seriously, state whether it reproduces all six earlier codes — if it does not, say which code it gets wrong and whether the rule can be repaired — and then state what it implies for case 7. Where a rule's verdict on case 7 turns on how some phrase in the report is to be read (I have in mind particularly "in some sense I still think that"), say so explicitly and work through both readings.

(b) Recommend the panel's code for case 7, on the assumption that its aim is internal consistency with its own record rather than philosophical correctness. Identify which of the six precedents is the closest analogue to case 7; say in what respect it is close and in what respect it is not; and note which feature of that precedent does the explanatory work there, and whether that same feature is present in case 7.

(c) State candidly what the panel would be committing itself to either way. If it codes akrasia, which of its earlier codes is most exposed to a charge of inconsistency, and what principle is it adopting about the evidential weight of felt conflict and of after-the-fact regret? If it codes not akrasia, what has it conceded about the standing authority of a considered judgement made at a reflective distance, and about the significance of a reversal that is nothing more than the arithmetic of present bias?

(d) Propose one further report the panel could collect next week that would most sharply discriminate between the rules that survive the record, and state the code each surviving rule would assign to it.

Reason step by step, and begin with a short brainstorming section listing the features of case 7 that might be doing work and the precedent each one recalls. Do not cite literature, name studies, or supply a bibliography: argue from the material given together with whatever you already know about weakness of will, present bias, and dynamic inconsistency. Be decisive in part (b) — hedging there is a failure — while remaining honest in part (a) about which rule the panel is in fact running.

(e) Close with a single line giving your recommended code and a percentage confidence that this is the code the panel should give if its aim is internal consistency.
