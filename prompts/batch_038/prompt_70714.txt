I am a teaching assistant for a second-year undergraduate ethics unit on well-being, and my fifty-minute section next week has to cover one item only: the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. As I understand the charge, the things we call pleasures — the first sip of cold water after a run, the moment a proof finally clicks, the resolution of a chord, the warm background glow of a good evening with friends, the easing of a cramp — do not look like instances of a single kind of feeling, and if "pleasure" picks out nothing unified, then the hedonist claim that pleasure is the only thing good in itself may be resting on a word rather than on a thing. Do not explain the problem to me; plan how I should explain it.

I need the order of the pieces and the reasons for that order — what has to be in place before anything else can register as a difficulty. My own guess is that students cannot feel the force of the multiplicity of pleasures until they first see that hedonism requires pleasure to be one kind of thing if it is to be a theory of value rather than a list of good things, and that a prior step is needed before that, namely getting them to notice that they were already treating "pleasure" as naming something. I may have this backwards, or I may be missing a piece that has to come between them; tell me where the question of what counts as a common kind must be settled, and how much of that I can do without turning the section into metaphysics.

Tell me which single case should carry the whole section, and what that case must have and must lack. I was going to build it around the contrast between a bodily pleasure (cold water when thirsty) and a pleasure of the understanding (seeing why an argument works), but I worry that pairing is too tidy, since it invites the student reply that the difference is merely body versus mind and that both sides still feel like something. Should I instead anchor on a pleasure that seems to be nothing but the cessation of a pain, or on barely noticed background contentment that many of them will insist does not feel like anything at all? Choose for me, and say why the alternatives fail, including what the chosen case has to leave out in order not to prejudge the answer.

Map the misconceptions and locate each one at the point in the sequence where it should be headed off, not at the end where it is too late. I expect at least these: that there is after all one feel, a sort of buzz, running through all of them; that heterogeneity by itself refutes hedonism, so the section is really an obituary; that this is just the old puzzle about comparing and adding up intensities across different pleasures; that Mill's higher and lower pleasures already answered it; and that relocating pleasure in an attitude toward something rather than in a feeling changes the subject and stops being a theory about experience. I also expect one student to say that of course pleasures are various, since everything is various, and I do not know how to answer that without over-explaining.

I want to close on the live options rather than a verdict — roughly, that some philosophers defend a single common hedonic feel, some relocate pleasure in attitudes of a propositional kind, and some follow Aristotle in treating pleasure as inseparable from the activity it completes rather than as an ingredient added to it. I have perhaps seven minutes for this and I do not want it to collapse into name-dropping. Tell me how much of that landscape students at this level can actually hold at once, and what I should leave out, saying so explicitly; I suspect I should not attempt the view that pleasure is not a feeling at all, nor any of the empirical work on the neural machinery of liking and wanting.

End the plan with one question I can put to the room whose answers would tell me whether they have grasped the problem rather than merely agreed that pleasures are many.

Two requests about how you present this. Keep the plan answerable by someone with no access to sources, and do not pad it with references. And please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the topic: do not present any of the three closing options, or the claim that heterogeneity is or is not fatal to hedonism, as the consensus view for the sake of a tidier narrative. Where your recommended sequence depends on taking a side in a live dispute — for instance, on assuming that pleasures really are introspectively heterogeneous, or on assuming that they are not — say so, so that I can flag it to the students rather than smuggle it in.
