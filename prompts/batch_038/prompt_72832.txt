I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and I want to pair Borges's story "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" with the dispute over original versus derived intentionality: Searle's claim that marks on paper and states in a machine carry only content borrowed from the minds of their users and producers, against Dennett's two-bitser, whose lesson is that nothing intrinsic to a physical state fixes what it is about, since the same state can be a detector of quarters, of dimes, or of counterfeits depending on how it is embedded and on what interests an ascriber brings to it. Menard looks like a gift to both camps at once. The pages he produces are, by the story's stipulation, mark for mark identical to Cervantes's, yet his narrator reports them as meaning something different and weightier — the difference living entirely in the history behind the ink and in the situation of the reader.

Does the story therefore show that all textual content is derived, or is it rather the best fictional case for something like original intentionality, since what makes the identical pages mean what they mean is a mental history no arrangement of symbols could supply? Please work through at least two specific moments — Menard's abandoned first method of trying to become Cervantes, and the narrator's side-by-side of one sentence under two attributions — and say where the philosophical vocabulary genuinely earns its keep there. Then play the honest critic: mark where the story refuses the reading, whether because its narrator is vain and plainly unreliable, because the difference in meaning is asserted rather than dramatized, or because Borges is mocking the very interpretive inflation the reading feeds on. Say also whether the closing recommendation of deliberate anachronism and erroneous attribution generalizes the thesis or turns it into a parlor game, and end with what Menard gives the intentionality debate that a philosopher's thought experiment could not.
