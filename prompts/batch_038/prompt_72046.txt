> The starting point is Gödel's first incompleteness theorem. If F is a formal system whose axioms can be listed by a program, which is strong enough to represent ordinary arithmetic, and which is consistent, then there is a sentence G(F) in F's language that F neither proves nor refutes. G(F) is produced by the diagonal construction and says, in effect, that G(F) is not provable in F. Strictly, Gödel needed a condition slightly stronger than consistency to get both halves of that; Rosser showed how to build a more complicated sentence that needs only consistency. The second theorem adds that if F is consistent, F cannot prove the sentence Con(F) expressing its own consistency.
>
> Lucas and Penrose turn this into an argument against mechanism. Suppose my mathematical reasoning is correctly captured by some Turing machine F, and suppose F is one of those machines whose outputs I can check to be sound, in the sense that everything it proves is true. Knowing F is sound, I know it is consistent, so I know G(F) is not provable in F. But that is exactly what G(F) asserts, so I can see that G(F) is true. F cannot. So I am not F. Nothing in that reasoning depended on which F we picked, so it goes through for every candidate machine, and human mathematical understanding is therefore not an algorithm.
>
> It matters what the theorem is and is not doing here. It does not show that there are arithmetical truths no proof of any kind could establish; a stronger system can prove G(F), and mathematicians do that routinely. What it supplies is a recipe for producing, given any machine we can see to be sound, a sentence that machine cannot reach and we can. Gödel drew the anti-mechanist conclusion himself, and Lucas in the sixties and Penrose in the eighties and nineties mainly sharpened and publicized it. Penrose is the one who pressed the point that the argument turns on soundness rather than bare consistency, and he tends to state his conclusion negatively, as the claim that mathematicians are not using a knowably sound procedure.
>
> The conclusion also isn't that the brain does something a computer couldn't do given enough time and memory. If reasoning were a non-computable physical process, no Turing machine simulation would reproduce it no matter the resources. Penrose's ideas about quantum gravity in microtubules are an attempt to say where such a process could come from: with Hameroff he argues that gravitationally driven collapse of superpositions in microtubules is neither random nor algorithmic, so it could supply the missing ingredient. That part is logically separable from the argument above. The Gödelian step is meant to show that something non-algorithmic is going on; the physics is meant to say how it is possible.
>
> The strongest objection, usually associated with Putnam and pressed hardest by Chalmers in the nineties, is that the argument can't be turned on ourselves. We might be able to recognize that some other machine is sound, but we can't recognize that we are, because that would require proving our own consistency and the second theorem rules that out. So the most the argument yields is a disjunction: either we aren't machines, or we are machines that can't know it. Penrose's reply is that a system running on objective reduction isn't bound by the second incompleteness theorem the way a formal system is, since it isn't a formal system at all, so it can stand outside itself and see its own consistency — which is why he thinks the objection dissolves if the physics is right.
