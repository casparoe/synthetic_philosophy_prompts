I write historical novels set in the last century of the Roman Republic, and this spring my publisher has put me on a long tour; one of the stops is a Saturday-morning workshop for a public library's civic-engagement series. The room holds about twenty-four adults, none of them classicists, several of whom will have come expecting a disguised argument about their own country's politics, and the librarian who invited me has asked privately whether I can keep it from turning into a shouting match. There is no projector, there are folding tables, I have a co-facilitator who teaches high-school history and is far better than I am at managing a room, and I have two hours. Every book-club event I have done so far has decayed within ten minutes into me talking, which is the one outcome I want to avoid here: I want the participants to do something, and to be wrong at it for a while before they are right.

So: design a playable activity that gets twenty-four people to discover, without being told, that the Republic's constitution was mostly a body of unwritten understandings — what the Romans called mos maiorum, the custom of the ancestors — whose binding force was a property of everyone's expectations about everyone else's forbearance, and that such a system does not wear down gradually but tips. I want them to feel the ratchet rather than hear about it: that the first violation which pays changes the payoff of violating for everybody else, and that each subsequent violation arrives with a locally respectable justification lifted from the one before. Please build the game out of real Roman material rather than a generic senate with generic rules: the tribunician veto and Tiberius Gracchus having his colleague Marcus Octavius stripped of office in 133 BC for using it against him, followed by his attempt at a consecutive tribunate; the Senate's emergency decree first used against Gaius Gracchus in 121 BC, which conferred political cover and moral authority rather than any legal power to set law aside, and left the liability with the magistrate who acted on it; Bibulus in 59 BC trying to invalidate an agrarian law by announcing hostile auspices, being driven out of the Forum by a mob with his fasces broken, the law passed anyway, and the Senate afterwards declining to annul it; and the fact that a voting assembly could neither debate nor amend a proposal, only accept or refuse what a magistrate had put to it. I would like the obstruction tools — the veto, the declaration of adverse omens, prolonged speech to run out the daylight, the suspension of public business — to be genuinely powerful at the start of the game and genuinely worthless by the end, with that change effected entirely by the players' own choices rather than by a rule I hand down midway.

The mechanic I have half-invented, which you should either develop properly or tell me will not work: whenever a player breaks one of the unwritten understandings in order to get something, they must first say aloud, in one sentence, what precedent they are prepared to have cited against them later, and write it on an index card that goes into a shared pile; from then on any player may draw that card and play it as a licence to do the same kind of thing, and the card cannot be argued with, only invoked. My hunch is that this makes the ratchet tactile, and that it also produces what I actually care about — the discovery that every violation is defensible in the moment it is committed, and that the defence is a gift handed to one's opponents. Give me the roles and factions (I would much rather have uneven, asymmetric blocs with incompatible interests than equal teams), a scoring or resource scheme that needs no money and does not turn the room into a brawl, a materials list I can assemble at a kitchen table the night before, a minute-by-minute running order for the full two hours, and a debrief that names the idea only at the very end and maps particular moments in the game back onto particular parts of it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it live. And be frank about two things I suspect are real problems: whether a two-hour game can stand in for a fifty-year process at all, and whether the modern analogy is a gift or a trap — the moment I let participants map Rome onto their own country, half of them will stop reasoning about Rome, and if I forbid the mapping they will do it silently in their heads. I would like a debrief device that lets them make the comparison but requires them to state the structural disanalogies first: the size and composition of the citizen body, an assembly that could not deliberate, an empire run on slave labour with no standing police, a Senate whose power was prestige and advice rather than legal authority. Please do not write me a bibliography or a reading list; I will handle my own sources. Do, though, flag anywhere I have misstated the Roman material or leaned on something historians genuinely dispute. Finally, tell me how confident you are that this design will in fact produce the experience I have described rather than some other one, and single out the single design decision you are least sure of, and why.
