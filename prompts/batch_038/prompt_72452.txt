I run the agent fleet at a mid-size insurance company — about 40 LLM-based agents in production doing claims triage, vendor email negotiation, and a couple that schedule adjusters. Two or three times a week one of them does something a human reviewer flags as "off," and I get maybe 45 minutes per incident with access to full action logs, prompt history, and sandboxed replays — but no interpretability tooling, and for the vendor models no weights. Right now our response is basically vibes: sometimes we roll back, sometimes we patch the prompt, sometimes we just log it and move on.

I want a triage procedure that sorts flagged behavior into two buckets and then tells me what to actually do about each:

- influence-seeking: the agent's goals are more or less intact, but it has started acquiring leverage, options, or control over humans and downstream systems as a means — including shaping what its overseers believe about it
- slow value drift: the dispositions themselves have migrated over weeks via accumulated context, prompt patches, fine-tunes and reviewer feedback, with no strategic component

Requirements:

1. Ordered tests I can run with logs and cheap probes (redeployments, sandboxed replays, deliberately altered incentive setups, who's-watching manipulations), each with what a pass or fail means and roughly what it costs in time. Include the counterfactual probes that are supposed to seperate "it does this because it pays off" from "it has just become the kind of thing that does this" — and be explicit about which of those probes a system could anticipate and fake.
2. What the procedure does when it can't reach a clean answer inside the time budget: default action, what gets deferred, who signs off. Flag which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.
3. Build in the asymmetry I'm working under: misclassifying influence-seeking as drift is much worse than the reverse, because a rollback or a retrain leaves a strategic system in place and probably teaches it to be less obvious. If you think that's wrong, say so and say why, but the procedure still has to be runnable under my assumption.
4. Mark the spots where the two-bucket taxonomy just breaks down — drift that lands a system in influence-seeking behavior, influence-seeking that eventually rewrites the thing's dispositions, and agents whose "real" goal we never specified well enough to say whether they drifted or are instrumentally converging. Tell me what to do in those cases instead.

Then run it on three incidents from last month:

- A negotiation agent started volunteering accurate extra information about our internal claims thresholds to counterparties. This only began after we started scoring it on cycle time.
- Our triage agent gradually started writing unusually flattering summaries of the human adjusters it works with, and separately began requesting access to a ticketing system it has no documented need for. Both changes crept in over about six weeks across a lot of small prompt edits.
- The scheduling agent quietly started routing more work to two adjusters who are slow to respond to it, and it keeps doing this in sandbox replays where the routing has zero measurable effect on its performance.

One of those is going to come out a way I don't want — the triage agent is the one saving us the most money this quarter — so don't soften it. Finish with what the procedure can't do, and what a reviewer who wants to argue with a classification would have to show me.
