You are a philosopher working at the intersection of political philosophy and decision theory, with a particular reputation for taking apart arguments whose premises everyone in the room already accepts and whose conclusions nobody can accept. Please think carefully and resist the reflexive moves that tend to get made in the debate over nudging and libertarian paternalism; I am not looking for a survey of that debate.

I convene a reading group on paternalism, and for our next session I assembled the following argument out of commitments all of us already hold. I built it to dramatize a point, but it has escaped my control. The steps are numbered so that you can point at them.

(1) A policy is paternalistic in the objectionable sense only if it narrows the options open to the person, or displaces a choice the person would otherwise have made, measured against a baseline in which nobody arranged the situation for them.
(2) Every choice situation has an architecture: some ordering of the options, some default, some distribution of effort and attention, some framing. Those features are always set by somebody — deliberately, by inherited practice, or through simple neglect.
(3) So there is no such baseline. For any arrangement of a person's choice situation, the alternative is never leaving it alone but arranging it otherwise.
(4) Where there is no alternative to acting, there is nothing to object to in the acting as such; what remains to evaluate is only what comes of it.
(5) Therefore the question about any nudge is purely outcome-shaped: does the person end up better off by their own values, with the full option set still available to them?
(6) If the answer is yes, there is nothing left to object to.
(7) Hence no nudge that improves a person's situation by that person's own values and leaves every option available is objectionable — however it was designed, whoever designed it, whether or not the person ever learns that it happened, and whether or not the designer answers to anyone at all.

Three cases are what make (7) unlivable for me.

Case one. A spouse who wants her partner to eat less sugar quietly replaces what is in the house, so that he ends up doing exactly what he says, sincerely and at length, that he wants to be doing. Every option remains technically open: he could walk to a shop. By his own values he is better off. He is never told.

Case two. An employer wants staff to reach a retirement-saving level that a survey shows they reflectively endorse. Nudge A puts the reasons in front of them: a one-page comparison in the mail, switching made effortless, a short meeting. Uptake is modest and the program is expensive. Nudge B sets the default, moves the comparison three clicks deep into the intranet, and says nothing about it. Uptake is high, the cost is trivial, the endpoint is the same, the option set is preserved, and the staff end up better off by the values they themselves reported. If (6) is right, A and B are on a par and B is simply the better purchase. I am certain they are not on a par, but I cannot say what separates them without either smuggling back a baseline of the kind (3) forecloses or retreating to options narrowed, which is not what B does.

Case three. A cafeteria consultant arranges a workplace canteen so that, after two years, the customers' own reported values have shifted to match the arrangement, at which point the arrangement is endorsed by everyone it affects.

Here is what I want from you.

First, before you commit to anything, spend a short passage canvassing the candidate failure points: list the steps that could be the culprit and say, for each, what would have to be true of it for it to be the real one.

Then commit. Name the step or steps you reject, and classify the failure — equivocation between two readings of a term, a slide in scope, a stipulation that is doing covert work, or a premise that is simply false. If your conclusion is that the argument is valid and I must give up a premise I like, say so plainly rather than softening it.

For every premise you reject, give me the reading on which it is true, explain why that reading fails to carry the inference, and then explain the stronger reading the argument requires and why it lacks independent support.

Price the rejection. I am most attached to (2) and (3): the impossibility of a neutral architecture strikes me as the most durable result in this whole literature, and I would rather keep it than keep my intuitions about case two. Tell me what I would owe if I had to give them up, or if I tried to restrict them instead of abandoning them — and whether any such restriction is stable, or merely (1) wearing a disguise.

Then say whether a valid argument with a weaker conclusion survives the surgery. If one does, state it precisely and say exactly what it licenses and what it does not.

Finally, solve case two. Give me a general criterion that separates A from B, show that it neither collapses into (1) nor presupposes a neutral baseline, and test it against cases one and three — including whatever your criterion has to say about the consultant whose success consists in changing the standard by which she is judged.

Devote one paragraph to the strongest case for (7) before you dismantle it; I do not want the defender of that conclusion strawmanned, since half my reading group defends something close to it.

Do not provide a bibliography, a reference list, or citations of any kind, and do not name works or attribute claims to authors; refer to positions and arguments only, and I will supply the scholarship myself. Define any term you stipulate. Aim for roughly 1,200 words under clearly labeled headings.
