I am a novelist, and in six weeks I have to give the address at the reopening of the Mechanics' Institute in the town where I grew up: a building shut since 1987, now restored, to be run as a free, staffed reading room and lending library open seven days a week, evenings included. I would like you to write the speech for me. It has to run nine or ten minutes aloud, so call it fourteen hundred words, and it must be written to be spoken — short clauses, audible places to breathe, nothing that asks a listener to hold a sentence in the air until the end of it arrives. The hall has a stone floor and an echo, and the paper will print the text in full afterwards, so anything that works only because I am standing there still has to work on the page.

The room will contain: the charitable trust that paid for the restoration, and its two representatives; three councillors and the MP; the retired volunteers who ran a twenty-year campaign and are mostly in their seventies; a sixth-form reading group who will actually use the place; two reporters; and — this matters more than the rest — something like forty men and women who lost their jobs eighteen months ago when the components plant on the edge of town closed. Several of them are in the campaign group. All of them will be listening.

My own position, which the speech must carry without ever announcing itself as a position. First: the case for free time has nearly always been made in a register that concedes what it is arguing against. People should work less because they will be healthier, more productive, better parents, less of a burden on the clinic; rest is justified as servicing, as the maintenance of a machine that then returns to work. That is a real argument and it wins things. But it grants that an hour counts only if it is spent, or spent preparing to be spent, and that any hour turned to no account must plead its case. I want the speech to stand in the other register — that free time is not what is left after work, not a reward for it, and not answerable to anyone. That it is prior. I do not want it to argue for a shorter working week, though: the campaigners made that case better than I could, and half the room would hear it as advice.

Second: this position has a theological skeleton in it and I would rather it were acknowledged than flattered. The culture I grew up in was shaped by a faith that made labour a sign and idleness a sin, that turned a whole life into an audit in which rest could only ever look suspicious; the faith has largely gone and the audit remains, running now with no auditor. A speech praising leisure in a former institute building while pretending that inheritance is not in the walls is telling a comfortable lie. But I want it present as pressure — two or three sentences, no more, and no sermon.

Third, and here the speech succeeds or fails. That building was not neutral about how a free hour should be spent. It was founded by people convinced that a working man's evening had to be taken back from the public house and pointed at improvement. I have read the minute book in the local studies centre: women barred until 1894; a minimum number of lecture attendances each quarter on pain of losing your ticket; fiction excluded from the reading room, on the grounds that novels were the wrong use of a free hour — absorbing, undemanding, not improving. I write novels. I will be standing in a building that would not have shelved me, unveiling a plaque, in front of the people who wrote the cheques, saying that free time is not for anything. The speech has to hold that without being clever about it and without apologising for the founders, who raised it out of subscription pennies and were, on their own terms, right that a lamplit room full of books beat a bar.

Concretely, I need it to: hinge on the fact that our word for school descends, through Latin, from the Greek word for leisure — that a classroom and a free hour used to be the same word — and use that hinge to say what the institute's improvement project and the idling it policed have in common. Distinguish, in flat plain language and without any terminology, between having hours free and having leisure: someone with three hours a night who is too wrecked, too anxious or too dulled to attend to anything has no leisure at all, because leisure is a capacity for attention rather than a gap in a timetable. That is the sentence the plant workers will recognise, and it is the only thing keeping the speech from being a taunt to people who have all the free time in the world and none of the freedom. Say one thing the trust will not like — that if this room is judged on footfall, loans and course completions it has been rebuilt as a workplace, and that a share of its success will consist of people sitting in it doing nothing for hours, unproductively, on purpose — said once, plainly, without hedging, and without letting the last third of the speech become a row.

It is not a plea for money; the money has been raised, and the harder job is to say what the victory is for. Give me one moment of real wit early, so the room relaxes, and one near the end where I turn to my father: thirty-one years of nights at that plant, no holiday longer than four days, dead six years, and certain this building was a criminal waste of a good site. I do not want him eulogised. I want the speech to concede something true to him — that he was right about idleness being expensive, that he paid for other people's free hours out of his own — without letting him have the argument. And end on something other than thanks.

Strict rules. Name no one: no philosopher, no economist, no reformer, no movement, no book, no country's legislation, no statistic, no study, no quotation of any kind. I cannot check any of it on the night, and name-dropping reads as a lecture delivered by someone who wants credit for having read. Put the ideas in with the labels peeled off. Do not use work–life balance, wellness or recharge, and do not let leisure slide into meaning entertainment, sport, hobbies or holiday. British English throughout, spelling and idiom alike. No anaphora unless it has earned itself, no adjectival weather, no tricolons for their own sake, no rhetorical questions in the final two paragraphs.

After the speech, give me a separate note of no more than a hundred and fifty words: where it is most exposed on the night — which lines are likeliest to land as an insult to someone who has just been made redundant, or as a slap at the people who funded the roof — and what you would cut or replace if I telephoned you afterwards to say it had gone badly.
