I convene an interdisciplinary reading group on risk and uncertainty at my university — about a dozen people: four from the law school who have met the precautionary principle mainly in its treaty formulations, three engineers who think in fault trees, redundancy and single points of failure, two philosophers of science, and a couple of people from the local authority who deal with actual regulatory decisions. Our next session is on the precautionary principle, and I have made the slightly provocative decision to open with Jurassic Park rather than with the literature, on the grounds that everyone in the room has seen the film and almost nobody has thought hard about what it is arguing. I want to circulate a critical reading of about 1,800 words in advance of the session, something the group can push against rather than nod at. What I do not want is the received version, in which the film is a cautionary tale about scientific hubris and Malcolm's lectures supply the moral. A colleague in the law school holds that view firmly and I could not dislodge her over lunch last week; I think it is far too crude, and I would like the piece you write to test it, and to tell me plainly if I am the one who is wrong.

Here is the sketch I have been developing, which you are free to attack. Set out the principle in its distinguishable versions first: the weak, treaty-style version the lawyers will have in mind, on which threats of serious or irreversible damage mean that the absence of full scientific certainty cannot be used to postpone cost-effective preventive measures; the strong version, on which the burden of proof shifts to the proponent of an activity, who must establish that it is safe before proceeding; and a maximin version, on which, when the worst outcome is both catastrophic and irreversible, probabilities drop out of the calculation altogether. Then ask which of these the film can actually bear. My suspicion is that it can support the maximin version and only awkwardly the others, because the irreversibility premise is doing all the work: one cannot un-create a breeding population, and the closing sequence pointedly refuses the reassurance of a threat neutralised, ending with the survivors flying away from an island still full of dinosaurs and the banner in the visitor centre unfurling as the tyrannosaur roars. But the film is simultaneously a case study in precautionary engineering, and this is where I think the interesting trouble starts. The electrified fences, the all-female population, the engineered metabolic dependency on a supplement the park must supply, the motion-sensor census: every one of these is a control measure, so the film is not about people who neglected precautions, it is about precautions. The one control that fails at the level of the animals themselves fails because of a patch introduced to resolve an earlier uncertainty — the gaps in the recovered genome were filled with amphibian DNA, and some amphibians change sex. That is precisely the structure the principle's critics, Cass Sunstein most famously, say makes precaution self-cancelling rather than the structure its advocates claim for it. Add the census, which never reports more animals than the system has been told to expect, and the failure is not in the biology but in the specification of the instrument. And note what the film never weighs: there is no serious account of what is lost by not building the park, and no acknowledgement that ordinary extinction is already doing to real species what the escaped animals do to the staff.

So there are four questions I want the reading to answer, and I would like them answered as criticism rather than as plot summary with philosophical labels attached. First, does the chaos-theory strand support a precautionary moral or undercut it? If complex systems cannot be controlled and the failure will find a path that no ex ante analysis could have anticipated, what is left of a decision rule that is supposed to operate before the fact; and is the film closer to fatalism about technological mastery than to precaution, borrowing its emotional force from the vivid, memorable, statistically atypical catastrophe that its critics say corrupts risk judgement in the first place? Second, the engineers will want to know whether this is a precautionary argument or a competence argument: the park has no defence in depth, one grid powering every fence, and one disgruntled programmer with a back door, so a good deal of the disaster looks like what happens when safety engineering is underfunded rather than proof that the project should never have been attempted — and if better engineering would have sufficed, the precautionary moral collapses into a moral about hiring and redundancy. Does the film know which argument it is making? Third, is Malcolm's famous reproach at the lunch table — that the park's creators were so taken up with whether they could do the thing that they never paused to ask whether they should — a precautionary argument at all? It looks to me like a different kind of objection entirely, about permission and standing rather than about probabilities and worst cases, and I would like the piece to say so if that is right. Fourth, the novel and the film differ in a way that bears directly on culpability, and since half the room has read only the film, please work from these details rather than reaching for ones you half-remember: in Crichton's novel the book opens with the small, ignoble scavenging dinosaurs killing a child on the mainland, the ones nobody at the park considers worth fearing; Hammond is a cold and self-interested promoter rather than a lovable enthusiast; after the crisis he rationalises the whole disaster as a fluke attributable to his staff's character flaws and resolves to rebuild, which is exactly the wrong update on a realised catastrophe; and he is then killed by that same pack of small dinosaurs while lying helpless with a broken ankle. The film lets him survive, makes him sympathetic, and redistributes the blame to a bribed programmer and a tropical storm. What does that do to the assignment of responsibility — does the novel punish a chooser while the film dissolves choice into complexity?

For the deliverable: name which version of the principle each strand of the film answers to; identify the three or four scenes I should point the group to and say what each is doing argumentatively; concede where the reading I have sketched will not hold, and say where the film simply resists being read as a work about precaution at all; and end with what the work contributes to the debate that the journal articles do not — my hunch is that it is something about the phenomenology of a risk that can only be recognised as a risk after the fact, and about the difference between having been warned and having been persuaded. Please do not quote the film or the novel, do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, and where you are unsure of a detail in the novel, work with what I have told you instead of filling the gap in.
