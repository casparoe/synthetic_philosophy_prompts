I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, and I've spent the last few months going back and forth with a friend who is a committed Christian about the argument from divine hiddenness — roughly, the claim that a perfectly loving God would not leave anyone who is open to him in the dark about whether he exists, and that since plenty of people are in exactly that state, such a God probably doesn't exist. I came across the technical phrase "non-resistant non-belief" and it seemed to me to be doing enormous work in a very small package, so I did the thing I do with any confusing specification: I wrote out concrete cases and recorded my gut verdict on each one, hoping a pattern would fall out. My friend's position is that all my cases are variations on a single underlying condition and that they only look like they pull in different directions because I haven't stated that condition properly. I'd like you to test whether he's right.

Here are my cases and verdicts, in the order I wrote them down. (1) A woman raised in a devout household who prayed sincerely for years for some sense that God was there, got nothing, and eventually stopped praying; she isn't avoiding the question, she's disappointed. Verdict: counts against a perfectly loving God, and counts more strongly than anything else on my list. (2) A man who grew up with essentially no religious exposure, vaguely curious, never pressed the question, and who would probably say yes if God made himself unmistakable. Verdict: counts too — but I feel it counts *less*, and I cannot say why, since the missing relationship looks identical to case 1. (3) A philosopher who has thought about it hard for years, judges the evidence genuinely balanced-to-against, doesn't believe, and would change his mind if better evidence appeared. Verdict: counts, fully; his being rational about it doesn't help God at all. (4) Someone who says he'd believe if he were shown enough, but doesn't want to owe anything to anyone, prefers to live as though answerable only to himself, and changes the subject whenever it comes up. Verdict: does *not* count — his non-belief is his own doing. (5) A woman with a controlling, abusive religious father who cannot think about God at all without panicking, avoids the whole subject, and suspects she would believe if she could face it. Verdict: does not count, because the cause here is human cruelty rather than divine silence. I can already see that 4 and 5 look identical from the outside — neither engages, both would believe if they engaged — and that I'm treating them oppositely; likewise 5 and 1 are both products of religious families and I'm treating them oppositely. (6) A long-standing believer going through a stretch of months in which God feels utterly absent, who keeps praying anyway. Verdict: does not count against God's existence, because she already believes — though I'm uneasy, since if the complaint is really about the relationship rather than the belief, this looks like precisely the thing the argument is about. (7) Someone who died in 1300 in a region where nobody had the concept of a personal creator who wants a relationship with individuals. Verdict: doesn't count, weakly held — I feel both that God can't be faulted for concepts people lacked and that a loving God would have made the concepts available. (8) Someone who refuses to say "I believe in God" but addresses the universe in the second person and sometimes feels answered. Verdict: genuinely uncertain; my instinct is that this person is closer to what God would want than the man in case 2, which suggests to me that the target state isn't binary belief at all. (9) If God showed up unmistakably — everyone convinced, no room for doubt — I think that would be *worse* for any real relationship; I'd want the room to doubt and to choose. Verdict: I hold this firmly, and I can see it colliding head-on with 1 and 3, because if overwhelming clarity would spoil the relationship, then the silence I'm counting against God might be exactly what love requires.

So: is there a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, that delivers these verdicts? I'd like at most two candidates, each stated as an explicit rule with its terms pinned down — in particular, what exactly counts as resisting, whose fault has to be located for a case to be excused, whether the required state is propositional belief or something relational, and whether the principle is about what God is permitted to allow or about what God would positively do. Then run each candidate over my nine cases in order and mark where it disagrees with me. Tell me straight which of my verdicts I have to abandon, and separate the ones that are cheap to give up from the one whose loss would hurt most; if the cheapest way to unify everything is to drop case 9, say so and tell me what the argument looks like from there. I'd also like to know whether the principle I end up with makes hiddenness evidence against theism in general or only against a particular picture of God — one who wants a personal relationship with each person in this life — because I suspect that distinction is doing more work than I'd realised. Finally, apply whatever principle survives to two cases I haven't listed: an atheist who finds herself addressing God while her child is in surgery, despite thinking no one is listening; and someone who believes firmly in God but has been taught that God is indifferent to individuals, so never seeks anything like a relationship. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question back to me; write in British English; use technical vocabulary freely but define anything a layperson plausibly wouldn't know; and don't give me a reading list or citations — I'll chase down names and papers myself if I want to.
