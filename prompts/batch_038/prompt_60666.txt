I spent thirty-five years as an analytical chemist and have come to philosophy only lately; in three weeks I give my first seminar paper, at a university philosophy of science group, and I should prefer to be prepared rather than surprised.

The thesis. Chemical elements are not a natural kind in any sense a scientific realist needs, and the authority of the periodic table comes from somewhere else. My line of argument: elementhood ceased to be discovered and became fixed by stipulation once isotopes forced a choice between chemical indivisibility and atomic weight, and Moseley's measurements of 1913–14 settled it by making proton number the criterion — a decision about what the term picks out, not a report of a joint in nature. But I deny that this renders the kind conventional or arbitrary, and here is where I want the most pressure. Kinds earn their standing by *portability*: a candidate kind is real to the degree that it can be individuated independently by procedures designed for different purposes, in different laboratories, without calibration to one another. Elements are maximally portable (spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, crystallography, decay chains); species are far less so, which is why the several species concepts never converge; the planet concept lost portability outright and was consequently settled by a vote of astronomers in 2006. Naturalness is thus a property of the interfaces among measurement practices rather than of the world's own articulation, and the standard tests — mind-independence, projectibility, homeostatic clustering, causal essence — either fail or reduce to portability.

The audience. Some twenty people: mostly scientific realists, several of them metaphysicians by training who arrived at these questions through Kripke and Putnam on natural kind terms; one sociologist of scientific knowledge whose agreement I suspect is a trap; and two or three practising chemists who regard the whole inquiry as idle. None will have read the paper, so they will work from the talk alone.

I want a preparation sheet, not a defence of my view. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ranked by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, and phrased in the register of the group that would raise it — the metaphysician will not put it as the chemist does. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, plus a frank note on where that reply is soft. Separately: what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, which single reply must land for the argument to survive the hour, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
