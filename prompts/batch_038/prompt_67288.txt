I am preparing my position paper for a structured graduate seminar debate in the history and philosophy of science. The motion assigned to me is this: that Malthus's principle of population was not refuted by the course of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries but was merely early — that it identified a genuine tendency whose operation was, for a time, masked by countervailing processes. I am not instinctively on this side of the question, so what I need from you is the strongest honest version of the defense rather than a caricature I can comfortably demolish.

I want the defense to satisfy a constraint of my own devising, which you should treat as a stipulation of the exercise rather than as something you will find argued for in the standard treatments. On my account, a theory earns the description "right but early," as opposed to merely not-yet-refuted, only when three conditions are jointly met. The mechanism it posits must have been actually at work during the very period in which its predictions appeared to fail. The failure must be traceable to a countervailing process that can be specified independently, one the theorist could in principle have absorbed as an auxiliary hypothesis without touching the core of the theory. And, most importantly, that countervailing process must itself be time-limited or self-undermining, so that the prediction is postponed rather than indefinitely deferred. A theory that can always claim its obstacle has not yet been removed is not early; it has simply been made unfalsifiable.

Your task is to argue that Malthus's principle meets all three. The third condition is where the difficulty lies, and I want you to spend the most effort there, saying something substantive about whether the offsetting forces — the sustained decline in fertility that accompanied rising real incomes, urbanization, and the changing economics of childrearing, together with the enormous gains in agricultural output per acre — are the sort of thing that can be relied upon to continue without limit. Be careful not to let the defense of Malthus's own argument shade into a defense of the neo-Malthusian revival of the late twentieth century about resource depletion; those are different claims with different histories, and conflating them would hand my opponents an easy point.

If, on reflection, you conclude that the third condition cannot honestly be met and that the motion therefore fails, say so — but only after you have set out what the best version of the defense looks like and identified precisely where and why it breaks.

Two further requirements. First, as you go, distinguish clearly between the premises that are settled among people who work on Malthus and on the philosophy of long-run prediction — the textual facts about what the Essay does and does not claim, the standard description of the demographic transition — and the premises that are genuinely disputed. Do not allow a contested reading of Malthus, or a contested view about whether tendency claims can be confirmed at all, to be presented as an agreed starting point. I am aware, for example, that Malthus rewrote the Essay substantially after the anonymous first edition of 1798, introducing moral restraint alongside vice and misery as a check on population and softening some of the harshest implications, and that scholars disagree about how far that revision altered the content of the theory; you should take a position on this, but flag that you are taking one.

Second, I want the result as continuous prose of the kind one would write in a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. The three conditions should be woven through the argument as it develops, not displayed as a rubric with the essay then filled in underneath it. Something in the region of twelve hundred words would suit my purposes.
