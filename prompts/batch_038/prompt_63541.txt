I'm doing an evening course in political philosophy through my local uni's continuing education department, and the tutor has asked each of us to hand in a one-page "profile" of our own views before the seminar on care and family policy, so the rest of the group can attack something real rather than a straw man. It isn't purely academic for me either — I sit on the steering group for our borough's family hubs, so half of these claims I've already said out loud in meetings and had to defend badly. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The gender pay gap that people make a fuss about is mostly a child penalty rather than employer discrimination, and where a couple freely decides that one of them will scale back at work I don't see an injustice there. Nobody had their arm twisted.
2. The state should be neutral between family forms — it's not in the business of steering people towards the dual-earner household any more than towards the single-earner one.
3. Free, universal, genuinely good quality childcare from about age one, funded out of general taxation. I think that's one of the best uses of public money available to us.
4. A non-transferable, use-it-or-lose-it paid leave quota for each parent, and I'm not troubled by the fact that this would change the behaviour of couples who'd rather Mum stayed home.
5. A preference isn't unfree just because social pressure helped shape it. The right test is whether the person would endorse it on reflection, with decent information.
6. Adult children don't owe their parents care or anything else. Benefits you never asked for can't put you in debt.
7. Care work is worth doing for its own sake, not only as an input into the next generation of taxpayers.
8. A big part of why mothers' employment matters to me is exit power — being able to walk away from a relationship you'd otherwise be stuck in.

I'm fairly confident these are all things I actually believe, and I suspect one or two of them can't survive in the same head, but I honestly can't tell which. So: acting as a political philosopher who works on the family, care and distributive justice, audit the set for me. Before you start matching claims up, work out what each one actually commits me to — the implicit premises, the things I'd have to say to defend it, the policies it rules in and rules out — and do that working internally rather than showing it to me.

What I want back is your verdict in the very first sentence — which claim is the load-bearing problem — and then the audit, with no preamble, no summary of what I've just written, and no time spent on the general empirical question of what drives the pay gap; take my beliefs as given and test their fit. Rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere. Please also separate out the pairs that only look like they clash and say why they dissolve: I expect some of the pairs that look worst are actually fine and some that look innocuous arent, and that's precisely the thing I can't see from the inside. End with the single revision that would clean up the most damage.
