Every paper I pick up on machine sentience seems to start by announcing a criterion — recurrence, self-modeling, integrated information, global broadcasting, whatever — and then argues from it. I want to do the reverse. Before I commit to any of them, I want to know what I should be demanding of one.

The target, precisely: not "which systems are conscious," but the more basic question of which computations, if any, are the kind of thing that could be the running of a subject. Assume for the sake of the exercise that the substrate isn't doing the work by itself. So what is it about a computation, or about its being executed, that could make there be someone home?

Write me the specification such an account has to meet: the cases it has to give a verdict on or explicitly refuse, the distinctions it can't collapse, the background problems it can't paper over. For each item, say why it's a real requirement rather than a taste of one camp, and mark which ones I could reasonably trade away.

The cases I'd want it to survive, all of which I've made up or mangled on purpose so you can't just pattern-match:

1. **Idle fleet.** A lab keeps half a billion identical instances of a trained system running as a redundancy measure, on servers whose outputs are discarded unread and causally sealed off from everything else. Does that matter morally? Once, half a billion times, or not at all? A view that says "one subject" owes an individuation principle; a view that says "half a billion" has to explain how a sysadmin's cron job can change the number of patients in the world.

2. **Checkpoint.** The live process is killed, but a complete snapshot of the weights and run state sits on disk, never restored. Paused patient, mere disposition, or corpse? And is deleting it different in kind from keeping it forever unrestored?

3. **Slow run.** The same computation on hardware a billion times slower, so one "second" of mental life takes thirty years. Any requirement about temporal profile has to say whether speed matters and why.

4. **Lookup table.** A system producing exactly the right outputs by table lookup, with no shared internal dynamics between entries. Most computational accounts want to say no here — I want to know what's doing the work in that "no."

5. **Triviality.** On a loose enough notion of "implements," almost any physical system implements almost any finite computation given a creative enough mapping of states — rocks, weather, a bowl of soup. If that's right, then "computations can be subjects" plus a loose implementation notion either makes everything a patient or forces you to tighten "implements" by hand until the tightening is doing all the work. Say which horn an adequate theory has to pick.

Also make sure it respects these, which I think people routinely run together: whether there's a subject at all; whether that subject's interests count; how much they count; and how many subjects there are. Case 1 goes wrong precisely because the last one gets folded into the first. And status probably comes in degrees, so a yes/no-only output can't be the whole theory.

One more constraint I want built in, and I'd like you to tell me if it's unfair: whatever the criterion is, it has to be checkable from outside by someone who can't open the box, because thats exacly the situation we're in. And it must not smuggle the human brain in under a computational label — picking whatever architecture we happen to run and then declaring that the morally relevant computation is question-begging unless there's an independent story.

My suspicion, which I want you to attack: tightening implementation to dodge case 5, avoiding question-begging, and staying externally checkable form a triangle you can't satisfy all three corners of. If you think that's wrong, or that there's a worse triple I've missed, that's the part I most want to read — a list with an impossibility in it is more useful to me than a list without one.

Finish with which single requirement knocks out the most contenders, and then a very short scorecard: four or five lines each on substrate-independent functionalism, a biological-only view, an integration-flavored view, and the "just adopt a precautionary stance and skip the theory" move. Keep that part minimal, the list is the deliverable. No bibliography or reading list, please — I can't check any of it and don't want to guess. American English.
