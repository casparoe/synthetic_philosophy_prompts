I write about science and ideas for a couple of magazines, and I'm also, through a family thing I never asked for, one of five trustees of a tiny foundation whose only activity is a prize. My aunt funded it in 1998 and the founding paper says the money goes to "the first machine that thinks," to be awarded whenever the trustees agree the bar's been cleared. It never says what the bar is. Twenty-some years, zero applications. In March we got one.

A lab sent us a dossier on a chat system and asked for the $40,000 plus permission to state in their press release that our foundation "certified that their machine thinks." That second ask is the real fight. Roughly what they submitted:

- 400 rounds of a 45-minute text conversation. Each judge chatted with two partners and was told one of them was a program. They picked the machine as the program 46% of the time — better than a coin flip, and better than the human in the other seat, who got picked 54%.
- The human foils were the lab's own staff, instructed to answer in short bursts, type fast, and make mistakes "like a normal user." Two trustees think that handicapped the humans, so the 46% is really a finding about the control condition rather than about the machine.
- A second condition where judges were told nothing and just rated how much the partner seemed to understand them: machine 4.1 out of 5, humans 4.3.
- Adversarial transcripts where judges asked the machine point-blank whether anything was going on for it while it talked. In one batch it said it didn't know and gave reasons; in a later batch it said the opposite. The lab attributes the difference to a sampling setting. They also argue the first answer — honest uncertainty — is their strongest evidence, which makes two of us furious, because it means they want us to score a machine on self-reports about the exact thing in dispute.

The deed has one more clause I keep coming back to: the prize may go out only "on evidence that would convince a reasonable person who was not present." None of us were in those conversations. We have transcripts and a spreadsheet.

We're suppose to vote in three weeks. One trustee, a retired engineer, argues the deed is behavioral on its face and that anyone demanding more than behavior is smuggling dualism back in through the wording — he thinks declining makes us the least rigorous people in the room. Another, a novelist, says paying converts a family eccentricity into something the public will hear as a claim about consciousness, and that we'd be doing the lab's marketing on letterhead. The lab has already told us that if we decline they'll publish the dossier themselves and describe us as having buried it.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. I need an actual motion — pay, decline, or something narrower like pay with the citation rewritten, or decline and petition to amend the deed — plus reasoning that could get five stubborn people to a vote. Tell me which features of that design should move us and which are noise, and take the engineer's question-begging objection seriously enough that he'd recognize it in the room rather than as a strawman. And tell me what we say to the lab about their press-release sentence, since that's the one that'll end up quoted in my colleagues' stories. American English, please. No reading list and no tour of the famous thought experiments — if you bring one up, it better be doing work.
