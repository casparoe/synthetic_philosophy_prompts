I am the instructor for an upper-level course on the foundations of rational choice (cross-listed philosophy/economics), and I am grading the take-home portion of the final assessment. Students had 48 hours and were told explicitly that the questions call for analysis rather than for references; no sources were required and none should be expected. Below are the question and one student's answer. I would like a score and comments specific enough that I can defend them to the student.

**Question 3 (20 points).** Consider the following four-node centipede game. Players 1 and 2 move alternately: player 1 moves at nodes 1 and 3, player 2 at nodes 2 and 4. At each node the player to move chooses T (take) or P (pass). The game ends as soon as a player chooses T, and it also ends if player 2 chooses P at node 4. Terminal payoffs, written (player 1, player 2), are:

- T at node 1: (2, 1)
- T at node 2: (1, 4)
- T at node 3: (4, 2)
- T at node 4: (2, 8)
- P at node 4: (6, 5)

(a) [10 points] Find the backward induction outcome and the subgame-perfect equilibrium of this game. Explain how that solution relates to the set of Nash equilibria of the game.

(b) [10 points] The backward induction outcome is worse for both players than at least one other terminal payoff in the game. Explain why this is regarded as paradoxical rather than merely unappealing, and evaluate the argument that the paradox arises because common knowledge of rationality cannot hold at nodes that rational play would never reach. What, if anything, follows for the prediction at node 1?

**Student's answer.**

(a) The game is finite and has perfect information, so by Zermelo's theorem it has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, and backward induction locates it. Start at node 4. If play ever reaches node 4, player 2 compares taking, which gives her 8, with passing, which ends the game at (6, 5) and gives her 5; she takes, so the continuation from node 3 onward is (2, 8). At node 3, player 1 compares taking, which gives him 4, with passing, which on the analysis just given leaves him with 2; he takes, so the continuation from node 2 onward is (4, 2). At node 2, player 2 compares 4 from taking with 2 from passing; she takes, so the continuation from node 1 onward is (1, 4). At node 1, player 1 compares 2 from taking with 1 from passing, and takes. The backward induction outcome is therefore (2, 1), and the subgame-perfect equilibrium is the profile in which each player takes at every node at which she or he might move.

That profile is also the unique Nash equilibrium of the game. There are, to be sure, other strategy profiles that look different on paper — for instance, one in which player 1 takes at node 1 but would pass at node 3, while player 2 takes at node 2 and at node 4. But a Nash equilibrium is fixed by the outcome it produces, and what a profile prescribes at nodes that are never reached makes no difference to anyone's payoff; so such profiles are not further equilibria, they are the same equilibrium written out in a needlessly complicated way. The game-theoretic prediction for actual play is accordingly unambiguous: player 1 takes at once and the payoffs are (2, 1).

(b) The outcome (2, 1) is strictly Pareto-dominated by (6, 5), which the players could have reached by passing at every node, and weakly dominated by (2, 8). The analysis therefore says that two players who are rational, who know the game, and who know each other to be rational must end up with less than they could have had without anyone's being irrational: the reasoning looks self-defeating rather than merely unflattering.

The usual diagnosis is epistemic. Backward induction requires common knowledge of rationality, and at node 2 that hypothesis is already inconsistent with what player 2 has learned. Reaching node 2 means that player 1 passed at node 1, which a rational player 1 would not have done; so at node 2 player 2 knows both that player 1 is rational and that he has not acted rationally. Her beliefs are then unconstrained, because from a contradiction anything follows, and nothing whatever can be predicted about what happens at node 2 or later. But the very first step of the induction depends on what happens at node 2 — player 1 takes at node 1 only because he expects 1 rather than 2 if he passes — so if node 2 is unpredictable, node 1 is unpredictable too. The theory is silent about this game from the first move onward, and that is what makes the situation a paradox rather than a curiosity: rationality fails to determine a unique line of play even at the opening node.

This also fits the laboratory evidence, in which players rarely take at the first node and games usually break down somewhere in the middle rather than running to the end. Since the analysis cannot say what should happen, there is nothing for that evidence to contradict; the experiments show only that people are willing to pay a little to keep the pot growing.
