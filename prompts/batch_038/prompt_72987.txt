My ethics reading group has been stuck on the dust specks case for three weeks and I've come out the other end with an argument I really don't want. The case, in case it isn't familiar: you've got two buttons. One lets a single innocent person be tortured for fifty years. The other gives each of a trillion people a speck of dust in the eye for a second — barely noticied, forgotten a minute later, no lasting effects. Half my group says press the specks button, because a trillion of them adds up to more than the torture; the other half says press the torture button, because no number of specks could ever add up to fifty years of torture, and they lean on the dial version of the case (a thousand torturers each turning a dial up one notch, every notch imperceptible, the total agony) to show that imperceptible contributions really can pile up into something awful. I'm nominally with the second half. But when I tried to write down why, what came out seems to prove that torture isn't worse than a speck, and I can't find the step that's wrong.

Here it is.

(1) Wrongness tracks badness by amount: an act is wrong in so far as it makes things worse, and how much worse is a quantity you can add up across people and compare.
(2) One speck costs its victim a tiny but non-zero amount, call it ε; fifty years of torture costs its victim some vast finite amount, H.
(3) So for a big enough number of specks the speck option costs more in total than the torture option, and you should torture. That's the first half of my group, and on (1) they're right.
(4) My friends deny (3). They say no number of specks is ever worse than the torture, because past some point the sums stop tracking the comparison — a speck is the wrong kind of thing to trade against torture.
(5) Grant them (4). But then the same must hold of the comparison between the torture and one single speck, since the only thing that ever made the torture come out worse was its size, and (4) says size stops mattering.
(6) And comparability can't come back once it's gone. Between any two outcomes you can build a chain of cases each exactly one speck apart from the last, and if no single step in that chain makes a difference, then no step is the one that restores the comparison.
(7) So nothing is worse than anything here — and, since every harm is either made out of smaller harms or isn't a harm at all, the same goes anywhere.
(8) Hence by (1) nothing is wrong: it isn't worse, and so isn't wrong, to torture someone for fifty years rather than let them get one speck — or rather than let nothing happen at all.
(9) Either my friends are simply wrong and the aggregationists win outright, or (1) is false and there are no amounts of badness to add up in the first place, in which case I've destroyed the only reason I had for siding with them.

(9) is the bit I can't live with, since both horns are things I'd have to argue against for the rest of my life. So tell me where the chain actually breaks. I've read (5) and (6) more times than I'd like to admit and they still look like the two steps I'd have put money on, which makes me suspect I've mis-stated my friends' view in (4) — 'the sums stop tracking the comparison' may not be anything any of them actually thinks, and what they do think might block (6) rather than feed it. Name the step you'd reject, explain why it looked innocent when I wrote it down, and then price the alternatives properly: what it costs to give up (1), what it costs to give up (5), what it costs to say the anti-aggregationist of (4) was a straw man and what the real view would be instead. If you think (1) and (2) are worth keeping at any price, say so, but I want to know what I'm paying for them. One last thing — be clear about which parts of your diagnosis you could state without anyone who works on this disagreeing, and which are genuinely contested between people who know the field far better than me; don't dress up your preferred exit as though it were the settled view. British English please.
