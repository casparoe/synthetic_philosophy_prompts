I am in the first year of a master's program in archaeology and am concurrently auditing a philosophy of the social sciences course. Last week our method seminar worked through a deliberately fictional assemblage that our instructor had composed for the exercise: beneath the plaster floor of a single-room dwelling, a tight concentration of burnt cattle shoulder blades, and beside it a human cranium whose mandible had been removed and whose base had been packed with clay. Half the section wanted to call this a foundation deposit of ritual significance; the other half maintained that the finds licensed nothing beyond secondary disposal of cranial remains alongside hearth sweepings. Neither side produced a decisive argument, and afterward one of the doctoral students in the section wrote the impasse up as a general argument. I have not been able to shake it. Each premise looked acceptable to me when he read them out; the conclusion did not, and I cannot locate the point at which I stopped agreeing.

1. Everything we claim about a past people's beliefs, norms, and institutions rests ultimately on material remains.
2. For any pattern of material remains, indefinitely many different systems of belief and practice would have produced it.
3. To narrow that range we compare the pattern with societies whose beliefs we know from testimony independent of their artifacts — ethnographic, historical, or textual.
4. But a people leaves us nothing but artifacts precisely when it is separated from every people we know well by time, distance, or way of life; otherwise there would be a record. So comparison is available in strength exactly where it is redundant and in weakness exactly where it is load-bearing.
5. Nor can the deficit be repaired by accumulating finds. Each additional artifact is itself underdetermined in the sense of (2), and a body of evidence every member of which is compatible with rival reconstructions is itself compatible with those rival reconstructions.
6. Therefore no ascription of belief or institution to such a people is better supported than a rival ascription: ritual foundation deposit and refuse disposal stand on equal evidential footing.
7. Retreating to mere function buys nothing. To identify the deposit as a hearth, a vessel as a granary, or a hollowed stone as a quern is already to say what people did with these things, and so what they took themselves to be doing; the functional identification of an artifact is an intentional identification.
8. And since (2) states nothing distinctively archaeological — behavior of any kind underdetermines belief — the reasoning generalizes, and the interpretation of persons as such is unfounded.

I should say where my sympathies lie, though I do not think they decide the question. I am most attached to (2) and (7); those are the two I would keep if I could keep only two, and they are the two my seminar treats as settled. I am least willing to accept (8), which strikes me as plainly false, yet I cannot see which premise I would have to sacrifice to make it false, or what I would lose by sacrificing it.

So: which step is the weak one — or, if the argument is in fact sound, which premise is cheapest to abandon? Please explain why the step you identify looked acceptable in the first place, and what makes it fail once examined. Then price the alternatives: for each repair you think worth considering, say what it commits me to and what the archaeology of belief looks like under it, including whether the fictional assemblage above can be read as evidence of anything at all. Please write in American English.
