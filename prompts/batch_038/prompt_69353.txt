I am setting the end-of-year examination for a final-year undergraduate metaphysics option. The cohort has completed a four-week unit on essence, necessity, and transworld identity, and they have read the standard material on the necessity of origin: the thesis, associated with Kripke's discussion of where a person comes from, that being the product of the particular egg and sperm one actually developed from is essential to that person. I need one essay question on that thesis which cannot be answered by reciting it, or by walking the reader through the familiar illustrations of a particular table and a particular monarch. It should force students to work out what the thesis really commits one to, and what would have to be surrendered in order to save it.

To that end, I want the stem to present two cases, both modelled on procedures that are actually performed in laboratories and in animal breeding, written out in ordinary language and in enough detail that nothing outside the stem is required:

Case one. A single early embryo, formed from one egg and one sperm, is divided into two viable halves, each of which is gestated separately, so that two people result from one pair of gametes. Include the complication that one half is cryopreserved for several years before transfer, so the two share an origin but are born years apart, and neither can claim to be the original by virtue of having developed first.

Case two. Cells from two early embryos, each formed from a different egg and a different sperm, are combined into a single embryo which develops into one person whose body is a mixture of the two cell lines. Include the complication that one of the two lines supplies all of that person's germ cells, so her own eggs descend from only one of the two pairs of gametes while the rest of her does not.

Please write three things.

First, the examination question itself, in two parts. Part (a), worth 10 marks, should require candidates to state the thesis precisely enough to be capable of being false, and to say, for each case separately, exactly which commitment it puts under pressure. Part (b), worth 15 marks, should require them to decide whether a revised thesis can survive both cases and still perform whatever philosophical work the original was recruited for, and to commit to a verdict rather than offer a survey of options. Candidates will have 75 minutes for the question in total.

Second, a model answer of about 900 words, of the kind a strong candidate could produce under those time conditions. It should engage both cases rather than only the more congenial one, and it should confront the prior question of how the origin is to be specified at all — as a pair of gametes, as a zygote, as a fertilisation event, or as a developmental process — since the two cases bite differently depending on the level chosen. It should say what survives if the thesis is weakened, and whether what survives can still rule out what essentialists wanted ruled out. I want a definite conclusion, not a balanced shrug.

Third, a marking scheme for the 25 marks. Open with two or three sentences naming the crux on which the question turns. Then list five or six must-hit moves, allocate marks to each so that they total 25, and attach one line per move identifying the standard way candidates get it wrong — for instance, treating the thesis as a single biconditional when the two cases separate its two directions, or assuming that a counterexample to a general principle disposes of it without asking what the principle was being asked to do. State explicitly what caps an answer at a C and at a B: a fluent reconstruction of the stock examples with no engagement with the two cases should not earn more than a stated number of marks. Close by saying how much credit is available to a candidate who raises the sorites-style objection that gametes admit of trivial variation, so the essentialist faces either a slippery slope or an arbitrarily precise origin, and how the marking should treat a candidate who argues that the germline detail in the second case is either decisive or a red herring.

The question should be self-contained: do not ask candidates for citations, and include no quotations from any text, no bibliography, and no reading list in your deliverable.
