So I'm TAing a discussion section on Bentham and sexual morality and I want a case built to order, not the stock stuff everyone recites.

Background as I have it: Bentham thought the revulsion of onlookers was no reason to punish private consensual sex between adults — that long manuscript he wrote in the 1780s about same-sex acts, which he then sat on and never published, basically rests on that. What I want my students to wrestle with is whether that commitment is principled or just an empirical bet about how expensive it happened to be to satisfy popular disgust in his England. So here's the ask:

Build me a thought experiment where Bentham's own machinery runs out of escapes — all four of the grounds he gives for punishment being unmeet (groundless, inefficacious, unprofitable, needless) come up empty — and his hedonic calculus therefore forces him to endorse punishing exactly the sort of act he wanted decriminalized.

Specs:
- Realistic. An actual kind of place: an isolated village, a remote work camp, a small tight congregation. No sci-fi, no experience machines, no surgery.
- The scenario proper is one paragraph. Keep the analysis seperate from it.
- The conduct is between consenting adults, in private, no force, no deception, no minors, no third party harmed, no promise broken. Strip out every source of mischief except the distress of people who know it's going on.
- That distress has to be real and big, but it cant rest on any false belief. No superstition, no "it'll bring plague on us," no fear of divine wrath, no misinformation a public education campaign could clear up. Think visceral taste reaction — genuine suffering that argument doesn't touch. The moment you let them hold a mistaken belief, the "needless" ground gets Bentham off the hook and the whole case collapses.
- Also close off "inefficacious": the act can't be hidden or relocated, and the community can't just move away or opt out of knowing.
- And the punishment you pick has to be mild and cheap enough that "unprofitable" doesn't rescue him either — on any sane arithmetic it costs less than the distress it prevents.

What the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating: I'm testing whether the anti-ascetic argument has philosophical force or just describes 1780s England. Don't stipulate the verdict. No "imagine the community's suffering outweighs everything, therefore punish" — the numbers should fall out of described features of the setting and I want to watch you run the check.

Then after the scenario: walk the case through all four grounds one at a time and show why each fails to exempt; say what Bentham is now committed to and how badly that sits with his own attack on the principle of sympathy and antipathy; name the single feature that's doing the work; tell me what happens to the case if I pull that feature out; and give me the strongest reply a Benthamite could make — what would they say breaks the case, or dissolves it, or amounts to refusing to run a calculus on invented numbers. Finish with a straight answer on whether this shows the decriminalization argument was principled or contingent, and what you'd have to bolt onto his framework to block the case (a lexical rule? a line between preferences about your own life and preferences about other people's private conduct? something else entirely?).

Don't bother with citations — I don't have the texts in front of me and this isn't for a paper. I mostly just want the design to actually work. American English please.
