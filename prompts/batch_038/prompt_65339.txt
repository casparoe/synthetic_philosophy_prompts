I am three-quarters through a novel whose middle section is set in the years after the South Sea collapse, and one of its principals is a clergyman-philosopher who holds, in broad outline, the Berkeleian account of money: that coin is no wealth but a counter, that its whole usefulness lies in stirring up industry and proportioning it to demand, and that gold is "a ticket or counter" no more and no less than a well-backed note. I have given him a set-piece speech in which he reasons from those commitments to a conclusion that my own sympathies reject and that, worse, leaves him indistinguishable from the projectors he spends the rest of the book attacking. I need to know exactly where the chain snaps, so that a second character can break it in a single sentence rather than in a paragraph of huffing. The speech runs as follows.

(1) Money has no value in itself; it is a token whose worth derives entirely from the industry it sets in motion.

(2) Whatever sets industry in motion may be multiplied without limit, since it is the motion that is valuable and not the thing moved.

(3) The quantity of tokens required to employ a given quantity of labour is not fixed: it depends on how swiftly each token passes from hand to hand and on how finely accounts are divided, and a sovereign may adjust both at will.

(4) Therefore the limits on a nation's wealth are the limits of its labour and its organisation, and a sovereign who controls the tokens can never want for money.

(5) It follows that the ruin of 1720 came not from too much paper, but from paper issued against nothing, by men who had no power to compel its receipt.

(6) The remedy is accordingly a public bank issuing notes secured on the land and manufactures of the nation, which no subject may refuse.

Premises (4) and (6) together seem to prove far more than he intends: they license the belief that a country may print itself rich, and they deprive him of any principled objection to the schemes he hates. Tell me which step you would reject, or which needs to be restated, and price the rest of the chain for that choice — in particular, whether he can hold on to (1), the part of him I most want to keep, without inheriting (4). I would also like to know where your own diagnosis is softest: what consideration, if I brought it to you, would move you to a different verdict about the failing step, and whether you read the chain as unsound or as sound but arriving somewhere my character has refused to look.

Answer me in continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. You may refer to his steps by number, but I want one unbroken argument rather than a scheme. Do not give me citations or a bibliography; I will supply whatever I need.
