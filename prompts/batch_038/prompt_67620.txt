I chair the selection committee for a residential leadership fellowship at a public university: forty places a year, roughly nine hundred applicants, a majority of them first-generation students. For the past decade our process has ended with a forty-five-minute structured interview scored against a rubric covering integrity, resilience, empathy, and initiative. Two submissions have landed on my desk this cycle, and the committee must choose between them on Thursday.

Our philosophy colleague argues the interview should be abolished outright. His case has three strands: that the situationist literature in personality psychology — the classic obedience studies, the mock-prison study, and experience-sampling work showing that the same person behaves very differently from one situation to the next — undercuts the assumption that there is a stable, cross-situationally consistent character for us to detect; that what our rubric in fact scores is verbal polish and interview training, which correlates strongly with family income in our own applicant data; and that the mock-prison study in particular has come under severe methodological and archival attack in recent years, which he concedes cuts both ways, since the critique of character partly leans on it.

The fellowship office proposes a replacement rather than a deletion: a paid half-day in which shortlisted candidates work through staged group tasks with embedded dilemmas while trained observers score them, on the theory that character is visible in behaviour under pressure rather than in self-description. They contend that personnel-selection research reports modest but non-zero validity for integrity testing, and that a structured observational exercise should do at least as well.

The three courses of action before us are: (i) retain the interview unchanged; (ii) remove character assessment from selection entirely and allocate places on academic record plus a written statement of purpose; (iii) adopt the half-day situational assessment.

Please draft the committee's decision memo. I want a recommendation with defended reasons, not a survey of the debate. Four things it must do:

First, take a position on the philosophical question underneath the dispute. Does the situationist critique entail that character cannot be assessed, or does it imply that character can only be assessed in a specific way — across many varied situations and over time, rather than in a single encounter? If the latter, does that rescue or condemn option (iii)? A half-day of staged tasks is itself one situation, and I need to know whether you think that is a fatal objection or a manageable one, and why.

Second, distinguish the selection function from the developmental function. Our charter commits us to cultivating these qualities during the fellowship, not merely detecting them beforehand. I suspect the argument that defeats assessing character at admission does not defeat cultivating it afterwards; tell me whether you agree, and what follows for the recommendation.

Third, name and defend the standard you are applying. Predictive validity is the obvious candidate, but this fellowship partly confers an opportunity rather than fills a role, so tell me whether validity is the right criterion, or fairness, or something else — and be explicit about how you are trading them off.

Fourth, set out the strongest case for the option you reject in terms its own defenders would recognise, and state what evidence or argument would move you.

Practical constraints: twelve readers, four with no background in psychology; about a page and a half; a clear recommendation stated in the opening paragraph. If you think we should gather data over the coming cycle before deciding permanently, specify precisely what we would measure, on whom, and which result would settle the question. No bibliography, footnotes, or named citations — argue from the concepts and from the case as I have described it.
