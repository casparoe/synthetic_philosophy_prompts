You are answering as a philosopher of social science with two specialties: explanation in the historical sciences, and the methodology and metaphysics of causal claims about culture. Please treat this as a serious piece of agenda-setting rather than a survey, and think carefully before answering; I would rather have a defensible shortlist than a comprehensive map.

Background on me: I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. I have been commissioned to write a two-part series, roughly ten thousand words in total, on the rehabilitation of cultural explanation in the social sciences, and alongside it I am convening a half-day, off-the-record roundtable with five people: two economic historians, a cultural psychologist, a sociologist of religion, and a philosopher of science. I have about six weeks of reporting time and one roundtable. I want your help deciding what the series should actually be about, and what those five people should be asked.

The material I am working with, in brief. First, the Weberian template: a religious ethic generates a distinctive motivational orientation among its adherents; that orientation expresses itself in economic conduct; the conduct crystallizes into an institutional form; and the historian's task is to say how much of the outcome is attributable to the religious factor rather than to material conditions. (Weber himself framed the question as comparative and partial, and explicitly disclaimed any attempt to substitute a spiritualist account of causation for a materialist one.) Second, a contemporary descendant of that template that has been drawing a great deal of attention: the claim that late antique and early medieval Church regulation of marriage and family — prohibitions on cousin marriage, restrictions on remarriage and adoption, and the erosion of the clan as a corporate property-holding and inheriting unit — dissolved intensive kinship in Western Europe, reshaping ordinary psychology toward individualism, impersonal trust, guilt rather than shame, and analytic rather than holistic cognition, which in turn made possible voluntary associations, impersonal markets, and law-governed institutions, and hence prosperity. Proponents point to comparative psychological tasks, survey measures, and behavioral residues (unpaid parking tickets issued to United Nations diplomats in New York are a favorite), and to gradients in kinship intensity across Europe that track historical ecclesiastical jurisdiction. Third, the objections I keep meeting in interviews: that the explanans is individuated by the explanandum, so the thesis is not independently testable; that reverse causation does the real work, since prosperous and urban areas adopted the new religion first, and since kinship systems may themselves be downstream of ecology, pastoralism, irrigation, or disease environments; that group-functional accounts explain why a norm persisted rather than what it caused; that "cultural causation" dissolves into institutional causation the moment a mechanism is specified; and that interpretive accounts answer a different question — the intelligibility of a unique historical configuration — than regression-based accounts, which estimate average effects across populations and may license no claim at all about the particular case everyone cares about.

What I want from you:

1. Begin with a brainstorming pass. List eight to twelve genuinely open conceptual questions in this area — not objections to be dispatched, but questions researchers could work on — before you commit to any ordering.

2. State your criteria explicitly and say how you weight them. I want to see something like: how many other questions turn on this one; whether it is tractable by argument and analysis rather than by data I cannot gather; whether settling it would change what my series is entitled to claim; and whether it is really open or only rhetorically open. Apply the criteria, do not merely list them.

3. Deliver a defended ranking of the four that should structure the series and the roundtable, with a sentence or two on each explaining why it outranks the runners-up. For each, say what a good answer would look like, what would count as progress, and which of my five seats is best placed to press it.

4. Name the single crux: the one question on which most of the others implicitly depend. Then say what you think the answer is likely to be, and how confident you are.

5. Tell me what to cut. Which questions from your brainstorming list, and which of the objections above, are tired, merely verbal, or would consume column inches without changing anyone's mind?

6. Rank the implications. My editors will want the series to end on what follows from all this. Candidates I keep hearing: that if WEIRD psychology is a historical accident, the universality of findings generalized from such samples is undercut; that if culture is a cause, it can be a policy lever; that if our convictions are culturally caused, this licenses — or undercuts — a relativism about them; that cultural and individual-level explanation are competitors rather than complements; and that the whole enterprise is unfalsifiable and should be abandoned. Sort these into tiers by how well they actually follow, with one sentence each on why it lands where it does. Be blunt about the ones that do not follow.

7. Finally, tell me what would move things: over the next six weeks of reporting, or the next few years of the field, what kind of result or argument would push a question up or down your list?
