Having spent forty years measuring the transport and thermal properties of solids, and having come to the philosophy of science only in retirement, I find that the literature will not give me a number, so I should like yours.

Proposition: the renormalisation-group account of universality — the fact that systems with quite unrelated microscopic constituents share critical exponents at a continuous phase transition — constitutes a genuine reduction of that macroscopic regularity to microscopic physics, rather than a demonstration of its autonomy. If you hold that this is truth-apt only once a standard of reduction has been fixed, say so plainly and give me the number conditional on whichever standard you judge most defensible.

My own credence is 0.6, and I feel the pull in both directions: the construction does begin from the microscopic Hamiltonian, yet almost everything about the constituents is washed out along the flow, and what survives is a handful of relevant parameters together with a symmetry.

Please decompose rather than survey. A rough probability, with one sentence of justification, for each of the following: that a Nagelian derivation with explicit bridge laws sets the right standard; that the fixed-point construction meets whatever standard you adopt, including its dependence on an infinite-system limit that no actual sample realises; that insensitivity to microscopic detail represents a loss of content rather than a gain in explanatory reach; and that talk of autonomy here does any work beyond recording that the microscopic details are explanatorily idle in this case. Then show me how those terms combine into your number, identify which of them you are least confident of, and say which single consideration — one of mine, or one from the literature — would move it furthest.
