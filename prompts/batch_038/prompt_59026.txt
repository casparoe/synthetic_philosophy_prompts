I am presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on cultural evolution and the normative study of social norms, and I would like a question-period preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view.

My thesis is a critique of the tightness–looseness framework. Stated compactly: the literature treats tightness as a single scalar property of a collectivity — a position on one dimension along which entire countries, and even regions within a country, can be ranked and compared. I argue that this scalar is an artefact of aggregation. It is what remains when enforcement intensity is averaged across behavioural domains that differ enormously in their structure, and the framework then re-describes that average as a feature of a society's normative culture. Undo the aggregation, and I claim, the headline generalisations weaken, disappear, or in some domains reverse, because whether strong informal enforcement is appropriate depends on properties of the domain: how costly a single violation is, how often the behaviour arises, and whether it can be detected and sanctioned by formal institutions or only by the disapproval of people who happen to witness it. I also distinguish endorsement strength, how sincerely a norm is subscribed to, from sanctioning reliability, how likely a violation is to actually cost the violator something, and I claim the scalar measure runs these together. On my view, then, the question "how tight should a society be?" is malformed; the well-formed question is how enforcement ought to be allocated across domains.

The room will be about thirty people: mostly cross-cultural and cultural psychologists who work with large-N survey instruments and who regard tightness–looseness as among the more robust constructs they have; two anthropologists broadly hostile to variable-centred comparison across societies; two or three political philosophers working on social ontology; and one programme officer from a governance funder who will want to know what to do with any of it. No formal modellers, and nobody works on rational choice accounts of norm emergence.

Give me the eight to ten objections this room will actually raise — not the ones a general political theory audience would raise against a claim about norms. Order them by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly, and for each tell me which subset of the audience it comes from and how they will phrase it, in their own vocabulary: the psychometricians will talk about construct validity, measurement invariance and effect sizes, the anthropologists about reification, the philosophers about the is/ought gap. Supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and state plainly where that reply is softest — the point at which I would be relying on a distinction I have not yet earned. Identify which two or three objections, if left standing, would sink the paper rather than merely embarrass it; tell me what I should concede in my opening remarks so that the concessions are not extracted from me later; and finish with the objection I am hoping nobody asks, together with an honest assessment of whether I have an answer to it.
