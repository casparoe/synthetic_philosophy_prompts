I convene a second-year philosophy of perception seminar: eleven students, one session of a hundred and ten minutes, a room with a single large table, a whiteboard, and no projector. I should like you to design a complete, runnable in-class activity together with its debrief, one that brings the students up against the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply to it in such a way that they manufacture the questionable intermediate step for themselves and then discover that it will not carry the weight they have loaded onto it. What I am after, specifically, is a sequence in which they are driven to posit something common to a veridical perception and a subjectively matching hallucination, because the rules of the game require them to produce and defend such a thing, and in which what they produce turns out either to be too thin to explain anything or to be something the subject who is supposedly in it could never use. The idea must surface in what they do rather than in anything I say beforehand, so the words disjunctivism, sense datum, argument from illusion, and highest common factor are not to be spoken by anyone until the debrief names them.

Please build the activity around a matching-and-description game. I have about twenty pounds and a day's notice for materials, and props are welcome, though you will have to tell me how you propose to handle the fact that a hallucination cannot be staged with a prop at all. I should like the case set to include at least a veridical perception of a lemon in good light, a wax replica indistinguishable from it at that distance and under that lighting, a total hallucination of a lemon produced by direct stimulation of the visual system, one further case of a non-veridical kind such as an afterimage or a dream, and a case in which the subject sees a real lemon in perfectly good conditions and is nonetheless convinced that it is the wax replica. Tell me what each case is doing in the design and where in the running order it enters.

The mechanic I have in mind is adversarial, and I should like you to refine or replace it as you see fit: pairs are asked to write down what two matched experiences have in common, a challenger earns points by naming a further case that the description wrongly admits or wrongly excludes, and points go to descriptions that survive challenge. My expectation is that the equilibrium of such a game is maximal thinness, and that the winning move is to describe almost nothing. If that is right, please arrange the scoring so the students discover it rather than being told it, and tell me what to do if a group refuses to thin out and simply stalls instead. After that phase I want a stage in which the surviving description has to be put to use from the inside, that is, in which a subject must apply it to work out which of the matched cases she is in, and in which the students run into the fact that any such test is itself the sort of thing that admits of a match. I also want a moment, deliberately placed, in which the class is made to feel the pressure of the obvious objection, namely that a common factor is needed to explain why the hallucinating subject reports, reaches, and behaves exactly as the perceiver does, and in which they must then say what the disjunctivist owes in reply.

Please give me the running order with approximate minutes, the groupings and any regroupings, the exact wording of the instructions and of every card the students receive, the scoring rule, and a debrief that maps each phase onto a component of the argument, including the merely negative characterisation of hallucination that disjunctivism appears to end up with and the question of whether that constitutes an explanation or a shrug. Note that eleven is an awkward number for pairing, and deal with it.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I already suspect that the students will guess there is a trick and hedge their descriptions from the outset, that the wax replica case will be collapsed into the hallucination case or the reverse, that someone will propose a brain state as the common factor, which is a different claim from a phenomenal one, and that one confident student will insist she could always tell which case she was in. Give me a handling for each of these and add the failures I have not anticipated. Finally, be plain about which feature of the dispute the activity cannot reproduce and which I shall have to supply myself; I suspect one is that my students never actually undergo an episode that is indiscriminable from the inside, so that the whole thing risks being reasoning about experience rather than experience, and another is that the collapse of the matching task may be blamed on the poverty of our vocabulary rather than on the metaphysics. Tell me whether those diagnoses are correct, and what else is true.

Do not include citations, a reading list, or quotations; where you wish to invoke somebody's formulation, paraphrase it in your own words. Please write the whole thing in continuous prose, in the manner of a letter, with no headings, no bulleted or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables or timetables; where a running order, a list of materials, or the text of a card would ordinarily be set out typographically, fold it into sentences instead while keeping the order of the session perfectly clear. Write in American English.
