I'm setting the final for a fourth-year formal epistemology module and I want one question on Jeffrey conditionalization that students can't get through just by writing down the formula and reciting the rigidity assumption. Here's the case I've cooked up:

Jo is judging whether a fabric swatch came from the 19th-century batch (call that H) on the basis of its colour. Priors: red 0.5, pink 0.3, white 0.2. Her conditional credences in H: 0.8 given red, 0.4 given pink, 0 given white. The showroom lights come back on and she revises to 0.75 on red. Describe that experience coarsely, as a shift over the partition {red, not-red}, and Jeffrey's rule — hold fixed P(H | each cell), redistribute — yields H = 0.66. Describe it finely, as a shift to red 0.75 / pink 0.2 / white 0.05, and the very same rule yields H = 0.68. Same woman, same burst of light, two answers.

Please turn this into a 20-mark exam question to be answered in about 45 minutes with no calculator: (a) get them to redo both calculations and show that the two updates disagree about more than just H; (b) ask what has actually gone wrong — is one of the descriptions simply inaccurate, or is the rule incomplete as a norm? — and what it would take to repair it, at what cost; (c) a short part on whether a bookie who knows Jo's priors and her updating habits could turn any of this into a diachronic Dutch book, and what exactly the bookie would have to be able to predict in advance.

Then give me a model answer of around 700 words and a marking scheme with marks allocated per part, naming the moves an A has to make and the errors that should cost marks — espcially the student who says "the fine partition carries more information so obviously you use that one" without ever saying why the coarse update isn't just wrong, or what has to be true of an experience for it to determine one privileged partition at all.
