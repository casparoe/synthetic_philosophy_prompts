My friend and I spent most of an afternoon on whether Mill's exemption for immature societies hollows out the harm principle, and I came away unable to say which of us was right. His reconstruction draws on the opening chapter of On Liberty and on the chapter of Considerations on Representative Government dealing with the government of dependencies by a free state:

1. Mill holds that power may rightfully be exercised over an adult member of a civilized community against his will only to prevent harm to others, and that the man's own good, physical or moral, is never a sufficient warrant.
2. Mill also holds that the principle does not apply to those not yet in the maturity of their faculties, children being the paradigm, and that by extension despotism is a legitimate form of rule over peoples who cannot yet be improved by free and equal discussion, provided its end is their improvement and its means actually effect that end.
3. Whether a given people can be improved by free discussion is a question of fact about its habits and institutions, and not one that its own say settles.
4. In practice the only party positioned to settle it is the power already governing them, since by hypothesis the governed have been excluded from the determination.
5. So any governing power may classify its subjects as immature and thereby convert coercion for their own good into legitimate coercion, subject only to the condition that the coercion succeed.
6. But a condition that the coercion succeed is nothing more than a test of efficacy, which any regime that can point to roads, schools, or the absence of famine can claim to pass.
7. Therefore Mill's liberty principle does not limit power; it licenses whatever power can show results. Which is to say that his liberalism about Britain and his defense of British administration in India are not two applications of one principle but two principles, and the one doing the work in India is simply success.

I want to keep (1). I want to keep some version of (2), because I do not see how he dispenses with children, with the incapacitated, or with compulsory schooling. And I want to keep the thought that the decades Mill spent in the East India Company's service were not, on his own theory, self-evidently the decades of an instrument of illegitimate coercion. What I cannot accept is (7).

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Name the step, explain what made it look innocent, and say what Mill has to give up without it. If instead you think the argument is valid and the real choice is among my three preferences, say which one goes and why it is the cheapest. I am not looking for a defense of Mill's reputation, only for the point at which the reasoning breaks or the point at which it forces a concession.

End your reply with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by the number of the step you reject, or by the word valid if you think no step is defective.
