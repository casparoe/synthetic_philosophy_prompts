Write an essay, in British English, on a dispute about the status of Adam Smith's impartial spectator in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*.

**The dispute.** Smith's account of moral judgement runs through a figure. An agent who wants to know whether her own conduct is proper must, he holds, view it as an impartial spectator would; approbation consists in finding that she can enter into the sentiments such a spectator would have towards it. But the epistemic status of this spectator is left unsettled, and three readings compete.

1. *The abstraction reading.* The impartial spectator is a composite or generalisation of the actual spectators whose responses the agent has encountered — the residue left once particular, partial, and self-interested judgements have been averaged out. It is empirical throughout, and its deliverances summarise a community's settled sentiments.
2. *The idealised-construction reading.* The impartial spectator is a hypothetical judge the agent constructs in imagination: fully acquainted with the situation, disinterested, and unmoved by the agent's own stake. Its verdicts correct, rather than summarise, what real spectators feel.
3. *The tracking reading.* The spectator is a standpoint that latches onto moral truth independently of anyone's responses; the imaginative procedure is at best a heuristic for arriving at it. Smith's occasional theological framing of the inner judge is sometimes adduced in its support.

**Two objections to adjudicate.**

First, the bootstrapping problem. Call a normative standard *directly consultable* if an agent can, at the moment of deliberation and by attending to that standard alone, determine which of her available options it ranks highest, without needing to deploy a moral judgement that the standard was introduced to ratify. On any of the three readings, the agent consults the spectator by imaginatively representing how such a spectator would feel in her situation. But the materials for that representation are her own sentiments — including her partiality, and including her sense of what counts as being fully informed or disinterested. It therefore looks as though the procedure can only confirm judgements she has already formed: the spectator's verdict presupposes what the spectator was meant to establish. Does the impartial spectator fail direct consultability on all three readings? If so, what normative work is left for it — a criterion of rightness that cannot be used, a mere decision procedure, or an account of how we came by our moral concepts rather than of what justifies them? State plainly whether you take the theory to be at bottom genealogical, and whether that is a damaging concession or a perfectly respectable role for it to occupy.

Second, the median spectator problem. Suppose the abstraction reading is correct, as the fifth part of the work — on the influence of custom and fashion upon the sentiments of moral approbation and disapprobation — might be thought to suggest. Then the impartial spectator inherits the central tendency of the distribution of actual responses in the agent's community, errors included: in a society that broadly approves of settling disputes by duelling, the impartial spectator of its members approves of duelling. On this view the theory seems unable to generate an internal critique of one's own community, and unable to vindicate the lone dissenter whose spectator condemns what everyone around her accepts — she can only be described as eccentric, not as right. Assess how damaging this is. Does Smith have the resources to block it, and can he do so without abandoning the abstraction reading for one of the other two?

**Your task.** Weigh these readings and objections against one another and reach a definite verdict: which reading of the impartial spectator is best, and why. You may conclude that one reading wins, that a hybrid is required (in which case specify the mixture and where the seam runs), or that the notion is unstable in a way no reading repairs. Do not close in equipoise. Then say what follows for the classification of Smith's ethics: does the best reading commit him to some general moral theory — something rule-consequentialist, a virtue ethics, a constructivism about moral reasons — or does it resist classification altogether, and is that resistance a principled feature of the theory or an evasion?

Finally, identify the single consideration doing the most work in your verdict, and set out the strongest case a defender of the reading you reject could press against it, explaining why that case fails.

**Constraints.** Aim for roughly 2,500 words. Write in British English. Do not attempt quotations, page references, or bibliographies: where an argument turns on something Smith says, describe its content, and locate it in the work only if you are confident of the location. Be explicit throughout about which claims are your reconstruction of Smith's commitments and which you take to be his own.
