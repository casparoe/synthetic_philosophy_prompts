So a friend of mine runs a small philosophy podcast and for his "big ideas" season he did an episode on the GEB chapter about the location of meaning. I read the book years ago and remember that chapter being stranger than his show notes make it sound, but I can't pin down where. Here's the summary he posted, more or less verbatim from the notes:

"Hofstadter's point is that a message doesn't carry its meaning around with it. Take a vinyl record: physically all there is is a wiggly groove. The music only comes into existence when the right decoder — stylus, amp, a listener — shows up and reconstructs it. He sorts any message into three layers: the frame message, which is just the surrounding context telling you how to decode it; the outer message, which is what the physical object conveys about itself; and the inner message, which is the real content, recoverable only by applying the decoding scheme the frame supplies. Once you see that, the conclusion is unavoidable: meaning lives in the decoder, not the message. The isomorphism between groove and sound isn't out there in the vinyl, its something the listener's head imposes on it. That's why a strand of DNA doesn't mean anything to a rock, and why Hofstadter is basically a reader-response theorist in a scientist's clothes: the text is inert until someone with the right interpretive habits animates it. The payoff is that the outer message is what physics and biology can study — mass, groove geometry, nucleotide sequence — while the inner message belongs to psychology, and so isn't really part of the physical world at all."
