I'm trying to figure out whether brain size has anything at all to do with how much an animal's suffering counts, going by my own verdicts rather than by what I'd like to believe. Before the cases, two framing things. First, I'm asking about moral *weight*, not moral status — I'm not trying to sort who's inside the circle, I'm asking, once something is in, how much its pain trades off against a comparable amount of mine. Second, I have a working principle already and I want it attacked: weight tracks the number of neurons in the cerebral cortex (or the pallium, for non-mammals), because that's where the integrating happens, with total brain neurons serving only as a floor so that a creature with a big cerebellum and a tiny cortex doesn't rank above one with the reverse.

Here are my verdicts. The numbers are the ones I've picked up from the neuron-counting literature and I know they're soft, so treat them as order-of-magnitude.

1. Adult human vs adult chimp: the human counts for far more, maybe ten to fifty times. Brains around 1.3 kg and 400 g. Consistent with size, consistent with my principle (human cortex has on the order of 16 billion neurons, a chimp's a fraction of that).
2. Cow vs dog: cow brain somewhere around 450–500 g, a medium dog's maybe 70 g. But I think the dog counts for *more*. I suspect this is familiarity — I've known dogs, never a cow — but I don't want to just write it off as bias, because that same move would let me dismiss every verdict below that I don't like.
3. Elephant vs human: elephant brain about 4.6 kg, roughly 257 billion neurons total, but 97.5% of them in the cerebellum and only about 5.6 billion in the cortex — a third of mine. My verdict: the human still counts for more, but the gap feels *smaller* than the human/chimp gap in case 1, which is embarrassing, since the elephant's brain is three times bigger than mine and the chimp's is a third of it.
4. Sperm whale vs human: biggest brain on the planet, something like 8 kg. I rank the human higher and I feel almost nothing about it. Is that just species loyalty, or is there something here?
5. Crow vs a small songbird-sized mammal: bird brains pack neurons far more densely than mammal brains do, and corvids and parrots are supposed to reach forebrain neuron counts in the primate range in a package the size of a walnut. I rank a crow well above a mouse and well above a rabbit, which is a point for counting neurons and against counting grams.
6. Octopus vs a pigeon: an octopus has something like half a billion neurons, but most of them are out in the arms, not in the central brain. I'm markedly less sure about the octopus than about the pigeon. Tell me honestly whether that's a real reason — distributed architecture shouldn't matter if only the count matters — or whether it's just that I can't imagine being an octopus.
7. Newborn human vs adult human: equal weight, full stop. But a newborn's brain is about a quarter of adult mass while the neurons it will ever have are more or less already there; what grows is glia, myelin, synapses. So the mass view says a quarter and the count view says equal, and neither is *why* I think what I think.
8. Adult human with severe microcephaly or late-stage neurodegeneration: full weight, same as case 7.
9. Honeybee vs mouse: bee has on the order of a million neurons, mouse tens of millions. The mouse counts for much more — but I don't think the bee counts for exactly nothing, and I can't say where that residue comes from.

The thing that's bugging me: cases 7 and 8 give full weight to humans whose brains are tiny or partly destroyed, while cases 3 and 4 discount elephants and whales. So brain size does no work whatsoever *within* my species and all the work *across* species. That smells like I've picked a physical variable and then bent it around a verdict I reached first.

What I want back: a small set of candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a species I've never thought about — say what function of what, and what the clauses are for. For each one, tell me which of the nine verdicts it reproduces, which it overturns, and which case is doing the most work in forcing the clause that saves it. Then say which of my verdicts has to go, and argue for dropping it rather than just noting the cost. Build in the constraint that the counts come from a method that literally dissolves the tissue into a soup and counts nuclei, so anything that only works if the numbers are right to the second significant figure is disqualified — I want principles robust to a factor of two. Finally, run your best candidate on cases I haven't listed: a bee hive as a collective (does it aggregate up past a mouse?), a severed octopus arm that keeps solving problems for an hour, a human cerebral organoid with a few million neurons and no body, and an elephant herd considered as a group rather than as twenty individuals. And if you think the whole framing is broken — if weight can't be a function of any physical variable like this, and what I'm really tracking is something about the shape of a life — say so plainly and tell me what I should be asking instead.
