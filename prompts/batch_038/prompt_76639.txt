Our department is running an adversarial-collaboration exercise on Hempel's raven paradox, and the two participants have spent a year talking past each other. Draft the joint statement they should have produced: the document both could put their names to.

The two positions, stated so that neither is a straw man:

**The degree camp.** Confirmation is probability-raising relative to a background corpus. Observing a non-black non-raven does confirm the generalization that all ravens are black, by an amount the background fixes; that amount is usually negligible but is not automatically zero. Nicod's criterion is a heuristic that misfires here precisely because it ignores the background. The paradox is generated by two errors: reading "confirms" as "confirms appreciably," and pretending the background corpus is empty. No primitive notion of projectible or natural predicate is required, because whatever work such a notion appears to do is done by priors and likelihoods.

**The restriction camp.** Evidential support is not a purely extensional relation between sentences, so the equivalence condition — the principle requiring that logically equivalent formulations of a hypothesis be confirmed by the same evidence — does not transfer from logic to epistemology. Nicod's criterion is not a crude heuristic but a principle that holds for predicates carving out kinds and fails for gerrymandered ones: "non-black non-raven" is not such a predicate, and satisfying it is not what makes an observation evidence. A tiny nonzero Bayes factor is not a small quantity of confirmation; it is a symptom that the background has already smuggled in the kind-facts doing the real work.

Both participants have agreed to fix a common formal playground before writing anything. Use it, and do not substitute another without saying why. In both worlds the generalization at issue is restricted to the reserve: *no raven in the reserve is non-black.*

**World I (fixed census).** A closed reserve contains exactly 1,000,000 objects; exactly 1,000 are ravens and exactly 100,000 are non-black. Both facts are common background. Rival theories: T₁ says no raven is non-black; T₂ says exactly one raven is non-black, which one being uniform. Under T₁ the 100,000 non-black objects are all non-ravens; under T₂, one of them is a raven. Prior ½ on each. One object is drawn uniformly at random and its ravenhood and colour observed.

**World II (free colouration).** Same reserve, same known raven count, but nothing fixes how many objects are non-black: each non-raven is independently non-black with probability ½. Rival theories: T₁ says every raven is black as a matter of constraint; T₂ says each raven is independently black with probability ½. Prior ½ on each. Same sampling procedure.

In each world consider two observations: **W**, the drawn object is a non-black non-raven (the white shoe); **B**, the drawn object is a black raven.

Produce the following.

1. **A jointly signed arithmetic table.** The four likelihood ratios — W and B in Worlds I and II — with the likelihoods displayed, not just the ratios. Then one sentence stating what the difference between the two worlds consists in, and one sentence per world explaining why W's ratio takes the value it does.

2. **A numbered list of agreed claims**, at least ten, that a partisan of each camp can sign without wincing: claims about the logic (the contrapositive, the equivalence condition, what does and does not follow from either), about the arithmetic, about what the paradox does and does not establish, about the role of background, and about which of the other side's intuitions each camp now concedes to be well founded. Flag any claim a side signs only under protest and state the protest. Include at least three claims each camp initially resisted, with a note on what the concession cost it.

3. **A disagreements table.** For each residual disagreement: a neutral one-sentence formulation; the formulation the degree camp would accept for its own side; the formulation the restriction camp would accept for its own side; and what each side says would have to be the case for it to abandon its position. Neither side's column may caricature the other, and the neutral column may not be a fudge both would reject.

4. **Cruxes** — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. Revise this list as you see fit, but I expect at least: (a) whether the value W's ratio takes in World II vindicates the restriction camp or merely illustrates the degree camp's background-relativity; (b) whether any case can be constructed in which the two frameworks yield different *verdicts* about confirmation rather than different numbers; (c) what to say about a black raven that lowers the probability of the generalization. For (c) use this case: the reserve is believed either to be raven-free, extirpated a century ago, in which case the generalization holds vacuously, prior 0.9; or to host a large population exposed to an industrial melanism outbreak documented downstream, in which case each raven is independently black with probability 0.1, prior 0.09; or to host a population untouched by it, in which case every raven is black, prior 0.01. A warden then reports a single raven encountered at random, and it is black. For each crux: the case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, and each camp's prediction, written down in the document before the computation is carried through. If a camp finds its prediction falsified by its own arithmetic, record the concession rather than adjusting the setup.

5. **A closing section separating the real disagreements from the verbal ones.** If "e confirms h" is doing two different jobs in the two camps' mouths, say precisely what the two senses are, which of the disagreements in §3 survive the disambiguation, and which evaporate. Be willing to conclude that the larger part of the dispute is verbal — but only by exhibiting a pair of sentences the two camps would evaluate differently and a reason the difference is not merely notational.

Constraints: do not adjudicate, do not rank the camps, do not propose a third view as the winner, and include no citations or reading lists. Every formulation must be one its target camp could sign. The test of the document is that a hostile reader from either camp would have to admit it is fair.
