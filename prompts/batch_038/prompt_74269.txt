I have no academic training in any of this. I run a small bakery with two employees, and several years ago I began keeping a notebook in which I write down each evening what I take the chances of the next day's outcomes to be — how many croissants we will sell, whether the flour delivery will arrive, whether it will rain. I taught myself enough probability to do the arithmetic, and I have lately stopped writing single numbers and started writing ranges, because a single number always seemed to claim a sharpness my evidence did not have. Then I tried to write down why the ranges are the honest thing to keep, and the argument I produced appears to prove considerably more than I can accept.

My state of belief about tomorrow morning looks like this. I allow a great many scenarios, and every one of them agrees on two points: that the chance of rain before nine is exactly one half, and that the chance of our selling more than a hundred croissants is exactly one half. The scenarios differ only in how those two matters are hooked together. In some, rain and a slow day always go together; in others, rain and a busy day always do; in others they are unrelated; and every arrangement in between is permitted as well. So my range for the claim that we sell more than a hundred is, tonight, the single point one half. In the morning I look out the window and see rain. I then work out, for each permitted scenario, what it says the chance of selling more than a hundred is given that it rained, and the results run all the way from zero to one.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) An assignment of probabilities is honest only if it claims no more precision than the evidence licenses.
(2) Where the evidence is silent about how two matters are related, an honest assignment must permit every way in which they might be related.
(3) Therefore the state described above — a point for the unconditional chance, the full spread for the conditional one — is the honest state for me to be in.
(4) An honest state is revised by conditioning each permitted scenario on what one learns.
(5) Revising in that way in this case leaves me, after the observation, with a range strictly wider than the one I had before it.
(6) Therefore honest revision makes observation cost me precision.
(7) If observation costs precision, the best-informed position available is the one held by someone who has observed nothing at all, and there is no reason for me to keep the notebook, to check the delivery records, or to do anything but guess.
(8) I cannot accept (7), and I cannot see which of (1) through (6) to surrender.

Either something has gone wrong above, or I have discovered that keeping records is self-defeating, and I do not believe that. Tell me which step fails, and why it seemed unobjectionable when I wrote it down; if in fact none of the steps fails, tell me instead which of them I should be prepared to abandon and what abandoning it would cost. I use these ranges to decide at four in the morning how much dough to make, so the cost is not an abstraction to me. If I am forced to choose, I would rather give up (6) than (2), though I suspect I have not understood what (2) commits me to.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
