Right, I need to get this off my chest, because my wife has banned the subject at mealtimes and you're the only one left.

Background: I spent thirty-five years in a government lab measuring nutrients in rivers, and I retired three years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy, which I'm thoroughly enjoying, mainly because nobody tells you the answer at the back. But what follows isn't from a book, it's from my own kitchen, and I'd be grateful for advice that isn't "communicate better", because we communicate fine. That's rather the problem.

We own a farmhouse that's been split in two — us on one side, and a younger couple, Dan and Maya, on the other — with a big shared kitchen in the middle that all four of us use. Years ago we wrote a one-page rulebook for the shared bits, half as a joke, and we've stuck to it religously ever since. Two clauses matter here.

Clause one: each household has the final word on its own allocated item and the other side can't overrule it. My item is the woodburner, whether it gets lit in the evening. Their item is the kitchen window, open or shut. That's all. Simple, and it kept the peace for a decade.

Clause two: if we both prefer one arrangement to another, then the house prefers it too. Never been controversial — how could the house want something both halves of it agree is worse?

Here's where we've got to. My preferences: window shut, I want the fire on, because I'm seventy-one and permanently cold; window open, I want the fire out, because I'm not paying good money to heat the garden. Theirs, as far as I can make out: fire on, they want the window open, because the room goes dry and stifling; fire out, they want it shut, because there's no sense airing a cold house. And there's one thing we all agree on without a moment's hesitation — fire on with the window shut beats fire out with the window open. Warm and cosy beats cold and aired. Every time, both households.

Now run an evening forwards. We start cosy: fire on, window shut. Maya opens the window — her item, I can't say a word. So I put the fire out — my item, they can't say a word. So Dan shuts the window — theirs. So I light the fire — mine. And we're back where we started, having burned an hour and a box of kindling. Its not an exaggeration, we genuinely did this on Tuesday, and there was a row about a draught excluder.

I sat down with a pencil and it does go all the way round, all four arrangements, and the arrangement we both agree beats another one is precisely the arrangement we can never come to rest in. In my line of work, when an instrument handed you a loop like that you assumed you'd wired it up wrong, and then you went and found the earth fault. I can't find the earth fault here.

So, if you don't mind:

- Is the rulebook broken, or are we? Can you show me where the loop actually is, in terms I could put to Dan across the table? He's a software man and he'll want it laid out properly rather than waved at.
- My wife's fix is to tear up clause two. She says the "both of us prefer it" business is the troublemaker and that once it's gone the whole thing settles down. I'm reluctant, because I don't want a rulebook that can rank something above an arrangement we both think is worse. But I'd like to know honestly: does dropping clause two actually stop the merry-go-round, or do we still go round without it?
- Dan's fix is a swap: they hand me the window for the winter, I hand them the fire. It works, I've checked it on paper. But it makes me uneasy and I can't say why. Is trading away a right you were given a proper solution, or does it quietly wreck the thing the right was there to protect?
- And a thought I can't shake: we don't need to rank all four evenings for ever, we only need to get through tonight. Does that make the loop harmless, or does it just mean we run into it once a day instead of all at once?

Tell me what to write into the rulebook, and tell me what the house gives up when I write it.
