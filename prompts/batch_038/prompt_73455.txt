Two months ago I joined a small research team that publishes an annual index of democratic backsliding, and my job is to draft the written justification for every country whose score moves. I assumed I understood the term when I was hired. A disagreement I sat through last week has made me doubt that, and I would like a careful, disinterested adjudication of it.

The dispute arose over a stylized case file we use to calibrate our judgments: a hypothetical country, described in the abstract, which both of my colleagues accept exactly as written, so nothing here turns on contested real-world facts. After winning a narrow legislative majority on a platform that said nothing about institutions, a governing party passes in a single session, entirely by ordinary legislative procedure, the following package: it adds four seats to the constitutional court and fills them with its own nominees; it transfers the drawing of electoral districts from a civil-service commission to a body appointed by the legislative majority; it requires a two-thirds vote for any future amendment to the electoral law or the judiciary act; and it creates a permanently funded civic-endowment agency whose twelve-member board serves staggered twelve-year terms. The reconstituted constitutional court upholds the package. The party then wins the next election.

Two colleagues — I will call them Anja and Rui — argued about whether this counts as backsliding. Neither would yield an inch. The exchange, as I recorded it:

1. Anja: This is backsliding, and the test that settles it is simple. Ask what each change does to the cost of reversal — how much it would take for a future government that opposes the package to undo it. Ordinary politics leaves the machinery of politics roughly as it found it; here a temporary majority has converted itself into a standing advantage that outlasts the majority. That is the thing to look for, not the content of any particular policy.

2. Rui: That criterion proves far too much. Central bank independence, life tenure for judges, an entrenched bill of rights, a written constitution requiring supermajorities to amend — all of these are deliberately designed to be hard to reverse, and they raise the cost of reversal far more than anything in this package. On your test the framers of every independent central bank were backsliders, and the best institutional design available to a democracy is indistinguishable from an assault on it.

3. Anja: The difference is in the direction of the entrenchment. Entrenchment is legitimate when it binds future majorities in the name of a standard that applies symmetrically to whoever holds power — rights that protect the opposition as much as the government, monetary rules that constrain the enacting party once it loses office. It is illegitimate when it entrenches a particular set of actors in the procedures by which power is allocated. Rights entrench the rules of the game; court-packing and locking the map entrench the players.

4. Rui: That sounds like a distinction until you try to apply it. Every party experiences its own procedural preferences as principles, and from outside there is no way to tell rule-entrenchment from player-entrenchment except by whether you approve of the outputs: an independent redistricting commission looks like a neutral rule if you like the maps it produces and like a partisan lock if you do not. Worse, your distinction assumes we can separate players from rules, but a party just is a platform, and platforms include proposals about rules — so there is no line to draw, only a preference dressed up as a criterion.

5. Anja: There is a line, and it is testable rather than aesthetic. Ask three things of any entrenching measure: was it adopted by the same body that stands to gain from it, does it outlast that body's tenure, and was the opposition given any hand in designing it. This package satisfies all three. Central bank independence and rights entrenchment characteristically fail them — they were enacted with cross-party support, on terms the enacting majority knew would bind it after it lost office. If a measure is designed so that you would accept it even from the other side, it is a rule; if it only makes sense as something you would never tolerate from your opponents, it is a lock.

I was not present as a participant, and I do not want to tell you which of them I found more convincing, because I genuinely do not know. What I do know is that I have to write a score and a defense of it, so a resolution that leaves the case undecidable is not usable to me.

Please act as a political theorist who works on democratic erosion and has also had to build cross-national indicators that survive scrutiny from people who disagree with the coders. Think the exchange through carefully before you answer — work through each move privately, including the objections you would raise to each, and to yourself if you were in the room; I want that reasoning done, but I do not want to see it.

Your reply must be exactly five numbered items, corresponding to moves 1 through 5, and nothing else. Each item should be a sentence or two: begin with a rating of that move on the scale clearly mistaken / mostly mistaken / mixed / mostly right / clearly right, then justify the rating by identifying the specific inference that succeeds or fails. Where a move is only partly right, say which part survives and what has to be dropped. Where Anja and Rui are both wrong about the same point, say so inside the relevant item rather than saving it for later. Where a rating has a consequence for how the case file should actually be scored, note it in a single clause. Do not commit to a mere draw out of politeness, and do not add any introduction, any closing remark about what the episode shows, any overall winner, or any suggestion of further reading.
