ok so I spent about three hours last night arguing with my friend Dana about repugnant markets and we got absolutely nowhere, and now I want a third opinion that isn't drunk at 1am.

Dana's position is basically: there's no principled line, the whole "some things shouldn't be for sale" literature is just squeamishness with footnotes. People decide a market is gross, then invent reasons, and the reasons always happen to fit. I said no — you can write down a rubric that does the ranking work without smuggling the disgust in at the front door. She said fine, build it, and it will rank things in exactly the order you already ranked them. Which is testable, sort of, so let's test it.

Here's my rubric. Five axes, scored 0–2 each. Two things I need from you: pick the weights (I couldn't, and that's half the fight), justify them in a couple sentences, then score and sort these markets.

the axes:
1. exit-cost asymmetry — can the seller buy their way back out later, at any price? (selling a car: trivially yes. selling a kidney: no.)
2. third-party drift — does the sale change the terms for people who never agreed to anything?
3. constituitive entanglement — is the thing sold part of what makes the seller a moral agent with standing, rather than a thing they merely happen to have?
4. desperation-tracking — is the willingness to sell itself a symptom of a background injustice that the sale then entrenches?
5. misuse-invisibility — can the buyer use the good in ways that hurt the seller or bystanders with nobody in a position to see it?

the seven markets:
- selling a kidney to a broker
- selling your vote
- paying a guy to stand in line for you (permit office, concert tickets, whatever)
- a municipality selling naming rights — police cars with a funeral home's logo on the door, a public middle school renamed after a credit union
- hiring an agency to deliver an apology, in your name, to someone you wronged, performed by a professional
- selling a slice of your future earnings to investors — the human-IPO thing, they buy 5% of you and collect
- a rich person buying to the top of a transplant list

what I want back:
- a total out of 10 for each, sorted, with a tier: S = genuinely shouldn't be tradable, A = tradable only under heavy constraint, B = fine with disclosure, C = fine
- one sentence per item naming the axis that actually drove the score, because my suspicion is that for most of them a single axis does all the work and the other four are decoration — tell me if that's true
- which item the rubric gets *wrong*: the place where its output diverges most from the ranking you'd give if you'd never seen the rubric, and which axis is lying to me
- and then the verdict. Is Dana right? Does this thing ever bite the hand that feeds it — does it rank something I find gross low, or something I find harmless high? If it never once does that, say so plainly and don't pad it.

no bibliography, I'm not writing a paper. I just want to know whether I have an argument or a mood.
