our reading group on ubuntu-style relational personhood hit a wall and I cant tell if we're wrong or just squeamish. One guy in it (call him D) built this as a reductio on the whole family of views, and he says if you don't like the conclusion you have to name the exact step that fails. Here's the argument:

(1) The relational view says personhood isn't a birthright you either have or lack — it's something that happens between people, and it's achieved, so it comes in degrees.

(2) If personhood comes in degrees then something has to fix where you sit on the scale, and on this view the only thing available is your actual relations: how many, how deep, how reciprocal.

(3) So a hermit who has renounced every particular tie is less a person than a grandmother at the centre of a big family, and that difference is a real difference in her being, not in our estimation of her.

(4) Personhood is what makes it wrong to harm someone; the wrongness of harming tracks how much personhood there is to harm.

(5) Therefore it's less wrong to let the hermit die than the grandmother, and the gap is exactly as wide as the relational gap.

(6) Which means a triage protocol that scores every patient on an embeddedness index — dependents, frequency of participation in communal life, record of reciprocity, quality of standing ties — isn't smuggling an alien value into the relational view. It's measuring the very thing the relational view says grounds moral status. A relational theorist who ran that protocol would be consistent, not cruel.

(7) And here's D's kicker, which is the bit that looks like it proves too much to me: the argument never used anything special about relations. Swap in rationality and it refutes any view grounding status in rational agency; swap in sentience and it refutes any view grounding status in capacity to suffer. So the same template refutes every candidate ground of moral status — i.e. it shows moral status has no ground at all.

D thinks (7) is a feature, not a bug: his line is that any property admitting of degrees is fatal to a theory that tries to ground status in it.

I want to keep (1), because I like the idea that being a person is something you do with other people rather than something you are, and I'd rather keep (4) than concede that personhood has nothing to do with why harming is wrong. But tell me if that's just incoherent on my part.

So: which step is the first one that doesn't survive? Explain why it looked perfectly innocent going in, then price the exits — what the relational theorist has to give up to block it, and what she gets to say about the hermit and the grandmother afterwards. And is there any force left in D's generalization at (7), or does it rely on the same move it's condemning?

Last line, machine-readable please: \answer{N} where N is the number of the first step you'd strike, or \answer{sound} if you think I just have to eat (3) and (5).
