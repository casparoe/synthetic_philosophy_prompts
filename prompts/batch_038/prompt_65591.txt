I am a doctoral candidate in cognitive neuroscience. I referee four or five split-brain manuscripts a year and supervise the lateralized-testing battery in my own lab, so I make the same call repeatedly: does a given behavioural result license an inference to two streams of consciousness, or even to two subjects of experience, or does it license only a claim about disconnection — information failing to reach a hemisphere that nevertheless belongs to a single conscious field? I would like that call turned into a procedure.

Build a referee's decision procedure that can be run in about an hour on a manuscript and its figures, with no access to the patient and no way to request new controls, and that a reviewer with no training in philosophy of mind can follow. State the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure terminates in "no two-subject inference licensed," and the points where it hands the issue back to the editor or to the author's chosen criterion of personal identity rather than settling it on data. It must engage, at minimum: the distinction between disconnection and division; why positive evidence of conflict and negative evidence of failed transfer warrant different inferences; the standing confounds (subcortical and ipsilateral routes, commissural remnants, eye movements and cueing, tasks one hemisphere can solve unaided); and the gap between absence of verbal report and absence of experience, given that a confabulating interpreter is not a lying one. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls in procedural dress.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a manuscript reporting accurate left-hand pointing to a left-field picture together with the patient's denial that anything appeared. Second, a manuscript reporting the intermanual conflict Akelaitis called diagonistic dyspraxia, in which one hand buttons a shirt while the other unbuttons it. Third, a manuscript that reproduces the failure of cross-field comparison after complete callosotomy yet finds above-chance verbal identification of left-field stimuli. The third is close to a study my own lab is preparing to submit: report what the procedure yields there even if the verdict is one I would rather not receive, and tell me if the grounds on which it is rejected would also sink the first case. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do — which questions it leaves permanently underdetermined by any amount of behavioural data.
