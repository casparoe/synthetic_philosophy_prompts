I am in my final year of high school. My school offers no philosophy courses, so for roughly two years I have been reading epistemology independently with the help of a retired university lecturer who volunteers as a mentor at our public library, and he has now pushed me to enter a paper in a statewide student philosophy conference: 2,500 words plus, as he insists, one genuinely original move rather than a book report. My topic is the relationship between two theses that Timothy Williamson attacks in Knowledge and Its Limits (2000) — the claim that some interesting conditions are luminous, and the KK principle. Please take on the role of a professional epistemologist who has taught a graduate seminar on the limits of knowledge and who is willing to be blunt with a promising but inexperienced student. Think carefully before answering, and where my own reasoning is sloppy, say so plainly rather than silently repairing it.

So that you can engage with my problem rather than with the general shape of the debate, here is how I have been setting things up, including the toy argument I built. Call a condition C luminous if, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. Call KK the principle that if one knows that p, one knows that one knows that p. My toy case: Nadia hears 100 tones, one per case, numbered 1 through 100. The pitch falls by a tiny, individually imperceptible amount at each step, and the tone counts as high-pitched in cases 1 to 60 and not high-pitched in cases 61 to 100, so the boundary is sharp and fixed even though Nadia cannot detect it. Nadia is stipulated to be an ideal listener: undivided attention, flawless memory of the earlier cases, no confusion about the task. I stipulate a single margin-for-error premise: if in case i Nadia knows that the tone is high-pitched, then in case i+1 the tone is high-pitched. It follows that in case 60 she does not know that the tone is high-pitched — for if she did, the tone would be high-pitched in case 61, which it is not — even though the condition obtains in case 60 and even though she is ideal. So high-pitchedness is not luminous. My intended original move is to run exactly the same reasoning one level up, on the condition K, meaning that Nadia knows the tone is high-pitched in the present case, using the corresponding margin premise for K; and I want to argue that the anti-luminosity argument and the argument against KK are not two arguments but one argument run at two levels. I have also coined a term I would like you to test: call C one-step luminous if, whenever C obtains in case i, the subject is in a position to know in case i that C obtained in case i−1. My hunch is that one-step luminosity survives my argument, since the margin premise blocks only knowledge of the current case, and that it might therefore rescue some of the internalist motivation luminosity was supposed to serve.

What I need is a careful comparison of luminosity and KK as theses, not a summary of either controversy. Please open with a short brainstorming section listing three or four genuinely distinct axes along which the two might be compared — for instance, what each is a claim about, what each costs its rejecter, how far each depends on the margin-for-error idea — and state which two you will develop and why. Then work through the following, in prose, with numbered steps wherever a derivation is clearer than a description of one. First, state precisely how the two theses are related, and settle whether a general denial of luminosity entails the denial of KK, whether the denial of KK entails the denial of luminosity in general, whether the entailments run in neither direction, or whether either direction needs extra premises that I should name explicitly. Second, test my toy argument at the boundary case: is my stipulated margin premise something that Nadia's idealization earns, or something that must be argued for independently, and does it smuggle in the conclusion? Third, assess my original move — whether running the argument on the condition of knowing refutes KK without circularity, or whether the margin premise needed at that level already presupposes something KK-like. Fourth, tell me whether one-step luminosity is stable, and whether it is philosophically interesting or merely a relabeled triviality. Close with a defended verdict on which of the two rejections does more work: which is harder to give up, and whether a coherent epistemologist could accept KK while denying that any interesting condition is luminous, or the reverse. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a survey of who has said what — I cannot check sources right now and would much rather have your reasoning than your citations. Please define any notation you introduce, aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, and if you think my conference thesis is simply wrong, the most useful thing you can do is show me exactly where it breaks.
