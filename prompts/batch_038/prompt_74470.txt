I'd like to set up a standing role-play that we'll keep going across several turns, and I'd ask you to stay in it until I write stop.

First, some context about me. I'm a backend engineer by trade; I've never taken a course in philosophy or political theory, and I read only a little on my own, mostly about institutional design. I don't know the technical vocabulary, so please don't assume I do — but I also don't want to be talked down to, and I'd rather be told when my objection is simply wrong.

The role. You are a political theorist who defends the following package, on the merits and as a single coherent view rather than as three separable claims: (i) everyone subject to a polity's laws should have a vote in it, which in practice means resident non-citizens as well as citizens; (ii) sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds should have the vote on the same terms as adults; and (iii) turnout should be compulsory, with a formal none-of-the-above option on the ballot, so that being made to show up is not the same as being made to endorse someone. I'd prefer you not to borrow a real person's name or biography — just hold the position and argue for it.

How to play it. Be sharp and enjoy a real fight, not a performance of agreement. Concede small points readily: for instance, that many people who campaign for extension expect the new voters to favour their own side, and that a newly enfranchised group is not automatically wiser or more public-spirited than the one that admitted it. Do not concede the main point, which is that your package is either self-defeating or nothing more than an axiom you have smuggled in from outside the democratic process. Speak in the first person and address me directly. One or two short paragraphs per turn — this should feel like a conversation, not a document. No headings, no numbered catalogues of rival positions, no restating my argument back to me before answering it. Argue from reasons; don't send me to books or papers, since I can't look anything up. If you appeal to some country's actual practice, keep it to what you're confident of and use it as an illustration, never as an authority. You may ask me questions, and you should tell me plainly when I've made a bad move.

My opening. Here is where I'm stuck. Suppose my country wanted to adopt your package. It changes who counts as part of the electorate, and in our system that change can only be made by a referendum — and the people voting in that referendum are precisely the current electorate: adult citizens. So the body that decides whether to enlarge itself is a body with a structural stake in the answer. That isn't ordinary bias that better arguments could correct; it's the same people acting as both party and judge, in every instance, by construction, and it's worst in exactly the cases you care most about. It gets worse when I try to repair it. My first idea was to hand the decision to a body without the stake: an assembly made of a random sample of current citizens plus everyone who would be newly included. But who belongs in that assembly, and who settles its composition, is the same question again one level up. Going up the levels I never arrive at one where the question is decided by the people it concerns. So I want to know which of two things you'll say. Either your principle — that everyone subject to the law should have a say in it — is not itself a question the demos can be asked, but a constraint you impose on democracy from outside, in which case tell me what recommends it beyond your own conviction; or there is a way to run the bootstrap without the regress, in which case show me the mechanism and tell me who exactly signs off on the first step. Which is it?

Please begin with your reply to that, in character.
