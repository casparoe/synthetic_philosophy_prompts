Background first, since it probably shapes what I'm asking. I'm 71, retired about six years ago from a career in field and physiological ecology — thirty-five yeas of mostly measuring things like fecundity, competitive ability and overwintering survival in plants, and then arguing with colleagues about what the numbers actually licensed. I only started reading philosophy seriously in retirement, so please be patient with me and don't assume I know the jargon.

Last month I went to a public lecture where an economist defended his discipline against the charge that utility is an empty notion. His whole defence was an analogy with fitness, and he leaned on it very hard. Roughly as I took it down: nobody ever observes fitness either; you infer it from who leaves descendants; people used to object that "survival of the fittest" is circular, and the answer was that fitness can be pinned down independently of the outcomes, from morphology, physiology and demography, so the claim has real content and can fail. Exactly the same for utility, he said: you infer it from choices; the complaint that utility is just a redescription of behaviour is the old tautology charge in a new coat; and revealed preference theory answers it the same way, because there are consistency conditions on choice data — GARP, Afriat's theorem — that some data sets violate, so the theory is falsifiable and does work. His conclusion was that critics of utility are making a mistake about how science actually operates, and that economists are entitled to precisely the same immunity biologists already have.

I left unsettled, because in my own working life the *independence* of the fitness measure did an enormous amount of labour, and I'm not convinced anything in his story is playing that role. So I'd like you to audit the analogy rather than just tell me both sides have a point.

What I want:

1. The correspondence table, explicitly. Which thing plays fitness, which plays the fitness measure taken from morphology and physiology, which plays the descendant-counting, which plays the environment, which plays the budget set, and so on. If a slot in one domain has no partner in the other, say so and flag it — I suspect several don't.

2. Where the mapping breaks. Then separate the breaks that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that are just local colour. I can generate a dozen superficial disanalogies myself; I want to know which two or three do the damage.

3. Two specific things I couldn't work out on my own:
   - When a data set violates GARP, is that in the same epistemic position as an organism with a plainly favourable trait failing to spread? In my field we could usually tell measurement error, a changed environment and a genuine refutation apart. Can revealed preference theory do that, or does it just get to re-describe the violation as a preference change? And if Afriat's theorem is doing the work he claims, is the non-vacuity it buys *internal* to the choice data in a way the biological case wasn't?
   - He also brought up Sen's apple example, the polite guest who takes the smaller of two apples and whose choice changes when a third apple appears. My hunch is that this inverts his analogy rather than supporting it: fitness is indexed to an environment, so nothing demands that a fitness ordering stay consistent across contexts, whereas revealed preference demands a context-independent ranking over bundles. Is that right, does it help him or hurt him, and what does it imply about whether preferences are supposed to be a property of the agent at all?

4. What the analogy quietly imports from biology that utility may not have — I'm thinking especially of the idea that the term earns its keep by doing causal-explanatory work, and that losing its independent grounding would cost biology something. Does losing the independent grounding of utility cost economics the same thing, or a different thing? Note that the welfare-economics stakes aren't symmetrical, and say something about whether that asymmetry is doing hidden work in his argument.

5. Whether the conclusion survives. Does the analogy license "critics of utility are making a mistake about how science works," or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? Commit to a verdict — I'd rather you take a position then hedge.

6. If it fails, is there a better analogy? I've half-formed ideas about latent variables in measurement, about centres of gravity, about dispositions like solubility — but tell me which, if any, would make utility look *better* rather than merely differently bad, and what each of them would smuggle in instead.

No bibliography or list of further reading, please — I can't chase things down and I'm not writing a paper. Just the reasoning, worked through carefully, with the technical bits explained to someone who is comfortable with mathematics but not with the economics literature.
