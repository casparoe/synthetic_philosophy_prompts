Ok so I'm on a county criminal justice advisory board and I got volunteered to write the memo that goes round before our vote on the 14th. I could use a hand — both sides have been talking past each other for about six months now and I want something that actually engages the arguments instead of splitting the difference.

The proposal: for a set list of offenses (aggravated assault, robbery, DUI causing injury) we'd stop using our sentencing grid. Instead, after conviction the person gets an indeterminate placement in a secure treatment facility, and a three-person panel reviews them every year. They go home when the panel decides they're no longer a serious danger to anyone. No other condition on release.

Two features are what everyone's really fighting about.

1. There's a cap. Nobody gets held longer than the number of years the old grid would have given for that offense, even if the panel still thinks they're dangerous. One camp wants this deleted outright: their view is that the only thing that can justify locking someone up is how much harm they'll go on to cause, so a ceiling set by how bad the *past* act was is superstition, and knowingly releasing someone you just judged dangerous is the actual harm. The other camp says the cap is the whole point — that number is the one thing the state can't trade away no matter how bad the forecast looks, and it's what keeps the panel from turning into a permanent preventive-detention machine.

2. There's no floor. Somebody who did a minor version of a listed offense but scores high on the risk instrument can be held up to the cap for that minor offense — say 18 months of grid time for something the panel thinks predicts a lot of future violence. Which sounds alot like locking people up for crimes they haven't committed yet.

And a third thing, which is mine. A board member floated that we could defend the cap without saying anybody deserves anything at all — just as a hedge against the instrument being wrong. That made me uncomfortable in a way I couldn't put my finger on until later: the cap in this proposal is set by offense severity, not by how confident the assessment is, so if error-hedging is really the job, shouldn't it move with the error? I want the memo to work through whether that justification can actually carry the cap, or whether it's a third position that quietly collapses into one of the other two.

Deliverable: a memo I can circulate to eleven people, roughly two pages. Recommend one of — adopt with the cap, adopt with no cap, adopt with a cap and a floor, or reject and keep the grid — and if we adopt, say what the pilot's term and review schedule should be. Give the reasoning that gets you there, including specifically what we tell the local paper about the case where risk and offense-gravity pull in opposite directions, because that's the case that'll be the headline. Plus a real section on the strongest thing the losing camp can say against our recommendation, not a strawman of it. Please don't spend any of it on whether free will exists or whether determinism is true — assume nobody on this board can settle that and make the recommendation turn on things we could actually argue about productively.
