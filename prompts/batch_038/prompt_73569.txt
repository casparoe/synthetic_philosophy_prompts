I teach a philosophy of social science seminar to eighteen advanced undergraduates and first-year graduate students, in a single 100-minute session, in a room with movable tables, a whiteboard, and no access to computers or phones during class. We have just finished Popper's *The Poverty of Historicism*. My students can recite his conclusion, but they hold it at arm's length: they treat it as a piece of verbal logic about other people's mistakes, and several of them clearly think that a competent social scientist armed with enough data would simply escape it. I want an in-class activity in which they run into the problem through choices they actually make, before anyone names it.

Please design the activity in full. Write your answer in American English, and keep it to roughly the length of a facilitator's handout I can print and run from — no more than about 1,700 words.

The spine I have in mind is a three-phase game, and I would like you to work out the specifics rather than replace the spine:

- **Phase 1.** The group plays a repeated simultaneous-choice game for at least eight rounds. Each round every player picks one of a small number of options, and each player's payoff depends on the distribution of everybody's picks, so that a stable aggregate regularity emerges endogenously from play — not one I have programmed in advance. One student, the Historian, sits outside the game and sees only the public tally record. Their job is to formulate the regularity in a single sentence, stated as a universal claim, and then stake predictions on later rounds. It matters that the regularity be genuinely well confirmed by the record at that point, so that no one can wave it away as a fake law.
- **Phase 2.** The Historian's prediction is announced publicly before each round, and scoring for the Historian and for the players is adjusted so that everyone has a reason to take the announcement into account. The regularity should come apart here, and it should come apart because of the announcement.
- **Phase 3.** Mid-run, a subset of players receives a sealed slip that changes their own payoff schedule — known at first only to them, and later, if they choose, to others. The Historian now faces a sequence that could not have been anticipated even in principle from the public record. In this phase the Historian should also be offered the option of switching to predictions conditioned on what is done to the system rather than forecasts of what will happen.

What I need from you:

1. The concrete game: the choice set (give the options plain thematic names, not A/B/C), the exact payoff table, and the Historian's and players' scoring in each phase. All scoring must use a paper ledger and a token currency with no real money and no grade attached; the tallying must be feasible for one facilitator with eighteen players inside a minute per round.
2. Roles beyond the players and the Historian — I would like at least a Chronicler who maintains the public record and an institution that can bind players' future choices — with a sentence or two on what each role is there to make visible.
3. A materials list, any preparation I must do before class, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes.
4. A debrief that maps particular moments in the run onto particular claims: the difference between a trend that has held and a law, the difference between unconditional forecast and conditional prediction, and the argument from the growth of knowledge. Students should be able to say which of the three phases threatened which claim, and why those are not the same threat.
5. An explicit answer to the objection I know will come up in the room — that the players were told the rules and the Historian was not, which is unlike any real society.
6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (including the ways Phase 1 can fail to produce a regularity at all, and the ways Phase 2 can stabilize instead of collapsing), with a fallback I can deploy mid-session if fewer than twelve students show up.
7. A frank closing note on what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking — in particular, what it does and does not show about the claim that the course of history depends on knowledge we cannot have in advance, given that everything the Historian fails to foresee here was in fact in the room, written on slips I handed out.

Nothing in the participant-facing material — the rules sheet, the slips, the scoreboard — may use the words historicism, prophecy, or Popper, or name any of the standard terminology. Those belong in the debrief only.
