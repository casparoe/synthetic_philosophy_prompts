You are a philosopher of historical reasoning who has also spent time inside government policy shops — someone equally at home with the epistemology of analogical inference and with the way analogies actually function in national-security deliberation. I would like you to think carefully about the problem below and treat it as a diagnostic task rather than a survey.

I convene a graduate seminar on the use of history in foreign policy, and for three years it has contained the same unresolved argument. On one side sit what I will call the analogy-users. They hold that statesmen cannot deliberate except from cases; that 1938 licenses a standing presumption against accommodating an aggressor's territorial demands; that the recurrent error of statecraft is failing to see a rising power's challenge as dangerous until it is too late to answer cheaply; and that the Thucydides-trap case file — sixteen historical rivalries between a rising and a ruling power, twelve of which ended in war — is precisely the kind of evidence policy should be built on. On the other side sit the analogy-skeptics. They point out that for any precedent there is a competing one, since Korea and Munich were both available to the architects of the Vietnam escalation and both were cited; that the similarities invoked tend to be selected after the preferred conclusion has been reached; that the case file is a hand-picked, non-random sample in which "rising," "ruling," and "war" are stretched to preserve the pattern; and that we have no observation of the counterfactual in which Chamberlain resisted, so the supposed lesson is not established even within its own case.

What puzzles me is not that they disagree but the shape of the disagreement. Both camps present themselves as the empiricists and accuse the other of importing prior commitments. Neither can name a case that would change its mind, yet each is fluent about which cases the other ignores. The skeptics' core claim, that analogical reasoning about politics misleads, strikes me as close to unfalsifiable, since any successful use of an analogy can be redescribed as luck or as a generalization reached by other means; the users' core claim, that no deliberation is possible without precedent, seems equally immune, since declining to act on one analogy is itself describable as acting on another.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that this is not a disagreement about history or about evidence. I suspect it is either a disagreement about whether the relevance of a similarity is an objective matter or is fixed by the question one happens to be asking; or else a case of two groups answering different questions under the single word "lesson," one asking whether a precedent justifies a decision, the other asking whether a precedent organizes attention and makes a novel situation describable to people who must act under time pressure, and so unable to contradict each other at all.

Please diagnose the impasse. I want four things.

First, a classification of the disagreement, argued for rather than announced: verbal; substantive; a difference in standards of evidence; a difference about what counts as a single case; a difference about what reasoning is for in deliberation; bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for; or a sign that the question "does history teach?" is ill-posed. Distinguish these candidates from one another and say which features of the debate as I have described it discriminate between them.

Second, an assessment of my hypothesis. Is the relativity-of-relevance reading correct? If it is, does it dissolve the disagreement or merely relocate it, and what survives of the skeptics' warning once relevance is indexed to purpose?

Third, an account of what would count as progress here: a distinction, a method, a formal result, a reformulation of the question, or a change in what the seminar is trying to establish. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say so plainly and explain why this impasse is stable rather than merely hard.

Fourth, a test of your own diagnosis. State how a committed user and a committed skeptic would each respond to it, and whether your diagnosis is one both could accept as a description of their disagreement even while rejecting its verdict. That is my criterion for success: the account has to be recognizable from inside both positions.

Before writing the diagnosis, spend a short section brainstorming: list the candidate diagnoses you can think of, including ones I have not suggested, and note for each what would have to be true for it to be the right one. Then commit to one, or to a specific combination, and defend it.

Write in continuous prose under a few headings, roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words. Do not give me a survey of Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides trap; I know these cases and so do the seminar members. What we lack is an account of why arguing about them goes nowhere. No bibliography and no citations: work from the positions as I have described them and from general knowledge.
