I am a third-year doctoral student in a department that runs a small standing working group on the semantics and metaphysics of vagueness. Before any member presents, the group circulates a one-page dossier of numbered commitments, and a respondent is assigned whose brief is to attack the dossier rather than the person. Mine is reproduced below, and I would like a hard audit of it before it goes out. Some background that may explain its shape: I came to philosophy from legal interpretation, and I have spent a great deal of time on bright-line thresholds in municipal ordinances, which is why one or two entries read as though a lawyer wrote them rather than a logician. I am not looking for references, a survey of the literature, or a reconstruction of anybody's published view — I want reasoning about my own set. Please do not assume I already know where the weak points are, and please do not read my ordering as a ranking of how much I care about each entry.

The commitments are these. (1) Every declarative sentence of ordinary English has a classical truth value, vague predicates included: the claim that a particular man with 4,312 hairs on his scalp is bald is either true or false, and there is a fact now about which; I want no truth-value gaps, no degrees of truth, and no revision of classical logic anywhere. (2) Vagueness is not in the world: mountains, heaps, and men have precise boundaries, and nothing in reality is itself indeterminate — vagueness is a property of representations. (3) The sorites argument is therefore unsound: for "bald" there is a unique number n such that a man with n hairs is bald and a man with n+1 hairs is not, and the inductive premise fails at exactly that point. (4) What fixes n is nothing over and above the total pattern of our use — the community's dispositions to apply and to withhold the predicate, the history of those applications, and the social and causal circumstances in which they occurred; there is no residual semantic magic, no tacit convention, no act of stipulation. (5) Nonetheless n is unknowable in principle and not merely in practice: knowledge requires that a belief not be false in nearby cases in which it was formed the same way, and any belief to the effect that this particular number is the cutoff would have been false in such cases, so the limitation is necessary rather than temporary. (6) At the same time I want to deny that there is any fact about where "definitely" runs out: although "Harry is bald" is true or false, as one iterates — definitely bald, definitely definitely bald — the hierarchy thins out and simply ceases to have a determinate terminus, and I resist the suggestion that at some level it is a fact that definiteness stops. (7) I reject supervaluationism, for two reasons: its treatment of the claim that there is a sharp boundary between the bald and the non-bald, on which that existential comes out true because every admissible precisification makes some instance true while no instance is true, seems to me an unacceptable semantics; and I do not accept that our usage leaves a range of equally admissible candidate extensions with nothing whatever to choose among them. (8) Consider what I call the Register case: a coastal municipality has for a century and a half administered a storm-surge levy keyed to whether a structure counts as "tall" under an ordinance; disputes went to a standing panel which, whenever it judged ordinary usage to give no guidance, settled the case by drawing lots, entered the result in a public Register, and thereafter treated Register entries as binding and taught them to surveyors, so that after 140 years the Register covers the disputed range entry by entry. My view is that in that community "tall" genuinely lacked a determinate extension before the Register and genuinely has one now, the panel's practice of settling disputed cases having created the boundary; and I take this to threaten nothing above, since no such device exists for vague predicates in actual English — indeed it is precisely that absence which is why our own boundaries are unknowable. (9) Arbitrariness is no objection: that there is no reason why the cutoff is 4,312 rather than 4,313 does not show that there is no cutoff, since plenty of facts are brute. (10) When a theory of vagueness conflicts with the application intuitions of competent speakers, I think the theory should win; I am not an ordinary-language conservative, and I do not treat "that verdict is counterintuitive" as a serious objection.

Two objections I have already fielded and do not think land, though I have been unable to say crisply why. First, a classmate argues that if the total pattern of use fixes n, as (4) says, and we have full access to that pattern, then sufficiently careful empirical work ought to recover n, so (4) and (5) cannot both stand. Second, another member argues that believing there is a unique cutoff while refusing to believe, of any number whatsoever, that it is the cutoff is an incoherence of the lottery-paradox family, and that likewise I cannot call the sorites unsound under (3) without being able to identify a false premise. I suspect both can be answered, but I would rather you told me whether they can than let me assume it.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the dossier and tell me which entries genuinely cannot be held together and which merely look as though they cannot. For the genuine conflicts, rank them by severity instead of listing them flat, and for each one state the minimal revision that removes it together with what that revision costs me — in particular, whether it expels me from epistemicism into some position that already has a name, or strands me in a view nobody holds. Then tell me which single change to the dossier would resolve the largest number of problems at once, and describe the position that results. Be equally explicit where you judge a tension to be only apparent, and give the reason it dissolves, since those are exactly the points on which I expect my respondent to press.
