You are a moral philosopher who has taught population ethics and theories of well-being for many years and who has watched cohort after cohort of students re-run the same argument in seminar without anyone budging. I'd like you to think carefully and to treat what follows as a diagnosis rather than a survey, and I'd like you to open with a short piece of brainstorming — five or six candidate kinds of disagreement that could produce an impasse of this shape (purely verbal, disguised empirical, methodological, a clash of bedrock intuitions, two different questions travelling under one name, an ill-posed question), with a line on which are live possibilities here — before you commit to one, or to a weighted combination of them.

I'm a novelist, four years into a book whose engine is a pair of siblings: a woman who has settled, on Benatar's grounds, never to have children, and her brother, an embryologist at a fertility clinic, who thinks her reasoning is not merely wrong but incoherent. To write their scenes honestly I have read the antinatalist argument and its replies, and what strikes me is how little of that literature moves. On one side: the four-quadrant table, with the claim that the absence of pain is good even where there is no one to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody whom it deprives; and behind it the supporting asymmetries — that we have reasons not to create the miserable but none to create the happy, that we can regret having created someone though we can never regret not having created them, that distant suffering moves us while the absence of distant happy people does not. On the other side: the charge that the third and fourth quadrants are not merely under-argued but mutually inconsistent, since the first licenses an impersonal evaluation that the second forbids; the claim that a symmetrical deprivation account would make non-existence itself a harm and so dissolve the table; the objection that the conclusion proves far too much, sweeping in non-human animals and the desirability of extinction; and the further objection that the intuitions treated here as data are precisely the intuitions that the non-identity problem and the impossibility theorems of population ethics have taught us to distrust. Neither side appears to think the other has committed a mistake, and no one seems to be disagreeing about any fact.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that this is not an axiological dispute at all but a dispute about the semantics and metaphysics of the phrase 'bad for' — about whether a welfare claim can have a truth value when there is no subject of whom it is true, and about whether the two sides are consequently answering different questions under a single heading: one asking what we owe to the merely possible, the other asking what it is for a state of affairs to be good or bad simpliciter. If that is wrong, say so plainly and tell me what the disagreement really is. Either way I want three things. First, the diagnosis, supported by what each side actually says rather than by a catalogue of the positions. Second, the hinge: the single question on which, if my two characters could be brought to agree, the rest of their argument would either collapse or resolve — stated alongside the one intuition each of them treats as non-negotiable and cannot itself argue for, so that I can see where the real friction sits. Third, an honest assessment of what would count as progress: a new distinction, a formal result in population ethics, a reframing of the question, or perhaps evidence from the psychology of harm and deprivation judgments — and if your view is that no progress should be expected, argue for that explicitly and tell me whether the stalemate shows the question to be ill-posed or merely hard. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list; I need the structure of the disagreement, not its citations. Organise the answer under clear headings for the brainstorm, the diagnosis, the hinge, and the prospects for progress.
