I am sitting in on an undergraduate game theory course, and the handout on dominance reasoning contains this passage:

'A strategy is dominated when some other strategy does at least as well against every possible combination of the opponents' choices and strictly better against at least one. You may delete dominated strategies and repeat on what remains; the order of deletion never matters, and the process never destroys a Nash equilibrium, so if it leaves a single profile, that profile is the unique equilibrium of the game. Randomized play is irrelevant at this stage: domination is simply a comparison between one pure strategy and another. Nor is the reasoning demanding — each player need only know that the others are rational, not anything about what the others know in turn. Where deletion stalls, as in matching pennies, nothing is dominated and the full machinery of equilibrium mixing is required.'

The handout then works an example in which the row player has rows T, M, B against columns L, R, with row payoffs T = (0, 3), M = (3, 0), B = (1, 1), and concludes that the row player has no dominated strategy.

Please audit the passage sentence by sentence: which claims would a game theorist accept as stated, which are subtly wrong or quietly conflate two things the theory keeps apart, and what the author could say in defense of each. Then tell me whether the two conclusions — that the order of deletion is irrelevant and that a single surviving profile is the unique equilibrium — still hold once the earlier sentences are corrected, and whether the worked example is right. I am inclined to think the remark about randomized play is too glib, but I could not say why, and I do not know whether the uniqueness claim is safe.
