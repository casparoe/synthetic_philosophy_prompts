We had people over for a county pre-K levy post-mortem at my brother-in-law's place last weekend — the measure was a universal pre-K expansion paid for with a surcharge on income over $400k, and it lost by eleven points — and around the second round of pie it turned into an arguement about the Nordic model that went on for like an hour. The two people doing most of it were Marcus, a high school history teacher who reads more economics than he'll admit, and Ellen, who does something with municipal bonds and has strong opinions about state finance. Neither has ever been to Scandinavia, which Marcus said was disqualifying and Ellen said was beside the point. I was elbow-deep in dishes for most of it so I'm reconstructing from memory, but here's roughly the shape of it:

(1) Marcus: the Nordic model can't be exported, full stop, because it isn't a policy package, it's an output. The load-bearing element is generalized trust — strangers trusting strangers, and trusting the tax office not to pocket their money — and that took a century of small, fairly homogeneous societies plus a state church to grow. You can't legislate trust, you can only spend it. A pre-K program here would be run by an apparatus people already assume is captured and incompetent, so the money leaks, the program underperforms, and trust falls further: a downward spiral instead of the virtuous one they had. (2) Ellen: that has the causality backwards, or at least unproven. The better argument is that impartial *institutions* produce trusting people — when the state visibly applies the same rules to everyone, you update on your neighbors — so exporting institutions is precisely how you'd export the trust, and "they had the trust first" is an assumption dressed as a finding. She also said "small homogeneous societies" was doing a lot of quiet work, since Sweden has taken in an enormous number of immigrants over thirty years and the machine still runs. (3) Marcus: fine, but then you've conceded the real point. If what makes generosity affordable is openness — the old line that small trade-exposed economies want a big insurance cushion against world-market shocks — that's a reason the Nordics have the model, not evidence anyone else can get it. We aren't a small open economy; we're a big, semi-closed one with a fragmented labor movement. And the Nordic bargain was wage compression: unions accepted compressed wages and let unproductive firms die in exchange for generous security and retraining. American unions could never make that trade, because with weak unemployment insurance and no real retraining system, compressing wages is just a straight loss for their members. (4) Ellen: then your own diaspora example cuts against you. You keep bringing up the stuff about Scandinavian-descended Americans — whatever the details, the shape of it is that the norms traveled and the politics didn't. If trust and work norms persist in the descendants but redistributive politics don't, norms weren't the binding constraint, politics were. And politics are contingent: proportional representation let Nordic labor parties govern for decades and build the thing before anyone could veto it, while a labor movement boxed into single-member districts and a two-party system never gets that runway. So your claim isn't "can't be exported," it's "can't be exported without also changing electoral rules and union density," which is a claim about sequencing, not impossibility. (5) Marcus: which is my thesis with extra steps. Once every component requires all the others, "the model" has swallowed the whole society, and it stops being falsifiable as a policy guide — any failure gets explained by a missing precondition and any success by a lucky one. Also, the thing on our ballot was never the Nordic model to begin with. They don't fund universal services by taxing the rich; the money comes from broad consumption and payroll taxes that hit nearly everyone, and the top income rates aren't the engine. It's the middle class paying for the middle class. A surcharge on top earners funding pre-K is a different animal in a Nordic costume. (6) Ellen: I'll take that as a correction, but it sharpens my point rather than saving yours. Sort the pieces. Denmark pairs easy firing with generous insurance and serious retraining, and that combination mostly needs administrative capacity — a benefits agency that functions and caseworkers who can actually place people — not a trust substrate. Capacity is buildable and observable; culture is neither. So there's a real partition: some elements need the trust substrate, some need only state capacity, and only the second bucket is exportable. What you and I disagree about is which bucket each policy sits in. (7) Marcus: and how would we ever settle that? The only test for "does this piece require the trust substrate" is whether it worked somewhere without one, and there's nowhere to run that experiment. Your partition is a promissory note.
