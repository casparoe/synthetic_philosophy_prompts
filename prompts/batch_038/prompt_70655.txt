I direct the research unit of a national truth commission charged with establishing the facts about a period of military rule roughly forty years ago. The registry files from that period were largely destroyed, so most of our case files rest on survivor testimony, a small number of surviving administrative documents from the regime itself, contemporaneous press coverage, and memoirs and human-rights reports written years afterwards. Our founding statute provides that a finding of fact must be corroborated by at least two independent sources, and that provision is breaking down in practice: read strictly it establishes almost nothing, and read loosely it admits testimony that turns out to be one account arriving three times.

My assessment officers — eight of them, none trained as historians — each work through twenty to thirty case files a week. Every file must end with one of three labels, established / probably occurred / not established, before legal counsel signs off and the label is printed in the public report.

Write a decision procedure an officer can run on a single file in about thirty minutes. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and above all a workable test for when two sources are independent enough to corroborate — including the partial-overlap case, where sources may share a provenance for some details of an account and not for others. It must say explicitly what to do when provenance cannot be determined at all, and it must keep separate the two different reasons for downgrading a file: that a source is biased, and that a source is not independent. Ground it in what the craft of source criticism actually offers rather than in the wording of our statute, and mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Also mark the points at which the question stops being evidential and becomes one of mandate — for instance, whether a perpetrator may be named in a file that ends up labelled not established.

Then run the procedure on three files:

(a) Three witnesses give closely matching accounts of a killing at a bridge outside a provincial town. All three were held in the same detention facility during the same months; two subsequently lived in the same exile community and were interviewed together by an NGO in the 1990s. A newspaper of the period reported only that there had been security operations in the region. This is a case the commission's chair very much wants established.

(b) A single memoir, written thirty years after the events by a former conscript, describes the transfer of prisoners from one facility to another. It is matched in detail by a surviving transport log from the Interior Ministry — a document the regime produced for its own administrative purposes, which records movements but says nothing about what happened to the people moved.

(c) A claim appears first in a 1986 article in an exile newspaper, then in two academic histories that both cite that article, and then in the statement of a witness who confirms having read the article as a young man.

Finish by stating what this procedure cannot settle, and where its labels will be systematically misleading.
