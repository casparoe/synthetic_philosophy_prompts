I'm putting together the take-home for our senior/first-year-grad philosophy of physics seminar and I've hit a wall with the Everett unit. Every year the same thing happens: students write a tidy summary of the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic argument, or of Zurek's envariance derivation, define the incoherence problem and the quantitative problem, announce that the first is solved and the second is solved by the same machinery, and walk off with an A without ever touching what actually makes the Born rule hard. I want a question that punishes that answer.

Here's the toy case I've been scribbling on with a colleague, tell me if it needs tightening. A spin-1/2 particle is prepared with amplitude sqrt(2/3) on up and sqrt(1/3) on down, and the same preparation-and-measurement is repeated N times with full decoherence between trials, so a branch-counting picture sees 2^N equally real outcomes. Now stipulate three rival weighting rules for those branches. Rule A (branch counting): every branch gets weight 1/2^N. Rule B (the "amplitude rule", a thing I made up as a foil): a branch with k ups and N-k downs gets weight proportional to sqrt(2/3)^k * sqrt(1/3)^(N-k), normalised. Rule C: the Born rule, weight (2/3)^k (1/3)^(N-k). I want students to work out the up-frequency each rule predicts in the limit of large N, and then to say whether a many-worlds theory using rule B is empirically distinguishable from Everettian quantum mechanics, and roughly how big N has to be before the two come apart at ordinary experimental confidence. The point I want them to reach is that this is not a question about interpretation at all: pick the wrong measure and you get the wrong frequencies, so the measure carries empirical content, and Everettian QM is falsifiable in a way that the "but everything happens so nothing is ruled out" objection misses. Then I want the question to turn the screw with the typicality problem: the rule-A sceptic says that a majority of branches contain observers who see about half ups, so unless we think we're special we should expect that, and the Everettian replies that "majority of branches" already smuggles in a measure. The crux to be tested is whether they can say why that reply isn't just question-begging, and what evidential weight, if any, attaches to the low-weight branch in which someone sees 100 ups in a row. If you want to use self-locating uncertainty as a resource in the answer, define it inside the question itself — about half my class has never seen that literature and it's not fair to grade them on whether they've read it.

Deliverables, please: (1) the question as it would appear on the paper, self-contained, around 350 words, with the setup spelled out precisely enough that a student can actually do the arithmetic; (2) a model answer of roughly 900 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 25 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — I want it to say explicitly that reciting the decision-theoretic argument without applying it to the three rules earns nothing — with marks per point and a one-line note on the mistake each of the three or four most common bad answers makes. Assume 3 hours, closed book, calculator allowed. And no reading list or "for further discussion" — they can't look anything up, so anything the answer needs has to be in the question stem.
