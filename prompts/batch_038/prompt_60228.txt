**The setup.** In 1997 David Lewis examined whether the doctrine of penal substitution — the idea that one person's suffering can discharge another person's liability to punishment — is something we already accept in secular life. His argument runs roughly like this. Debts come in two kinds: monetary debts are plainly transferable, since anyone can hand over the cash, but penal debts seem different, since it looks incoherent to say that a stranger's imprisonment is *my* punishment. Yet our ordinary practice is inconsistent with treating the distinction as absolute: we have no trouble at all with someone else paying an offender's fine, even a very large one. And fines are not a soft option — a sufficiently heavy fine is just as burdensome as a custodial sentence and carries much the same public disgrace. So if penal substitution were categorically impossible, we ought to conclude that fines never satisfy justice's demands; but we do not conclude that. Lewis therefore suggests, tentatively, that a restricted form of penal substitution may be available: the substitute must undertake the suffering voluntarily, must not be a paid mercenary, and must stand to the offender in some relation of devotion, so that the suffering can be accepted as discharging the debt rather than merely as an equivalent quantity of pain.

You do not need to know the paper beyond this summary. Treat the summary as the source material.

**The assignment.** Write a mock client disclosure document — the kind of numbered offering paper a wealth-management firm or an insurance broker hands to a prospective purchaser — issued by an invented company that arranges penal substitutions commercially. Call it something plausible and bland. Total length: 750–900 words. Format it as a real disclosure: a short summary box at the top, then numbered clauses under headed sections, then a boxed risk-warnings panel at the end, and finally a one-line complaint-handling notice naming a fictitious regulator.

**The target of the satire** is the position that penal liability is a transferable asset like any other, together with the compliance-register idiom in which such a position would naturally be documented. Do not satirize any religion, any religious doctrine in its theological setting, any real company, or any named person, living or dead. Lewis should not appear as a character.

**The content requirements.** The humor must come from the firm's getting the philosophy exactly right while remaining oblivious to it. Every clause should be a genuine feature of the transferability problem, exaggerated only in its commercial packaging. The document must include, in whatever order you like:

1. A product-range clause distinguishing fine-backed instruments from custodial instruments, and explaining how the firm converts the second kind into the first — with the conversion mechanics spelled out in enough bureaucratic detail that a reader notices the firm has simply repriced a prison sentence as a sum of money, and notices that the firm considers this a solution rather than the problem.
2. A substitute-eligibility section implementing the voluntariness and non-mercenary conditions. The firm must screen for devotion, must certify that no payment was made, and must therefore explain its fee structure, which is paid by the offender. Let the incompatibility sit there unremarked.
3. A hedged clause on whether what the substitute receives counts as punishment or merely as equivalent suffering. Offer both, at different price points, and include a disclaimer about which one the firm will undertake to have satisfied a retributivist customer.
4. Provisions for overpayment and underpayment: who owns the surplus if the substitute suffers more than the sentence required, and what residual liability survives if the offender reoffends after a completed discharge.
5. A clause on whether the offender's repentance is a required co-payment, written so that the answer is clearly whichever one protects the firm.
6. One short piece of quoted dialogue — clearly invented, not drawn from any real source — from a concerned counterparty: a compliance officer, an underwriter, or an in-house retributivist who objects in terms that are entirely procedural and never philosophical.
7. A risk-warnings panel of at least four bullets, in the flat style of the warnings on an investment prospectus, each of which is a real objection to penal substitution rendered as a disclosure. The objections must never be identified as objections.

**Constraints on voice.** Stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no narrator's aside, no sentence that tells the reader what to think. The register should be legal-financial, not jokey; the comedy should be entirely in the content. A reader with no philosophy background should find it a funny piece of corporate nonsense; a reader who works on punishment and desert should recognize that every clause is load-bearing. By the final risk panel, a careful reader should be unable to say confidently whether the firm's logic is absurd or merely an honest description of what we already do with fines.
