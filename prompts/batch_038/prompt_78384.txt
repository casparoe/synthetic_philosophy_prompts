ok so I'm a junior and I'm the opinion editor for my school paper, which sounds more impressive than it is — it's four pages once a month and me and two other people decide what runs. I also do policy debate. Between the two I probably read like 150 student op-eds a year and I've noticed something: a huge chunk of them don't actually have an argument, they have an analogy doing the job of an argument. Every fall it's "the school board is Chamberlain-ing the dress code, this is our Munich moment." Every spring it's something about Vietnam and quagmires applied to, like, whether we should fund the robotics team. This year everyone's doing the Thucydides trap thing with the US and China even when the piece is about the hallway traffic flow at lunch, I'm not kidding. And in debate I see the same move from the other side, where a team's whole case is "this policy is like [historical thing]" and if you break the analogy the case collapses but nobody ever checks whether it holds.

What I want is a screening procedure I can actually run, not an essay about why analogies are dangerous (I know, I've read the takedowns). Something like: the questions to ask in order about any proposed historical analogy, what a good enough answer to each one looks like, which question failing means the piece gets sent back for a rewrite versus which one means I just make them add a paragraph, and where the procedure runs out and it's my call as an editor and I should own that it's a judgment call instead of pretending the test decided it. Hard constraints: I get maybe 15-20 minutes per submission before the Thursday deadline, our faculty advisor signs off on everything but she teaches English and doesn't want to be the history referee, and the writers are 15-17 so the procedure needs to be something I can hand to a sophomore and they can run it on their own draft without me in the room. Ideally it's short enough to fit on one side of a page.

A couple of things it has to handle, because my own attempt at this (I made a three-question thing: is the source case real, is it similar, is the difference relevant — obviously useless) keeps breaking on them. One, the analogy-swap piece, where it starts as Munich and by paragraph six it's quietly Sarajevo or Vietnam, and both halves seem fine on their own. Two, the "it's NOT X, it's Y" piece, which is 60% disanalogy — is that automatically better, or does it inherit the same problem? Three, cases where the source event is itself contested by historians, so the writer is importing a disputed version of 1938 or of what actually happened in the Gulf of Tonkin. And four, please run it on at least two or three real examples end to end, including one where the procedure says to kill or heavily gut a piece that I would personally agree with politically — I want to see it bite on something I like, otherwise I'll just be using it to filter out the kids I already think are wrong. Also: be straight with me about which parts of this are settled and which parts people who actually study this argue about. Like, is drawing policy lessons from history considered a legitimate thing at all, or is the expert view that it's mostly a rhetorical trick? I get the sense the field is split and I don't want you giving me one side dressed up as the consensus.
