You are a philosopher of mathematics with serious working knowledge of constructive and intuitionistic analysis, of assertability-based (proof-theoretic) accounts of the meaning of the logical constants, and of the differences among the main schools of constructive mathematics. Read the case below carefully and think it through before writing. I want analysis, not a survey.

**The case.** For two years I have been sitting in on a group that redoes real analysis in a constructive setting. Their working norms, as far as I can tell, are these:

- A real number is not a completed magnitude but a sequence of rationals together with an explicit rate of convergence: part of giving a real number is giving a function that says, for each n, how far out you must go for the terms to be within 2^-n of one another. A Cauchy sequence with no such rate is not, for them, an object you may reason about.
- Uniform continuity is defined so that the modulus of continuity is part of the data of the function. A function that is continuous "in principle" but comes with no modulus will not be called uniformly continuous.
- They distinguish a set's being *inhabited* (someone can exhibit an element) from its being *nonempty* (it is not the case that it has no elements), and they use the first where a classical text would use the second.
- For reals they treat apartness — |x − y| > 0 — as the primary positive relation and define equality as its negation. Where a classical text says "suppose x ≠ 0 and consider the two cases x > 0 and x < 0," they require the strictly stronger hypothesis that x is apart from 0, and remark that the weaker one will not license the case split. Likewise they decline to assert, for an arbitrary real x, that either x ≥ 0 or x ≤ 0.
- They refuse to pass from "it is contradictory that no n has property P" to "some n has property P," even when P is decidable. Yet for every statement A whatsoever they will prove in two lines that it is impossible for both A and not-A to fail, while declining to assert A or not-A. To me these look like the same claim; to them the gap between them is the whole subject.
- And then, throughout, they use countable choice without flagging it. Asked about this, one of them said it is not really an assumption at all: a proof of "for every n there is an m such that …" already consists in a way of producing the m's, so the choice function was there in the proof from the start.

What I cannot get straight is the shape of the package. They are punctilious about what counts as being *given* an object, they refuse steps from double negation to assertion that look tiny from the outside, and they nevertheless accept a choice principle that asserts the existence of a function while, on the usual reading, constructing nothing. Separately, I understand that some choice principles are known to be explosive in constructive settings — assuming them lets you derive excluded middle, the very thing the edifice exists to avoid — and that countable choice is supposedly not one of them. I cannot see why not, and I cannot see whether the group's comfort with choice is a principled stopping point or just a place they happen to be standing.

**What I want from you.**

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning and assertion that makes the package cohere — not each norm separately, but the single account of what a mathematical statement says, what asserting it commits you to, and what a mathematical object is, under which all of the norms above are the *natural* thing to do. State it precisely enough to be testable: then predict, for two or three further practices I have not described (for instance, how they would treat the least-upper-bound property, the intermediate value theorem, or a quotient of the reals by an undecidable equivalence relation), whether the group would accept, reformulate, or reject, and why your reconstruction entails that answer.

2. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits. For each rival account — a reading on which every function from naturals to naturals is Turing-computable; a reading on which "proof" is replaced by local truth in a sheaf or topos; a type-theoretic reading on which meaning is fixed by canonical forms and judgments; a purely instrumental reading on which the definitions are shaped so that the theorems run as programs, with no claim about meaning at all — say which norms it licenses, which it does not, and where the group's actual practice would visibly differ if that rival were what they believed. Then sort the norms into a table: strictly presupposed / merely compatible / actively ruled out.

3. Solve or dissolve the choice puzzle. Give the strongest rationale available for accepting countable choice while refusing the step from ¬¬∃ to ∃: is the line drawn by logical form, by what the quantifiers already commit an asserter to, or by something about which objects are involved? Say whether the mechanism by which stronger choice principles yield excluded middle could in principle reach down to countable choice, or whether there is a genuine barrier, and say what the barrier consists in if there is one.

4. Ask whether the strictness about rates and moduli is driven by the meaning-theory you reconstructed in (1) at all, or by something else — a demand that objects be *given* rather than a demand that assertions be *warranted*. Consider that a group with no views whatever about the connectives, wanting algorithms only for engineering reasons, might adopt the very same definitions. If they might, say what that implies about how much philosophy this practice actually pins down, and what further evidence would discriminate.

5. Counterfactual reconstruction. Say concretely what would have to change — in the definitions, in which theorems remain available, in what a finished proof must contain, in what a graduate student must produce — under (a) keeping the rate-carrying definitions while adopting classical logic and classical semantics, and (b) keeping the logic while dropping countable choice. For each, identify where the change bites hardest and what that reveals about which parts of the package are load-bearing.

If you recognize this package as belonging to a known school of constructive analysis, say so in a single sentence and move on; do not let the label do the work of the reconstruction. Where a principle has a standard name (the "omniscience" principles, the choice-implies-excluded-middle results), state its content and not merely its name, and name only what you are confident of.

**Process and format.** Begin with a short brainstorming section: five to eight candidate theories of what the practice presupposes, one line each, noting for each which of the norms it struggles to explain. Only then commit to a reconstruction. Use headers corresponding to tasks 1–5. Roughly 1400–1900 words. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — argue from the content of the practice. Where you judge a part of the group's position unstable, self-undermining, or merely unexamined habit, say so plainly and say why.
