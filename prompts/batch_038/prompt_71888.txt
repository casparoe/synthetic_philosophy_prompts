For thirty-five years I was an observational astronomer, and most of my working life was spent on selection effects: Malmquist bias in flux-limited surveys, and the general embarrassment of drawing conclusions from a sample you did not choose and whose edges you cannot see. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and I have been reading about anthropic reasoning — the Self-Sampling Assumption and the Self-Indication Assumption, and the dispute over which of them the Doomsday reasoning and the presumptuous cosmologist's bet really depend upon. The literature presents this as a choice between two postulates, adjudicated largely by which thought experiment's conclusion you find least disagreeable. That is not how I would want a sampling assumption justified, and I do not believe the logical space has only two cells in it.

I convene a small reading group — retired scientists, two philosophy graduate students, one statistician — and I should like to hand out a decision tree that sorts us by what we actually believe about our own location in the world, rather than by which camp we announce ourselves to belong to. Please write it subject to the following conditions.

**Shape.** Between six and eight branching questions, each a yes/no or a three-way choice, and between ten and fourteen leaves. Label every branch with the claim that separates it from its sibling, never with the name of a position. At each leaf, give the position's name if it has one, or write "unnamed" if it does not, together with one sentence on the most awkward thing its occupant is thereby committed to.

**Answerability.** The questions must be answerable by someone who has never read this literature. The first three nodes in particular should be phrased entirely in terms of cases — an urn of consecutively numbered balls, a subject who wakes without knowing how many times she has been woken, a universe whose constants permit life only in a narrow range — and must not contain the words indexical, reference class, self-sampling, or self-indication.

**Branches I want**, in whatever order best serves the tree:
1. Whether a complete objective description of the world, together with everything I can presently perceive, still leaves it open which of the observers described I am, or whether that is no genuine further question.
2. Whether the collection from which I am supposed to be a random sample is fixed by the world, fixed by me, or is scaffolding that a better formulation would dispense with.
3. Whether what gets sampled is whole persons or individual moments of experience — and whether some leaf instead samples over everything I could possibly be rather than over a restricted class.
4. Whether the mere fact that a hypothesis posits more such observers ought to raise my credence in it, before I bring in any of my own particular properties.
5. Whether adopting a sampling assumption commits me to the Doomsday conclusion, licenses me to reject it, or leaves the argument valid while unmotivating its premises.
6. Whether the position still functions when the number of observers is infinite, or is known only to within an order of magnitude — a situation I met constantly in cosmology and never see addressed.
7. Whether the preference for the larger universe is a consequence to be accepted, a reductio of the assumption that generates it, or a symptom of counting the same datum twice.

**Test case.** After the tree, run it on one case of my own devising, which I doubt appears anywhere. Call it the survey-limited case. Hypothesis A says the universe contains of order 10^11 galaxies of the sort my catalogue resolves, each bearing observers. Hypothesis B says the universe is vastly larger and contains of order 10^22 such galaxies, locally indistinguishable from A's. I hold exactly one entry from that catalogue — my own galaxy — and I did not select it; it came to me by way of my existing in it. For each leaf, state which hypothesis its occupant should favour and by roughly what factor, and then state whether the answer, or merely its direction, changes if I additionally learn that my galaxy sits in a region whose density is atypical of the universe as a whole. Where two leaves give the same answer here, say that the case fails to separate them; do not manufacture a difference.

**Unoccupied cells.** List the combinations of answers that are logically available but that nobody holds, and for each say why the cell is empty: is the combination incoherent, is it merely unattractive, or has it simply not been tried? I would rather see three of these than none.

**Two cautions.** I have no library access and want no references, reading lists, or citations — the names of positions are useful to me, page numbers are not. And do not settle the debate: the tree's business is to locate us, not to tell us who is right. Where two leaves that are ordinarily treated as rivals cannot in fact be separated by any of your questions, mark the merger explicitly at the node where it occurs.
