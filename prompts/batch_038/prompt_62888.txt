My grandmother died in March, and I have spent much of the summer helping my father work through her estate, which turned out to be more instructive than either of us expected. She had bought a block of stock in the early 1970s for what now looks like a trivial sum; at her death it was worth roughly two hundred times that. Her accountant explained that for income-tax purposes the shares' cost basis is simply reset to their value on the day she died, so the entire half-century of appreciation vanishes and is never taxed at all — not by her, and not by us when we eventually sell. My father was visibly relieved. Then, about a month later, he asked the obvious follow-up: what would have happened if she had handed me the same shares while she was alive? Economically the outcome looks identical — I own them, she no longer does — but in that case the basis travels with the shares and the gain stays on the hook forever. So the same asset, transferred to the same person, is treated as a taxable accretion if it moves one week before death and as though it had never appreciated at all if it moves one week after. I have been unable to find anyone who can tell me what principle, as opposed to what administrative convenience, makes that the right line to draw.

The surrounding practices deepen the puzzle rather than resolving it. The federal government does tax very large transfers at death, at a top rate of forty percent, but only above an exemption now in the low tens of millions of dollars per person, so that well under one in a thousand estates ever owes anything; the tax is aimed almost entirely at what people call dynastic wealth, and it reaches almost none of it. Meanwhile the accountant walked us through the standard maneuver for anyone below that threshold who nonetheless holds appreciated assets: borrow against the portfolio, since loan proceeds are not income, live off the borrowed cash for thirty years without ever selling, die, and let the heirs take the shares with a fresh basis and sell them tax-free to retire the loan — a sequence she described, with a shrug, as buy, borrow, die. Other countries run the mirror image. Canada, as I understand it, has no tax on the transfer at death but treats death as though the decedent had sold everything that day, so the accrued gain is taxed on the final return. And I have read that Congress briefly enacted something like that rule for American inheritances in the 1970s, then repealed it before it ever applied. Several states add a third layer: they tax inheritances at rates that vary with how closely related the heir was to the decedent — nothing for a spouse or a child, a moderate rate for a sibling, a much higher one for an unrelated friend — so that the state's claim on the same dollars depends on the genealogy of the recipient.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the theory of ownership that the American pattern presupposes, and an assessment of whether it has one. The basis reset seems to require that a person's ownership of a thing does not pass to her heirs but terminates at death, leaving no continuing subject to whom the accumulated gain can be attributed — yet the estate tax, applied to the very same moment, plainly does treat death as a transfer in which value moves from someone to someone. Can both be true of one system, or is one of them philosophically parasitic on the other? What has to be true about a gift, as against a bequest, for the first to transmit a gain and the second to annihilate it? And what does the kinship-graduated state tax assume about what an inheritance is — a completion of the decedent's own plans, a family's ongoing common stock, or an unearned windfall to a stranger — given that it charges more the further the recipient stands from the decedent's household? Please separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely happens to fit it comfortably; I suspect a great deal of the commentary treats a bookkeeping accommodation to the realization requirement as though it were a considered theory of property, and I would like to know how much of the pattern survives once you strip away appeals to valuation difficulty, liquidity, and lock-in. Tell me which rival accounts of ownership, of the family as an economic unit, and of the state's residual claim the practice quietly rules out; where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license it equally; and what would have to change — in the rules, and in the ordinary way we speak about what a person owns and for how long — if we replaced it with the Canadian approach.
