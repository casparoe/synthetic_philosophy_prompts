Could you do me a satirical piece? About 700 words, in British English please.

It's a parody of one of those internal corporate project-approval documents — a business case / project initiation document — written by a delivery manager who has to get sign-off before a world is created. Standard furniture: scope, constraints, dependencies, a risk register in table form, an assumptions log, and a sign-off box at the bottom with initials against various roles.

The client is God, but specifically as Molinism has it, and the whole joke is that the client's famous sovereignty has been quietly reduced to a procurement exercise. So the document needs to get the machinery exactly right:

- Three inputs from upstream teams, in the right logical order. Natural knowledge: all necessary truths plus the full space of possible worlds. Middle knowledge: what every possible free creature would freely do in any circumstances the client might place them in. Free knowledge: what actually happens — which the PM has to note is only available *after* the decision is taken and is therefore useless for planning.
- The middle-knowledge input is read-only. Nobody wrote it, nobody can amend it, nobody can escalate it, and there is no owner. The document must nonetheless insist, in flat corporate register, that this is not a reduction in the client's sovereignty, since He didn't cause it and it constrains only His options, not His power.
- Scope should turn on the strong/weak actualisation distinction: the project can strongly actualise only the circumstances it puts in place, while everything the creatures then freely do is weakly actualised — i.e. delivered by a third party the client can neither instruct nor predict except by reading the read-only data set. Free creaturely actions are out of scope but sit squarely on the critical path, and the PM should be annoyed about this.
- Risk register to include: the possibility that every candidate person goes wrong at least once in every world where they're significantly free, so no candidate world meets the stated objective of moral good without moral evil; the possibility that no feasible world meets the objectives at all, which cannot be established in advance; a selection risk that if there's no best feasible world, only an endless run of better ones, then any choice can be second-guessed at review; and the fact that nobody can say what makes the read-only counterfactuals true, with no owner for that question — I'd like that one logged as an assumption, escalated twice, then closed as accepted becuase there's no alternative.
- One bit of quiet horror about the counterfactuals concerning possible people who will never exist: still true, still in the data set, still had to be read.

The humour has to come from getting the theology right rather than making it silly. Someone who knows this literature should be able to point at any line and say yes, that's the actual objection — hollowed-out sovereignty, the grounding problem, the feasibility problem — and think it's fair rather than a straw man. But it also has to work for a reader who's never heard of Molinism: use the technical terms, and let the corporate context make them inferable from how they're deployed, so a lay reader picks up the gist of each one without a glossary.

Deadpan all the way through — no winking, no asides, no punchline underlined. The best lines should be completely straight.

Two other things. Don't name any real philosophers, theologians, denominations or books; invent the firm, the delivery manager and the stakeholders. And don't put anything in quotation marks unless you invented it yourself — no quoted passages from actual texts, and no references list at the end.
