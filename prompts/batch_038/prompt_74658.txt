Third-year spacetime ontology seminar, 18 students, 75 minutes, and what I want is a knot in the stomach rather than a knot being explained to them. They've done intro physics but zero GR math — no tensors, no "a manifold is a set of points with charts", none of that vocabulary exists for them yet. By the end they should have viscerally hit the problem that treating spacetime points as things with an identity independent of the fields running on them creates for determinism, and they should be able to state two ways out and what each one costs. They havn't heard the name of the argument and shouldn't until the debrief.

I had a first design and I'm now fairly convinced it's bad, but I can't tell if it's fatally bad or just badly framed. The plan was a trampoline-style elastic sheet with marbles on it, and then I shift the marbles while holding the rim fixed. My worry: the sheet already has its own bending, and the marbles are matter sitting on it, so what I'd actually be demonstrating is that moving the marbles changes the arrangement — which is trivially true and is the opposite of the point. Is that salvageable, or do I throw it out? Then design me the real thing.

What I need:

- a materials list made of stuff a teaching cupboard actually has (paper, stickers, transparent overlays, string, pins, whatever),
- a staged sequence with rough timings,
- and above all a rule that forces a judgment they can't duck: a moment where two physically built set-ups are sitting on the tables and they have to commit, out loud and on a vote, to whether that's two worlds or one world described twice — with no hint from me about which answer the argument wants.
- a plan for the three or four students who'll immediately say "obviously it's the same world, you just relabelled it, this is silly." I want them to run into the wall, not step around it. So build in something that makes the relabelling reading cost them a prediction or a fact they were previously happy to assert.

Then the debrief: map each stage onto the machinery (the bare point-set, the region we're free to shuffle while leaving everything outside untouched, the two readings, the determinism claim), and lay out the escapes — the bite-the-bullet-on-indeterminism move, the one that says the two descriptions are a single physical possibility, and the relationalist denial that the points are real at all — with the price tag on each.

Finally, be blunt about which feature of the actual argument my paper-and-sticker version flatly cannot reproduce, and what I'll have to just talk them through instead.

700 words max, please. Tables and bullets are fine, I don't need prose.
