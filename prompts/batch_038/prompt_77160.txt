I cover mathematics, artificial intelligence, and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a piece on a claim that has become commonplace in writing about large language models: that a system which reports mutually inconsistent probabilities can be Dutch-booked, and that this is a practical diagnosis of unreliability rather than a mathematical curiosity. Two of my sources have described the situation in ways that cannot both be correct, and before I commit anything to print I would like a careful explanation of where the disagreement actually lies. Please write for a reader who is comfortable with expected-utility reasoning and with the statement of the probability axioms, but who has never worked through a betting construction.

The first source, a formal epistemologist, said in substance that a book is possible only against a single, unified bettor whose degrees of belief can be read off from one consistent set of betting dispositions. Because a model's reported probabilities shift with the phrasing of a question, there is on this view no such set to read off, and the theorem never engages; their formulation was that you cannot make a book against a committee.

The second source, who works on the reliability of deployed systems, said in substance that the model writes to one ledger: it feeds a single downstream decision procedure, its inconsistent estimates get acted on across many contexts, and the resulting losses land on one balance sheet. On this view the fragmentation is beside the point, because a guarantee of net loss is a fact about where the money ends up, not about how it is represented inside the system.

What I need is an account of the conceptual issue that separates them. In particular:

1. State precisely what the Dutch book theorem proves, and about what object the proof is a proof — a set of numbers, a set of dispositions to accept wagers, a decision procedure, or something else. Then lay out the bridge assumptions required to get from *these numbers are not probabilities* to *this agent is guaranteed to lose*: that wagers are acceptable to the agent in the units in which payoffs are denominated; that wagers the agent would accept separately may be combined into a package the agent must also accept; that the agent's acceptance of a wager does not itself alter the agent's mind; and any further assumptions doing real work that I have not listed.

2. Identify which of those assumptions the first source is attacking, and say whether the attack succeeds. It matters to me whether the requirement of a unified bettor is an explicit premise of the theorem, a background idealization the theorem quietly depends on, or an independent thesis about what counts as one agent.

3. The second source's reply appears to rest on the observation that the losses come out of one pocket. Explain whether one pocket is sufficient for a book, or whether the argument additionally requires that the agent evaluate the set of wagers as a set. If the latter, describe what a fragmented agent would have to be like for the book to bite, and say whether that description resembles anything anyone actually deploys.

4. So that I can generalize correctly: does the same problem arise for an ordinary person who, asked one and the same question in different contexts or moods, sincerely gives different answers? My impression is that some philosophers hold that inconsistency sealed off between compartments is harmless, while others hold that such inconsistency is precisely what the argument was built to detect, and I cannot tell whether there is a settled view. Please say plainly where the disagreement is live rather than manufacturing a consensus that does not exist.

5. Finally, my editor uses the phrase for any situation in which muddled preferences cost an agent money, including cases where the agent goes around in a circle of trades and ends up worse off having paid for the trip. Explain the difference between a Dutch book and a money pump, and whether the fragmentation worry carries over from one to the other.

I do not need references and will handle sourcing myself; what I need is to be able to describe the dispute accurately and to say which of my two sources is making a mistake, or whether they are talking past one another.
