The question as it appeared on the assignment:

In lecture we split Devlin's case for using the criminal law to enforce morals into two steps. Step (i) is a descriptive-functional claim about what a shared morality does for a society; step (ii) is the move from that claim to a conclusion about the proper reach of the criminal law. Call an objection aimed at (i) a *premise objection* and one aimed at (ii) an *inference objection*. (a) State both steps in your own words. (b) Classify Hart's main criticisms as premise objections, inference objections, or both, and say which of them does the most damage and why. (c) Say whether Devlin could concede the most damaging objection and still hold onto some version of legal moralism, and justify that.

The student's answer:

The question sets up two steps, so I'll take them in order. Step (i): Devlin thinks a society isn't just a collection of people who happen to share a territory and a government. It's held together by a shared morality, a common set of felt ideas about what's decent and what's disgusting, and that shared morality does real work in making collective life possible, roughly the way a common language makes communication possible. You don't choose it and you can't just opt out of it without ceasing to be part of that society. Step (ii): if the shared morality is doing that job, then a society is entitled to defend it in the same way and for the same kind of reason that it's entitled to defend itself against treason, and the criminal law is one legitimate instrument of that defence. What counts as an offence against the shared morality isn't settled by a philosopher's theory but by the feelings of ordinary people — intolerability, indignation, disgust — which is supposed to keep the law anchored to something like a democratic consensus rather than to a sect's views.

Hart's criticisms are mostly inference objections, I think. The big one is his attack on the disintegration idea: there's no evidence that societies come apart when their sexual morals shift, and lots of evidence that they absorb quite large moral changes and carry on, so you can't get from morals have changed to society is under threat. That's an inference objection because it's about whether the conclusion about the law follows from the facts. He also says the treason analogy is misleading, since treason is a betrayal of a political structure that keeps functioning, while Devlin treats a change in what people believe as though it were an attack on the state. And he says the whole argument runs together the positive morality a community happens to hold with the critical morality by which that positive morality can itself be assessed; if disgust is the test, the law just entrenches whatever prejudices the majority has at a given moment, which is what the Wolfenden recommendations were trying to avoid.

The one that does the most damage is the disintegration attack, because it's empirical. Devlin asserts a causal mechanism and offers nothing for it, and once the mechanism is gone the treason analogy has nothing left to lean on. The positive/critical morality point is nearly as strong, but it reads more like a diagnosis of where Devlin goes wrong than a refutation of him.

On (c), yes, he can concede it and keep something. Legal moralism as such only says that the immorality of conduct can be a reason to criminalize it, not that it always is one, and Devlin never claimed more than that. A Devlin who drops disintegration can still say the state may sometimes act on the community's sincere moral convictions, so long as it does it through the ordinary-person standard and leaves genuinely private life alone. There's a related difficulty for Hart, though: he wants to appeal to critical morality in order to judge the community's positive morality, but as a legal positivist he's committed to denying any necessary connection between law and morality, so it's not clear he's allowed a moral standpoint from outside the community's own standards. Since positivism is the better-established position of the two, Hart's objection looks unstable from within his own framework.
