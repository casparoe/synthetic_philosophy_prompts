I chaired a reading group last term and ended up in exactly the position I tell my students to avoid: refereeing an argument I couldn't score myself. It was between Ravi, who does the EEG/masking work in our cognitive science unit, and Marta, a postdoc in philosophy of mind. The topic was Block's access/phenomenal distinction and whether global workspace theory has any use for it. Neither was being obtuse, which is the annoying thing. Here's how it went, roughly in order:

(1) Ravi: every measure of consciousness we actually have — verbal report, forced choice, confidence wagering, post-decision accuracy — is a measure of access. So a state that is phenomenally conscious but inaccessible could never show up in any experiment at all. If it can't show up, the distinction buys nothing, and the workspace people are right that consciousness just is global availability.

(2) Marta: too quick. The A/P distinction isn't a rival empirical hypothesis to GWT, it's a distinction inside the explanandum. GWT tells you which states get broadcast; it doesn't tell you why broadcasting feels like anything. Without the distinction you can't even state that gap, because you've already defined consciousness as the thing you measure.

(3) Ravi: you can state the gap without it — "why does availability come with experience?" needs no appeal to inaccessible phenomenology. And when you do appeal to it you land on a dilemma. If P-without-A is possible, you've got undetectable experience floating around, a claim no evidence could ever touch. If it's impossible — and honestly it sounds incoherent, what would it be for an experience to be one that can in principle affect no report, memory, judgement or behaviour? — then A and P coincide everywhere and the distinction is empty. Either way it does no work.

(4) Marta: neither horn. It does conceptual work even if P-without-A never actually occurs: it's what lets us notice that our measures measure something else, and it's what makes the blindsight and partial-masking data look like puzzles rather than noise. Above-chance discrimination alongside a sincere denial of seeing anything is precisely where you'd expect the two to come apart, briefly and locally.

(5) Ravi: those cases are evidence for me, not you. Each one is contested — the "denial" is a conservative response criterion, the iconic memory results depend on assumptions about report timing, and above-chance-without-awareness can be redescribed as weak access rather than access-with-phenominal-left-over. If your only positive instances dissolve, you're back on my second horn: a distinction that might be true but could never be shown to be.

(6) Marta: suppose every empirical case collapses. It's still true that your theory's success conditions are stated in terms of reportability, so GWT is a theory of access consciousness, and calling it a theory of consciousness is a substitution you've made without argument. A theory can be perfectly successful and simply not be about the thing you wanted a theory of.

(7) Ravi: but if the thing you want a theory of is characterised only as whatever access fails to capture, it has no content — and a theory can't fail to be about something with no content. You defined your target by negation and then charged me with missing it.

Can you adjudicate this properly? Treat yourself as a professional philosopher of mind who also knows the experimental literature, and assume I've got the vocabulary. Before you hand down verdicts, do a bit of brainstorming first: for each of the seven moves, write out the strongest version of it, then judge that rather than the weaker one that may have come out at the table. Then rate each move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is right at each specific step and who's more right overall — and actually commit, please, don't fob me off with "there are subtle points on both sides". Finish with what both of them are missing; I have a suspicion there's at least one move neither made.

No citations or reading lists, I know the field, just argue. British English throughout. And at the end tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the one point in your adjudication you're least sure about.
