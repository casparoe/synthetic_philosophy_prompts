I lead people analytics at a mid-sized employer. Twice a year we field an engagement survey, and the report we publish to leadership includes a breakdown by generation — Baby Boomer, Generation X, Millennial, Generation Z — which we derive for each respondent from their date of birth using a standard set of cut years. We report average engagement scores, intention to leave, and perceived fairness of manager ratings by generation, and our learning-and-development team designs programs around what the breakdown shows. Two features of this arrangement have come to bother me, and I cannot tell whether they are practical complaints or philosophical ones.

The first is that our own policy elsewhere forbids reasoning from date of birth. Age is treated by our legal department as a protected characteristic that must not enter employment decisions at all, and our posting, review, and promotion workflows are deliberately built to keep it out. Yet here we compute a variable that is a deterministic function of date of birth, publish group averages derived from it, and hand those averages to the same managers who make staffing decisions. Nobody involved sees a tension.

The second is arithmetic. Our Boomer respondents were all born within a nine-year window and are all, in any given survey year, within a nine-year age window; our Gen Z respondents are all roughly in their twenties. Every difference we report is therefore at once a difference in age, a difference in the period of measurement, and a difference in birth cohort — and I have been told, without quite following why, that survey data of our kind cannot separate those three, since fixing any two of them fixes the third.

Given all this: what does the dashboard presuppose about generations as a kind of thing? Does it commit us to the claim that birth-year cohorts are genuine kinds whose members share stable motivational properties, or is it equally at home with the buckets being useful fictions for targeting communications and designing onboarding? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out — in particular, what does it exclude about whether the reported differences belong to people because of the conditions that formed them, because of how old they currently are, or because of what is happening to everyone right now? Does it presuppose that the boundaries fall sharply at the cut years, given that a respondent born in December and one born in January land in different buckets and are reported as differently engaged? And what does it presuppose in computing the category from a date of birth rather than asking people which generation they take themselves to belong to?

Separately, is the age tension real? Does treating generation as a legitimate analytic variable while treating age as off-limits require an affirmative claim that generation is something other than age in disguise, and if so, can this practice supply one, or does it merely happen to be consistent with such a claim?

Throughout, please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and flag the places where it is underdetermined — where several incompatible accounts of what a generation is would license exactly the same dashboard. Close by telling me what would have to change in the variables we collect, the numbers we publish, and the way learning-and-development acts on them if we replaced the implicit view with the strongest available rival.
