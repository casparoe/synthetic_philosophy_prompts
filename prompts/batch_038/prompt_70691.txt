Two years ago I left a job surveying invertebrates for a conservation charity to take up a lectureship, and this spring I inherited my parents' twelve acres of orchard. I have spent the last few months trying to work out whether the pest-control programme I signed up for — a broad-spectrum spray aimed at codling moth, applied four times a season — is something I can carry on doing in good conscience. That question has made the so-called scale argument uncomfortably concrete for me: the thought that even a very small probability that any given insect is sentient, multiplied by the sheer number of them, yields an expected total of moral importance that dwarfs almost anything else we might attend to. I now have to set the final exam for my module Animals, Evidence and Policy, a third-year interdisciplinary course whose students tend to arrive having read one or two popular essays and are entirely liable to recite the argument back at me in slogan form, confident that the arithmetic has settled the ethics.

Please write one essay question on that argument, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The constraints: third-year undergraduates, 45 minutes, closed book, and the question must be fully answerable from its own text plus general reasoning. That means no attached reading list and no requirement that students name or cite authors — I want the marks to go to argument, not recall. To make recitation useless, please build into the question a stipulated case with explicit numbers: assume a global population of wild insects on the order of 10^18 individuals; a per-individual credence *p* in morally relevant experience, left as a variable the student has to reason about rather than a figure they need to remember; and a spray programme whose effect on total insect numbers is known in magnitude but whose effect on net welfare is not known in sign. Then spell out two objections inside the question itself, in neutral terms and without attribution — call them Objection A and Objection B — where A is the claim that the per-individual probability is so small that no plausible population size rescues the conclusion, and B is the claim that insect lives are so brief, and so dominated by death in the egg or early larval stage, that their welfare contribution would be negligible even if they were sentient.

What I want the difficulty to come from is a crux, not a summary. The question should force students to say which premise of the argument is genuinely load-bearing, and it should be structured so that a slogan-level answer misses at least two things: first, that the relevant credence may not be per-individual and independent at all but cluster-level — either a whole taxon has the relevant capacity or none of it does — which changes what the expected-value calculation is even a calculation of, and may change the decision-theoretic picture rather than merely the numbers; second, that establishing that insect welfare matters in the aggregate is a quite different claim from establishing that a particular intervention is required, permissible, or worth funding, especially when the sign of the welfare effect of the intervention is unknown. Please have the question ask explicitly which of the two stipulated objections does more damage to the argument, and require a reasoned verdict rather than a comparison.

For the marking scheme, give five or six must-hit points with the marks each carries, and under each a one-line note on how students typically lose the mark — for instance by treating the size of the insect population as though it settled the moral question by itself, by sliding from expected moral importance to expected net benefit of spraying, or by assuming that independence of the probabilities is harmless. Then add a short paragraph saying what separates a first-class answer from a competent but unspectacular one, in terms of the specific moves each must contain. One request about tone: I do not want a balanced menu of options. The model answer should commit — it should say which of Objection A and Objection B is stronger and why, and take a definite line on whether the cluster-level reading of the probability is fatal to the scale argument or merely a complication for it — and give its reasons for those choices. Afterwards, in a separate note of no more than a few sentences, flag where that committed position rests on contestable assumptions, such as how moral weight aggregates across species or whether expected-value reasoning is the right frame at all under this kind of deep uncertainty, so that I can tell my markers where disagreement with the model answer is legitimate and where it is simply a mistake.
