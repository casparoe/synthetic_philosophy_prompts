Ok, I've talked myself into a pretty specific version of the cyclical view of history and I want someone to try to break it. Not Spengler's seasons-of-culture stuff — I think that's metaphor wearing a lab coat, and the dating is loose enough that nothing could ever falsify it. What I actually hold is closer to Ibn Khaldun, but with the mechanism stated out loud:

group solidarity is produced by shared hardship and spent by the security that success buys. So a ruling group has a finite budget of it. Hardship at the founding → discipline and cohesion → conquest, office, revenue → comfort → by the third and fourth generation nobody remembers the hardship and nobody needs the cohesion, so the group gets displaced by one further out on the periphery that still has it. Three to four generations, call it 100–150 years, then the timer resets with the new group. And the real claim isn't that history repeats. It's that the *unit* repeats: what recurs is elite replacement, and the whole does-the-arc-bend question is a category error, because there's no arc, just the same pump cycling.

So: what would count against this? I want the actual defeaters, sorted.

- Which observations are decisive versus merely awkward? I know the Ottomans and a bunch of Chinese dynasties ran way past four generations, and that Britain and America have gone eight or ten generations without being displaced by a harder group from outside. Is that fatal to the mechanism, or is there a non-ad-hoc way to absorb it, or does it need a seperate excuse for each case?
- What does my view predict that a progressive or teleological view doesn't? If it predicts nothing they don't also predict, that's a serious problem and I'd rather hear it now.
- Where have I already insulated the thing with qualifications just by writing the paragraph above? Point at the specific words doing the insulating work.
- What would a committed defender of this position themselves concede counts against it, as opposed to what the arc-of-history people say? I care about the honest version, not the debate-club version.
- Does it risk collapsing into a rival theory? Is "the unit repeats but history doesn't" really just a theory of elite turnover dressed up as a philosophy of history — and if so, is that a downgrade, or is it the correct, modest way to state the view?
- And the cases cyclical theorists usually skate past: Toynbee's arrested civilizations, plus the long-run trends in administrative capacity, record-keeping, and violence that don't seem to reset at each turnover. Can a cyclical view take those seriously, or does it have to explain them away?

It's fine — encouraged, really — to end by telling me the view is well insulated, and then to say whether that's a genuine problem for a theory of this kind or just what you get when the subject matter doesn't produce repeatable experiments.

Push hard rather than being polite about it. And please answer in American English.
