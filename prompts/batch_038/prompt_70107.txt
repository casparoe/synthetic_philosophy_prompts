I run a four-person cost and schedule estimating unit inside a state infrastructure agency. We do maybe 25 estimates a year, and they go straight into budget submissions the legislature votes on, so when we're low a program gets cut later. Our method right now is basically "find the three most similar past projects and average their overruns," and our internal auditor just wrote us up for it — similar by what criteria, and why three, and who picks them. Fair hit, I can't answer it.

I know this has a name in philosophy (the reference class problem) and a more applied version in forecasting. I don't want an essay about either. I want a written procedure my analysts can run on a single estimate in an afternoon, with no stats background beyond means, percentages, and reading a spreadsheet. Two pages of instructions plus a worksheet they fill in. It has to answer these things, roughly in this order:

1. How to generate candidate reference classes for a given project. I want the procedure to force at least a broad class and a narrow class, and to say how to tell you've gone broad enough / narrow enough rather than just stopping when you like the answer.

2. A test for gerrymandered classes. Someone will always be able to cook up a class where the base rate looks great — "all projects whose sponsor had previously delivered a project in the same county" or whatever. Give me a rule an analyst can apply and that a hostile auditor can check, something better than "use your professional judgment." If the honest answer is that part of it can only be "was the class definition fixed before anyone looked at the outcomes," say that and build the paper trail around it.

3. What to do when relevance and sample size pull opposite ways — the narrow class is mechanistically similar but has n=4, the broad class has n=200 and includes stuff that obviously isn't like this. I want an actual tie-break or a weighting rule with numbers in it, not "balance the two." If you're going to say shrink toward the broad class, tell me by how much and what makes the shrinkage bigger or smaller.

4. Whether the analyst should be thinking in frequencies or in odds. Half my team naturally thinks "what are the chances this comes in over budget," the other half thinks "what price would I take on that bet." Tell me whether your procedure gives a different answer under the two framings, and if it can, which framing the worksheet should use and why — I suspect the betting framing quietly punishes you for picking a class where you'd never actually take the bet, and I want to know if that's a feature.

5. Mark every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedure costume. I'd rather have three flagged steps I know are soft than fifteen steps that look mechanical. And say where the procedure hands the decision up to me because it's about what we're willing to commit to, not about the data.

Then run it end to end on two real-shaped cases, showing the worksheet filled in:

- A 40 km light rail extension in a metro area where we've never built rail. We have built eleven bus rapid transit corridors. Is BRT in the reference class or out, and what does the procedure do when the narrowest defensible class is empty?
- A 300-bed hospital replacement. We've done six comparable hospital builds, all six under design-bid-build; this one is design-build, and the procurement change is the whole point of the project.

For the hospital one, don't soften the result. I'm fairly sure our hospital numbers have been optimistic for a decade and if your procedure says the six-project average is the wrong thing to reach for, I want it to say so in a form I can put in front of my director.

Finally: one paragraph on what the procedure can't fix. If the base rates in our own project database are themselves contaminated by the fact that we've always estimated optimistically, say whether the procedure detects that or just inherits it.
