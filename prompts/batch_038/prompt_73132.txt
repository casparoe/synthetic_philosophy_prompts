So I got into a four-hour argument with my friend Dana on Saturday about when a promise actually binds, and I lost, and now I want to write the whole thing down and get a second opinion. Dana isn't a philosopher, she's a mediator for a living, but she has extremely confident verdicts and she refused to tell me what principle she's running — she just said "you'll figure it out if you list the cases." Fine. Here's what I've got.

(1) You promise a friend you'll drive her to the airport at 6am, and at 5:40 another friend calls, stranded on the highway in the cold. Dana: you can go help the stranded one, but the airport promise isn't cancelled — you still owe the first friend an explanation, the cab fare, and she's entitled to be annoyed with you. She was weirdly insistent about that leftover part. (2) A promise pulled out of someone during a crisis — her example was a guy who got his roommate to co-sign something by refusing to drop the subject while the roommate was in the middle of a family emergency. Dana: never binding, and not because it was a bad deal, but because he never really had the option to refuse. (3) Someone says "sure, I'll be there" about a party as a polite noise, and the host takes it as a promise and buys food for twenty. Dana: no promise, no obligation, though she thinks you have a smaller duty to be careful about the noises you make. (4) You promise to help someone and it turns out the help is a fraud on a third party. Dana: void from the start — she got mad when I said "overridden," she said it's not that you had an obligation and lost it, you never had one. (5) Someone releases you from a promise but the release doesn't reach you until after you've made a big sacrifice to keep it. Dana: you were released the second she said it, whether or not you recieve word of it; the sacrifice was yours and nobody owed it. (6) New Year's resolutions aren't promises. But she also said that when she promises herself out loud that she'll finish a chapter before checking her email, and then checks her email, she's done something wrong — and she wouldn't say who was wronged, she just laughed at me for asking. (7) A promise to her six-year-old nephew binds exactly as much as a promise to an adult, and she thinks treating kids' promises as second-class is how you teach them their word is worthless. But the nephew's promises to her don't bind him at all. She saw no tension there whatsoever.

Here's my case, and it isn't hypothetical, which is part of why I want it sorted out. Three years ago my mom — lucid, and just after her diagnosis — made me and my sister promise that if she ever ended up in a memory-care place and begged to go back to her house, we would not move her back. Her husband died in that house, she was terrified of dying there alone and terrified of being a burden. Now: she doesn't recognize me most days, she's mostly content, she asks about the house sometimes but not insistently, and my brother, who's her legal guardian, says he releases us from the promise and thinks it was a bad promise in the first place. I keep it anyway. I drive out every weekend, it costs me money and my Saturdays, and I'm honestly not sure anymore that I'm doing it for her. Someone at the table said the phrase "precedent autonomy" and I never got an explanation of it.

So: what does Dana say about my case, and why? I want the candidate rules that are consistent with everything in (1)–(7) — she's clearly not running just one obvious theory, or maybe she is and I couldn't tell them apart — laid out with what each one predicts here, including whether any of them split internally on this case. Give me your best guess at her verdict with reasons, tell me which of (1)–(7) is the closest analog and whether it's close enough to actually decide the thing, and if her track record genuinely runs out, say what principle she'd be committing herself to with each possible answer. Then name one further case I could put to her that would tell the candidate rules apart, because I fully intend to keep arguing with her. And separately, since I'm the one driving four hours a week: what should I conclude about whether the promise still binds, and about whether my brother has the standing to release me? Please write the answer in American English.
