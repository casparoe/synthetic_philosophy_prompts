I have no academic training in this, but I have been turning over a comparison puzzle of my own construction for several years, and I have come to think the two camps it divides are largely talking past each other. The case: a dial with a thousand settings, running from an hour of the music I love most at one end to a minute of the worst pain I can imagine at the other, each step trading a sliver of the one for a sliver of the other. Three stipulations. (i) Asked about any two adjacent settings, people say the difference is too small to make either worse than the other. (ii) At every step, the same people will accept a modest payment to move one step toward the pain end, and the payments they would accept all the way along the dial total far less than the lump sum they would refuse for jumping straight from the first setting to the last. (iii) About the two ends, they insist the hour of music is better, and not by a hair.

One camp holds that all-things-considered betterness must be transitive, on pain of exactly the step-by-step exposure that (ii) describes, and therefore that one of my stipulations has to go — usually (i), at the cost of a sharp cutoff somewhere on the dial or an appeal to vagueness. The other camp holds that the dial is coherent and it is transitivity that fails: some adjacent pairs are genuinely neither better nor worse nor exactly equal, and (ii) only looks like exploitation because it assumes that being paid to move proves the move was for the worse.

Please draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration ought to produce. I want numbered claims both camps can sign, about the dial and about comparison generally; a table of the residual disagreements, each entry worded so that the side it describes would accept the wording; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one camp; and for each crux a case, experiment, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. State plainly which of the disagreements you judge to be merely verbal, and whether any part of this could be settled by observation rather than argument.
