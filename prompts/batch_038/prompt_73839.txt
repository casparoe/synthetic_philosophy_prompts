I am the municipal archivist of a mid-sized German city, and every November we hold a short outdoor ceremony in the market square honouring twelve of our former citizens who were executed or died in camps between 1943 and 1945 for sheltering a Jewish family and for printing an underground newspaper in the cellar of a bakery. This year I have been asked to give the address: six or seven minutes, standing, in the cold, with the twelve names read aloud by the choir immediately before me and the mayor speaking immediately after. Treat everything below as the record; these twelve are ours and not a famous case, and nothing here needs checking against the world.

The printed programme, already at the printers and beyond my reach, describes the twelve as "ordinary people, just like you and me." That sentence is the problem I want the speech to solve rather than step around. After twenty years in the archive I think it is backwards. What made the catastrophe possible was not a surplus of monsters but a normality so complete that nothing in it registered as a decision; ordinariness was the mechanism, not its opposite. So telling four hundred people in a square that the honoured twelve were ordinary is either a demotion of them or a quiet indictment of everyone standing there — and the programme presents it as a compliment. I want the address to take up that sentence, in front of the mayor who approved it, and find a third thing it can honestly mean, without contradicting it outright and without sounding like a correction of the programme.

There is material for that third meaning, and I want it used. One of the twelve's granddaughters wrote to the local paper last month objecting that our official version has turned her grandmother into a saint. Her grandmother, she says, was stubborn, bad-tempered, owed money up and down the street, and had been feuding with the butcher next door for eleven years; asked after the war whether she had been brave, she answered that she had not been able to bear the thought of herself afterwards. The paper ran to perhaps three hundred copies over eleven issues before the press was found. The bakery is a mobile phone shop now; the cellar is still there. What interests me in all this is a refusal that does not look like virtue from the outside and does not need to.

That connects to a second thought of Hannah Arendt's, about the few who said no: that their criterion did not seem to be a moral principle held more firmly than other people's, but something closer to an inability to go on living with themselves — conscience there as a by-product of the habit of thinking, of keeping up an argument with oneself, rather than as a special faculty the heroic are equipped with. Use this as substance. Do not name Arendt more than once, do not use the phrase "banality of evil" anywhere in the speech, and do not let the address become a lecture about her. The idea should shape what the speech can and cannot say; it should not be its subject.

The audience is mixed and impatient: a delegation of about thirty sixteen-year-olds from the Gymnasium who have been set the task of writing down what the ceremony means for young people today; the two surviving descendants; the fire brigade and a veterans' association; the council; a rabbi and two pastors; the local paper. Roughly four hundred people, standing, cold, and nobody wants to be there longer than necessary.

Constraints I will not relax:

- Do not indict the rest of the city, living or dead. The archive is unambiguous that most people here were neither heroes nor villains; they were compliant in small, unglamorous ways. I will not spend seven minutes prosecuting the dead, and a speech that does will fail with the families in the front row.
- Do not make the twelve into heroes in the ordinary sense — but do not use that as licence to make them small. They did what almost no one else did, and the address must not explain that away.
- Address the school delegation once, directly, without telling them what to feel or what to do next, and without a single sentence a sixteen-year-old would recognise as being addressed to a child.
- Name at most two of the twelve. All twelve have just been read aloud; a second litany is dead weight.
- Do not end on remembrance, on a pledge to the dead, on "never again," or on any variant. The mayor does that two minutes later and does it better.
- Make it speakable: short sentences, no clause that needs a second hearing in the wind, nothing that depends on the listener having read anything.

Write the address in full, between 850 and 950 words, as the text to be read aloud, with no stage directions or bracketed notes in the body. If you conclude that no honest third reading of the programme's sentence exists, say so in one sentence before the speech and write it instead as an address that quietly declines to cash that sentence — but I would much rather be given the third reading.

End with a single line beginning "Spine:" followed by one sentence stating the claim the whole address turns on — the third thing the programme's sentence can mean. Nothing after that line.
