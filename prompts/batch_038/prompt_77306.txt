Two years ago I left a Ph.D. program in mathematical logic to build machine-learning systems, and this spring I have been asked to moderate the closing Q&A at the retirement colloquium for the professor who once ran my qualifying exams. He has just published a book advancing an anti-mechanist argument from the incompleteness theorems, and since I now work on the very systems his book says can never amount to genuine mathematicians, the invitation is awkward. I would like to ask hard questions without humiliating him, which means questions that engage his argument on its own terms rather than rehearsing the textbook objections.

His version is not the familiar Lucas-Penrose one, and the questions need to reflect that. He concedes that human mathematicians are fallible and very likely inconsistent, so he does not claim that we can see the truth of our own Gödel sentence. His argument instead centers on what he calls unbounded reflective ascent: given any recursively axiomatizable body of principles we can recognize as capturing our own reasoning, we can eventually come to accept that body's consistency, then accept the consistency of the enlarged body, and so on with no fixed bound — whereas any machine's strength is capped by some ordinal. He also insists the conclusion is metaphysical rather than epistemic: not that we cannot know whether we are machines, but that we are not.

Please draft nine or ten questions, in the order I should ask them, each with a single follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one question on each of the following:

- the self-identification premise: what makes some formal system ours, given that anything he can actually write down is an idealization of us, and the ascent cannot begin until that idealization is recognizable as our own reasoning;
- why the reflective step does not simply assume knowledge the second incompleteness theorem puts out of reach, and how he handles the possibility that the system being reflected on is inconsistent, so that its consistency statement is false;
- whether unboundedness is even well defined, since iterating reflection transfinitely requires settling on a system of ordinal notations, and different choices yield different theories;
- the moving-target reply, that any ascent he can describe can be built into a program, including a program handed its own consistency statement as an extra axiom;
- the gap between showing that we cannot verify we are machines and showing that we are not, and what survives of his thesis if he is pushed onto the weaker claim;
- whether the conclusion reaches every individual human being, or only the idealized, collective practice of mathematics, and why expert proof practice is the right evidential base for a claim about minds generally;
- Gödel's own framing of the consequences of incompleteness as a disjunction, and why he does not take the other horn — that there are number-theoretic questions which are absolutely undecidable;
- how a non-mechanical capacity is supposed to sit with everything we know about insight being degraded by anesthesia, lesions, sleep deprivation, and pharmacology, and what such a capacity is doing causally if not that;
- and, as the closer, what a machine would actually have to do to convince him he is wrong — a concrete test rather than a philosophical one.

Write each question so that a mixed room of mathematicians and philosophers, most of whom have not read the book, can follow it without a lecture first: two to four sentences, carrying its own pressure inside the question, and not depending on his having used any particular terminology. No warm-ups about his career and no softballs.
