write me a satirical appellate judgment, about 900 words, from a court in a made-up 18th-century kingdom. Invent everything — no real eruptions, no real cases, no real citations, made-up party names only. The setup: a volcano blanks a province in ash, three cold summers of harvest failure follow, then a fever arrives on a relief ship and a few thousand people die. Descendants of the dead sue the crown's grain commissioners for not building reserves and not lifting the export ban in year two, and the eruption, the cold, and the fever all get dragged in as putative co-defendants, so the court has to apportion blame between human and non-human parties.

The humour should come entirely from the court doing causation doctrine correctly and getting wrecked by it: but-for causation fails against the commissioners because the harvest was collapsing anyway, so the court reaches for a material-contribution test and immediately regrets it; whether the three cold summers are one continuous event or three; whether the fever on the relief ship is a superseding cause or a dependant intervening one the commissioners ought to have foreseen; the act-of-God defence and what it assumes about who counts as an agent; and a full section on whether the mountain can be joined as a party at all, given that the clerk cant serve process on it, it hasn't filed an answer, and there's an unresolved question about whether it acted or merely occurred. Aim the satire at both moves equally — "nature did it, the humans were just along for the ride" and "nature is never an actor, only a backdrop for human choices" — without ever naming the debate, without a character explaining it, and without winking. No footnotes, no afterword about what it all means.

Then a short dissent, 150–200 words, that makes the better argument and still loses: its complaint being that the majority's 61 percent to the mountain is numerically meaningless because causal shares don't sum to a whole, and that once you admit the mountain as an actor you either exculpate everyone or you're chasing liability backwards into the eruption's tectonic prehistory, for want of a nail all the way down. End on a dry costs order. Keep it legible to someone who's never opened a torts casebook, but get the doctrine right enough that someone who has laughs instead of winces.
