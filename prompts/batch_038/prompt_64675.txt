The passage runs as follows:

The forger's paradox begins with a simple observation. If a forgery is genuinely perfect — indistinguishable from the original by any means available, including instruments — then there is no difference in what the two objects offer to experience, and a difference that can make no difference to experience cannot be an aesthetic difference. Yet the moment a forgery is exposed, experience does change, and instantly: we feel foolish, and the canvas we admired an hour ago now looks inflated, derivative, faintly ridiculous. So the history of an object plainly matters to how it looks, which means aesthetic value cannot live in the surface of the object at all. It lives in the causal chain running back to the artist's act.

Once you see this, the controversy over forgery largely dissolves. A forgery is not bad art because it looks bad — that is the crude view — but because it is not an achievement. The original artist solved problems: he worked against the resistance of his medium, his tradition, and his own abilities, and the finished object is the record of that struggle. The forger solved a different and lesser problem, the problem of matching a surface. This is why the wrongness of forgery and the wrongness of plagiarism are the same wrongness: both misassign credit, and both are objectionable for that reason rather than for any effect on the work itself.

The same framework explains a distinction museums handle better than philosophers do. An exacting copy made in the artist's own workshop, or a replica produced by a museum for handling sessions, involves no wrong and no aesthetic loss, because the audience was never deceived and no credit was misassigned. What passes for a single notion of authenticity is in fact two notions, only one of which carries aesthetic weight. There is nominal authenticity — whether the object before you is the one the artist made, a matter of provenance, documentation, and expert eyes. And there is expressive authenticity — whether the work is a genuine performance rather than mimicry, pandering, or pastiche, a matter of the work's own character. Forgeries fail the second test and not the first: nobody claims a van Meegeren is a Vermeer once it has been exposed, but the work remains something made in bad faith, and that bad faith is visible on the canvas. Which is why a good forgery is always, in the end, a bad painting.
