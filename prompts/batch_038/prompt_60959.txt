I'm putting together a session for a reading group doing Middlemarch this autumn — mostly non-academics, two of them pretty devout, so I want to handle this carefully — and my angle is George Eliot's own religious views. My working thesis, which I want stress-tested rather than applauded, is that Middlemarch is basically the Feuerbach book she translated in the 1850s turned into a novel: God as the projected essence of the species, theology as covert anthropology, and the novel's job being to show character by character what happens when the projection starts to come loose. So I'd like a proper reading of the book through Feuerbach's projection theory, taken seriously as a philosophical claim and not just waved around as a loose metaphor about "seeing through" religion.

Where does the lens actually bite? I'm thinking Bulstrode, whose private religion tracks his own interests so exactly that he looks like a textbook case of projection, and who then gets broken not by losing his belief but by the outside world finding out about him; Casaubon and the Key to all Mythologies, a man hunting the common origin of all the world's myths and finding nothing behind them; Farebrother, who seems to hold the office of clergyman with none of the metaphysics, and is treated warmly for it; Lydgate as a kind of secular priest of science, complete with a vocation and a fall; and Dorothea, who's the hard case, since her religious temperament gets enormous sympathy even tho the novel hands her no evidence for any of her doctrines. Which of these genuinely dramatizes the Feuerbachian claim, and where does the novel resist it? I have a nagging feeling that the window scene — the one where she's wrecked after the codicil and the sight of ordinary people going about their business outside drags her out of herself — plus the closing meditation about hidden lives and unvisited tombs are doing something projection theory can't cash out. Tell me my thesis is too neat if it is.

And please correct the history where I've got it wrong. I know the translation predates the novel by nearly twenty years, I know she wrote that piece on Dr Cumming for the Westminster Review in the 1850s, and that she had the row with her father about churchgoing back in 1842 — but I honestly don't know whether the Eliot of 1871 was still thinking in Feuerbach's terms at all, or whether she'd moved somewhere else by then, and I want the reading to say where the novel sits relative to her earlier essays and translations instead of collapsing the whole arc into one fixed position.
