I sit on the operations committee of a metropolitan transit authority. Ahead of a stadium event Saturday night, our forecasting unit predicts a dangerous crowd crush on the north station concourse at 10:40 p.m. and proposes pushing an in-app advisory that steers riders to two alternative stations. The unit's own simulations, however, show that a push of that kind relocates the crush to one of the alternatives at comparable severity, and that partial compliance — riders who reroute but do not reschedule — degrades both sites at once. Backtests also indicate that riders treat our advisories as reliable news about what will happen rather than as attempts to influence them, which is precisely why the unit's forecasts have been accurate until now. One member wants to suppress the advisory on the ground that a warning which moves the danger instead of preventing it is worse than silence; another wants to publish an all-clear for the north station that we know to be false, in order to keep the crowd dispersed. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: a definite recommendation with a timeline and assigned actions, an explicit account of the standard by which we should judge the advisory — its accuracy at the moment of issue or the outcome it produces — and a serious engagement with the suppression argument rather than a dismissal of it. Close with a standing rule the committee could apply the next time a forecast has this structure.
