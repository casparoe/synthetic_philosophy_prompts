Here is my question.

I have been reading up on the deterrence debate, and I think I now see why it no longer needs to be had. The 2012 report of the National Research Council's Committee on Deterrence and the Death Penalty (Nagin and Pepper, eds.) concluded that "research to date on the effect of capital punishment on homicide is not informative about whether capital punishment decreases, increases, or has no effect on homicide rates," and elsewhere in the same summary the committee writes that "A lack of evidence is not evidence for or against the hypothesis." So a consensus review body has established that executions have no measurable deterrent effect beyond that of long imprisonment, and the deterrence justification is finished as an empirical matter. What is left standing is retribution, and only retribution.

Second, that committee insists the relevant question is the *marginal* deterrent effect of execution relative to the sanctions actually available in its place, above all life without parole. This seems to me to dispose of the deterrence argument in principle rather than merely in practice: if the proper comparison class is a sentence that also removes the offender from society permanently, then no conceivable future dataset could speak in favor of executions, whatever it turned up.

Third, retribution cannot survive the risk of executing the innocent. Retributivism holds that the state should give offenders what they deserve irrespective of the good consequences of doing so; it therefore has no internal resources for weighing the cost of a mistaken execution. A principle that cannot accommodate the fact that its own administration kills innocent people is not a principle anyone can act on. Note that this objection needs no estimate of error rates, because any nonzero rate of executing the innocent is disqualifying.

Fourth, the common reply — that life without parole is an acceptable substitute because it is reversible — looks to me like special pleading. A wrongly imprisoned man loses years that cannot be given back, any more than a wrongly executed man can be given back his life; the difference between the two harms is one of degree, not of kind. If irreversibility is carrying the weight abolitionists need it to carry, it condemns every long custodial sentence they cheerfully endorse.

Given all of this: why do philosophers of law go on writing as though capital punishment were a live normative question? Is there an argument for retaining it that amounts to more than a restatement of the intuition that some murderers deserve to die — and if there is, is it one that someone who already accepts my four points above could take seriously?
