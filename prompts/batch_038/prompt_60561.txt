I'm a backend engineer on a persistent-world game, and for six years part of my job has been adjudicating player disputes. I've accumulated a set of verdicts about what counts as real that I cannot unify, and I have no philosophy training, so please treat my vocabulary as engineering slang rather than as terms of art.

My verdicts:

1. An admin deletes a build someone spent four hundred hours on during a botched server migration. Real harm, roughly on par with demolishing that person's garden shed.
2. A player cons another out of a rare item using a trade-window trick. Real harm. We banned him and I felt good about it.
3. A duping bug makes that same rare item common. The original owners are harmed, in my view, even though nothing was removed from anyone and no one was touched.
4. A player is subjected to sustained abuse in voice chat by someone using a distorted voice and no slur I can put in a report. I called this not our problem, just a game. I still lean that way, and I cannot see what distinguishes it from case 2.
5. A content patch removes an NPC a player has traveled with for three years. I said that harmed no one, then read her forum post and reversed halfway. I do not know which half I kept.
6. One player assaults another's avatar in VR, haptics disabled, no audio. Real harm, I think, though I could not say what the harm attaches to beyond the victim's own distress.
7. In a single-player game, a player burns down a village of NPCs that no other person will ever observe. Not a harm. I am confident here, which strikes me as strange given 5 and 6.
8. An item sold for real money becomes something I would protect; the identical item, freely given to a friend, does not feel like property at all.

My tentative principle is dependency-weighted: a virtual thing is real in proportion to how many people's plans, promises, and record-keeping route through it, so harm tracks broken dependencies rather than broken matter. Test it against all eight. Tell me which it delivers, which it cannot accommodate, and what clause each failure forces. Then state the repaired principle precisely enough that I could hand it to a moderation team as a decision procedure, and give me its verdict on three cases I have not ruled on: shutting the entire game down on ninety days' notice with twelve years of standing builds in it; a training environment reset millions of times a day for an RL agent that reliably avoids certain states; and a deceased player's account kept alive by a model fine-tuned on his chat logs, which his widow logs into most nights. If the principle has to be split in two, one for objects and one for experiences, say so plainly instead of forcing a single formula, and tell me which of my eight verdicts each half overturns.
