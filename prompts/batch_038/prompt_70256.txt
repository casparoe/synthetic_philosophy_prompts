So, bit of an odd request. I cover science and research integrity for a general-audience outlet, and I've been invited to give the main talk at a one-day workshop a journalism school is putting on in about three weeks, on trust and evidence in news. Its not a philosophy department affair, it's mostly practitioners, but they've asked me to actually argue a position rather than tell war stories, so I've decided to defend something I've been chewing on for a couple of years: that the two-source rule — the newsroom norm that you don't publish a serious allegation about a person or institution unless two independent sources back it — isn't a safeguard against error so much as a filter that skews which *true* claims survive, and that newsrooms hold it while running a completely different and much more credulous epistemology whenever the source is a published paper. My argument runs in three moves: (1) corroboration-as-counting rewards testimony that's cheap to duplicate — officials, spokespeople, anyone working from the same brief or sharing an institutional interest — and punishes testimony that's expensive to duplicate, which is usually one insider sitting on documents; (2) so the rule doesn't just add reliability at the margin, it changes the composition of what gets through, and in a predictable direction; (3) meanwhile a single underpowered study in a glossy journal gets written up as news with no corroboration at all, which makes the newsroom reductionist about human witnesses and near-credulous about print. The punchline I want is that the rule is doing legal and ritual work that people have mistaken for epistemic work.

Here's who'll be in the room, because I don't want generic objections. Roughly forty people. The majority are ex-reporters and editors now teaching, and they hold the two-source rule in a bone-deep way — for them it's Watergate, it's not getting sued into the ground, and it's protecting a source who could be identified by elimination if we ran the story alone. One actual media lawyer is on the program. Two or three academic philosophers and social epistemologists were added late; they'll know the Hume-Reid reductionism debate and the testimonial-injustice stuff cold and will absolutely use that vocabulary, and I suspect one of them thinks my framing is philosophically naive. And there are maybe five quantitative communication researchers who only care about experiments and corrections data and will want to know how I'd operationalize any of this.

What I want from you is the Q&A prep, not a defense of me. Give me the eight-ish objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, each phrased the way that person would phrase it — the editor's version and the epistemologist's version of the same worry are different sentences, and I want both registers. For each, a two-minute reply I could actually say out loud standing up, plus a note on where the reply is soft or where I'd be bullshitting a bit. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to take the wind out of the biggest one, and which single reply has to land for the whole thing not to collapse. Include the question I'm dreading; my guess is it's somebody pointing out that my best anecdote is a story that got published anyway by someone else, i.e. the system worked, just slowly. The anecdote, for what it's worth: two years ago I had a postdoc with a spreadsheet showing duplicated images across figures in a high-profile paper, she wouldn't let me identify her lab and I couldn't get a second source, my editor killed it, and seven months later the paper came down after an anonymous blogger made the same argument publicly using the same images. I'll probably tell that story on stage. Please don't send me reading — no library access and about as much prep time as you'd expect, so no bibliographies, no citations, no named studies, just the arguments.
