My friend Miriam chairs the review panel of a small foundation whose entire remit is unusually narrow: it funds only the removal of multipolar traps, not the correction of ordinary bad incentives, so before her panel can award anything it has to decide whether the competitive dynamic described in an application genuinely has the structure of a trap. I helped draft one of the applications now sitting in front of her, which makes me a poor neutral, and last night we argued about it for something like three hours on her balcony without her committing to a vote. She would only say that the case turns on whether you count the ships or the sea, which I found either profound or evasive depending on the hour. What I want from you is a reconstruction of the rule she is actually running, and a prediction. Her record, as best I can recall it from her letters and from a training memo she circulates to new panel members, runs roughly as follows. An application to convene a standing allocation compact among the astronomy consortia that compete to build ever-larger ground-based telescopes: declined, on the grounds that the loser of that race still ends up with an instrument, that the engineering carries into the next generation, and that the resulting telescopes are shared and oversubscribed rather than hoarded, so nobody is left worse off and there is nothing to escape. A proposal for a multistate compact ending the competition for a single large manufacturer by way of property-tax abatements: funded briskly, because the abatements relocate a plant that would have been built somewhere anyway, the winner's gain is the losers' loss, and the money spent is simply gone. The supersonic transport race of the mid-century, treated in her memo as a worked example: not a trap, however ruinous, because the American program was ended by a legislative vote and the British tried to leave the Anglo-French treaty almost immediately and were held only by a penalty clause; a competition that one player or one electorate can terminate is, in her phrase, a mistake rather than a trap. A compact among Olympic candidate cities to stop the bidding war: funded — and here I pressed her, because cities demonstrably do withdraw, as when voters in a German port city killed a bid at a referendum, a newly elected mayor in Rome killed hers, and a Nordic candidate dropped out once its government refused the guarantees. Her answer was that withdrawing is not exiting, because the slot is never empty and another city fills it within the year: the unit that cannot leave is not the city but the class of cities. A registry for simultaneous release of results in a fast-moving experimental field, proposed to end the scooping race: declined, because the finding gets published either way and the race reallocates credit among competitors without costing the public anything. And the Cold War nuclear buildup: funded as the paradigm case, with a complaint that applicants invoke it far too freely. When we argued, we kept reaching for candidate criteria — whether the payoffs are positional or real, whether the race yields anything of value to people who are not competing, whether a single participant can stop without being destroyed, whether the players consent knowingly, whether some shared stock is being consumed — and each seemed to fit her record until we hit a case where it did not.

The application I helped write describes the competition now opening among flag states to offer the most permissive approval pathway for large uncrewed ocean-going cargo vessels. A handful of open registries already carry most of the world's tonnage; they collect real revenue and real maritime-services employment from it, and whichever registry licenses remote-operated and then fully autonomous ships at scale first stands to capture a share of a genuinely new market rather than merely to outrank a rival. Our claim is that no flag state can insist on a slow, stringently reviewed transition without watching tonnage migrate to a competing registry within a season or two; that the technology carries large benefits for people who are nowhere in the competition, since uncrewed ships would eliminate a substantial share of seafarer deaths and much of the crew-accommodation and hotel cost that drives freight rates; and that what is being spent down in the process is not any one state's budget but a shared and non-material stock — the evidentiary basis for believing autonomy at scale is safe, and the assumption, baked into the law of the sea, that a flag state effectively supervises what flies its flag. The money is for convening a mutual-recognition compact: a floor that member registries enforce jointly, so that tightening does not punish whoever does it first.

So: predict her verdict on that application. I care considerably more about the reconstruction than about the coin-flip. Set out the candidate rules her six verdicts are jointly consistent with, note where each strains, and show that at least two of them come apart on my proposal — saying explicitly what each predicts and why. Then commit to a best guess and defend it against the alternative, identify which of her past cases is the closest analog to this one and precisely where that analogy breaks down, and tell me whether there is a version of the same proposal — reframed, rescoped, or with a different intervention attached — that her own record would commit her to funding rather than declining. After that, do the part I most want: design the next application. Describe, concretely enough that it could be written, a hypothetical case on which her candidate rules split cleanly with the confounds that make mine hard stripped away, so that a single vote on it would settle which rule she is running. Finally, say what would change your mind — which of her verdicts, read differently, would flip your prediction, which of the candidate rules you are most likely to have mischaracterized, and where in this reconstruction you think the reasoning is weakest. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter rather than a brief: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized labels, no tables.
