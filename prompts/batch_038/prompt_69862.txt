My working hypothesis about Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* is that the novel is engineered to make Dennett's design stance systematically unavailable, and that this is philosophically load-bearing rather than merely atmospheric. Victor never discloses how the animation was performed — he refuses Walton explicitly, and the reader is given no mechanism to inspect — so every character who wants to anticipate the creature's behaviour has to attribute beliefs and desires to it. On Dennett's account, such an attribution is warranted when there is a real pattern in the behaviour, one that is capturable at the intentional level and not (practically) at any lower level. I want a reading of the novel that tests whether it vindicates that account or exhibits something the account cannot accommodate. I am preparing this as a seminar reading, and I would rather have the reading argued than have the novel summarised.

Three moments I would like treated with particular care. First, the blind De Lacey: he is the one character who cannot use surface information about the creature, and he alone receives him without recoiling; the sighted household, which has full access to how the creature looks, treats him as a threat and drives him off. Read this as the novel's own controlled experiment in stance selection — the character with the least information at the physical and design levels gets the intentional attribution right — and say whether the scene licenses a general claim or is doing something narrower.

Second, the creature's autobiography and the etiological account he offers of his own character: misery, he insists, has made him malicious, and benevolence will return if his conditions change. That is a design-stance explanation of a system offered by the system itself, and one he wants Victor to accept as exculpatory. Does accepting it count as finding a real pattern, or does it threaten to dissolve the intentional stance altogether, since a being whose states are fully explained by causal history is no longer someone to negotiate with? Please also take seriously that this autobiography is a text addressed to a listener with an obvious interest in the outcome.

Third, Walton. He hears the creature's account over Victor's body, is partly persuaded, and then refuses to go on being persuaded and orders him away. Nothing about the creature's predictive reliability has changed at that point. Is this a counterexample to the view that stances are adopted and abandoned on grounds of predictive utility, or is Walton's reversal itself a change in what he predicts?

You should also engage the objection I expect, which I find harder to dismiss than I would like: that the withholding of the design stance is gothic convention and plot convenience, that Victor's refusal is about hubris and forbidden knowledge rather than epistemology, that the animation is gestured at in vitalist and galvanic terms a materialist reading would treat as the real explanatory level, and that the intentional stance "succeeding" for the creature is simply the novel making him sympathetic — a rhetorical effect, not evidence about real patterns. Where this objection wins, say so plainly. Relatedly, consider Victor's failed prediction on the wedding night, where he attributes the right form of intention to the creature and the wrong object: whether that misfires because the stance was the wrong tool or because Victor's model of the agent's values was bad.

No quotations or citations are needed — paraphrase the scenes and describe the arguments. Please correct me where I have misremembered the novel, since I am working from memory. Finally, mark your confidence at each of the three main claims, and single out the point in the whole reading you are least sure of: the place where you would most expect a careful reader of Dennett, or of Shelley, to tell you that you have got it wrong.
