In our Wittgenstein seminar we spent two sessions on Kripke's rule-following argument, and afterwards two classmates — I'll call them Daniel and Ruth — argued for close to an hour about whether that argument has any bearing on large language models. Neither conceded anything, and since our professor has asked us to bring the dispute back to the group resolved, I've reconstructed the exchange as accurately as I can and would like an adjudication.

Daniel opened with a case. A model is trained on finitely many demonstrations of following rules — sorting lists, conjugating verbs, extending a formula. Kripke's argument shows that no finite record fixes which rule was being followed, since the record is equally consistent with a deviant variant such as: sort ascending, except reverse any list whose length is prime. So, Daniel said, the familiar objection that a model merely extrapolates while a person genuinely grasps the rule has no content — the person's past is just as finite and just as compatible with deviant variants.

Ruth granted the premise and turned it around. The argument is universal: if it shows the model means nothing by its outputs, it shows the same about us. An argument with that scope cannot underwrite a distinction between minds and machines; it can only underwrite a skepticism that nobody in the room, Daniel included, is prepared to endorse. So Daniel has proved too much, and therefore nothing.

Daniel replied that Kripke does not leave us in skepticism but offers a skeptical solution: stop asking what fact constitutes meaning and ask instead when a claim about meaning is legitimately assertible, where the answer is fixed by a community's practice of correcting one another. That, he said, is where the asymmetry re-enters. A model is not held to the community's corrections in the right way, so no assertibility conditions attach to its outputs, and it therefore gets no benefit from the solution.

Ruth said the model is held to correction constantly — users flag bad answers, evaluators score them, and the corrections shape the next version — so its conformity to public practice is monitored far more closely than any human's ever will be. On her diagnosis, Daniel was smuggling in an extra requirement that the individual be a member of the community in some further sense, and that unstated requirement, not the skeptical solution, was doing all the work in his conclusion.

Daniel said there is a further sense and it is not smuggled: being correctable presupposes that it matters to the subject whether it was right, since a person who is corrected can be shown to have failed at something they were attempting. The model has no stake in its own conformity, so the practice of correction gains no purchase on it; corrections merely reconfigure it.

Ruth's last move was that this concedes her point: if stakes are what's missing, Daniel's conclusion should be that the model is not an agent, not that it fails to follow rules. He has changed the subject.

Please go through this move by move and say who is right at each stage — which moves succeed, which overreach, and which merely restate the other side's position in different vocabulary. Two questions I'd especially like answered: does the universality of the skeptical argument really neutralize it, as Ruth claims, or can a universal argument still license a distinction between cases? And is Daniel's appeal to the subject's stake a legitimate completion of the skeptical solution, or Ruth right that it substitutes a different question for the one about rule-following? Finally, tell me what both of them are leaving out — in particular, whether some standing line of response to Kripke's argument (a reply identifying meaning with dispositions and their refinements, or a reply that refuses the argument's question rather than answering it) would have broken the deadlock, and how it would have interacted with each of the six moves.
