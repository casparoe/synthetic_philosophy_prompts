Both camps in the long-running dispute over how much the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem should matter for actual electoral design accept the theorem itself: any resolute, deterministic rule that is onto, admits three or more alternatives, and is not a dictatorship permits some voter to benefit by casting an insincere ballot. What they cannot agree on is what follows from this. The first camp treats strategy-proofness as a condition of legitimacy — a rule that pays a reward for misrepresentation makes outcomes turn on gaming skill and information rather than on preferences — and reads the theorem as condemning plurality, runoff, and ranked-choice systems alike, with the only escapes lying in domain restrictions such as single-peakedness, transfers in the quasilinear setting, randomization, or weakened notions like obvious strategy-proofness. The second camp replies that the theorem quantifies over manipulators who know everyone else's ballot and compute without bound; that worst-case computational hardness plus the observed rarity of decisive strategic voting in large elections reduces it to a curiosity; and that rules should instead be ranked by proportionality, monotonicity, simplicity, and auditability.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is largely an equivocation: 'manipulable' as a property of a function versus as a frequency-weighted property of a practice, with a normative disagreement about how much idealization institutional design may tolerate underneath it. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive formal or empirical question that the two camps keep talking past? Distinguish the strands, say which of them a theorem could settle, which a dataset could, and which neither could — and tell me what would count as progress.
