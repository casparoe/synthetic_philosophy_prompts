You are a philosopher of probability who has worked both on the foundations of statistical explanation and on the contemporary literature on calibrated prediction. Please think carefully and work through the sub-questions before producing any headline figure; where it helps, begin by brainstorming what would have to be true for the answer to come out affirmative, and what would have to be true for it to come out negative.

I am drafting a dissertation chapter, and my advisor and I disagree about how much is genuinely at stake in the reference class problem. To keep the disagreement concrete, here is the case we keep returning to. A sixty-two-year-old patient is offered treatment T for condition C. In the pooled data T looks strongly protective; among women with C — who are ninety percent of the sample — it is protective; among men it looks neutral; among the fourteen recorded patients carrying the rare marker M alongside C it looks harmful; and among patients enrolled under the older trial protocol, which is the one she was actually treated under, its effect is unmeasurable at the available sample size. Every one of these classifications is true of her, the data are what they are, and there is no obvious further fact about her that selects among them. The textbook response is Salmon's: some of these classifications are screened off by others, and only a maximally homogeneous class yields the real probability. My worry is that this either pushes the choice up a level — which partitions even count as candidates for homogeneity? — or it quietly imports a judgment about what we happen to care about.

Here is the proposition I want a number on. Call it R: there exists a general procedure, statable without reference to anyone's interests, context of assessment, or choice of vocabulary, which takes as input a single case together with a body of statistical information and outputs the unique class relative to which that case's objective probability is defined — and which does so for all cases in which such a probability exists at all, not merely for tidy textbook ones. Give me your credence that R is true. Then decompose it, assigning a rough probability and two or three sentences of justification to each of the following, telling me how you aggregate them and whether you are treating them as independent: (i) that single cases have objective probabilities of the sort R is meant to locate, rather than probability attaching to events-under-a-description or to degrees of belief; (ii) that homogeneity or screening-off conditions can be closed off non-pragmatically, without either an idealized infinite limit or a prior restriction on the admissible predicates; (iii) that the explanation-theoretic version of the problem, the version arising inside the frequency interpretation of probability, and the version arising in the forensic and clinical use of base rates are genuinely one problem rather than three cousins, so that a single result could resolve them together; (iv) that the technical work on calibrated prediction under partition-sensitivity — group-wise calibration audits, and the incompatibility of the natural accuracy criteria once groups are held fixed — constitutes progress toward R rather than a relocation of the choice into which groups one audits; and (v) that a proposed solution would in fact be recognized as a solution by working philosophers of probability within, say, thirty years, rather than being reabsorbed as one more position in the landscape. Flag any term where your number is close to a guess, identify which term the headline figure is most sensitive to, and say what single argument or result would move you furthest.
