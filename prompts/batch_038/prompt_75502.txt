I'm a third-year in philosophy of mind and our reading group just got through the Chase/Sanborn stretch of Dennett's "Quining Qualia" — the bit with the two coffee tasters, the nitpicker, and the inverted spectrum that's supposed to be undetectable. There's a postdoc in the group, call her Ines, who's the resident qualia skeptic and who has been handing down verdicts on every variant we invent. I'm writing my quals prospectus on exactly this terrain and I want to know what rule she's actually running, because she wont say — she just reacts to cases and moves on.

Her verdicts so far, more or less in her words:

1. Guy wakes up and everything is chromatically inverted, notices right away, misnames colors for a few days, then re-learns. — "Boring. That's a disruption of his dispositions. No mystery, no qualia."
2. The "real" inverted spectrum: inversion happens overnight and nothing downstream changes at all — same reports, same matching, same sorting, and no test on him or anyone else could ever reveal it. — "I don't think that's a coherent story. And if you insist it is, I say there's no fact of the matter about which way it looks to him."
3. Coffee taster A, twenty years on the job, coffee now tastes flat and worse to him, and testing confirms his discrimination between roasts has measurably degraded. — "Real change. And boringly real, its exactly the kind of change a lab can find."
4. Coffee taster B, coffee tastes different to him now, but his discrimination, matching, sorting and unprompted reports are identical, and he agrees no test could tell the difference. — "Then there's nothing there. I don't mean I can't know it. I mean there's nothing to know."
5. The nitpicker who swears two physically identical chips look faintly different to him but sits at chance on every forced-choice we can build and can't be trained up. — "He's reporting his own urge to say something, not a property of his experience."
6. A patient who after an injury can no longer tell you which of two blues is bluer, but still points to the right one every time when you say "don't think, just point." — "That's still a genuine difference in experience. The information's in there, it just isn't feeding the report."

Here's the case I'm bringing next week and I need a prediction on it. A woman's color experience has drifted slowly warmer over fifteen years — a few nm of effective shift. She notices nothing at any point, because her memory of how things used to look drifted right along with her vision, so there's no internal comparison available to her. All her reports, matching, sorting and verbal discriminations are unchanged; she'd fail every test you could give her awake and attending. But a lab runs her through masked forced-choice — chips flashed briefly, then a two-alternative guess — and she lands around 68% correct on old-standard vs. current-standard pairs, well above chance, and her pupillary and orienting responses differ between them too. She insists she's guessing: no hunch, no inclination, nothing it's like to pick one. So the difference is detectable, just not by her, and not by anything she'd count as her own doing.

What I want from you:

- Your best guess at her verdict, stated flatly — real difference in what it's like for her / no such difference / no fact of the matter. Pick one.
- The candidate rules that fit 1–6 equally well. I've got at least three in mind: something like "a property is part of experience only if the subject can form a judgment about it on the basis of having it"; something like "real iff some test, run on anybody, could reveal it"; and something like "experience just is the person-level pattern of reactive dispositions, so sub-personal detection is neuroscience and not phenomenology." Tell me which of those survive all six — and note that 6 is doing a lot of work here, since it seems to kill the report-only version of the first rule.
- Which rule you think she's on, and why, with an actual confidence level attached.
- The single further case that would most cleanly separate the survivors. I was torn between giving the woman an external anchor (her own fifteen-year-old calibrated photograph, so she can finally form the judgment "it looks different from that") and stripping the physiology out entirely (the drift shows up on imaging while she's sedated and asleep, with no person-level trace whatsoever). Say which of those does more work, or give me a better one.
- And if you think she isn't entitled to the verdict you predict, say so — which of 1–6 she's leaning on that she hasn't earned.

Please don't hand me a menu of readings and leave me to choose. Commit to one and argue for it.
