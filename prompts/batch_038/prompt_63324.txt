I would like a disciplined case analysis of Sweden's 1967 switch from left-hand to right-hand driving, treated as a test case for David Lewis's account of convention in Convention: A Philosophical Study (1969). Two constraints on the deliverable. First, work from the facts I supply below plus your own understanding of Lewis; I want no reading list, no catalogue of the secondary literature, and no retelling of the history beyond what is needed to make the philosophical points bite. Second, where Lewis's framework has nothing to say, say so explicitly rather than recruiting machinery that is not there.

The facts I will assume, so you need not worry about reconstructing them. Sweden drove on the left until 05:00 on Sunday 3 September 1967. A non-binding referendum in 1955 returned roughly 83 per cent in favour of keeping left-hand traffic; the Riksdag nevertheless approved the change in May 1963, with the switch scheduled for 1967 and overseen by a dedicated state commission that ran a four-year public education campaign. The arguments for change were that all of Sweden's neighbours drove on the right, including Norway and Finland, with which Sweden shared land borders and some five million vehicle crossings a year, and that more than 90 per cent of Swedish cars were already left-hand drive, which produced head-on collisions on narrow two-lane roads and made overtaking and lane changes hazardous. Car ownership roughly tripled over the period, from about half a million to a million and a half vehicles, and was projected to reach 2.8 million by 1975. On the night of the switch, non-essential traffic was banned from 01:00 to 06:00; vehicles still on the road stopped at 04:50, moved across, and stopped again before proceeding at 05:00. Around 350,000 road signs were replaced or removed, intersections were pre-fitted with a parallel set of signals and markings hidden under black plastic and tape, headlamps were exchanged for right-traffic units, over a thousand new buses were bought with right-hand doors and some eight thousand old ones retrofitted with doors on both sides. Reported accidents fell temporarily on the day and the Monday after, and motor insurance claims dropped by about 40 per cent, but claims returned to normal within roughly six weeks and by 1969 accident rates were back at pre-change levels. Railways and Stockholm's metro did not switch and have continued to run on the left.

Lewis's analysis, as I read it, and please correct me where I have it wrong: a regularity R in the behaviour of a population P in a recurrent situation S is a convention if it is true, and common knowledge in P, that almost everyone conforms to R; that almost everyone expects almost everyone else to conform; that almost everyone has broadly the same preferences over combinations of actions; that almost everyone prefers that any given individual conform to R, conditional on almost everyone conforming to R; and that almost everyone would likewise prefer conformity to R conditional on almost everyone conforming to some possible alternative regularity R′; where S is a coordination problem and R is arbitrary in the sense that R′ would serve nearly as well.

With that in hand, I want the following.

1. Map the coordination problem as it actually stood in mid-1960s Sweden. Who are the players, what are the options, and where do the payoffs come from? Then explain what Lewis's conditions predict about the 1955 referendum result. My own suspicion is that an overwhelming vote to preserve the status quo is exactly what the conditional-preference clauses entail even when nearly everyone would prefer the alternative equilibrium, because no voter can vote herself into it. Assess whether that is right, and whether it means the referendum measured something other than preference over equilibria.

2. Assess the arbitrariness condition. By 1967 the alternative regularity no longer looked like an equally good option: the vehicle fleet, the border traffic, and the whole of continental Europe had already tilted the payoffs. Does that mean Sweden's left-hand rule had stopped being a convention in Lewis's sense some time before the decree, so that 1967 was not a switch between rival conventions but the belated removal of a lag between material incentives and behaviour? Or can the arbitrariness clause be read weakly enough to survive that objection?

3. Pressure-test a refinement I am toying with. Suppose we distinguish a convention proper, where conformity is sustained by mutual expectation alone and the cost of a single individual deviating is confined to the coordination loss, from what I would call a settled practice, where the same expectation-structure is welded to a capital substrate: steering wheels, headlamps, door placement, signage, junction geometry, and driver muscle memory, all of which make individual deviation physically expensive and any collective revision a large one-off investment. On this view Sweden's driving rule in 1955 was a settled practice rather than a convention, and what the state supplied in 1963 to 1967 was not a change of heart among agents but a dated, simultaneous conversion of the substrate plus a common-knowledge cutover moment. Does this distinction do explanatory work that Lewis's own resources of salience, precedent, and common knowledge cannot do, or is the capital substrate just a payoff-shifter that drops straight into his coordination problem, making the refinement redundant?

4. Deal with the statute. After 1967, is driving on the right in Sweden still a convention on Lewis's account, given that it is now also legally required and penalised? Does the sanction displace the convention, merely reinforce it, or leave two independent supports in place? Propose a discriminating test, such as asking what would happen if the law were repealed tomorrow, and say whether that test is one Lewis's theory is entitled to run.

5. Say what the episode shows about a theory that is static and equilibrium-shaped being applied to a transition. Lewis's conditions describe a regularity already in force; the interesting question is whether the account has any resources for the interval in which no equilibrium holds, and for the machinery Sweden used to make that interval short and orderly: the traffic ban window, the staged 04:50 and 05:00 stops, the four-year campaign, the wrapped signals. Treat the accident and insurance data in this light: is the temporary drop, and its disappearance within six weeks and the return to baseline by 1969, evidence about expectations and perceived risk, or is it beside the philosophical point?

6. Use the railways as a within-country control. Roads flipped by decree while rail kept to the left, in a system where the regularity was also arbitrary. Explain the divergence on Lewisian grounds and say whether it confirms the analysis or embarrasses it.

7. Close with a verdict in both directions: what the case confirms, what it strains, and what it leaves untouched in Lewis's account; and then say how far the resulting analysis generalises, for instance to other state-engineered revisions of long-standing regularities such as the decimalisation of British coinage in 1971, where the same combination of a dated cutover, an education campaign, and a converted substrate was deployed. Identify the feature that predicts success in such cases, if there is one, and be candid if Lewis's framework simply does not address the question.
