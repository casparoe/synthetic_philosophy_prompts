QUESTION 3 (25 points). The Meridian Study measured a composite index of cognitive flexibility (VCFI) in adults aged 25–40. In the national sample it reports: MZ twins reared together, r = 0.68 (n = 2,400 pairs); DZ twins reared together, r = 0.375 (n = 1,900 pairs); MZ twins reared apart, r = 0.58 (n = 310 pairs). Applying Falconer's formula to the reared-together pairs yields h² = 0.61 (95% CI 0.55–0.67). A separately funded subsample, the Northgate cohort (n = 1,100), all attended the same well-resourced early-childhood program; in that subsample the estimated h² is 0.24, total VCFI variance is roughly 40% smaller than in the national sample, and the distribution is compressed against the upper end of the scale. Genome-wide association data account for 0.19 of national-sample variance. The Northgate cohort's mean VCFI is 0.8 SD above that of the rest of the national sample. Explain what these figures do and do not license you to conclude. In particular: interpret the difference between the national and Northgate estimates; comment on the relationship between 0.61 and 0.19; and state what, if anything, follows about the 0.8 SD mean difference.

STUDENT ANSWER. "Heritability is a property of a population, not of a person: it is the proportion of phenotypic variance in a given population, over a given range of environments, that is attributable to genetic variance. It therefore says nothing about how much of any individual's score comes from their genes, and it is compatible with the trait being highly responsive to intervention — which is exactly what Northgate shows, since the same gene pool in a different environment produced a different mean.

The contrast between the two estimates is the most informative part of the data. That h² falls from 0.61 to 0.24 when the environment is made uniformly good tells us that genetic influence on VCFI really is weaker in Northgate: once everyone is raised well, what is left to differentiate people is no longer their genotypes but the residual accidents of their experience. So the trait is genuinely more environmental there, in the only sense that carries causal weight, and this is why a heritability estimate quoted without its environment is meaningless.

The variance components are exhaustive by construction — 0.61 genetic, 0.39 environmental — and a partition that clean is evidence that gene–environment interaction contributes little in the national sample; substantial interaction would have to show up somewhere, and there is nowhere for it to go. The gap between the twin-based 0.61 and the 0.19 from identified variants is most simply explained by the equal-environments assumption failing, since MZ twins are treated more alike than DZ twins, which inflates r_MZ and hence h².

The MZ-reared-apart correlation of 0.58 is the cleanest number in the study: those pairs share their genes but not their environments, and no feature of the design could push that correlation upward.

Finally, on the mean difference: within-group heritability cannot by itself tell us whether the 0.8 SD gap is genetic, for the reason Lewontin gave with his two seed lots — the same genetically variable stock can yield different averages in different soils. But because heritability is markedly lower in Northgate than in the rest of the sample, genetic variation is evidently doing less work there, and that asymmetry makes it reasonable to think the gap has both an environmental and a genetic component."
