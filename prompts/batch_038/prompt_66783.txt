I teach a philosophy-of-social-science seminar, 14 grad students, 50 minutes, one course of probability behind them but no measure theory and no real game theory. We've just done testimony and peer disagreement, and next week I'm supposed to hand them Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result. I want an in-class game where they run into the thing themselves and I don't have to say his name until the last ten minutes.

The part I want them to feel is *not* "sharing evidence makes people converge." They already believe that, it's completely banal and I don't want to spend an hour proving it. What I want is the specific weirdness: that once the estimates themselves are out in the open and everyone knows everyone has them, continuing to differ stops feeling like a legitimate standoff and starts feeling like somebody has made an error they can't locate. And then the rug-pull: that the common prior is what's doing all the work, and it does it invisibly, because from the inside a difference in priors is experientially identical to a difference in evidence. I want at least one student to say something like "but I just weighed it differently, that's not irrational" and I want the room to not know how to answer them.

So build me two hidden-state setups on the same physical apparatus — an urn, a deck, whatever you think is cheapest and least see-through. Round-robin announcements, repeated. In the second setup, heterogeneity in priors has to be smuggled in as background framing in a handout, never labeled as a prior, never announced.

What I need: a materials list (nothing over a few dollars, no real money, but I want actual stakes — a points currency with some grading-neutral payoff is fine), exact turn structure, a scoring scheme where being accurate genuinely pays and where sandbagging or vaguing out your announcement doesn't, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto particular hypotheses of the theorem (common prior, common knowledge of the posteriors, the partition structure of who-knows-what).

Also tell me what will break the first time I run it. I'm specifically worried about: (a) someone's first announcement leaking their private signal exactly and the whole game collapsing in round one; (b) a stubborn holdout who refuses to move and becomes a folk hero; (c) two or three sharp students reverse-engineering the state from everyone else's numbers, leaving the rest of the room feeling stupid and then just copying. For each, say whether to prevent it, house-rule it, or lean into it as pedagogy.

And be honest about which feature the game can't reproduce. My guess is common knowledge — in a real room people only ever hear the announcements, not the infinite stack of everyone-knows-that-everyone-knows — so if that's right, tell me exactly what I have to supply by hand in the debrief to close the gap.
