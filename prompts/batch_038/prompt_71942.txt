I run a small graduate seminar on persistence this term — six students, mostly second years — and we've just come off the temporary intrinsics argument. One of them (bright, but a bit of a bull in a china shop) handed me a printed sheet after class with the steps numbered, and announced that it shows the whole unit is a waste of our time. Here it is, lightly cleaned up, keeping his own running example:

(1) Consider the weathervane on the old physics building: it was straight in January, and it was bent after the March storm.
(2) The four-dimensionalist's answer to the puzzle is a reformulation. Instead of "the weathervane is bent and is not bent," we get: the weathervane timelessly and permanently has the two relational properties straight-in-January and bent-in-March. Since it neither gains nor loses either of them, nothing contradicts anything.
(3) But notice that nothing in that reformulation mentions parts. All it does is add a slot for a time to a predicate — and anybody can add such a slot, whatever they think about how objects persist.
(4) So the endurantist can give exactly the same answer, word for word, while insisting the weathervane is wholly present in both months. If that is the whole answer, temporal parts were never needed.
(5) Worse, if slot-adding is the whole answer, it proves far too much, because it would dissolve outright impossibilities. That Claire is wholly in Paris at noon and wholly in Rome at noon becomes: Claire timelessly has the relational properties in-Paris-at-noon-relative-to-her-Paris-life and in-Rome-at-noon-relative-to-her-Rome-life. No logic forbids one object from having two relational properties that differ in their extra argument.
(6) But Claire's being wholly in two cities at once is impossible, and stays impossible however you notate it.
(7) So slot-adding never removes an incompatibility. At best it relocates the incompatibility into the new slot, where it sits unexamined: the question simply becomes whether an object can have both of those relational properties, which is the original question wearing a longer name.
(8) Therefore the reformulation in (2) is not an answer to the temporary intrinsics argument — not for the perdurantist, and not for anyone else.
(9) And the same reasoning sinks every reformulation strategy in metaphysics: the presentist's past-tense operator, the ersatz modal realist's truth-at-a-world predicate, the adverbialist's sensing-redly adverb. Each adds a slot and declares victory.
(10) So there is no problem of change, hence no argument from change to temporal parts, hence no paraphrase-driven argument anywhere. We should be teaching something else.

My own instinct is that he's cheating at (7), since that's the premise doing all the generalizing in (9). But (7) looks to me like it just falls out of (6), and I'm not about to deny (6) — nobody has ever convinced me that Claire can be wholly in Paris and wholly in Rome. I'd also very much like to keep (4), because I'm an endurantist of long standing and (4) is my favorite line on the whole sheet: it says the four-dimensionalist gains nothing at all. If (4) survives tho, I can't see how to answer (2), and if (2) stands then (5) and (7) look hard to block, and then I've conceded that my own paraphrases about the weathervane are worthless too. So: which step do you reject? Or, if you think the thing is valid and it's just a matter of paying up somewhere, which premise is cheapest to give up? I want the diagnosis sharp enough that I can use it Thursday without him simply repeating (7) back at me.
