Okay so, situation: I'm starting an assistant professorship in the fall in a cognitive science dept, and my start-up basically buys me one grad student for three years plus a couple of summers. No fieldwork budget worth speaking of — I'm not flying anyone out to a remote community to run card-sorting tasks, and I've got no scanner. What I do have is a behavioral lab with a subject pool, the ability to recruit speakers of, say, Spanish/German/Mandarin online or on campus, some corpus tooling, and (because everyone has these now) access to a bunch of open-weights LLMs I can poke at.

I want to spend that on one coherent bet in the language-and-thought / linguistic relativity space rather than sprinkle it across five small studies. Which open questions there are actually worth a three-year run like that, and in what order? Please give me a defended ranking, not a survey — I care way more about the reasoning than the list itself. Specifically: which questions are upstream of which (is there a measurement problem that has to get solved before any debate about effect sizes even means anything?), which ones you'd deprioritize as either settled or hopeless, and which could plausibly get moved with the resources above. My own hunches, correct me if I'm wrong: the color-cognition work is a swamp of small effects and contested replications, the Piraha number debates are more anthropology-feud than science, and the Hopi-time stuff has been dead for decades.

Here's the angle I keep coming back to, tell me if its naive. I suspect "language" is doing triple duty in this whole debate. There's lexical effects (does having a word change the category?), there's obligatory grammatical encoding (gender, aspect, spatial frames, evidentials — the stuff you're forced to mark), and then there's whatever Slobin's thinking-for-speaking idea is pointing at, habitual patterns of attention during online formulation. Almost all the reported results seem to me to be about the first two, but the claim people actually care about when they say language shapes thought is the third — and I cant see how you get at the third without the second contaminating everything, since obligatoriness is exactly what forces the habitual attention. Is there a design that separates them, and is that separation the real crux of the field or a distinction nobody else thinks matters? Related: since I'll have the models sitting there anyway, does it make sense to treat LLMs as a testbed — same architecture, different pretraining language mixes, see whether performance on the nonverbal-ish tasks shifts — or is that a cute toy that tells you nothing about human cognition? I genuinely can't tell, and if you think it's a distraction please say so early rather than humoring me at the end.

Constraints on the answer: hard cap of 500 words. No bibliography and no citation list — I'd much rather you describe research in prose than risk mangling author-year tags, and only name a researcher if you're confident. Lead with the ranking in a few lines, then defend it. And close by telling me how confident you are in the ordering overall and which single point in it you're least sure about.
