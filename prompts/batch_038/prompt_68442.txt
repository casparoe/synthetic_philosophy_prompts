Two years of editing Wikipedia now, mostly local history and geography, roughly 4k edits, and I've just come out of the most depressing deletion arguement of my life. I'm 17, and this is also going into a history essay for school, sort of.

The article was about a woman from my town who organised laundry workers and helped run a rent strike around 1912. Everything I could find about her lived in the board of guardians' minute books, a police log, two parish registers, a census return, and one pamphlet the local history society put out forty years ago that gives her about three paragraphs. At AfD three people said basically the same thing: primary sources don't establish notability, the pamphlet isn't independent enough or detailed enough, there's no significant coverage in reliable sources independent of the subject, so delete. Nobody disputed that she existed or that any of it happened. They just said the project has no way of knowing that it mattered.

Then in history class we got an extract about archives and power, and it felt like it was describing my AfD from the other end. As far as I understood it, the position goes:

1. An archive isn't a storehouse that happens to have holes in it. The holes get produced. Which bodies generate records — courts, tax offices, police, missions, hospitals, estate ledgers, school boards — decides in advance who is even capable of appearing.
2. And it decides the form of the appearing. Silenced people are often *in* the archive, but as the object of the record rather than a voice in it: a name in a column, a charge, a diagnosis, a valuation, a line in an inventory. So "there are documents about her" and "she is recorded" are two completely different claims.
3. Silence gets made at several separate stages: when the record is created, when it's filed and arranged, when it's kept or binned, and again much later when the historian decides which traces count as significant.
4. Power decides what counts as an event. An unrecorded happening isn't a missing event, it never entered the category in the first place.
5. Two rival ways of responding: read against the grain, treating the record as evidence about the record-keepers; or concede that the person can't be recovered and write something openly speculative around the gap.

Here's what I want. Translate that position, claim by claim, into the vocabulary I actually live in — notability, significant coverage, independent reliable sources, primary vs secondary, verifiability, no original research and original synthesis, due and undue weight, systemic bias, AfD. I know that vocabulary cold and have essentially zero theory background, so don't explain GNG back to me. For each of the five: does it map onto something the project already has a name for, does it only map with distortion (and say what gets bent out of shape), or is it literally inexpressible in policy language?

Please don't cheat. I know the temptation is to render "the archive silences people" as "those people just fail the notability test", which isn't a translation at all, it's the framework eating the claim and calling the meal a result. Flag every place you catch yourself doing that, including the harmless-looking ones.

Then the residue bit, which is what I actually care about. For everything that doesn't survive: is that a hole in Wikipedia's vocabulary — something true about sources that the project structurally cannot say — or is it evidence that the archive-and-power people are being rhetorical and there's no content policy is missing? And go the other direction too: are any of the five things Wikipedia already states more sharply than the historians do?

My hunch going in is that my article dying and the historian's claim about silence are the same fact, and that policy has no way of saying so. My second hunch, which I don't like, is that claim 5's reading-against-the-grain move is precisely what the original research and synthesis rules ban, which would mean the method the historians recommend is a deletable offence over here. Tell me if either hunch is wrong, and tell me whether the translation changes what I should do next.
