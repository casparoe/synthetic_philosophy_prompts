I serve as executive director of a regional family foundation, and next Thursday I must say something difficult in front of roughly forty people. I would like you to write the speech.

Here is the situation. Our charter language, drafted twenty-six years ago by my father, commits the foundation to the reduction of inequality across a seven-county metropolitan region. For most of my nine years in this role I have read that instruction literally. Our flagship initiative has been a grant program that matches local fundraising for school enrichment, and the headline figure on our public dashboard has been the gap in student participation between the region's wealthiest and poorest districts.

Last spring I encountered something in our own reporting that I have not been able to put down. In one of three sub-regions, that gap narrowed sharply over two years. When program staff traced the cause, it turned out to be almost entirely a decline on the wealthy side: a district budget contraction, the cancellation of a summer program, families withdrawing. Participation in the poorer district did not rise. No one's circumstances improved. Our headline measure got better, and the draft annual report described the change as progress.

I have concluded that a measure which can be satisfied in that way cannot be what we are actually trying to bring about, and I intend to propose a reorientation: henceforth no program will count as a success unless we can identify a group of people whose lives went better than they otherwise would have, and where those people are among the worst off in the region, that improvement will be weighted more heavily than the same improvement for anyone else. The gap becomes something we monitor and sometimes act upon, rather than something we aim at.

The room will contain: my father, eighty-four, the founder, whose egalitarianism came out of a childhood of scarcity rather than out of reading; three family trustees with no training in economics or philosophy; our program officers and evaluators, several of whom hold policy degrees and will hear this proposal as a capitulation to cost-effectiveness thinking; two academic board members, an economist and a political philosopher, who will be listening for whether I have actually got the argument right; and the director of our housing and civic-participation program, who has told me privately that I am making a serious mistake, because on her view what is objectionable about inequality is hierarchy, domination and standing rather than the shape of a distribution, and she believes my proposal will quietly license us to stop attending to who has power over whom.

Please write the speech: about seven minutes, eight to nine hundred words, to be delivered from a lectern without notes.

It should satisfy the following requirements.

- It must convey why I changed my mind in language the family trustees can follow, without becoming false or vague about what the change actually consists in. The reasoning should be fully present in substance, in my own idiom, and must not be announced or labelled.
- It must not concede that inequality does not matter. I continue to think it matters greatly, but not as a target in itself, and the speech must hold those two claims apart without collapsing them into one another.
- It must not, even by implication, license removing anything from anyone. Nothing in the reorientation proposes that any person be made worse off, and the speech should establish that clearly enough that no one leaves the room alarmed.
- The housing director's objection must be met honestly and with respect, neither absorbed nor waved away. She is right about something, and the speech should say what that is.
- My father will be present, and the speech must contain at least one claim he can hear as continuous with his reasons for founding the institution, which I must also be able to believe.
- It should land as affection and as a hard-won correction of our own instruments, not as a lecture and not as an apology.
- No quotations, no allusions to particular philosophers or books, and no labelling of positions with isms. Technical vocabulary is permitted at most twice in the entire speech, and each instance must be cashed out within the same sentence in terms a trustee without economics would follow.

After the speech, add four or five sentences identifying the line the political philosopher on the board is most likely to press, stating precisely what the pressure is, and telling me what I should say in reply during the discussion that follows.
