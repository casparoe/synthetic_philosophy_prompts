I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and for our annual knowledge issue I'm building an interactive diagnostic for readers — a "what kind of history-of-science person are you?" thing, but one that actually sorts them by what they'd say in cases rather than by the label they'd pick for themselves.

The frame I want to steal is Nietzsche's early essay on history for life, the one that sorts doing history into monumental, antiquarian and critical. Everyone's heard of those three. But the essay also opens with two other attitudes, and I want those as leaves too, because I'm pretty sure half the people I interview are sitting in one of them and would deny it instantly: the unhistorical one — the animal that just lives, forgets, acts, and is happy without any of this — and the suprahistorical one, the person who looks down on the whole process from outside, concludes it's finished and doesn't amount to anything, and turns away from it toward art and religion and general nausea.

What I need:

- Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, all set in science and its history. The sort of situations I mean: a university is naming a building after its founding physicist and you've just turned up his correspondence; a grant proposal argues we should try because "we put people on the moon with less computing power"; a retired lab still calibrates and runs an instrument that's been obsolete for twenty years; a young researcher wants the field's foundational results re-derived from scratch because they were settled by people with reputations to defend; a historian says the priority dispute everyone cares about is beside the point since none of it will matter in a century; a working scientist says don't read the history of the controversy, just run the experiment. Every question should be a situation, not a self-description — nobody should be able to answer by thinking about what a monumental historian is supposed to sound like.
- A scoring key: which patterns of answers land on which leaf, and what you do with a reader who answers inconsistently, which I expect will be most of them.
- For each of the five leaves, one sentence on Nietzsche's own account of what goes wrong when that mode is run too hard — the heirs-of-greatness problem for monumental, mummification for antiquarian, sawing off the branch you're sitting on for critical, whatever he actually says. Paraphrase only: no quotes, no bibliography, no reading list. I can't verify anything and I don't want to end up quoting something wrong in print.
- Flag the pairs of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate. My hunch is monumental and suprahistorical will blur somewhere, and that the critical type will look a lot like the unhistorical "don't read the history, just run the experiment" type. If they can't be pulled apart, say so instead of papering over it.
- And tell me about any combination of answers the tree makes room for that doesn't correspond to a position anyone actually holds — is that slot empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's wandered into it?

Then the bit I care about most: tell me how confident you are in the attributions, and specifically whether the unhistorical/suprahistorical pair is really in the essay or whether I've picked it up from somebody's summary of it. And single out the one node in the tree you're least sure about — the place where you think you're fudging.

It's a print piece, so keep the whole thing tight enough to lay out on a spread.
