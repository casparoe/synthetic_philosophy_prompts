I cover philosophy and the mind sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am under contract for a long essay — call it 4,500 words — on the causal exclusion argument, due in six weeks. My editor has pushed the obvious framing: has philosophy of mind established that mental states are not real causes? After a dozen interviews I have come to think that framing is useless, because the people I spoke with agree about nearly everything on the surface and about almost nothing underneath. I talked to two philosophers who defend a nonreductive physicalism, one who leans toward elimination, and a neuroscientist who considers the whole problem a relic of bad metaphysics; all four accepted some version of the causal closure of the physical, all four insisted that mental states are not literally inert in the way the argument's rhetoric implies, and not one of them moved an inch over the course of an hour. What I want from you is a diagnosis of that impasse rather than a tour of the positions — I can assemble the tour from my own notes.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to treat as a hypothesis and argue against if you can: the disagreement is not really about the mental, or even about causation in general, but about what the closure principle is being asked to do and which account of causation the argument is entitled to borrow. The exclusion argument appears to need something like causal production or sufficiency at a level in order to generate its conclusion, whereas the familiar nonreductive replies — the interventionist ones, the proportionality ones, the ones holding that a mental property is a determinable of the neural state that realizes it — appear to need something like counterfactual or difference-making dependence. If each side may select its account of causation after seeing which conclusion it delivers, the argument may be unresolvable in principle rather than merely unresolved, which would be a much better story than the one I was assigned. A second suspicion, which may be unrelated: a good deal of the recent action seems to have migrated into the choice of variables and their granularity, which would make part of the dispute a question about how we describe a system rather than about how the world is.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it substantive, is it verbal — turning on "cause," or on what counts as "physical," which I understand is itself a live problem for anyone who wants to state the closure principle — are the two camps answering different questions under a single heading, or is this a genuine metaphysical dispute that is simply very hard? Please anchor the diagnosis in what each position actually commits its holders to, since I will be checking your claims against my interview transcripts, and not in how proponents advertise themselves. Then tell me what would count as progress given the diagnosis you land on: a distinction that undercuts a premise, a formal result about variable choice, a change of subject, or a principled reason to expect none of these. Aim for 1,200 to 1,800 words that I can mark up. One requirement I have to insist on as a matter of professional obligation, because a fact-checker will read over my shoulder: mark clearly the difference between what is settled among people who work on this problem and what is genuinely contested, and do not let a view that happens to be fashionable get written up as the consensus. No bibliography or list of sources — I will handle attribution myself.
