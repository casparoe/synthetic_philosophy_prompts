I keep seeing the banality of evil trotted out online like it's a causal mechanism, but almost nobody says what the mechanism actually is. As I read Arendt, the claim is roughly two things glued together: Eichmann's disability wasn't stupidity or malice, it was an inability to occupy anyone else's standpoint, plus a mind that ran entirely on bureaucratic clichés; and later she cashed this out with the idea that thinking is a soundless dialogue you hold with yourself, so that the habit of thinking is what makes most people unable to live with themselves after doing something awful — which is why she thought thoughtlessness, not evil instincts, was the bigger danger. Whether those two halves actually connect is what I want to pressure-test, and I want a designed case rather than an essay about it.

So: build me two matched thought experiments. Same company, same job tile, same rough seniority, the same kind and magnitude of harm flowing from the same decision point, and as far as you can manage it, the same information available to both men at the time. Agent A has every feature on the Arendtian list — thoughtless, talks in corporate boilerplate, in it for the promotion, no sadism, no ideology, perfectly ordinary in every other way — and he is NOT blameworthy for the harm. Agent B is genuinely thoughtful: he imagines the situation of the people downstream of his decision, argues with himself about it, writes himself notes, loses sleep — and he IS blameworthy.

Constraints. Realistic and contemporary, no sci-fi and no mind control. No camps, no genocide, no war-crimes analogue — the moment you set it in 1942 the shock value does the work instead of the structure. Pick something like a pharma supply chain, a freight or logistics operation, a lending platform, a hospital system. One paragraph per case, and make them vivid enough that I could drop them into a blog post unedited. Nobody is coerced, nobody is a sadist, nobody is a saint. A's contribution has to be real and causal, not decorative — no getting him off the hook by making him a cog that could have been replaced by a stapler. And by blameworthy I mean: an informed outsider who knows the whole story would say he shouldn't have done it and that some moral criticism is owed, not that he'd lose a lawsuit or get fired. Above all, don't stipulate the verdicts. If I can only get the intended judgment because you told me everyone agrees A did nothing wrong, the case is cheating; it has to generate the verdicts from the description.

Then run the check for me:
- List which Arendtian features are actually instantiated in each agent and which are missing, so I can see how well the two are matched and where the match had to bend. If you had to give them different information to make the verdicts come out, say exactly where and how much that weakens the comparison.
- Say what Arendt's account predicts for each agent and whether the prediction lands, misses, or lands for the wrong reason.
- Name the single feature of case A that is doing the work in producing the no-blame verdict, and tell me what happens to the case if I delete it and hold everything else fixed.
- Finish with the strongest counterattack an Arendtian could make. I'd expect something along the lines of: you've described a man who lacked the relevant facts, not one who lacked thought; or: thoughtlessness was never proposed as an excuse-eliminator, it's a claim about the conditions under which large-scale wrongdoing becomes possible. Take that attack as seriously as you can and tell me whether it actually rescues the thesis or just makes it unfalsifiable.

Audience is my usual crowd: smart, argumentative, mostly haven't read Arendt, and happy to nitpick a badly built case. So gloss any jargon, and don't pad.
