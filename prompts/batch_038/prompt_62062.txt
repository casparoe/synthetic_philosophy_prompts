I chair a four-person working group — one philosopher, two evaluation engineers, and a lawyer who handles disclosure questions — inside an organisation that publishes capability and safety evaluations of deployed language-model systems. We have eighteen months, no ML research budget of our own, and the ability to commission only a handful of small experiments from partner labs. Our output is standards and protocols, not papers.

Our mandate is narrow: to produce a rule telling our report writers when an evaluation document may ascribe a mental state to a system under evaluation — sentences of the form "the model believes it is being tested," "the model wants to appear cooperative," "the model does not represent the user's actual goal." Two incidents have made this urgent. In one, a draft report said that a system appeared to distinguish evaluation conditions from deployment conditions and adjusted its answers accordingly; a regulator's public summary rendered that as the model *knowing it was being watched*. In the other, a writer cited interpretability results about an internal representation as justification for a belief ascription, which our house style does not permit.

That house style has been loosely Dennettian: ascriptions are licensed by predictive success, and we claim nothing about inner states. It no longer matches practice, for two reasons. Interpretability methods sometimes do give us evidence about internal structure, so a purely predictive licence looks artificially restrictive. And our ascriptions are now quoted back at us in legal and regulatory settings, where "the model believed" carries weight it does not carry inside a technical report.

The group is deadlocked along a line I will label for this memo, because I do not think the existing vocabulary captures it:

- *Thin ascription*: to say the model believes that p is to say no more than that p-ascribing prediction succeeds across a specified battery of behavioural tests; nothing is committed about mechanism.
- *Thick ascription*: to say the model believes that p is to say that some internal structure carries p and plays an appropriate role in the system's own processing — available across a wide range of the system's tasks, recruited in further inference, and not merely reconstructible by an outside observer with a probe.

Two of us hold a third position: that the thin/thick dispute is verbal unless someone can exhibit a divergence case — a situation in which the two licences come apart and the choice between them changes what our reports should actually say.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or attributed positions from the literature; assume none of us can look anything up and that named authorities add nothing we can check. Instead:

1. Name the open questions our eighteen months should be spent on. You may reject my framing and substitute others, but for each question say why a standards-writing group — as opposed to a research lab or a philosophy department — is positioned to make progress on it with argument, protocol design, and a few commissioned experiments.
2. Order the questions and defend the ordering against explicit criteria: how many downstream clauses of our rule hinge on the answer; whether an answer would change the operative text of the standard or only its preamble; whether the question is likely to be settled by outside ML work regardless of what we do, in which case our job is to wait and translate rather than to theorise; and whether we can reach a defensible verdict at all within our competence.
3. Lay out the dependency structure. Which questions are upstream, such that answering them dissolves or reshapes the downstream ones, and which are safely deferrable?
4. Tell me what to drop, and give a direct verdict on the verbal-dispute charge. If the thin/thick divergence is real, sketch the divergence case you would bet on and say what our commissioned experiment should look for. If it is not real, say so and describe what our rule should become once we stop treating it as substantive.
5. For each retained question, state what would count as settling it to a standard a lawyer could rely on — given that philosophical questions rarely yield proofs — and what development over the next eighteen months would move it up or down your ordering.

I care about the reasoning considerably more than the list. And if you think the mandate itself is misconceived — for instance, if prediction, intervention, and accountability each demand their own threshold, so that no single ascription rule can serve all three — say so plainly and restructure the agenda around that conclusion rather than hedging between it and my original brief.
