I am in the final year of my A-levels, and my Extended Project concerns how national commemorative ritual comes into being; I have been working with Hobsbawm's account of invented tradition and Anderson's of the imagined community. My case is the formation of the British rituals of remembrance between 1919 and 1921.

The facts I mean to rely on are these. The two-minute silence was not devised in London: it grew out of a daily midday pause observed in Cape Town from 1918, was pressed on the Colonial Secretary by a private individual whose own son had been killed, reached the King through his private secretary, was approved by the War Cabinet on 5 November 1919 and first observed six days later. The Cenotaph was raised as a temporary monument for the victory parade of July 1919; more than a million people visited it within a week and calls to rebuild it permanently began almost immediately, so that the stone version was unveiled on 11 November 1920 alongside the burial of the Unknown Warrior. The poppy came later still, out of an American and then a French campaign, and was taken up by the British Legion only in 1921.

Please analyse this sequence through both theories. First, a disciplined mapping: for each of the three elements, who is the inventor, who the audience, and which of them, if any, actually performs the Andersonian function of letting strangers imagine themselves as a community by doing the same thing at the same minute. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. My working suspicion is that these are cases of what I have been calling ratified rather than imposed tradition — the state endorsing practices whose meaning a crowd had already fixed — and that Hobsbawm's framework has no natural place for them. Test that suspicion rather than indulging it, and say whether the episode confirms the two theories, strains them, or shows that neither was built to handle ritual that acquires its authority from below.
