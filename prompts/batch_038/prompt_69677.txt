So my roommate Nils and I got into it last night about whether you're allowed to add up harms across different people, and three hours in we were both hoarse and neither of us had moved an inch. He's doing a public health rotation and he's defending a screening program that will, predictably and by design, leave a small number of identified people worse off than if it had never run, on the grounds that the expected numbers are overwhelmingly good. When I said the seperateness of persons blocks that kind of move — that you can't compensate someone's loss with a stranger's gain because there's no single life that receives the compensation — he said that's just a slogan in a nice jacket. His argument: you already accept the trade within your own life. You'll take a painful afternoon in the chair now to avoid a much worse decade later, and nobody says your future self is "a different person" in a way that makes the trade illegitimate. So if a single point of view is what licenses aggregation, and psychological continuity comes in degrees and bleeds across people anyway, then "different people" is just another morally arbitrary boundary, like distance or nationality. I said he was equivocating. He said I was repeating myself. Honestly I think we're both partly right, and the reason I couldn't press the point is that when I actually go case by case, my gut isn't clean at all. I want to know whether there's a principle back there or just a pile of reactions.

Here are my verdicts, roughly in the order we argued them. (1) Me now vs. me at sixty: accept a miserable root canal to avoid losing the tooth and years of pain — obviously fine, no puzzle whatsoever. (2) Two strangers: give person A a migraine to spare person B a root canal — not fine. It doesn't matter that the amounts balance; there's nobody for whom it balances. (3) Five strangers drowning on one side, one on the other, I can reach only one side, no ties, no information about any of them: save the five. I feel totally solid about this one and I think Nils is right that (2) and (3) can't be pulled apart by anything as crude as "they're different people." (4) One person left paralyzed versus five thousand people each spared a week of mild nausea: save the paralyzed one. A million people each spared a hangnail: still the paralyzed one. But a billion people each condemned to decades of moderate chronic pain: now the billion wins, and I don't think I'm being inconsistent, I think there's a threshold — I just can't tell you where it is or what shape it has. (5) The same screening program described in advance, as a lottery where each of a million people has a small chance of a big benefit and a tiny chance of a serious harm, expected value strongly positive, nobody identifiable ahead of time: run it, clearly. The identical program described afterwards, where the handful who drew the bad outcome are named and ask to be compensated: not fine, they're owed something, and "you took the bet" is a disgusting thing to say to them. I know these are supposed to be the same choice wearing different clothes and I simply cannot feel it. (6) The hemisphere transplant guy who wakes up as two people, both fully continuous with him: I think it's fine for one to absorb a small pain so the other avoids a big one, because they're "him enough." Which is bad news for me, because then what's doing the work in (1) isn't personhood, it's connectedness, and connectedness is a matter of degree between ordinary people too — so I seem committed to aggregating partially between people who are very close and not at all between strangers, which feels like a dodge I'd laugh at in anyone else. (7) Spending my savings now on care for the severely demented person I'll become, who won't remember me, won't share a single value with me, and who by the lights of (6) is barely me at all: fine, required even. If connectedness is what matters, this one should bother me a lot more than it does. (8) A million lab mice each mildly distressed versus one human mildly distressed: I go with the human, and I hate that I do, because the only thing I can point to is species.

I'm not after a reading list — I've done the obvious homework, Taurek and Kagan and Parfit and the contractualist pairwise stuff, and knowing who said what won't settle anything between me and Nils. I want the machinery. So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets (1) through (8), or most of them, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without consulting my stomach? Give me two or three candidates if no single one fits, and be explicit about what each clause is buying — which of my cases forced it in. And if the price of the best candidate is one of my verdicts, tell me which and make the case that the principle has earned the right to overrule it rather than just being tidier.
