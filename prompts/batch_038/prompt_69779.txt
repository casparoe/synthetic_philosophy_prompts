So I'm on a county task force that's been asked whether we should seat a citizens' assembly — roughly forty residents picked by sortition — to make recommendations on the comprehensive plan. There are nine of us, we meet Thursday mornings in a library conference room, and about half the group already thinks the whole idea is a boondoggle, so I got handed the section of the background memo that's supposed to explain why a random sample of ordinary people might actually be trustworthy. I leaned on the Condorcet jury theorem, the way everybody does. Then over the weekend I started pushing on it and wrote down the following, and now I can't tell whether I've refuted my own memo section or something much bigger. I took one epistemology course years ago and have been over-reading since, so assume nothing here is clever on purpose.

(1) If the members of a group vote independently and each is better than even odds at picking the right answer, then the majority's verdict is more likely to be right than any individual member's verdict is, and that reliability approaches certainty as the group grows.
(2) So for any member of such a group, the majority's verdict is a better guide to the truth than her own judgment.
(3) So each member, if she's trying to track the truth, ought to believe what the majority concludes rather than what she concludes on her own.
(4) But if every member does that, then every member believes the same thing — the majority verdict — and their votes are no longer independent at all; they're perfectly correlated.
(5) Perfectly correlated votes just repeat one opinion N times over, so the majority verdict ends up exactly as reliable as a single member's judgment, and the approach-to-certertainty result in (1) is gone.
(6) And the majority verdict can't function as the independent check on your own judgment that (2) said it was, because there aren't independent judgments sitting behind it anymore.
(7) So the theorem instructs each of us to follow the majority, and if we follow the instruction the majority stops being worth following: a theory of collective reliability destroys the very collective reliability it certifies.
(8) Since that theorem is the main reason anyone has for trusting large bodies — juries, assemblies, electorates, my citizens' assembly — we should stop trusting them and use small expert panels instead, where you can actually check that the members are reasoning seperately.

The (8) part I definitely don't want, and (7) makes me feel sick about the memo. I'd much rather keep (1) and (2), since without them the memo has nothing in it at all, but I can't see which of the later steps is the bad one — (4) and (5) feel like bookkeeping, and (3) feels like precisely what a truth-seeker should do. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine to me? And what does rejecting it cost — if I deny (3), am I committed to saying a member should vote for what she thinks is probably wrong? If I deny (4) or (5), am I saying the worry about herding and people reading each other just doesn't matter, which seems insane given how actual groups behave? For what it's worth I've been assuming our assembly members won't know each other and will vote by secret ballot, which I thought handled the independence worry. I'd also like to know whether (7) licenses (8) at all, or whether that last leap is doing most of the work. Answer in American English, please — whatever survives of this is going in front of the county board.
