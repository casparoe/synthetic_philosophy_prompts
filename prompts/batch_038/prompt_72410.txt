I spent thirty-eight years in low-temperature physics, retired two years ago, and have come to philosophy by the only route I know: starting from results that announce themselves as theorems. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem was the first that stopped me. From premises that look merely technical — each person's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, the social preference over the same prospects satisfies them too, and the social ordering respects unanimity, in the weak or the strong form as you prefer — one derives a representation in which social welfare is a positively weighted sum of individual utilities, with equal weights once an anonymity or equal-treatment condition is added. It reads like a derivation rather than a manifesto, which is precisely why I found it persuasive, and why I was unsettled to discover that the standard classroom treatment of Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" announces that the story refutes utilitarianism without ever identifying the step that fails.

I am preparing a paper for my town's philosophy society this autumn, and I want the story tested against the theorem rather than against the slogan. Please give me a reading, in prose, that does the following.

First, audit the machinery. Take each assumption the theorem genuinely needs — cardinal and interpersonally comparable utilities; the Pareto or unanimity condition; anonymity; and the independence implicit in representing the social evaluation as a sum over separately specified individual utilities — and say for each whether the world Le Guin describes satisfies it, violates it, renders it inapplicable, or leaves it indeterminate. Two deserve particular attention. The citizens' happiness is stipulated to depend on their knowing that the child is wretched, and to be of a kind they could not have without that knowledge; if each person's utility is partly constituted by another person's state, is the additive representation still a representation of that world, or has the story quietly altered the informational basis the theorem is proved over? Second, the terms as the narrator gives them are that to bring the child up into the sunlight would wither all the prosperity and delight of the city. If that is right, there may be no feasible alternative that is unanimously at least as good and strictly better for the child, in which case unanimity has nothing to bite on and the theorem has nothing to rank. Does the story then attack an ordering at all, or the absolute level of an aggregate — and is that a different objection from the one usually lodged? Bring in Harsanyi's own identification or impartial-observer gloss, on which ethical preference is what a person would have while imagining himself in each individual's position with equal probability, and say whether Omelas is best read as inverting that device, since every citizen knows exactly who he is and knows with certainty that he will never be the child. If you think the Rawlsian objection about which decision rule governs choice behind such a veil belongs here, say so and say how much work it does.

Second, account for the ones who walk away. They propose no alternative, cast no vote, offer no argument; they leave a choice set from which exit was not among the alternatives. Is their departure a denial of comparability, of aggregation, or of the framing that there is a single social state to be ranked? And what becomes of the narrator's remark that the place they go toward may not exist — does it make their option incoherent, a refusal to rank, or an appeal to something outside the model?

Third, treat the narrator as a modeler. He repeatedly invites the reader to fill in details and confesses he may have added some. Does that vagueness function like a family of models rather than a single model, and does it make the story a weaker counterexample or a stronger one?

Fourth, tell me where the reading strains, and do not force the fit. Le Guin acknowledged a debt to William James's 1891 formulation of the same bargain, in which the offense lies in accepting a happiness bought on such terms; if the story is about the acceptability of a bargain rather than the representation of a social ordering, then the social-choice lens may be a Procrustean bed, and I would rather hear that from you than discover it at the lectern.

Fifth, evaluate a variant of my own devising. Suppose the child's misery is no condition of anyone's joy: the citizens simply never noticed it, and releasing it would cost them inconvenience and a small loss of comfort, with no withering of the city. Which world is the real objection — the one Le Guin wrote or this one — and what does the difference show about whether the story's force comes from the aggregation or from the non-separability?

Finally, say what the story contributes that argument alone cannot: something, perhaps, in the phenomenology of acquiescence, in the adolescents who go home in silence and never speak of it again, that no representation theorem has a place for.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have the literature and I want your analysis. Describe scenes and stipulations rather than quoting them. After the prose, give me the audit as a table with exactly these columns: Assumption, naming the item as precisely as you can; Status in Omelas, taking exactly one value from satisfied, violated, inapplicable, or indeterminate; Deciding feature, the stipulation or scene that fixes the status in a phrase or two; and Consequence for the reading, in one or two sentences, what follows if the status is as you have given it. Include one row for each assumption you audit, plus a row for the impartial-observer gloss and a row for the walkers' exit if you treat either as an assumption rather than a move. Close with a JSON object of exactly four fields: target, one of premises, conclusion, framing, or neither; why, one or two sentences justifying that choice; stronger_version, one of original or variant; and adds, one or two sentences on what the story contributes that the formal argument cannot.
