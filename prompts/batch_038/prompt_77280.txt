I'm preparing a seminar paper on whether famines should be classified as political crimes rather than natural disasters, and I've reconstructed a skeptical argument against my own thesis that I can't defeat. The trouble is that its conclusion is far stronger than anything I'd accept, and I can't locate the slip.

Take a political crime to be a mass harm attributable to some agent's choices rather than to nature.

(1) A mass harm is a political crime only if some identifiable agent's choices made a difference to its occurring.
(2) On Sen's entitlement account, famines are not caused by an aggregate decline in food availability; they result from the collapse of particular groups' exchange entitlements — their capacity to command food through wages, trade, or transfer.
(3) What determines a group's exchange entitlements is the surrounding structure of property rights, labor markets, relative prices, and public provision.
(4) That structure is nobody's choice. It is inherited and evolved, and at the relevant time no single agent, and often no feasible coalition, could have altered it.
(5) So no agent's choices made a difference to the famine's occurring.
(6) By (1), famines are not political crimes. They are structural, and attributing them to a government rather than to nature is a category error.
(7) The reasoning generalizes: any mass harm produced by standing institutional arrangements rather than by particular decisions — enslavement, enclosure, segregation — escapes the label as well.
(8) But I certainly want to say those were political crimes, and the point of the entitlement analysis is precisely that Bengal was not something nature did on its own. So (6) and (7) cannot both be tolerated.

I'd rather keep (2) and (8) than anything else. (2) strikes me as the best-supported premise, and abandoning (8) would make my topic empty. But I suspect the damage is done earlier, and I don't want to patch the argument ad hoc.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look safe on first reading? If you think the argument is valid instead, say which premise is cheapest to give up and price it: what does a defender of the entitlement account lose by conceding that attribution requires something beyond entitlement collapse? Finally, does your diagnosis hold across all three cases I'm working with — Bengal in 1943, Ireland in 1845–52, and the Great Leap Forward — or does one of them resist the treatment the other two accept?
