I want a position of mine pressure-tested rather than summarized. It concerns satisficing and bounded rationality, and I will state it as carefully as I can before saying what I need from you.

The position has three parts.

First: a standard of practical rationality that an agent cannot follow is not a standard for that agent. The maximising norm, on its ordinary reading, requires the agent to select the alternative supported by the strongest reasons overall, and that presupposes a comparison across the whole feasible set. When constructing such a comparison exceeds the agent's search and computational budget, the norm does not become demanding — it becomes empty. There is nothing there for her to fail.

Second: in choice problems of that kind, satisficing is not a discounted version of maximising. It is the only available candidate for rational choice. Describing it as rational-given-constraints concedes too much, because that formulation keeps maximisation in place as the norm and treats satisficing as a fall from it, when in the relevant domain there is no norm to fall from.

Third: the aspiration level is not an arbitrary stopping threshold. It is set by the agent's history with comparable problems and calibrated against outcomes she has actually obtained. A satisficer with a well-calibrated threshold is not less rational than a maximiser; she is executing the only procedure that could have been executed.

Two objections have already been pressed on me, and I want your judgement on whether either is fatal.

The first comes from a colleague and is conceptual. On her reading my view collapses back into maximisation one level up: if the agent selects the best decision procedure she can execute within her budget, she is maximising over rules. Satisficing is then optimisation restricted to a feasible set of procedures, the second part of my position is false, and I have relocated the maxim rather than escaped it.

The second comes from a collaborator and is empirical. She ran a repeated-choice task with displayed option sets, a per-decision time limit, and an instruction either to accept the first option clearing a stated threshold or to look for the best of what was shown. Subjects in the threshold condition did not hold their thresholds stable; the thresholds drifted systematically with recent payoffs, rising after good runs and falling after poor ones. Her conclusion was that nothing corresponding to a satisficing rule was being implemented at all — that the label describes the shape of the output rather than any procedure producing it — and so the third part of my position has no support.

What I want is a map of this position's exposure.

Which states of affairs would make it false? Sort the candidates by decisiveness, and say for each whether it would be fatal, merely awkward, or absorbable by reformulation. I would like conceptual and empirical defeaters distinguished, since I suspect they fail differently.

Which of these would a defender of the position concede as genuinely counting against her, and which would she dismiss as changing the subject? I want the internal assessment, not only what critics would say.

What does the position predict that its main rival — maximisation with explicit search costs — does not? Are there choice situations in which the two come apart, and if so, are they constructible in principle or only describable in the abstract? If they are extensionally equivalent everywhere, say so, and say what that costs me.

Whether the position has been qualified into immunity. The obvious candidate is the appeal to budget in the first part: budgets are unobservable, are nominally set by the agent, and can be revised after the fact. Tell me plainly whether this renders the view untestable, and whether untestability of that sort is a defect in a claim about practical rationality or is unobjectionable given what the claim is for.

Bear in mind as you work through the collaborator's result that threshold drift with recent experience may be precisely what the third part predicts, which raises the question of whether any outcome in that experiment could have counted against me. For what it is worth, the objection I would find hardest to answer is the colleague's: if satisficing is best understood as optimisation over procedures, I am not certain I have a substantive thesis left. I would rather be told that directly than reassured.
