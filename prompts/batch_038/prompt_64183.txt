My friend and I spent most of last night arguing over whether anything morally significant can happen inside a game world, and we made no progress because every example either of us reached for was contaminated: his all involved other players, and mine all leaned on what the act would do to the agent's character. I would like a clean case built to specification rather than another anecdote.

Design a thought experiment in which virtual realism — the view that the objects and events inside a simulated world are real objects and real events, of a digital kind — and the ordinary irrealist view that nothing in such a world can bear moral significance deliver different verdicts on a single act. The realist must be able to say that a genuine wrong occurred; the irrealist must be unable to say that any wrong occurred, rather than merely that a lesser one did.

Constraints:

1. One paragraph, set in a near-present-day environment that already exists or could plausibly be sold: a persistent world, or a single-player game with a long-running AI companion character. No mind uploading, no neural interfaces, no simulations of the afterlife.
2. Nothing in the world is conscious, and the agent knows this and does not believe otherwise. The wrong may not rest on the entity's being sentient, or on its possibly being sentient.
3. No real person observes the act, learns of it, or is affected by it in any way. If a real person's interests were engaged, both sides would agree that a wrong occurred and the case would prove nothing.
4. The act leaves the agent's character, dispositions, and habits exactly as they were. No desensitization, no spillover, no habituation — the self-cultivation and vice replies must be foreclosed by construction.
5. The agent takes no pleasure in it and feels no distress afterward. The verdict may not ride on guilt, attachment, or any other feeling, on either side.
6. The wrong must be generated rather than announced. Your description may contain only neutral facts about what was said, what was done, what followed, and the agent's prior history with the thing in question; no sentence may assert that anything valuable existed or was lost. Both disputants should be able to accept every sentence of your setup and still reach their own verdict. If any sentence would in fact be rejected by one side as question-begging, mark it as such.

Then do the following:

- Check the case against each of the six constraints, one line apiece, and say plainly if any is only partly met.
- Give each of the three accounts available in this space — irrealism, the character account, and virtual realism — its verdict and the reasoning it must supply out of its own resources. For the account your constraints were designed to block, say what it is reduced to claiming instead.
- Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and run at least two removals: what each verdict becomes when that feature is taken out, and whether the two views then converge.
- Give my friend his strongest attack on the case, and say whether the realist has a reply that does not concede the point.
- Finish by telling me whether the case settles our argument or merely relocates it; if it relocates it, name the precise question it lands on.

No reading lists and no citations, please — I want the argument, not the apparatus. You may name positions freely, but attribute nothing to a named philosopher unless you are confident you have it right.
