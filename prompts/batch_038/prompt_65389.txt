I am assembling a session for an interdisciplinary seminar in which we read literary works against live debates in analytic metaphysics, and I would like help with the pairing of Anthony Burgess's *A Clockwork Orange* (1962) and Stanley Kubrick's 1971 film adaptation with the literature on dispositions, finks, and masks.

The technical frame I want to work in is the one developed after C. B. Martin's "Dispositions and Conditionals." A fink is a case in which a thing would lose a disposition in the very circumstances that would trigger it — Martin's electro-fink is a machine wired to a conductor that makes a dead wire live when touched and, on the reverse cycle, makes a live wire dead when touched — so that the simple conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions fails. A mask is a case in which the disposition is retained but some further feature interferes with its manifestation, as with protective packing that lets a fragile glass survive being dropped while remaining fragile. David Lewis's "Finkish Dispositions" responds by reformulating the analysis so that the object must retain the disposition through stimulus and response. There is also standing disagreement about whether finkish dispositions are possible at all — some hold that in the fink case the object simply never had the disposition — and about whether finks and masks can be intrinsic to their bearers rather than imposed from outside.

Against that frame, I want a reading of the two texts. Specifically:

1. Does the Ludovico technique fink or mask Alex's disposition to cruelty? Note that the intervention is not an external blocker applied at the moment of the stimulus: the nausea and the paralysis arise from within Alex, in response to his own intentions and even his own imaginings, and they also capture his response to Beethoven's Ninth, which had been part of the affective economy of his violence. Is that an intrinsic mask, a fink, or neither — and does the case put pressure on the assumption that masks are paradigmatically extrinsic packing?

2. What should we say about whether Alex still has the vice? If the disposition is masked rather than removed, then the counterfactuals that seem to license character ascriptions come apart from what he is able to do, and the moral evaluation of the treated Alex becomes unclear. Is the state's claim to have cured him a claim about his dispositions or about his manifestations, and does that distinction matter to the political point each text is making?

3. The two endings. Burgess's original British edition has twenty-one chapters; the American edition Kubrick worked from omitted the last, in which an older Alex simply loses his taste for violence. The film ends instead with the conditioning reversed and Alex restored to his former appetites. Which ending is the better test case for the fink and mask literature, and do the two endings amount to two different accounts of character change — a disposition genuinely lost, a manifestation condition withdrawn, a mask lifted? I would like you to say which of the two the metaphysics of dispositions should predict to be more stable, and whether Burgess's concern with the value of moral choice is served or undermined by that prediction.

Please be faithful in both directions. I do not want a reading that turns the novel into an illustration of Martin's taxonomy, and I do not want the technical notions stretched until they carry no content. Tell me where the texts resist the frame: Alex narrates in the first person in an invented argot, and much of the novel's force lies in an aesthetic and erotic sensibility that third-person dispositional ascription may simply not be built to capture; the film, for its part, arguably makes its points about spectatorship and complicity rather than about the metaphysics of character. Say plainly where you think the pairing is strained. End by telling me what the works contribute to the debate that the papers do not — the abstract literature stipulates finks and masks as isolated thought experiments, whereas here the interfering condition is installed deliberately by an institution, for political reasons, and someone has to live with it.

Two practical notes. Please paraphrase rather than quote either the novel or the film; my readers may not have them to hand and I will not be able to check quotations. And where you invoke the technical literature, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on dispositions: do not present the existence of finkish dispositions, the coherence of intrinsic masks, or the superiority of Lewis's reformed analysis as matters of consensus if they are not. Flag explicitly which of your claims about the metaphysics are your own extrapolation from the texts rather than positions anyone has actually defended, and correct me if my summary of the fink/mask distinction above is inaccurate.
