I am in my fourth year of a PhD in philosophy of education, and the second chapter of my dissertation defends a claim about textbook controversies that I call the asymmetric-revision-cost thesis. It says the following. In any regime where access to a large student market is mediated by a gatekeeper — a state adoption board in the United States, a ministry screening process for privately authored manuscripts in Japan, and, briefly and differently, the South Korean government's attempt in 2015 to produce a single state-authored history textbook, abolished in 2017 before it ever reached a classroom — the marginal cost of removing or softening a contested passage is systematically lower than the marginal cost of retaining it. Removing a passage requires only that one organized group credibly threaten the publisher's or author's access to that market; retaining it requires the diffuse beneficiaries of the passage, chiefly disciplinary historians and unorganized teachers, to sustain collective action they typically cannot fund. The thesis's central claim is that the equilibrium content of widely distributed school history therefore drifts away from what professional consensus would license and toward whatever the most organizationally efficient complainants can credibly object to, and that the direction of the drift tracks mobilization capacity rather than merit. I further hold that this is regime-general: the same asymmetry governs the American fights over 1619 and 1776 and the Japanese and Korean disputes over colonial and wartime history, and it explains them better than culture-war accounts that posit sincere, roughly symmetric ideological conviction on all sides. One corollary matters for what follows. Because publishers and authors anticipate the objection, most of the work is done before a complaint is ever filed, so the drift is largely invisible in finished texts; the right unit of observation is revision history and screening commentary, not the published book.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Give me the observations, results, and arguments that would count against it, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit for each about which of the two it is and why. I want to know what the thesis predicts that its rivals do not — the culture-war account, a straightforward public-choice account of publishing, and the professionalization account on which textbook content tracks the disciplinary guild over time — so that a disagreement between them is not merely verbal. Then tell me which defeaters a committed defender would concede as genuinely counting against the view and which she would explain away, and, of the explain-aways, which are legitimate. My two readers have already staked out opposite objections, and I want your assessment of both. The first says the thesis is nothing but the logic of concentrated benefits and diffuse costs applied to an industry, that it therefore explains nothing specific to school history, and that it inherits every problem of that general account. The second says the thesis cannot fail: with the preemption corollary in place, a complaint that succeeds confirms the asymmetry, a complaint that fails shows the threat was already priced in, content that is identical across jurisdictions shows the drift happened upstream in the manuscript, and content that differs shows the drift happened in the market. I have a reply to the second — that successful deterrence is unobservable in just the same way, so unobservability is not by itself disqualifying for a thesis about equilibrium outcomes — and I want to know whether that reply holds or is itself a dodge. Say plainly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and, if it has, whether that is a defect for a thesis of this kind or a normal feature of one.

Two further questions. First, on scope: in the Japanese and Korean cases the gatekeeper is the state rather than a market, and there is no large purchasing jurisdiction for an interest group to threaten. Does the mechanism survive that transposition, and if it does not, is regime-generality simply the first commitment to surrender — or does losing it leave a thesis with any content? Second, on repair: state what the minimal qualification would be that preserves something worth defending, what the qualified version would have to give up, and what kind of comparison across jurisdictions, publishers, or screening cycles would discriminate it from the culture-war account using data a graduate student could actually assemble. Tell me where you would place your own bet about which defeater lands first, and, if your honest conclusion is that the position is well insulated and that this is or is not a problem, say so rather than softening it.
