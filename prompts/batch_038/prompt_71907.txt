I am at work on my fourth novel, which consists entirely of a deposition: a woman answers questions over the course of nine days, and the reader receives her answers and nothing else — no clerk's marginalia, no cross-examination, no scene in which anything she says can be checked against the world. My editor's note on the draft was that she could not tell how far she was supposed to believe this woman, and that this was either the book's great virtue or its fatal flaw. Before I answer her, I want to think the problem through properly, and the text I keep returning to as a model is Henry James's *The Turn of the Screw* — not as a ghost story, but as the most carefully engineered piece of testimony in English fiction. What I would like is a reading of the novella through the epistemology of testimony, and specifically through the dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists over whether a hearer owes a speaker default credibility or must first acquire independent, positive reasons for trusting her.

The reading should work through the novella's layers of report rather than treating it as a flat first-person account: Douglas and the Christmas gathering, at which a man with an avowed attachment to the author reads aloud a manuscript he has long kept locked away; the governess's own written account, composed at some unspecified remove from the events it describes; and, inside that account, the testimony of the household — above all Mrs. Grose, who sees nothing at all at the lake or at the window and is nevertheless converted by the governess's report alone. I want to know what that conversion is evidence of. Is Mrs. Grose a portrait of the credulity the reductionist warns against, or of the ordinary trust without which, as Reid insists, nobody could learn anything, since any audit of testimony must itself be conducted by testimony? Handle the children as well: Flora's flat denial at the water, Miles's inability or refusal to say what he said at school and to whom he said it — a theory of testimony that accommodates only willing speakers is of little use to a novelist. Take Hume's test for the miraculous seriously too, and ask whether James builds a case designed to fail it: wonders reported, no second pair of eyes to confirm them, and a reader left to weigh whether the governess's being mistaken or deceitful would be stranger than the wonders themselves. Then say plainly which side of the dispute the structure of the book favours, and whether it favours that side because James believed it or because the first-person form simply renders the other side unavailable.

One further thing, which I suspect is my own invention rather than anything in the literature, so please treat it as a proposal to be argued with. Besides believing a report and disbelieving it, I think a hearer has a third stance open to her: taking the report's content as evidence about the speaker's mind while withholding belief about the world — believing that the governess saw something on the tower without believing there was anything on the tower. I have been calling this quarantined testimony, and it is precisely the posture my editor says she could not hold toward my deponent. Tell me whether it is a genuine third option or merely local reductionism with a permanent defeater bolted on, and whether the novella rewards or punishes a reader who adopts it. Finally, be honest about where this reading overreaches: James appears to have wanted his reader held in doubt rather than sorted into a position, and I would far rather be told that the ambiguity of the novella is not an epistemological puzzle at all — that it belongs to genre, to the market for ghost stories, to the withholding the form requires — than be handed a tidy map the book does not support. Close by saying what the novella can do for the debate that argument cannot: the price of each stance, paid at the last page by a child. Fifteen hundred to two thousand words, in continuous prose rather than headings and bullets if you can manage it. Please do not assemble a bibliography or hunt down citations; I want the reasoning, not the apparatus, and where you gesture at Hume or Reid or their modern successors, paraphrase rather than quote — I have no way of checking you and would prefer that you not guess.
