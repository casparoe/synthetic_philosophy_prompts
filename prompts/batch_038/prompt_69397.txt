So I'm in a phil of psychiatry seminar this term and we just did Hacking on looping effects and the interactive vs indifferent kinds distinction. The example everyone immediately reaches for is multiple personality disorder: basically no reported cases before around 1970, Sybil comes out as a book in the early 70s and then a movie, MPD goes into DSM-III in 1980, the numbers blow up through the 80s tangled together with the satanic ritual abuse stuff and therapists using hypnosis to fish out alters and buried memories, then the retracted memories and the lawsuits against therapists in the early 90s, the renaming to dissociative identity disorder in DSM-IV in 1994 as the whole thing deflates, and then a fresh uptick over the last few years from people self-diagnosing off TikTok and YouTube. I know that arc roughly, don't need it retold.

What I actually want help with is this. A few of us in the reading group got annoyed that looping effect is used as a catch-all for any diagnosis that gets fashionable, so we tried to write down conditions that seperate a genuine loop from mere fashion. We landed on three:

(i) publicity — people who might get classified have to be able to find out about the classification and about what its like to have it;
(ii) payoff — being classified has to be worth something to the person, materially, socially, or just as a way of making your own misery intelligible;
(iii) uptake — the classified people need some channel for changing the criteria, not just for being sorted by them.

My position, which half the group disagrees with: (ii) does essentially all the work in the MPD story and (i) and (iii) are decoration. On my reading Sybil is just (i) getting dramatically better, and the engine was that MPD paid off — it got you a diagnosis instead of a character judgement, it got you inpatient time, it got you a clinician who took you extremely seriously for years.

Run the boom and the bust through the three conditions properly. I want the actors mapped out first — who's doing the classifying, who's getting classified, and who's the audience that makes the payoff real (families, insurers, courts, the APA committee, the media, the skeptical counter-movement), plus which feedback channels actually existed. Then a condition-by-condition check on the rise and separately on the fall, because I suspect they aren't the same loop. Where do (i) and (iii) genuinely bite, if anywhere? Was the DSM-III listing an instance of uptake, or is uptake supposed to run the other direction, patients to criteria? And the collapse is the part I cant figure out: if MPD stopped paying off because of the lawsuits and the debunking, is that a loop running backwards, or is that a separate mechanism — payoff being removed from outside by courts and journalists — that looping-effects language just isn't built to describe?

Also stress-test the framework in two directions. What does the three-condition version predict about the social-media uptick that a plain Hacking-style story wouldn't, and which account better handles the fact that a lot of those people are classifying themselves with no clinician in the loop at all — is that a case where (i) goes maximal and (iii) collapses, and what's supposed to happen then? And hunt for a place in the episode where the conditions come apart in a way that actually breaks the framework: high payoff and total publicity but no uptake, or uptake with no payoff.

Then be straight with me about where Hacking is silent rather than forcing him to speak. Looping effects is supposed to stay neutral on whether anyone really had MPD, but I'm not sure it can stay neutral, since the account seems to need the classified people to be responding to the classification rather than being correctly described by it. Tell me whether that's a real tension in the view or whether I'm misreading it.
