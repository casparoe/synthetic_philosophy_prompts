Second-year DPhil student in philosophy here, and I also keep the minutes for our centre's release panel. Six of us rule on whether research artefacts from the group get put out into the world. The panel exists because of what our director calls the unilateralist's curse: if each member privately reasons 'if this is going to happen anyway, better it happens at our hands and done properly', then the thing happens even when all six of us agree it shouldn't have. The norm is supposed to be that you don't act on your own optimism.

Our record so far, in order:

- A postdoc found a jailbreak for several deployed models that anyone could rediscover in an afternoon. Panel approved immediate disclosure, vendor given two days' notice.
- Someone built a small model producing very convincing synthetic speech in a language with essentially no other tooling. Panel refused the weights, approved a paper describing the capability and the evals.
- A paper showing that a widely used benchmark was contaminated. Panel held it two months so three partner labs could try to replicate, then published it unchanged.
- A member released a scraped dataset unilaterally after hearing that a rival lab would have a version out within weeks with the redaction done badly. Panel endorsed it retroactively, 6–0, explicitly citing the rival timeline.
- A member pulled our own preprint overnight because they thought it described a filter bypass, telling nobody. Panel censured the going-round-the-group, said the pull itself was probably right, and reposted a redacted version themselves.
- A member sat on an experiment they were confident was safe because three colleagues were uneasy and couldn't articulate why. Panel praised the restraint.

Now it's my turn, which is awkward. I have a replication package for a de-anonymisation attack on a widely used public dataset: my method re-identifies around 40% of respondents from published attributes, and the package ships with working code. The panel deadlocked 3–3 in Michaelmas and we agreed to revisit it once Id gathered the mitigations. Since then, someone at another institution has told me privately and off the record that they'll have an equivalent out by the spring, without the responsible-disclosure step or the redaction. I was one of the three in favour of release, and I'm tempted to just put it out over the vacation: a deadlock isn't a decision against, it's coming anyway, and mine is the version with the guardrails.

What should I do if I want to be consistent with us rather than with myself? Which candidate rules are actually consistent with all six precedents, what does each imply for my case, and which precedent is the closest analogue? If the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so, and tell me what principle the panel would be adopting with each choice. Also name the further case that would separate the two rules you think most likely to be ours.
