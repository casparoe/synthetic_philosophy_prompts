You are a philosopher of history who also has a genuine working command of psychometric measurement theory — not a popular acquaintance with it, but the technical apparatus: multi-group confirmatory factor analysis and the hierarchy of configural, metric and scalar invariance; differential item functioning; item response theory, with its anchoring, linking and equating problems; and Messick's unified treatment of construct validity, including the consequential facet. Before you write the substantive answer, spend some time brainstorming candidate mappings and discarding the ones that fail, and note briefly at the outset which candidates you rejected and why.

I am in the fourth year of a doctorate in quantitative psychology. My dissertation concerns longitudinal measurement invariance across birth cohorts: whether a construct measured by the same item battery in 1990 and in 2020 is the same construct, and what may legitimately be compared once the indicator structure has drifted. I read historiography for pleasure rather than professionally; I know Butterfield's polemic against studying the past as a prelude to the present, and Thompson's complaint about the condescension of posterity, but I have no training in the methodological disputes those remarks belong to.

The position I want translated is the anti-presentist strain in that dispute, together with the standard presentist replies to it. Please take the following five claims as your source material, and restate each of them in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can:

1. Past agents should be understood and assessed only against the standards that were available within their own form of life; importing ours distorts the very object the historian is trying to recover.
2. Moral standards are not indexed to epochs: what was done to the enslaved was wrong whether or not the surrounding society took it to be so, and a historian who withholds judgement on the ground of local acceptability has not avoided adopting a normative position but has adopted the period's own.
3. Butterfield-style anti-anachronism is a claim about explanatory selection — reading the past as a march towards the present — and is therefore a different charge from the moral one, although the two are habitually run together.
4. The non-culpable ignorance defence: an agent who could not reasonably have known better is not thereby blameworthy; and the rejoinder that this defence is disproportionately available to those who benefited from the practice in question, and that the existence of contemporaneous dissenters shows the relevant standard was available at the time.
5. The reflexivity worry: the historian's own standards are themselves historically situated, so there is no neutral vantage point from which to settle which standards apply.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the main deliverable rather than as an afterthought. For each of the five claims, say which of the following applies: that it translates cleanly into the language of invariance, differential item functioning and anchoring; that it translates only with a distortion you can specify, in which case state precisely what is lost; or that it has no expression at all in my framework. Where you are tempted to make a translation work by quietly importing a psychometric commitment — that moral standing is a unidimensional latent trait, that a construct has a determinate meaning prior to the choice of reference group, that non-invariance is always a technical defect to be corrected rather than sometimes a fact about the world — flag the import explicitly rather than presenting it as translation.

Three probes, which are where my own interest lies:

First, I have argued in a reading group that the entire dispute reduces to an anchor choice plus a failure to keep descriptive and evaluative claims apart: once one says openly that the present cohort is being used as the reference group for the construct, and that this is a convention rather than a discovery, the quarrel evaporates. Tell me whether that is right, or whether I am relying on an identification assumption I have not stated.

Second, the dissenter case. An abolitionist writing in the 1780s scores high on the same latent dimension as a contemporary one; in my framework the two are simply two locations on a single scale. Yet the historian treats the dissenter as doing conceptual work, as evidence that the standard was available then, and it is that which defeats the ignorance defence. The availability of an alternative reading within a cohort does not look like a scalar property of a person at all. Is there any place for it in an invariance framework, or is this a genuine expressive gap?

Third, claim 5. If the measuring instrument is produced by the very population whose scores it is used to interpret, is that captured by the unremarkable fact that the reference cohort writes the items and supplies the construct definition, or does the historians' worry reach beyond anything measurement theory can represent?

Finally, tell me which of these you take to be true: that the residue shows my framework to be expressively impoverished; that the residue shows the historiographers' disagreement to be less substantive than it sounds; or that the translation succeeds for the descriptive half of the debate and leaves the normative half entirely untouched, which would make my reading-group claim a category error rather than a discovery. If you think a fourth possibility is more accurate, say what it is and defend it.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list or citations — I want the argument, and I would rather you say plainly where you are uncertain about the historiography than pad the answer with confident generalities.
