I handle desk triage at a broad theoretical physics journal. Between eight and twelve manuscripts a month reach me in which a substantial part of the case for the proposal is non-empirical, and I have three working days and about forty-five minutes per manuscript to decide whether to desk-reject, to send out for review, or to send out with a written instruction telling referees what they are and are not being asked to adjudicate. I am a working theorist, not a philosopher. I cannot evaluate the technical physics of most of these submissions, and I cannot solve the problem by outsourcing it to reviewers, because my referee pool is split at the level of starting assumptions and I reliably get back two reports that each assert what the other denies. The journal has been burned in both directions: we published a landscape-cosmology piece that drew a public complaint that we had ceased to practice a science, and we desk-rejected on untestability grounds a paper that is now among the most cited things we ever turned down. I must be able to write four sentences to the author justifying any rejection, and any instruction I send to referees has to read to a physicist as something within a physicist's competence to answer.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run on these manuscripts, built out of what the literature on non-empirical (or meta-empirical) confirmation actually says. My own understanding, which you should correct where it is wrong or oversimplified, is that three argument forms are in play: the no-alternatives argument (after sustained effort by people who would have found a rival if one were there, no rival has been produced that does the same work); unexpected explanatory success (a framework built for one purpose turns out to solve problems it was not built to solve); and the meta-inductive argument (frameworks that previously enjoyed the first two kinds of support have tended to hold up when they were finally tested). I also understand that each has a standing objection: that how many alternatives exist depends on how finely one individuates theories and on the sociology of who is working on a problem; that the same forms of argument can be run, and have historically been run, for programs that failed; and that unexpected explanatory success is evidential only if the things explained were independently pinned down rather than adjusted to fit the framework.

The deliverable should be a numbered sequence of questions I ask of a manuscript, in order. For each step give me: what I need in order to answer it, from the manuscript's own text or from one short written question to the authors; what counts as a sufficient answer and what a non-answer looks like in practice; whether that step can terminate the triage in a rejection, and if so the grounds I would state to the author; and where the step exceeds what a non-specialist editor can check and must instead be escalated, and to whom. Where a question is really about the standards the discipline ought to adopt rather than about the manuscript in front of me, say so and route it out of the procedure instead of burying it in a step where it will look like a finding. Include for every exit the sentence or two I would write to the authors and the instruction I would write to the referees. Suggest roughly how the forty-five minutes divides across the steps.

Tell me which distinctions the procedure must preserve and which it may safely collapse for triage purposes. I suspect the difference between a theory's being viable and its being true, and the difference between no test being available in principle and none being available yet, both have to survive the simplification; tell me if you think something else survives, and be explicit about anything from this literature you are deliberately dropping and what a user loses by not having it.

Then run the procedure end to end on three cases. First: a cosmology paper proposing a particular vacuum-selection measure, argued for on the grounds that no rival measure reproduces both the observed cosmological constant to within an order of magnitude and the observed dark-matter-to-baryon ratio, that the measure also yields a neutrino mass ordering compatible with current data which the authors say they did not design it to yield, and that several groups have worked on measures for two decades without producing a competitor; no prediction is within reach of any funded experiment. Second: a beyond-the-Standard-Model construction with no signature at any collider plausibly fundable within thirty years, supported by the fact that it derives from its structure a relation between two couplings that had previously been fitted to data, plus the authors' claim that no rival construction proposed so far is simultaneously anomaly-free and compatible with existing measurements. Third: a perspective submission with no equations, arguing that these argument forms have repeatedly been run for programs that later failed, that publishing them redirects resources away from work that could be tested, and that the journal should require an empirical prospectus as a condition of review; it names no manuscript of ours, offers no positive methodology of its own, and asserts its historical examples without documentation.

One of the three should come out with a verdict I would rather not have to sign, and it should be the procedure that produces it rather than my taste. Say which of the three you expect to be the uncomfortable case for editors in my position generally, and why. Mark plainly every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, and give me the failure profile: what kinds of good paper this will reject and what kinds of bad paper it will pass through. Close with what the procedure cannot do at all, including anything I have asked for that you think cannot honestly be operationalized at triage stage.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present either side of the underlying dispute — that theories with no empirical prospects can be rationally confirmed, or that they cannot — as the consensus view. Wherever the procedure bakes a contested premise into a step, name the premise, say which side of it you have assumed, and say briefly what that step would look like if the other side were right. Write this as something I can keep at my desk and use, not as an essay about it. No bibliography, no reference list, and no citations: attribute views by describing them, and do not supply titles, dates, journal names, or page numbers. If you are not confident that a particular objection or example is in the literature, state it as a structural point about the argument form rather than as something someone said.
