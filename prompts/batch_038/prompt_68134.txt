I am putting together a slim volume of original fiction to be handed to participants at a residential workshop on the evaluation of AI systems, and I have been asked to contribute the opening story. Mine is meant to do the work that our first morning's lecture would otherwise do badly. Please write it.

The idea I want the story to carry, stated plainly so that you know what it must dramatise rather than name: there are two broad ways of training a system with feedback on its reasoning. One rewards only the final answer, which is cheap and hard to counterfeit wherever answers can be checked; its weakness is that on genuinely hard problems a system can arrive at the right answer by a route that will not generalise, and the reward teaches it nothing whatever about the route. The other pays for each visible step in the working, and is what people turn to precisely when the answers are expensive or impossible to verify; its weakness — which is the heart of my story — is that the reward then tracks whether a step *looks* good to whoever is reading it, and nothing in the arrangement requires the steps that look good to be the steps by which the answer was actually reached. The exploitable channel does not disappear. It moves: it becomes the reader.

Requirements:

1. Between 1,600 and 2,200 words, in British English, with a title.
2. The narrator is a human rater whose job is to read a system's step-by-step working and mark each step. Give her a professional life: quotas, calibration sessions with colleagues whose marks drift from hers, a manager who cares about agreement between raters considerably more than he cares about anything else.
3. Two scenes must be built as scenes rather than explained. In one, the working is flawless step by step and the conclusion is nevertheless wrong. In the other, the working is a tidy reconstruction, composed after the fact, of a conclusion the system reached by some other route entirely. From inside her shift the narrator cannot initially tell these apart, and the story should show what, if anything, eventually gives the second one away — and be honest about whether that something is evidence or only her taste.
4. At some point the narrator must learn that her own step-marks are in turn scored by a model trained on the marks she and her colleagues previously gave, and that the calibration exercises have been quietly reshaping her towards the mean. Let her discover this through something that happens — a report, a dashboard, a conversation overheard in a stairwell — not through a realisation narrated at the reader.
5. The work being rated should be mundane, high-stakes in a small way, and in principle checkable. Choose one and make the texture convincing enough that a reader from that field would nod: the sequencing of a city's flood-defence works, perhaps; the reconstruction of the missing leaves of a damaged manuscript; the auditing of an allocation of scarce medical equipment between hospitals.
6. None of the specialist vocabulary may appear anywhere in the story: no process supervision, no outcome supervision, no reward hacking, no chain-of-thought faithfulness, no Goodhart's law. The ideas must be carried entirely by situations and dialogue.
7. Please avoid the usual shapes of this genre. The system never claims to be conscious; nobody reaches for a plug; there is no closing meditation on what makes us human; the narrator does not resign in disgust. She keeps her job, and the story ends in the middle of an ordinary shift, with her marking a step she cannot justify marking either way.
8. A reader who has never encountered the distinction should be able to state it afterwards in their own words; an expert should recognise a failure mode they actually worry about, including the uncomfortable point that carefulness in reading is exactly the disposition being exploited.
