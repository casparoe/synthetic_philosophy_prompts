Our team is finalizing the internal policy that governs an assistant deployed to answer medical and financial questions, and the policy contains exactly one hard truthfulness constraint: the system must never assert a statement that is false. Everything short of that — evasion, careful phrasing, selective emphasis, a literally correct answer to a question the user did not really ask — is left to what the draft calls acceptable discretion. To justify drawing the line precisely there, the draft leans on an analogy from tax law: lying is tax evasion, whereas misleading is tax avoidance. On this picture one is a violation of a rule and the other is merely a distasteful exploitation of the rule's gaps; since a policy can prohibit violations but cannot legislate taste, it prohibits the evasion, tolerates the avoidance, and concludes that a system guaranteed never to lie is a system users can trust.

A colleague objects and proposes a rival analogy: misdirection is like a feint in basketball or a bluff at a poker table — not a breach of the rules but part of the game the participants have consented to play, with an expected penalty structure and a referee who knows what counts as a foul. On her version, faulting the system for misleading is a category mistake about what communication is, and the draft's constraint is not too lenient but conceptually confused.

Please audit both analogies as arguments for their respective conclusions. I am not after a survey of the literature or any references; I want the analysis itself. Specifically:

1. Give the correspondence table for each analogy. What in the target plays the role of the tax code, the gap in the code, the filing obligation, the revenue authority, and the filer's intent? What plays the role of the constitutive rules of the game, the participants' mutual consent to adversarial norms, the scoreboard, the referee, and the possibility of calling a foul? Note any slot in the source that has nothing to correspond to it in the target.

2. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the intended conclusions from the ones that are merely decorative, and say why each belongs in the category you put it in.

3. Identify what each analogy quietly presupposes about the relationship between speaker and hearer — arm's-length adversaries transacting under a written code, or someone relying on another for information they cannot check themselves — and say whether the target case has those features.

4. Assess whether the constraint is even aimed at a well-defined target: does "never asserts a falsehood" pick out a determinate class of behaviors when what a system counts as asserting depends on how it is designed, how it is presented, and what users reasonably expect from it?

5. State whether the draft's trustworthiness conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether the category-mistake charge survives, and whether the two analogies are genuine rivals or are in fact governing different subdomains that need to be distinguished.

6. Offer a better analogy if one is available, or explain why no analogy can carry this particular load and what has to be argued directly instead.

7. Supply five realistic exchanges that would discriminate a merely literally-truthful system from a non-misleading one, and say which of the two policies each exchange exposes.

Close with the single point at which your audit is most contestable — the place where a defender of the tax analogy has the strongest reply — and phrase that reply as they would. Please write in American English.
