I keep going back and forth on the surprise exam paradox and I've realized I don't actually know what I should be demanding of a resolution — there's like a dozen of them floating around and most of them seem to "work" if you squint hard enough, so I can't tell which ones are actually responsive to the problem. So before I wade back in: write me the spec. What does any adequate solution have to deliver?

Give me the requirements with the case or consideration sitting behind each one. Stuff I already suspect belongs on the list: does it have to say where the students' backwards reasoning goes wrong, or is it enough to insist that it does? Does it have to leave room for the teacher to have made an announcement that was true, and known by the students to be true, at the time she made it? What has to happen differently in the one-day version versus the five-day version — if a solution can't tell me why the n=1 case feels unproblematic and the n=5 case doesn't, is that a failure or just an incompleteness? Does it owe me an account of why the same style of inductive reasoning is perfectly respectable in ordinary cases where nothing self-referential is going on? How should it treat the fact that the content of the announcement is partly about the students' own epistemic states — is that feature essential to the puzzle or incidental to it? And is a solution allowed to lean on these particular students being bad reasoners, or does it have to be a story that would still bite for ideally rational ones? Add the ones I'm missing, I'm sure there are several.

Then sort them: which are non-negotiable for anything to count as a solution at all, and which are really just the tastes of one camp — the people who want it cashed out in epistemic logic versus the people who want it cashed out in degrees of belief versus the people who think the whole thing is a verbal trick. You don't need to name anyone, just characterize the camps.

The part I actually most want: tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, because if it is, then the list is itself the result and the "solutions" are just people quietly dropping different items. My hunch is that something like (a) the announcement was true when made, (b) the students knew it and knew they knew it, and (c) the surprise is a genuine epistemic surprise rather than a merely psychological one, can't all be held at once — but I might have the wrong triple. Check my hunch, say whether you agree or which three items you'd pick instead, and tell me which single requirement buys the most if you give it up.

Two constraints on the exercise. First, I care about a variant where the teacher only promises that on the morning of the exam their creedence that it's happening that day will be under some threshold, not that they'll flat-out fail to know. So the list should say what a solution has to handle in that setting, and whether reframing the paradox probabilistically counts as solving it or just relocating it to a seperate puzzle about thresholds and updating. Second, no bibliography and no scoreboard of named solutions — I don't want a lit review, I want the criteria and the tensions between them.
