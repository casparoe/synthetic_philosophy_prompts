I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of language, and at the end of next month I have been asked by a family rather than by my department to speak at a memorial service in a university chapel that seats about a hundred and twenty people. It is for Adela Soru, who died in March at eighty-eight. She was the last fluent speaker of the language I will call Orenai here — the community's own name for it is longer and I would rather not have you approximate it, so please leave a bracketed slot wherever a proper name is wanted — and, unusually, she was also a trained linguist who spent her second career documenting her own first language. The third chapter of my dissertation is about that work, so this is not an abstract commission for me; but almost nobody in the room will have read it. The audience will be roughly a third her family and people from the town she grew up in, a third former students and colleagues from a faculty of education, and a third linguists, anthropologists, and two or three philosophers, of whom I am one. Her daughter Ana will be in the front row. Ana was raised in the city, never learned Orenai, and has said in interviews that she feels she has lost her mother twice: once to death, and once, decades earlier, to a language she does not have.

I want the speech built out of Davidson's account of radical interpretation, but built out of it rather than about it: his name should appear at most once, and the phrases "principle of charity" and "triangulation" should not appear at all, though the ideas had better be unmistakable to anyone who knows them. Here are the materials. Adela disliked, and said so publicly, the usual consolation offered at a language death — the one about libraries burning, or about a whole way of seeing the world going out of the world. Her objection was philosophical, and it is the objection I want the speech to carry: what died with her was not a store of information we had failed to copy down, but the last person whom we could be corrected by. She used to say that the documentation team's real achievement was not the corpus but the fact that, after thirty years, they had finally become able to disagree with her about her own language — disagreement being, on her account, the evidence that interpretation had succeeded rather than a sign that it had failed. I would like that thought to be the spine of the address, because it is what lets me speak to Ana honestly: she and her mother disagreed about nearly everything, in a shared world, without a shared vocabulary, and understood each other completely. For the last decade of her life Adela was in an unresolved argument with the team about a single form they had glossed as "to remember," which she thought should be glossed more like "to be kept by," with the remembered thing as the agent; she never settled it, and came to think her own testimony could not settle it. I want the speech to hold that dispute — the way one decision about one verb pulled on everything else she had ever said — without resolving it, and specifically without resolving it by an appeal to her private access to her own meanings, which she did not claim to have and which Davidson spent his life arguing nobody has. There is also one grammar fact I would like used carefully: Orenai has no single verb that translates as "know," using one construction for what you found out yourself and a different one for what someone who was present told you. Please do not deploy this as evidence that her thought was incommensurable with ours, which would be both the wrong moral and a bad reading of Davidson, and do not deploy it as a charming factoid either.

A colleague of mine has been arguing that the principle of charity is a tax on the strange: the more agreement you are obliged to assume at the outset, the less your interlocutor is permitted to astonish you, and on this view thirty years of documentation was a long process of making Orenai sound like a regional dialect of our own thinking. I do not accept that, but I cannot dismiss it, and at least one person who will hear this speech does accept it. So the address should take it seriously and answer it — not with a slogan, and not by retreating into the archive cliché it is a reaction against. If you think the speech has to choose between honoring Ana's sense that part of her mother was unreachable and refusing the thought that any language is in principle untranslatable into any other, I would rather you found a way to do both, and I would rather you told me plainly if you think that cannot be done.

Some further constraints. About seven minutes, so roughly nine hundred words; secular at the family's request, and Adela was an atheist of long standing, so nothing in it may presuppose her continuing anywhere or watching over anyone, and nothing may say what I would consider false about death. Address Ana directly at least once, but do not tell her that her mother was partly closed to her, and do not comfort her by explaining linguistics at her. No technical vocabulary, no quotation from Davidson, and no mention of my dissertation: this is a funeral, not a seminar. Leave bracketed slots for any concrete particular you would otherwise have to invent — dates, place names, the two or three memories I will supply myself — and do not add linguistic details beyond the ones I have given you. Finally, after the speech, add five or six lines telling me which idea is doing the structural work in which passage, so that I can check whether the philosophy is load-bearing or merely decorative, and flag anywhere you think you have bent Davidson to make a better sentence.
