Six months ago I was appointed to my city's Historic District Commission, and Thursday's hearing presents the first case that has genuinely split us. I would like a written opinion to circulate to the other commissioners in advance of the meeting.

The facts, so far as the record supports them. In 1979 the commission designated the Calloway Grist Mill — a timber-frame structure on its original stone foundation at the same bend in the creek since 1848 — under an ordinance protecting any structure that substantially retains its historic fabric. The ordinance nowhere defines that phrase. Commission guidance from 1998 added a rule of thumb: no single campaign of replacement may remove more than half of the principal structural members within any rolling twenty-year window. Every campaign the mill has undergone passed that test at the time it was reviewed: roof rafters in 1986, sill beams in 1994, roughly a third of the siding in 2003, two of the five floor-joist bays sistered or scarf-spliced in 2011, the waterwheel rebuilt in 2016. Cumulatively, the petitioner's engineer now counts 68 percent of principal members and 41 percent of all fabric as post-1979 material. The petitioner argues the designation has lapsed by degrees and asks us to declare it void. The owner argues it has been the same building throughout — same foundation, same work, same silhouette — and that every change was approved by this commission, which followed the national rehabilitation standards that prefer repair over replacement and accept matching new work where replacement is unavoidable.

A wrinkle the petitioner intends to press: in 2019 a developer raised a replica mill three blocks away, to the same drawings and joinery, entirely from new timber, in eighteen months. Their argument is that if our concern is design and craft, the replica satisfies it completely and the Calloway Mill no longer does; if our concern is fabric, the Calloway Mill has lost most of it. On either reading, they say, we are protecting nothing in particular.

I know roughly what the philosophical literature makes of heaps, and I am not asking for a seminar on it. No reading lists, no citations, no invented case law, and no pretending the outcome turns on what some author held. I want that material used the way a commissioner would use it: to determine whether part of this dispute is a dispute about what our own words commit us to, and to choose a rule we can actually administer. Please gloss any technical term in a clause rather than sourcing it.

Write the opinion in the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

1. **Recommendation** (~150 words). What I should move on Thursday — uphold, void, or uphold subject to conditions — with the conditions stated concretely.

2. **What the ordinance is asking of us** (~350 words). Whether "substantially retains its historic fabric" has a determinate answer here; if not, whether the shortfall lies in the building, in our language, or in our knowledge of where the line falls. Say explicitly whether any of those three diagnoses changes how we should rule, and if none of them does, say that too.

3. **The rolling window and why it failed** (~400 words). How a rule that every individual campaign satisfied can still condemn the cumulative result, and whether the remedy is a cumulative test, a shorter window, a cap on total loss, or a different object of assessment altogether. Address the 2011 joists directly: a member that is sistered and spliced is neither wholly original nor wholly new, and our ledger has to count it one way or the other.

4. **The replica** (~300 words). The strongest form of the petitioner's either/or, and whether any rule can retain the Calloway Mill without thereby admitting the 2019 replica — including whether a rule can turn on the difference between eighteen months of construction and forty years of it.

5. **On drawing a line we cannot justify** (~350 words). If the honest position is that we must fix a bright boundary where none exists, whether that is legitimate or arbitrary in a way that matters to this owner and to anyone who relied on our past approvals; and whether a threshold adopted in 2026 can properly be applied to fabric already lost, or only prospectively.

6. **The strongest case against my recommendation** (~250 words). Written so that the petitioner could sign it without revision.
