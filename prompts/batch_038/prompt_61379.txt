You are a philosopher of mind and language whose research covers theories of mental content — covariational and informational accounts (the Dretske–Fodor line), teleosemantics (the Millikan line), and conceptual-role semantics (the Block–Harman line) — and who follows current debate about what large trained systems contribute to those older arguments. I am a professor preparing a graduate seminar session on the symbol grounding problem, and I would like you to design a thought experiment for it. Please think the problem through carefully and at length before writing anything down; I would much rather have one well-engineered case than three promising sketches, and I want the design to drive the analysis rather than the other way round.

**What the case is for.** My students arrive already committed to a deflationary line: any observable difference between a system whose terms are wired into a sensorimotor loop and a system whose terms were acquired entirely from other people's words can be re-described as a difference in the quality or quantity of the information it was given, so grounding turns out to do no explanatory work of its own. I want a case that puts that line under maximum pressure. Whether your design actually breaks it or ends up confirming it is something I want reported honestly at the end, not assumed in advance.

**Start with a design phase, before the scenario.** Sketch three candidate designs in a few sentences each, identify for each the way it would most likely be dissolved by a deflationist, and then say why you are rejecting two of them and building the third. Do not let this phase run past roughly a third of your total answer.

**Then the scenario itself, to the following specification.**

1. Setting: present-day, mundane, commercial or agricultural. The system is a machine with sensors and effectors doing a job somewhere; no creatures, no brains, no humanoid robots, no virtual environments, no speculative technology.
2. One predicate in focus — a single term the system applies — and it must be a term about which the language community is in perfect agreement, so that the case cannot be dissolved by saying the second system was simply misinformed or under-instructed.
3. Two variants. G acquires the predicate through a closed loop: its classifications drive an effector, and the results of that effector's work come back into its sensors and shape subsequent classification. U acquires the predicate entirely from other symbols — it has been given, in words and labels, everything the community has to say about the term and its relations to other terms. The difference between them must not be reducible to how much either was told.
4. Matched performance: across an arbitrarily long pre-test period, G and U are indistinguishable to an outside observer, including on every question a linguist could pose about how the predicate relates to other predicates. They diverge on exactly one realistic episode, and on that episode one of them is right about the world rather than merely different.
5. Explanatory hygiene: no consciousness, no qualia, no inner theatre, no "understanding," no what-it-is-like. Every claim you make about either system must be cashable in third-personal description of inputs, outputs, dispositions, and histories.
6. No stock cases. Not a person in a room consulting rulebooks, not a dictionary that defines its words only by other words of the same language, not a cephalopod acquiring a language purely by watching its speakers, not a duplicate planet, not a scientist who has never seen a colour. If your case is one of these repainted, it fails.
7. Length: the scenario proper in a single paragraph, under roughly 250 words.

**Then the demonstration that it meets the specification.**

(a) State the predicate, and for each variant give the causal chain that produces its tokens, what its tokens go on to cause, and the inferential relations the token stands in to the system's other representations.
(b) For each of the three content theories named above, say whether G's tokens have the content they intuitively have, whether U's do, and what the theory predicts about the divergence episode. Be explicit wherever two theories coincide; the informative result is whichever theory predicts the divergence and whichever predicts none.
(c) Identify the single structural feature of your case that is doing the work, and then run two ablations: keep the sensors but remove the effector, so the system is a purely passive observer coupled to the world; and keep the effector but remove the return path from its results, so the loop is open. In each case say whether the verdicts of the three theories change, and whether G and U still come apart.
(d) Give the strongest attack available to whichever theory comes out worst — in particular, whether the divergence can be recast as a difference in reliability rather than in content, and what your reply is.
(e) Verdict: does the case show grounding carrying explanatory weight that a difference in information supply cannot absorb, or does my students' deflationary line survive it intact? Commit to one, do not hedge, and say what further fact about the two systems would settle it.

**Two final housekeeping rules.** If any two of my specifications turn out to conflict, say so explicitly and explain how you resolved the conflict, rather than quietly dropping one. And please build the answer out of your own exposition: gloss each standard distinction you rely on in a sentence or two, do not assemble it from citations, and append no bibliography or reading list, since my students cannot look anything up. Paraphrase rather than quote; I check every set of quotation marks against a source, so put nothing in quotes unless you are certain it is verbatim, and it is better to put nothing in quotes at all.
