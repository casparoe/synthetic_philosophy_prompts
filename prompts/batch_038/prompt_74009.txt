I'm a fourth-year philosophy PhD student and this term I'm co-teaching a cross-listed seminar, something like "Explanation, Reduction, and the Computational Turn" — about half philosophy students, half from the CS department, which is honestly a nightmare to pitch for. I've been put in charge of the take-home final and I want ONE essay question on the philosophical significance of computational complexity that the CS students can't answer by reciting definitions and the philosophy students can't answer by recycling the popular-science riff about how, if P = NP, there'd be no gap left between appreciating a work of genius and producing one. I got into an argument with a friend in the complexity group last week that clarified what I actually want to test: whether the philosophical payoff everyone attributes to this area comes from the NP-completeness theorems, which are *proved*, or from P ≠ NP, which is a conjecture — and whether anybody has noticed that they're doing very different kinds of work in the same argument. The inference I want them to have to dismantle is the one from "problem X is NP-complete" to "X is intractable, and therefore no mind or machine can solve instances of X that matter." That looks licensed by the theory, but it smuggles in a pile of separate assumptions: worst case versus average or typical case, exact solution versus approximation or heuristic, an idealised resource model (asymptotic polynomial time on an abstract machine) standing in for "hard in practice," the question of whether the reduction even maps realistic instances to realistic instances, and finally the unproven conjecture itself. I'd like the question to force them to say which of those assumptions can be discharged and which can't, and what's left standing.

To make sure nobody has to look anything up — the exam is closed book and I don't want them guessing at references — I'll hand them a short technical appendix with the question, so please write the question so that it explicitly leans on these two items and tell me roughly how to gloss them there. First, Impagliazzo's five complexity worlds from his mid-nineties average-case paper: Algorithmica (P = NP, so NP is easy even in the worst case), Heuristica (P ≠ NP but the hardness never bites, because NP problems turn out to be easy on average or in practice), Pessiland (NP genuinely hard on average but no one-way functions, so you get the difficulty without any of the cryptographic benefit), Minicrypt (one-way functions exist, private-key cryptography possible, public-key not), and Cryptomania (public-key cryptography possible). I want them to use the taxonomy to answer something like: which world would we have to be living in for a given "this real-world problem is NP-complete, therefore it's beyond us" claim to be true, and what would we have to know to rule the others out — noting that we can't currently tell Heuristica from Minicrypt, which is exactly what makes the philosophical inference shaky. Second, the Razborov–Rudich natural proofs result, which says roughly that if sufficiently strong one-way functions exist, then a broad and very natural family of proof techniques — ones that identify a property satisfied by many functions and checkable quickly — cannot establish the circuit lower bounds that P ≠ NP would need. That's the fun part for my purposes: on plausible assumptions, the conjecture that would deliver the philosophical payoff is one that resists the most obvious strategies for proving it, and I want students to have to say what a philosopher is entitled to build on a premise in that epistemic position, and whether "conditional on an open conjecture" is a fatal defect or just ordinary philosophy.

So, deliverables: (1) the question itself, under about 250 words, anchored in two or three concrete NP-completeness claims about real practice — airline crew scheduling, optimal facility location, exact probabilistic inference or MAP in Bayesian networks, and if you like a lattice-model version of protein structure prediction, though I know that last one is contested, so maybe have them contest it — rather than a free-floating "discuss the significance of P versus NP"; (2) a model answer of around 800 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 25, split into five or six named points with the marks each is worth, which separates an A from a B by saying what specific moves the answer has to make, and for each point a blunt one-liner on what a typical weak answer does instead. I already know the two failure modes I need the scheme to catch and cost marks for: the philosophy student who treats "NP-hard" as a synonym for "impossible" or "mysteriously beyond human creativity" and never notices the worst-case/average-case gap or the idealisation in the resource model, and the CS student who waves the whole thing off as a technicality, gets the computer science exactly right, and never engages with why conditionality on an unproven conjecture is a genuine epistemological problem rather than a bookkeeping annoyance. 90 minutes, no notes, and please keep the question and the appendix seperate from the model answer so I can paste them straight into the exam paper.
