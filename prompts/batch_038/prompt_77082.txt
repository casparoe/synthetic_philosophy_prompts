I have spent most of my working life in an accredited calibration laboratory, and for the past year I have been reading philosophy of science in the evenings, which has made my day job look strange to me. Our uncertainty budgets are built to the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (the GUM, currently JCGM 100:2008), and accreditation to ISO/IEC 17025 effectively requires it. The routine is this. Some error contributions we estimate by actually measuring repeatedly and taking a standard deviation of the mean; those are called Type A. But most contributions come from knowledge we did not generate ourselves: a certificate states that a gauge block's deviation from nominal is no worse than some half-width, a digital multimeter displays to a fixed number of digits, a bath is specified to hold within a stated band. For each of those, called Type B, we are told that in the absence of anything further we should treat the possible values as uniformly spread across the interval, take the half-width a, and divide by the square root of three, giving roughly 0.577a as a standard uncertainty. If we think values near the centre are more likely, we use a triangular shape instead and divide by the square root of six. We then combine everything in quadrature and multiply the result by a coverage factor of two, reporting that as approximately 95 percent coverage. Nobody in my lab has ever questioned the divisor; it is simply what the software does.

Three things now bother me. First, the divisor is not invariant under how we choose to describe the quantity. A resistance known to lie within plus or minus half an ohm is uniform in resistance, but that same state of knowledge is decidedly not uniform in conductance, which is its reciprocal, nor in the logarithm of the resistance, and each of those parameterizations would hand us a different number after the same division. The rule seems to pick out the scale in which the bound happened to be written on the certificate, as though that scale were metaphysically privileged. Second, we are told the flat shape is licensed by knowing nothing beyond the bounds, yet the alternative triangular shape is licensed by a vague feeling that mid-interval values are commoner; if the shape can be changed by an unquantified hunch, what is the flat shape actually asserting about the world, or about us? Third, we take these knowledge-based numbers, which are not frequencies of anything, and add them in quadrature to genuine observed standard deviations, then multiply by two and make a coverage claim that is only roughly right if the combined distribution is close to normal, which it plainly is not when one large rectangular term dominates the budget.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory here, not a defence of the procedure. Which view of probability, and of what an uncertainty statement is about, does the Type B rule presuppose in order to make sense at all, and which rival views does it thereby rule out from day-to-day practice? Please be careful to separate what the rule strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably alongside it, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories would each license exactly the same arithmetic. Does the parameterization worry I raised actually bite, or is it dissolved by the fact that our bounds are inherited from instruments that literally display values on one scale, so that the scale is not a free choice? Is there a genuine inconsistency between the epistemic Type B terms and the frequentist Type A terms and the coverage claim, or is the combination coherent under some single account I have not spotted? Finally, take each of two replacements in turn: a fully personalist Bayesian treatment in which every budget is just somebody's prior updated on data, and a strict frequentist treatment in which only long-run behaviour counts. For each, say concretely what would have to change in what a working lab does on a Tuesday morning, and what the lab would lose.
