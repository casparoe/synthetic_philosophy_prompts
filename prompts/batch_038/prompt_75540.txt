I am a junior analyst in a unit that publishes long-horizon political forecasts, and every estimate must be signed off by our director of long-range estimates. Her function at sign-off is narrow and specific: she rules on which reference class the team is permitted to use, and her four years of short memos now operate as our case law. A proposal of mine goes to her next week. I want to anticipate her ruling before I draft the memo, both so that I can argue it well and so that I can see clearly what she is actually committed to.

Her rulings to date, paraphrased from the memos:

1. On the expected tenure of a military government that had just seized power in a lower-middle-income state, the team offered three classes: all governments installed by coup since 1945 (n = 141); juntas that took power during a balance-of-payments crisis (n = 22); and juntas that took power in a state where the military had governed at some point in the previous generation (n = 4). She required the class of twenty-two, writing that narrowness is not itself a virtue and that four cases are a list of anecdotes rather than a distribution.

2. On whether a long-ruling single-party state would remain under the same party a decade out, the team proposed a class matched on region, regime type and decade (n = 61) and a class matched on the party having just completed a contested succession inside its own politburo (n = 7). She required the class of seven and accepted the very wide interval it produced, arguing that pooling cases whose outcomes were generated by different processes yields a spread that describes nothing. She noted that the effect of contested intra-party succession on party survival was supported by work independent of those seven cases. She also refused the team's request to shade the estimate upward on the grounds that the regime's economic management had been competent, on the basis that this was a story about the case rather than a difference she could have coded for the class members in advance.

3. On the durability of a personalist regime after the ruler's death, the team proposed personalist successions in which the successor had been head of the internal security service (n = 11). She refused it on two grounds: all eleven fell within a single decade and a single region, so the class spread captured that period's global conditions rather than the phenomenon; and the causal account linking security-chief succession to consolidation was an appealing hypothesis whose evidence came almost entirely from the same cases it was invoked to explain. She directed the team instead to begin from all personalist successions since 1945 (n = 178) and to adjust from that distribution, with each adjustment documented and each one tied to a feature that could have been coded for class members ex ante.

4. Twice she has rejected classes assembled by looking at who endured. In one instance a team proposed regimes that had faced a severe debt crisis in their third decade of one-party rule and were still standing five years after the crisis. She struck it, writing that a class may be specified only from what could have been known at the moment of forecasting and never by reference to how its members turned out.

5. On whether a federal coalition would survive its first full term, the mechanism-matched class contained three members. Rather than revert to a broad class, she permitted a weighted composite — sixty per cent on the mechanism-matched three, forty per cent on twenty-nine coalitions matched on parliamentary arithmetic — and required the memo to display the estimate under each weighting. She observed that the mechanism in question was among the better-established in the field.

The new proposal concerns a dominant-party regime in a resource-dependent middle-income state, now in its thirty-eighth year of continuous rule. A succession occurred fourteen months ago, to the former head of the internal security service; a coup attempt failed eleven months ago; external debt denominated in foreign currency stands at roughly forty-five per cent of GDP; the economy has recorded its first annual contraction in twenty-two years; there has been no sustained mass protest. We must publish a probability that the same party still holds power, in substantially its present form, fifteen years out.

Our dataset supports five classes, each specified solely from information available today:

(i) dominant-party regimes that had passed twenty years in power at the forecast date (n = 38);
(ii) dominant-party regimes in which a coup attempt failed within the preceding twenty-four months (n = 4, drawn from three decades and two regions), the proposed mechanism being that a failed coup simultaneously exposes and purges a ruler's elite rivals — our literature note cites one study coding four decades of cases and one case-study collection drawn largely from these same four regimes, which disagree about the sign of the effect;
(iii) resource-dependent autocracies that suffered a first contraction in two decades while carrying foreign-currency debt above forty per cent of GDP (n = 21, spanning four regions and five decades);
(iv) dominant-party regimes in their fourth decade of rule that weathered a debt crisis and were still standing five years afterwards (n = 9);
(v) all autocracies, of any institutional type, in their thirty-eighth year of continuous rule (n = 74).

With that record in hand:

- Reconstruct her decision procedure. Which general rules governing reference class selection are consistent with all five rulings, which are excluded by them, and where does the record leave more than one rule genuinely standing? Be explicit about the evidential conditions she appears to impose on a class justified by a causal story, as distinct from a class justified by descriptive matching, and about the status of any sample-size threshold.
- Predict her ruling on the new case: which class or composite she will require, and which she will strike. Address (iv) directly in light of ruling 4, and then address the harder question of whether the principle she stated there also reaches (i) and (v), which condition on the regime's having survived to the forecast date but not beyond it. Give the reading you believe she holds and defend it rather than merely noting that her wording permits two.
- Identify which of the five rulings is the closest precedent, where the analogy strains, and — since (ii), (iii) and (v) are each defensible under a coherent reading of her record — what she would be committing herself to by ruling as you predict. Note in particular that ruling 2 supplies an argument against (v) and ruling 3 an argument for it.
- Say whether she would allow the team to adjust away from the chosen class's distribution on account of the failed coup, given that ruling 2 refused an adjustment and ruling 3 permitted a series of them, and state the criterion that distinguishes the two.
- Finally, specify the case that would most cleanly discriminate between the two readings you cannot separate on the present record: describe its features and its candidate classes with their sizes, and say what each surviving rule would produce. Say also whether any single case could separate the two grounds she offered in ruling 3, or whether they travel together.
