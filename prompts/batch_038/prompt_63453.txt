Last month I was appointed to a state advisory panel that recommends how our Medicaid agency should divide a fixed pool of money among competing new programs, and the work has dropped me into the middle of an argument I am not equipped to resolve. I am a clinician by training, not an economist. The panel is currently choosing between two proposals: one would fund a therapy for roughly sixty patients with an advanced illness, expected to add several years of decent-quality life to each; the other would fund long-term management of a mildly disabling chronic condition for about four thousand people, each of whom would gain a small but durable improvement. Our staff produced quality-adjusted-life-year estimates for both, and one member of the panel — a retired economics professor — objected that the entire exercise rests on a category mistake. His position, as I understand it, is that utility is ordinal and that each person's utility function is determined only up to a positive affine transformation of that person's own, so there is no common zero point and no common scale across people; consequently, summing wellbeing gains across individuals is not an empirical finding but a smuggled value judgment, in the Robbinsian vein. He insists this is fatal to the comparison, not a quibble about it.

Other members push back. They note that the compensation criterion used in cost-benefit analysis appears to make just such comparisons all the time; that various aggregation results in welfare economics seem to derive an equal-weight sum of individual utilities from quite thin assumptions; and that if the professor is right, the Pareto principle alone cannot rank our two proposals at all, since the two programs help disjoint groups of people and no one is made worse off by either. That last point is what worries me most: if the objection succeeds, I do not see what is left to write.

I have been asked to draft the methodological appendix defending whatever the panel finally decides, and I do not want to write something I privately believe to be hollow, nor to wave away a serious objection with a slogan. To think it through, please write me a dialogue.

Requirements:

- Two characters, invented names of your choosing, meeting over the panel's two proposals so that the abstractions have something concrete to bite on. One is a rigorous skeptic about interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing; the other defends both the possibility and the legitimate practical use of such comparisons. Both must be first-rate: no strawmen, no softballs, and neither may win by a trick of rhetoric.
- The dialogue should genuinely engage, rather than merely name, the following: the affine-transformation and zero-point problem, and whether it rules out comparisons or only one particular metric for making them; the charge that any comparison imports a value judgment, and whether granting that charge makes such comparisons illegitimate or merely non-positive; the route from Harsanyi-style aggregation results to an equal-weight sum of utilities, together with the strongest objection that this is a representation theorem whose conclusion is already latent in its assumptions; the idea that comparability may come in degrees or be partial rather than all-or-nothing, and what a merely partial informational basis would license for our choice; whether the compensation criterion escapes the objection or simply conceals the comparison inside a counterfactual; and, if the skeptic prevails, what he can offer the panel instead — and whether that alternative can actually decide between sixty large gains and four thousand small ones.
- Make it a real exchange. Each character should respond to what the other just said, probe weak points, and at least once concede something or refine a position under pressure. No alternating essays.
- Roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in dialogue format with speaker labels. Scene-setting and stage directions should be sparing.
- Do not let either character abruptly convert at the end; if they end closer together, show precisely which ground was given up and why.
- After the dialogue, add a short out-of-dialogue section under a clear heading listing the points on which the two would honestly agree — material I could put in my appendix without misrepresenting either side — followed by the one or two places where their disagreement looks irreducible, and what would have to be settled to close them.
- Write in American English. You may refer to real economists and real arguments by name where it helps, but do not put words in anyone's mouth: no invented quotations, no page or section references, and no passage presented as verbatim from a real text. Describe arguments in your own words instead.
