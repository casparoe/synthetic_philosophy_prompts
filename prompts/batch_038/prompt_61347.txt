I convene a third-year seminar in the philosophy of history whose closing unit is on periodization, and I am setting the final examination. The paper is closed book and consists of a single essay question worth 40 marks, to be attempted in 90 minutes. I would like you to draft the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

What I want to avoid is the recitation my students are best equipped to produce. They arrive able to rehearse, accurately and at length, the standard debunking material: that "the Middle Ages" is a label fastened on an interval by people who conceived of themselves as standing after it and above it; that the great nineteenth-century account of the Renaissance tied the period to the emergence of the modern individual; that the "Scientific Revolution" is to a considerable degree a construction of the century that named it. None of that should earn marks, because none of it requires understanding. I want the question to force the conceptual issue instead — what would have to be true for a period label to pick out something real rather than something merely convenient, and whether the lateness and the interestedness of the coinage bear on that question at all.

The device I want the question built around is a proposal I have invented for this paper. No historian has advanced it, and neither the question nor the model answer should imply that it is an established term of art. A colleague proposes to retire "the Renaissance," in its broad cultural sense, and replace it with "the Long Recovery," running from the mid-fourteenth-century demographic collapse to the early sixteenth century. The claim is that the new label earns its place not by enumerating admired achievements — linear perspective, humanist philology, the rediscovery of a lost ancient poem on the nature of things — but by naming a mechanism: the rebuilding of population, wages, textual supply, universities, and lay administration after the crisis, a mechanism with a discernible onset and a discernible exhaustion, after which a different set of dynamics takes hold. On this view the boundaries of a period are set by the mechanism rather than by the historian's taste.

The question must be self-contained, so that a candidate can attempt it with nothing beyond the description above and the seminar. It should require candidates to do three things: give an account of what makes a period label pick out something real; state whether late and interested coinage is relevant to that account, and if so how; and rule on whether "the Long Recovery" improves on "the Renaissance" by the account they have defended. A candidate should not be able to reach a verdict on the third part without having committed themselves on the first two.

Please supply the following. First, the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the mark allocation and the stipulated description of the proposal included. Second, a model answer of 900 to 1,100 words, written as a self-contained argument with no footnotes, no bibliography, and no citations; where it deploys philosophical machinery — cluster-based accounts of what makes something a genuine kind, the difference between classifications that merely sort their objects and classifications that change what they sort, the possibility that a dispute is merely verbal — it should explain that machinery rather than attach a name to it and move on. Third, a marking scheme totalling 40 marks, organised around five or six must-hit moves, each with the marks it carries, a statement of what a candidate must actually say in order to earn them, and the characteristic way in which candidates get it wrong. No marks should be allocated for attribution or for the recitation of scholars' names. Fourth, a short paragraph separating a first-class answer from a strong second-class one in terms of moves rather than topics.

Three sophisticated-but-wrong manoeuvres should be singled out in the marking scheme as costing marks, each with a sentence explaining why it fails: inferring from "constructed" to "not real"; treating the interest-relativity of every classification scheme as though it were a debunking argument against some particular ones; and appealing to the feedback effects of naming an age so as to make any period label whatsoever automatically real. Finally, tell me what a fluent, well-informed answer consisting entirely of historiographical survey should score, and justify the figure in two or three sentences, so that markers who disagree with me can see the reasoning.
