I'm a software engineer — eight years of backend and ML infrastructure work, no philosophy coursework beyond a survey text I read on my own — and I'd like to pitch a 4,000–5,000 word paper to an interdisciplinary workshop on the epistemology of machine-generated text. I have three months of evenings to write it. Please turn my half-formed idea into a proposal.

The idea is this. As I understand it, the classical dispute about testimony runs between those who hold that a hearer needs positive reasons, grounded in observed reliability, before she is entitled to believe what she is told, and those who hold that there is a default entitlement to trust a speaker absent some specific defeater. What strikes me is that both sides seem to be arguing under a background condition that my day job routinely violates. For a human informant, positive reasons about reliability are expensive, coarse-grained, and mostly unavailable; for a deployed model they are cheap, fine-grained, and continuously regenerated. We run evaluations. We know that a given system handles one class of question at roughly 94 percent and another at 61 percent, that its accuracy degrades on inputs drawn from outside a distribution, that a change to the retrieval pipeline moves its reliability on a particular topic. If the reductionist's "positive reasons" just means measured reliability at the appropriate granularity, then in the machine case reductionism stops looking like a contested thesis and starts looking like an engineering routine. And yet none of that appears to settle whether I should trust a given output on a given occasion, because the numbers I hold are aggregate and the question is local.

There is a further wrinkle that I suspect makes things worse rather than better: an increasing share of those measurements are produced by one model grading another, so the positive reasons I would cite for trusting a machine speaker are themselves the output of a machine speaker. Either that loop is vicious, in which case the reductionist demand cannot be met even where measurement is cheapest, or it is simply the ordinary human predicament made visible.

So the question I want the paper to answer — and I want your help sharpening it until it has a determinate answer — is whether the availability of large-scale, fine-grained reliability statistics about machine speakers collapses the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate in that domain, reveals it to have been non-substantive all along, or leaves it exactly where it was, with each side able to absorb the statistics. Connected to it, a second question: if the domain in which the reductionist's demand is actually satisfiable turns out to be the domain where the speaker is not a person, does that show the default-entitlement view was doing work that had nothing to do with reliability and everything to do with features of persons — sincerity, an interest in being believed, participation in a shared practice — which a model lacks?

The proposal should include: a precise statement of the question or questions; an account of why the existing positions do not already settle it, including any place where I have misdescribed those positions, since I am working from memory of secondary summaries; the method, that is, what I would actually do, with a rough month-by-month plan given evenings only; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing, since I would rather learn now that the question is a dud than in month three; the alternative approaches I should avoid and why, and in particular why this is not simply the familiar question of whether an artificial system can testify at all, which I suspect has been covered extensively and is not what I want to write about; and an honest verdict on scope, including which of the two questions I should cut if only one fits.

Two notes on how you write it. I will add the references myself, so please do not produce a bibliography or a reading list; describe positions and arguments and I will match them to sources. And throughout, mark clearly which of your claims about the state of the debate are common ground among people who work on it and which are contested readings — I have no independent way of telling the two apart, and the last thing I want is to walk into a workshop asserting as settled what is in fact one philosopher's disputed reconstruction. Where my framing above assumes something the literature does not assume, say so directly rather than quietly building the proposal on top of the assumption.
