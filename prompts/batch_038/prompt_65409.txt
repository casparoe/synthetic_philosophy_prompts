I have been asked to write a commentary on a research programme in computational psychiatry that describes its goal, in every document it produces, as a periodic table of psychopathology. The argument runs roughly like this: today's diagnostic categories are what the alchemists' elements and the four humors were — a clutter of surface appearances, drawn from complaint and convention, awaiting reduction; the periodic table shows what a mature classification actually looks like, a small number of underlying properties generating a lawful structure whose gaps turned out to be filled by things no one had yet found. From this the programme draws two conclusions: that effort should go into identifying the ordering property rather than into negotiating diagnostic manuals, and that philosophers who object that psychiatric kinds are interest-relative, disjunctive, or partly constituted by our own practices of labelling are simply repeating the alchemists' objections to chemistry, and can be waved away.

I want to audit the analogy rather than either accept it or sneer at it. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what plays the role of the element, of the ordering property, of periodicity, of the gaps, of the predicted substances, and of the alchemist who will not look up from his furnace. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the argument from those that are merely cosmetic. Two worry me in particular. First, whether there is any real counterpart to the ordering property: a candidate (a latent dimension, a biomarker, a polygenic score) may be many-to-one with the phenomena it is supposed to order, and the chemical ordering property turned out to have a basis in the structure of the atom, which is hard to imagine a psychiatric candidate matching. Second, whether the analogy can survive the fact that the classified respond to being classified, reorganize themselves in light of the categories offered to them, and thereby shift the extension of those categories over time — something samples of gallium conspicuously do not do.

I would also like you to say what conclusion, if any, the analogy can bear. My suspicion is that it supports something weaker and better than what the programme claims — an ideal of generative economy, or a standard for when a taxonomy has begun to earn its keep — and that it cannot carry the dismissal of interest-relativity; but make the case on both sides. And tell me whether the critics' preferred analogies, that these categories are like constellations or like the folk kind weed, fare any better under the same treatment, or whether they are guilty of the same smuggling in reverse.

Throughout, keep the philosophy of natural kinds honest: essentialist, cluster-based, and causal-explanatory accounts of what makes a kind natural will not agree about whether a periodic table is even the right thing to aspire to, and I would rather see that disagreement do work here than be papered over. If you think some other analogy would carry the programme's legitimate ambition without importing chemistry's metaphysical comfort, offer it and show me the mapping.

Finally, please mark where your own analysis is most likely to fail. I would like a closing passage saying what would change your verdict: which consideration, if the programme's advocates pressed it, would persuade you that the analogy is load-bearing after all, and which of your disanalogies you would give up first if one of them is wrong.
