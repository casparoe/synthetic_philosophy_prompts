I retired four years back after 38 years counting things in shallow lakes — bryozoan colonies, hydra, whatever turned up in the quadrat — and lately I've talked my way into a philosophy of biology reading group at the university near where I live. I'm the oldest person there by a decade and the only one who's ever had mud on him. It's been genuinely good but it's also driving me up the wall.

We're working through the question of what counts as a unit of selection. The woman running it — call her Dana, she's a philosopher, never held a seine net — has handed down verdicts on a string of cases over the last few weeks and I've been copying them into a notebook, because I'm convinced there's a rule in there and I cannot extract it. Her verdicts so far:

- The mouse t-haplotype, the one that sabotages the sperm that don't carry it: yes, and she called it the cleanest case on the list.
- An ant colony: yes, the colony, not the workers — she said the workers are more like organs.
- A grove of aspen that's one root system, forty hectares, some thousands of stems: yes.
- A slime mould fruiting body built out of cells from several different clones that came together by chemotaxis: no. She called it a temporary coalition.
- A reef coral whose larvae pick up their algae from the water column: no.
- That poultry experiment where the breeder selected whole cages of hens on total output instead of picking the best individual hens, and got a big response in a few generations: yes.

Two rules of my own I had to throw out. "Anything whose parts cooperate counts" — dead, because the t-haplotype cooperates with nothing, it's outright antisocial, and it's her favourite. "Anything that gets copied counts" — dead, or at least useless, because the slime mould cells obviously get copied and she still said no to the fruiting body, and because on that rule my whole career of counting colonies was counting the wrong things. So it isn't cooperation and it isn't bare copying, but I can't get past those.

Next week's case is mine, which I now regret proposing. A lichen — specifically a sorediate one. Soredia are those powdery granules that blow off the thallus, and each one is a little ball of fungal hyphae wrapped around a handful of algal cells, so both partners arrive on the new rock together, every time, and the offspring of both are clones of the parents. No spore that has to go out and find a photobiont. But also: a single thallus can carry more than one algal genotype, swapping of algal partners has occured more than once in lichen evolution, and the propagule is a clump of cells rather than one founding cell, with the two lineages inside it reproducing on thier own schedules and not locked together the way a queen and her workers are.

So: what does Dana say?

1. Give me a straight prediction on the lichen, with your reasoning and a number for how sure you are.
2. Write out the rules that are actually compatible with all six of her verdicts — I'd like at least three, and I want each one stated as a test I could run on an organism I'd just fished out of a bucket, not as a name or a slogan. If two of them are really one rule in different clothes, say so.
3. For each surviving rule, what does it say about the lichen, and which ones stay alive if she says yes versus if she says no? I want to be able to sit in that seminar knowing what her answer rules out.
4. Then take the two rules that end up closest together and name me one further case — a real sort of organism, or a described experiment I could go and find in a monograph — that separates them. I have a candidate of my own, a seed-saver's polyculture where the plot's yield comes out exactly equal to the sum of the varieties grown apart, and I want to know whether that does any work or whether I'm fooling myself.

One last thing and I mean it. I spent a career in a field where the settled view turned out to be wrong more than once, so I'd rather be told where the ground is soft. When you write this up, keep straight which parts of the levels-of-selection business are genuinely settled among the people who work on it and which parts are arguments that have been running for decades with no sign of ending. If a rule you're giving me is a minority position, say it's a minority position. I don't want a contested line dressed up as the consensus just because it makes the answer tidier — I've been on the wrong end of that trick before.
