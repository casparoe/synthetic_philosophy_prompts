I sit on a small grantmaking committee that scores candidate causes with the standard three-factor template — how much good addressing the problem would do, how much room there is for us to move it at the margin, and how few resources are already directed at it — and after several funding rounds I have started to notice something in our own records that I cannot characterize with the vocabulary I have. It is not that our scores are biased in some obvious direction, and it is not the familiar complaint that "nobody else is working on this" often just means nobody wanted it. The pattern concerns the relationship between the three axes and the reliability of the estimates feeding them. The causes that reach the top of our ranking are disproportionately ones where our evidence is thinnest: a far-future extrapolation resting on a chain of speculative assumptions, a single underpowered study of an intervention, a three-person expert survey. Thin evidence means wide uncertainty, and when we rank by expected value across such a wide distribution, the upside tail does the work — a cause whose importance is known only to within an order of magnitude will, more often than not, outscore a cause whose importance is well measured and merely very good. But thin evidence also pushes the neglectedness score up, because the problems that are hardest to evaluate are precisely the ones other funders and governments have steered clear of. So the same feature that inflates one score is simultaneously inflating a second score that the template treats as an independent factor, and the two are correlated through a common cause, which is our own ignorance. We are not, as far as I can tell, selecting the most important causes; we are selecting the least well measured ones, and then congratulating ourselves on their importance.

I keep reaching for a term and every candidate lands close but not on it. The winner's curse, and what decision analysts call the optimizer's curse, capture the mechanism of choosing the maximum over a set of noisy estimates, and I am happy to grant that this is part of what is happening — but both seem to me to presuppose that the noise is of roughly comparable magnitude across the options being compared, whereas my case depends on the noise being systematically larger for the options that also score well on a separate criterion, which strikes me as a different and nastier structure. Regression to the mean describes what happens after the selection, not why the selection is skewed in the first place. Goodhart's law involves someone optimizing against a measure until it stops tracking the target, and nobody here is gaming anything; we are all trying to be honest. Adverse selection is the nearest analogy I have managed to construct, since it concerns a pool of applicants skewed toward those you cannot assess properly, but the insurance-market setting turns on information asymmetry between two parties, and here the ignorance is entirely on our side — the causes are not concealing anything from us. The Matthew effect describes the downstream prestige dynamics rather than the epistemic selection, and "exploration versus exploitation" describes the trade-off we are failing to manage without naming the particular way we are failing to manage it.

There is also a second-order version that I would want any adequate name to cover. When this criticism is raised in our meetings, the standard reply is that we should commission better data before committing serious money; but the decision about which cause to investigate further is itself made using the same inflated scores, so the research agenda tilts toward the causes that already looked best for the wrong reason, and a year later they look better still, now with a citation trail of our own making. In our records, the causes we funded an initial scoping study for are nearly always the ones we end up funding properly, and I have no independent reason to think they were the best candidates for a scoping study either. So: is there an established concept for this configuration — a selection procedure whose criteria are correlated with the variance of the estimates they operate on, such that the procedure preferentially returns the least well measured candidates and then treats their poor measurement as a virtue? I would like the nearest existing concepts identified one by one, with the differences stated rather than merely listed, and a verdict on which, if any, is the best fit. If none fits cleanly, please coin a term and give it a genuine definition: necessary and sufficient conditions, and two or three boundary cases that should not count — for instance, whether an ordinary multi-criteria ranking with noisy inputs but no neglectedness-type factor qualifies, whether the correlation between criterion and variance is required or merely sufficient, and whether the phenomenon is still the same thing once the compounding research-agenda effect is absent. A bare label will not help me; what I need is the carving.
