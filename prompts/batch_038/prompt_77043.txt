Thirty-three years running a structural biology lab, and I've only gotten properly into philosophy since retiring. My nephew is a grad student and he keeps feeding me arguments; this one has me stuck. He says it shows that being modest about your own evidence -- always deferring to the base rate for cases like yours -- proves far too much.

(1) Any hypothesis I'm confident about falls into some reference class of past cases that looked, from the inside, just as good as mine does.
(2) A large fraction of those cases turned out wrong, and the people in them were exactly as confident as I am now.
(3) I have no way of knowing I'm the exception: whatever feature I point to (clean controls, data that replicated twice, I considered the alternatives), members of the class pointed to features that looked to them just as strong.
(4) So my inside evidence can't lift my confidence above the class base rate.
(5) Since nearly every empirical belief belongs to some such class, this applies across the board.
(6) So I ought to hold everything I concluded in the lab at roughly fifty-fifty.
(7) But that's absurd -- I'd bet my house on several of those results, and I was right.

He claims (1) through (7) is valid and I have to swallow one of the steps whole. I dont buy that, but I can't say why. Which step actually goes, why did it look innocent on the way in, and what do I pay for giving it up? Be specific about (3) and (4), those are the two I keep circling. I'd much rather keep (7) than the modesty principle in (3), but tell me if that's just an old man being stubborn. And separately: does the argument undercut itself when you apply it to the argument, the way he insists it doesn't?
