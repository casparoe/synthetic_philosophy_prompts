I'm coordinating an eighteen-month working group on self-binding devices in clinical and personal planning — psychiatric advance directives, so-called Ulysses clauses, competent refusals of future treatment, and the various informal arrangements people with episodic conditions or addictions use to tie their own hands. The group is four people: a philosopher who works on personal identity, a clinical psychiatrist with a caseload of bipolar and substance-use patients, a legal scholar who works on capacity law, and a behavioral economist who studies intertemporal choice. Our charge is dual and a little awkward: a conceptual white paper, and a draft model clause for a jurisdiction that is considering making Ulysses clauses enforceable rather than merely advisory. Everyone in the group has read some Elster on precommitment, and the psychiatrist and the legal scholar have both spent time on the Dworkin–Dresser exchange over whether a directive written by an earlier, competent self should govern a later self who, like Margo, appears content — but nobody has managed to convert that literature into an agreement about what we should actually be arguing over, and our first two meetings dissolved into three unrelated debates.

To give you something to work with, here are three framings we've been using internally, none of them standard, and I'd like your judgment on them as much as on the field. First, we've been distinguishing what we call content-binding (the directive specifies an outcome: no antipsychotics, no discharge before a given date), procedure-binding (it specifies only how a decision must be reached: two clinicians plus a designated layperson, a mandatory cooling-off period), and gate-binding (it specifies only who gets to certify that the person is no longer competent to decide, and thus when the directive activates at all). Second, one member presses what we've been calling the certification regress: any rule for determining when the later self is too compromised to be trusted is itself a judgment the earlier self encoded, so it doesn't escape the problem of the earlier self's authority, it just relocates it — and on this view only procedure-binding is defensible, since it never claims to know the future content of the person's wishes. Third, I have a parity worry of my own: if we grant that a competent earlier self may bind a later self who has changed and would object, we seem to have licensed institutions to bind members who never consented to the binding, and I can't tell whether personal and constitutional self-binding are one problem wearing two costumes or genuinely different problems with a superficially similar grammar.

With that in place: given roughly four people, eighteen months, one semester of genuinely shared time, and the obligation to produce both a defensible model clause and a paper, which three or four open conceptual questions should this group spend its joint effort on, and in what order? I want the ordering argued, not merely stated — for each question, tell me how much downstream work depends on its answer, whether it is tractable by this particular mix of people rather than requiring expertise we don't have, and whether settling it would change a single word of the model clause or only the paper's framing. Please also tell me which familiar questions you'd deprioritize as overrated, and be specific about why: I suspect that the metaphysics of whether the earlier and later selves are literally the same person, and the empirical question of whether discounting is really hyperbolic, are both places where a clever answer would leave our clause untouched, and I'd like to know whether that suspicion is right or complacent. Then say what single disagreement among the four of us — content-binding versus procedure-binding, the regress, the parity worry, or something I haven't named — functions as the crux, in that resolving it would reorganize the rest of the agenda rather than merely settling one item on it. Finally, describe what developments over the next two years would move questions up or down this list: a capacity ruling, a change in how directives are actually drafted and honored at the bedside, a clinical finding about insight in the conditions we care about, or a jurisdiction adopting enforceability and generating real case history. I am not looking for a survey of the literature and I don't want a reading list or bibliography — I want the defended agenda, including the parts of the field you'd tell us to leave alone.
