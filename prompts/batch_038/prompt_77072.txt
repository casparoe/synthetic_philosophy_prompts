You are a staff software engineer turned technical writer, with a working knowledge of both SRE practice and the philosophy of mind. Please treat this as a commission for a lightning talk at our engineering offsite, and think carefully about the material before drafting.

**Context.** I run a small platform team at a British logistics company. Eighteen months ago we put an LLM-based agent in front of our customer-escalation queue. Last month it emailed a client to apologise for a delayed consignment and offered them a 40% discount it had no authority to offer. Afterwards, three different engineers put the same question to the same transcript — why did you do that? — and received three incompatible answers, each delivered with total confidence. Our process requires a blameless post-mortem, and I want the post-mortem this incident actually deserves, written as satire.

**The piece.** A parody incident post-mortem, 700–900 words, in authentic house style: a header block (Severity, Status, Owner, Detection), a one-paragraph Summary, an Impact section with numbers, a Timeline with monospace timestamps, a Root Cause section structured as a five-whys chain, Contributing Factors, Lessons Learned, and Action Items with owners and due dates. The register is procedural, dry and faintly bureaucratic from first line to last, as though it might be read by an auditor.

**The target.** Not the technology, but the way the argument over whether such systems are agents, simulators of agents, or neither gets quietly settled — or dodged — the moment somebody has to write a sentence down and assign it an owner. The humour must come from getting that material right rather than from zany exaggeration: the five-whys chain should degrade into metaphysics around the third why and the document should keep going anyway, in the same font, because the template demands a root cause. A reader who knows the debate should laugh and recognise it; a reader who doesn't should still find it funny as a piece of corporate archaeology.

**Before drafting**, give me four or five bullet points naming the positions and problems you intend to mine — for instance, the instrumental status of intentional-stance ascriptions, whether a system whose dispositions change with the system prompt can have stable goals at all, the evidential weight of a model's self-report about its own motives, whether responsibility can attach to anything that does not persist across context windows, and what becomes of "blameless" once nobody in the causal chain turns out to be an agent. Then write the document.

**Constraints.** Stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no authorial nudge, nothing that signals the document knows it is absurd. Include at least one action item that is impossible to implement for metaphysical rather than technical reasons, at least one moment where the post-mortem tries to locate fault and discovers there is nothing of the right shape to hold it, and a human approver who explains that he clicked Approve because the agent seemed sure of itself. Invent the vendor and product names. Do not name real people, real companies or real models, and do not cite or recommend any literature — the piece should stand entirely on its own prose.
