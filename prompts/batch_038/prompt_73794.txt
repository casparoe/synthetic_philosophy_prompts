Write an internal style-guide memo of roughly 600–700 words from the editorial board of an invented journal devoted to the study of societal collapse, announcing sweeping revisions to its house style: numbered rules governing what authors may and may not write, a glossary of approved euphemisms to replace the word "collapse" (with at least one entry in which the euphemism treadmill becomes visibly absurd), and one worked example pairing a rejected sentence with its compliant rewrite. The target is the concept itself and the discourse built on it, not any real person — invent any bestselling polemicists you need, and do not include references or a bibliography.

The humor should come from getting the methodological problems right rather than from bureaucratic silliness alone: whose collapse is actually being measured when what stops is a dynasty's inscriptions and a palace's redistribution accounts while the surrounding population goes on farming; the abandoned southern Maya lowlands set against a north that kept building; the fact that Rapa Nui's demographic catastrophe is far more entangled with European contact than with a pre-contact "ecocide"; Late Bronze Age palatial states as interdependent networks whose failure propagates rather than as isolated moral failures; the impossibility of a non-arbitrary baseline against which decline is scored; and the survivorship bias of a literature that studies only the cases that ended badly. Include one rule the board plainly cannot satisfy, and keep the register deadpan throughout. The memo must obey its own style guide even in its closing paragraph, where the board announces its own demise without using a single banned term. A general reader should be able to follow the absurdity while an archaeologist recognizes the real disputes and finds the satire fair.
