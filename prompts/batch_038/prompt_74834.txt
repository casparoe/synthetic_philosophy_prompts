For eleven years I have bought green coffee for a small roastery in Bristol: cupping dozens of lots a week and deciding which we purchase. We were acquired last month, and the new operations director has asked me to convert that judgement into a written scoring sheet — descriptors, defect counts, numeric thresholds — so that a junior buyer can run the desk without me. I drafted one in good faith. In her first fortnight using it she bought two lots I would have refused and passed on three I would have bought, and none of those errors were marginal.

His response is that the sheet is merely incomplete and that I should keep adding criteria until it reproduces my decisions. His stated premise is that a decision counts as informed only if I am entertaining the right propositions while making it; if I cannot name them, then on his account I am not judging at all but guessing with confidence. So whatever I do, he says, there must be a set of facts I know that she does not, and my only task is to write them down.

Two things trouble me. First, I do not expect further additions to converge, and I cannot give a reason beyond a strong intuition, which is exactly the sort of thing I have trained myself to distrust. Second, my retrospective explanations turn out to be unreliable: asked why I had rejected a lot, I said it showed a fermentation defect, and when we re-cupped it blind a week later I could not find one — though I still would not have bought it.

Is he right that a completed sheet could in principle capture what I do? And should that second discovery make me doubt the expertise itself rather than only my ability to report on it? I would welcome advice both on the argument and on what I should actually put to him.
