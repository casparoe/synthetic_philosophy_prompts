I retired four years ago from a chair in neuroethology, having spent most of my working life recording from the giant interneurons that carry wind-borne information from the cercal organs to the thoracic ganglia of a cockroach, and trying to explain, identified synapse by identified synapse, how the animal manages to turn away from the thing that is about to eat it. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more enthusiasm than prudence, and last term I set out the following argument to a reading group I attend. Every step seemed to me to bear scrutiny when I wrote it down. The conclusion does not bear scrutiny, and a colleague has told me the argument is self-refuting, which I take to be a courteous way of saying it is worthless. I should like a precise diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The argument, as I stated it:

(1) Darwin showed that the most intricate adaptive design in the biosphere is produced by a process that is supremely competent and wholly without comprehension.

(2) Turing showed that any procedure, however ingenious its outputs, can be executed by a machine following rules mechanically, with no comprehension required of the machine at any stage.

(3) These are two instances of a single move — the inversion of reasoning that Dennett is fond of naming, I believe — in which competence proves to be not the offspring of comprehension but its precursor and its sufficient condition.

(4) Call a principle explanatory displacement: if some ingredient can be struck from a complete account of a phenomenon without loss of explanatory power, then that ingredient is not genuinely part of the phenomenon, and talk of it survives only as an expression of our admiration.

(5) The nervous system of an animal is a physical system; hence what it does is in principle specifiable as a procedure, and how it came to do that is in principle explicable by selection and learning.

(6) From (2) and (5): a complete account of any cognitive performance, human performance included, can be given without crediting the performer with comprehension.

(7) From (1) and (5): a complete account of the origin of any such capacity can be given without crediting the process that produced it with comprehension.

(8) From (4), (6) and (7): comprehension is not genuinely present in human beings; what we call understanding is admiration for competence we have not yet finished tracing.

(9) The same reasoning applies to the making and the assessing of this argument. My writing it and your evaluating it are processes of the kinds described in (6) and (7); therefore nothing here is understood by anyone, and the argument's apparent cogency is one further instance of the phenomenon it describes.

I cannot accept (8), and I cannot accept (9). I should most like to keep (4), since without a principle of that kind the two inversions seem to me to record a change in our habits of speech rather than to establish anything about the world; and I confess a prejudice against (5), though only because I dislike the locution in principle, which is hardly an objection.

So: which single step should I surrender, if only one may go? Tell me why that step looked acceptable to a careful reader when I wrote it — I want the mechanism of the error, not merely its name. Then price the alternatives: what does each of the remaining steps cost if it is the one given up? And on (9), say whether the self-application is an independent defect or the very thing that locates the culprit, as a balance that misreports its own weight is thereby shown to be broken. Where a step is ambiguous rather than false, give me both readings and state which reading each neighbouring step requires.

Please open with a bottom line of no more than three sentences naming the step you reject and the shortest sufficient reason. Then analyse every step in a table with exactly these columns: step (its number); classification (one of sound, ambiguous, false, unwarranted inference); defect or cost (the specific defect if this is the step you reject, otherwise what is forfeited by rejecting it); who objects and what they pay (the position or positions, named, that would give up this step, and the price they incur for doing so); residue (the weakest claim in the neighbourhood of this step that survives, and that I could go on holding). Close with one short paragraph stating what the inversion thesis ought to say, on your diagnosis, if it is to remain both true and interesting rather than trivial or self-defeating.
