I'm an ML engineer and my whole working world is loss functions, calibration and Bregman divergences. I've shipped models scored with Brier and log loss, I know what a reliability diagram is supposed to look like, and I know Bregman divergences from mirror descent and online learning. No philosophy training past wikipedia-level reading.

I keep running into philosophers who argue for the probability axioms, and for conditionalisation, using Dutch books: you assemble a package of bets that loses you money no matter how the world turns out, so incoherent credences are irrational. My gut says this is the proper-scoring-rule story wearing an unnecessarily adversarial costume -- the betting machinery isn't doing anything a strictly proper score wasn't already doing, and the "sure loss" business is really just a separating hyperplane / projection-onto-the-convex-set-of-probability-functions fact. I want to know if that's right or whether I'm flattening something.

So: restate the Dutch book arguments in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Three of them -- (1) the static one for the axioms, (2) the dynamic/diachronic one for conditionalisation, (3) whatever betting-based argument there is for the reflection principle, if there even is one; say so if there isn't. For each I want the actual mapping: what plays the role of the bets, what plays the role of guaranteed loss, what assumption on my side corresponds to "you'll accept any bet your credences license", what corresponds to the fact that a Dutch book works even when you don't know which way the world will go. Dont water it down: if a step genuinely uses something my vocabulary has no slot for, mark that step as untranslated rather than quietly substituting the nearest thing I'd recognise.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Sort the claims into: carries over exactly, carries over only with distortion, no counterpart at all. In particular tell me:

- Is the accuracy-dominance argument -- incoherent credences are accuracy-dominated by some coherent one under any strictly proper score -- literally the same theorem as the Dutch book, or a different theorem sharing a proof technique? I suspect both are the same convex-geometry fact and the whole "pragmatic vs non-pragmatic" dispute is about whether you're allowed to count a loss of accuracy as a cost. Check that suspicion rather than assuming it.
- What happens to the bite of the conclusion. Money you actually lose is a cost in a way that "you'd have scored worse in a forecast contest you never entered" doesn't obviously seem to be. Does the translation expose the Dutch book as pragmatically loaded, or expose that the pragmatic loading was never doing any work?
- Whether coherence and calibration are even the same kind of target. I care about calibration and sharpness jointly; the betting arguments seem to care about coherence alone, and I can't tell whether coherence is a degenerate special case of my objective or something orthogonal.
- Whether the "you'd accept the whole package" premise is a claim about psychology or just bookkeeping, and whether that distinction survives into a scoring-rule formulation, where you never accept anything and just get evaluated.

Keep it to something I can read in one sitting. End with how confident you are in each bullet of the residue report, and single out the one claim you're least sure of and why -- including if the least-sure thing is my Bregman-projection hunch. If that hunch is half-right or just wrong, say it bluntly instead of hedging both directions.
