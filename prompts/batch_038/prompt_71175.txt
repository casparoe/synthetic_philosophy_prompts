So, some background. I spent six years as a ward-level organizer in a mid-sized Rust Belt city — mostly canvass logistics and volunteer retention — and I quit two years ago after watching the campaign I worked on get hollowed out by a nationalized primary fight that had almost nothing to do with the actual office. I now run a tiny civics podcast (about 200 regular listeners, recorded in my basement), and next month's guest is a political theorist who has built an entire research program on Simone Weil's late essay arguing that political parties should be abolished outright. Not reformed, not made more transparent, not subject to anti-defection rules: abolished. He's smart and charming, and from the two talks of his i've watched he's extremely good at steering around hard questions with some variation on "you're assuming parties are natural, when they're really a nineteenth-century technology."

Here's what I need: nine or ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. No softballs, no "what first drew you to Weil," nothing that lets him recite the view back at my listeners. I want questions that turn on the actual argument, so please make sure the set includes: (a) one on his reliance on Weil's three-part definition — party as a machine for manufacturing collective passion, as an organization built to exert collective pressure on the thinking of each of its members, and as something whose only real end is its own growth without any specified limit — and on why all three are supposed to be jointly damning rather than severable, since he usually concedes the second and defends the third; (b) one on the Rousseau move, that the general will gets distorted precisely when people deliberate inside partial associations, and whether that argument proves far too much, since it seems to indict unions, churches, legislative caucuses, editorial boards and his own book tour, none of which he wants suppressed; (c) one on Weil's positive proposal — candidates standing as individuals with no organizational label on the ballot, and loose journals or reviews standing in for parties, where you can be near a revue without adhering to it — and whether a revue is anything but a party running slower and with better manners; (d) the accountability problem: if no legislator owes anything to an organized bloc, what mechanism actually punishes a lazy or corrupt incumbent, and how does a government get formed at all without the coordinating device he's removing; (e) the reflexive problem — who does the abolishing, and doesn't the transition require exactly the disciplined collective machine he's indicting; and (f) a closing question on what would count as evidence against him, i.e. a real place where abolition was tried or approximated, with outcomes he'd accept as disconfirming rather than explaining away.

Dont hedge on the follow-ups. For each question, state flatly what his most probable dodge is and why your follow-up forecloses it, instead of listing three possible dodges and leaving me to pick. Then commit to two judgments of your own at the end and argue for them: which single question in the set is most likely to actually unhinge the interview — not merely annoy him — and why; and whether any of the questions above rest on a contested or uncharitable reading of Weil. If you think the "revues are parties in slow motion" line misreads what she means by proximity without adherence, tell me so directly and rewrite that question, rather than quietly softening it. I'd much rather be told I'm wrong by you now than find it out on air.
