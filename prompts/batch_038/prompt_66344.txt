Our reading group meets once a month and it's a weird mix — two philosophers of physics, a couple of working physicists, a neuroscientist who likes arguing, and me. Last session we tried to talk about laws of nature and it went nowhere, because three people announced "I'm a Humean" and turned out to mean three completely different things by it, and one of the physicists insisted that whatever the rest of us were arguing about, the Lagrangian of the Standard Model is "just real" and there was nothing left to discuss. So for the next meeting I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts people into a position on laws of nature *before* we start talking, so we can see where the disagreements actually are. Crucially it has to be answerable by someone who has never heard the word Humeanism — the physicist in question would bail if he saw "supervenience" on the first page — so every branch node needs to be phrased as a concrete case or a plain question about what laws do, not as a question about labels or about which camp you belong to. Names and jargon should appear only at the leaves, after the sorting is done.

Build it out of the logical space rather than out of a list of views: first the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Please make sure the tree includes branches on these things, roughly in this order of intuitiveness: whether a law could be true even if it is never actually instantiated anywhere in the history of the universe (a law about a kind of interaction that never happens, say); whether two universes with exactly the same total pattern of events from beginning to end could nonetheless differ in what their laws are; whether a genuine law can have exceptions, or whether anything with exceptions just isn't a law; whether "simple" and "systematic" are features of the world or features of how creatures like us describe it — i.e. could beings with very different cognitive architecture, facing the same universe, disagree about its laws without anyone being wrong; which way the explanation runs, laws explaining regularities or regularities explaining laws; and whether the fact that a certain regularity is a law should make you more confident about unobserved cases, or whether it adds nothing over the observed pattern itself. Six to nine nodes total, each with two or three options, and if a node's answer follows automatically from an earlier one for most people, say so. The leaves should cover at least: a Lewis-style best-systems account; an Armstrong-style account with a necessitation relation between universals; a Maudlin-style view where laws are fundamental and productive and nothing reduces them; a powers/dispositional-essentialist view where necessity comes from what properties are; a van Fraassen/Giere-style deflationary view where there aren't really laws, just the models or regularities we use; and a Cartwright-style view where the fundamental laws describe capacities and are literally false of the messy world. Add any seventh leaf you think is needed, and mark anything nobody actually defends as "unnamed."

Format, in this order: **How to use this** (~120 words); **The questions** (the numbered nodes, each with its case in plain language and its answer options, plus one line saying what each option rules in or out, ~700 words); **The map** (an indented outline showing how answer patterns combine into leaves — no need to draw a real flowchart, text is fine, ~300 words); **The leaves** (one tight paragraph each: the name, the core commitment, and the single most awkward thing it has to say, ~600 words); **Empty cells** (combinations the questions permit that no position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of each — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, ~250 words); **Pairs this can't separate** (which leaves my instrument will confuse, and why, ~200 words); and **Dry run** (walk three respondents through the questions and say where they land: the physicist who thinks the Lagrangian is real and everything else derives from it, a philosopher who thinks laws are our best summary and nothing more, and someone who says laws are useful fictions we should stop taking so seriously, ~400 words). Keep the whole thing under about 2,700 words. One more thing: don't let the dry run flatter anyone — I'd rather it turn out that the physicist and the philosopher who thinks they're opposites end up in neighbouring leaves than have me discover that on the night. Also if you think some of my six required branch questions are actually the same question wearing different clothes, say so in the "Pairs" section rather than quietly merging them, I want to know which distinctions I'm over-selling.
