I am in the second year of the International Baccalaureate History course, and my extended essay concerns the causes and the extraordinary duration of the Thirty Years' War, argued through Braudel's scheme of historical time-scales. My supervisor's comment on the last draft was that several of my explanatory sentences are event-history dressed in structural vocabulary, and that I need a way of testing each sentence rather than trusting the words I chose to write it in. She would not tell me which sentences she meant. I would like you to build me the testing instrument, and then use it on me.

**Part one: the tree.** Please design a decision tree that takes one explanatory sentence about the seventeenth century and terminates at a named historiographical position. The constraints are these:

- No more than eight leaves, and compact enough that I can set it on a single page as a figure in my appendix.
- Every branch node must be a yes/no question that a student could answer from the sentence alone, without already knowing which leaf it leads to. Do not use any node that simply asks whether the sentence is about geography, or about the economy, or about individuals: those are the answers I am trying to derive, not the questions I should be asked.
- At each node, state in one line which diagnostic criterion it is really applying — how fast the supposed cause changes, what kind of thing it is, how long the thing to be explained persists, or whether the slow variable would still do any work if the events were held fixed — and flag any node at which two of those criteria would sort the same sentence in different directions.
- At least one node must test whether a slow variable is load-bearing or decorative: that is, whether taking it away changes the outcome, or whether it merely supplies the scenery for an explanation that a decision, a battle, or a treasury is actually doing.
- Leaves I need present (you may merge two of them if you argue that they should be merged, and you may add others you think the space requires): environmental and geographical; slow economic and demographic structure; medium-scale cycles and conjunctures, at the scale of decades; the event; the decision of an individual; shared ways of thinking that change only across generations; and a residual leaf for sentences the scheme cannot place at all. Each leaf should carry one sentence saying what a historian who genuinely works at that scale has to give up.

**Part two: my own sentences.** Here is the argumentative core of my draft, as eleven separate claims. Run each through your tree.

1. Because the Alpine passes and the upper Rhine corridor joined the Spanish and Austrian branches of the Habsburg family by land, a revolt in Bohemia was from the start a conflict about the communication routes of central Europe, whatever the rebels themselves intended.
2. The cold, wet summers of the late 1610s and 1620s shortened the growing season across the Empire, so that a rebellion which might have been fed down became a competition for harvests that were no longer there.
3. Had Frederick not accepted the Bohemian crown in 1619, the grievances of the Estates would most probably have been composed by negotiation, as comparable grievances had been composed earlier in the century.
4. Silver from the American mines slowly eroded the real value of the fixed dues on which the landowning nobility of Bohemia and Austria lived, so that by 1618 their legal privileges rested on a fiscal base that had been shrinking for three generations.
5. The Habsburg habit of financing war out of hereditary domains rather than out of taxes granted by estates pushed imperial finance toward the debasements of the 1620s, and it was that debasement, not any defeat in the field, that destroyed the credibility of the imperial cause among the German princes.
6. Every power bordering the Baltic had to reckon with the grain that passed through the Sound and the tolls levied upon it; this was the framework inside which each of them calculated how long an army could be kept in the field at all.
7. Christian IV entered the war in 1625 because the toll revenues of the Sound gave him, uniquely among the northern princes, a standing income large enough to raise one — the Baltic grain trade explains his intervention in the sense that it explains what he could afford to do.
8. After the Swedish landing in 1630 the war continued chiefly because French subsidies paid for it, and French policy rather than any Swedish grievance accounts for the fighting that followed.
9. By the 1630s there were not enough men left in the German lands to finish the war decisively: the depopulation produced by two decades of marching armies and plague meant that neither side could raise the force that would have compelled the other to terms.
10. The war did not end when it might have because, once armies were quartered on and fed from the regions they occupied, each side's capacity to keep fighting depended on the exhaustion of those regions — a process measured in harvests rather than in treaties.
11. The negotiators at Westphalia produced the settlement they did because they were men formed in the same faculties of law, arguing inside a shared imperial vocabulary that had been assembling for a century and a half and that no single delegation could have chosen to abandon.

For each sentence, report the leaf it reaches, whether a second leaf fits it as well and why, and which pairs of sentences your tree cannot tell apart from one another. Sentences 6 and 7 are meant to be close relatives; if your tree treats them identically, say so plainly rather than finding a distinction to preserve. Then tell me which sentences my supervisor most likely meant, and what it is about them that makes the slow variable decorative rather than load-bearing.

**Part three: the empty spaces.** Lay out the space your tree is implicitly assuming — I take it to be something like the time-scale of the cause set against the time-scale of the effect — and identify which combinations of answers no leaf corresponds to. For each unoccupied cell, say whether nobody is there because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been much explored. Note also whether any of my eleven sentences falls into an empty cell, since that would mean the tree is at fault rather than the sentences.

Please give me no reading list, bibliography, or page references, and do not reproduce anything as a quotation from Braudel or any other author — paraphrase wherever you would otherwise quote. Nor should you fact-check my history: assume each of the eleven sentences is factually sound and classify it exactly as written. Roughly twelve hundred words in total would be ideal, with the tree itself kept short enough to redraw as a figure.
