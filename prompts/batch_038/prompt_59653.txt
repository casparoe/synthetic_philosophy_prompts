I teach a final-year metaphysics seminar and I'm setting the three-hour final. I want one essay question on the necessity of origin — specifically on whether a person could have come from a different sperm and egg — worth 25 marks and answerable in about 50 of the 180 minutes.

The bar: anyone who's done the reading can recite that origins are supposed to be essential, and can gesture at the intuition that you couldn't have been conceived a year later and still been you. I don't want a question they can pass by doing that. What I want it to turn on is the gap between two claims that get blurred together constantly — that you couldn't have come from a different origin, and that nothing else could have come from yours — and the fact that monozygotic twins drive a wedge into it, since there two people share one zygote, so indexing origin to the fertilization event rather than the gamete pair doesn't rescue anything. I'd also like the question to make them handle the tolerance worry: if you could have come from a gamete differing by a single point mutation, why not two, and where does that leave the essentialist — and a case where the origin is spread over three contributors because the nuclear material comes from two people and the mitochondrial material from a third, so its not obvious which contribution the thesis is even about.

Give me:

1. The question exactly as the students would see it, with whatever case descriptions it needs written out in full — no relying on them having seen the cases before.
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words that actually takes a position and defends it, rather than surveying the options.
3. A marking scheme out of 25, broken down into the individual moves the answer has to make, with the marks attached to each, and for the top two bands a sentence on what seperates a first-class answer from a competent summary that never commits.
4. Two or three answers I should expect to see in the pile, what the most likely wrong turn is in each, and roughly what it should cost — for instance treating the twin case as a straight refutation of origin essentialism instead of as forcing a choice between the necessity claim and the sufficiency claim, or answering the sorites by denying that origins are vague without noticing that this commits them to a hyper-precise metaphysics of gametes.

No reading list or bibliography, they've done the reading — I want the thinking, not the references.
