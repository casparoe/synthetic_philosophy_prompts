I have been trying to settle my views about the epistemic evaluation of credences in the right order: first collect verdicts on cases, then ask what account they add up to. Below are the verdicts, followed by a half-formed proposal of my own. I want to know whether the list is coherent, and what has to go if it is not.

The verdicts.

1. Two forecasters, same claim, same day: one says 0.9 rain, the other 0.6. It rains. I judge the first the better believer. Had it not rained, I would have judged the second better. Which state is superior is settled by how things are, not by how carefully the agent hedged.

2. Yet where the evidence fixes 0.5 — a coin I know to be fair, not yet tossed — I think the agent at 0.6 is the worse believer, even though that agent comes out better in half the futures. So the word in verdict 1 is not doing all the work: there is also an assessment made from where the agent now stands, looking forward.

3. A coin has been tossed by a machine about whose construction I know nothing. I resist 0.5. I want my state to be a spread, roughly 0.3 to 0.7, and I do not think that spread is worse than a sharp number; if anything it reports my evidence more faithfully. I do not think anyone is obliged to be sharp on every question.

4. An agent who assigns 1 to a contingent claim and is wrong has, I think, failed in a way no finite amount of getting other things right can offset. But an agent at 0.999 who is wrong has merely done slightly badly. My verdicts treat 0 and 1 on contingent matters as forbidden and 0.999 as permitted.

5. My credences about matters no one will ever investigate — whether an unnamed valley burned two centuries ago — are still more or less accurate, and I can make them better, though nothing will ever disclose the answer. Accuracy is not a matter of what I expect, or of what could in principle be checked.

6. A calibration audit informs me that in this domain my confidence has run about fifteen points high, systematically, over years. I think I should deflate across the board, including on the questions where my confidence feels best earned.

7. I have slowly checked a piece of reasoning three times and am confident of its conclusion. Someone observes that most people who believe they have checked a reasoning three times have erred anyway. I think this should not move me at all. Verdicts 6 and 7 pull against each other and I cannot say why one statistic bites and the other does not; I suspect that is a defect in the list rather than in the cases.

8. Where my conditional credences were formed badly — set years ago by an enthusiasm I have since outgrown — I still think the right response to learning E is to adopt exactly those conditional credences, having nothing better to work with. But if I learn before E arrives that those conditionals were corrupted in a way I can identify and correct, I think simply conditionalizing when E comes would be wrong.

9. An error about a proposition that settles many others seems to me worse than an equally sized error about an isolated one. I do not think propositions should all count the same in the ledger.

10. An agent whose credence is accurate because a wish produced it seems to me worse off epistemically than one whose equally accurate credence was produced by looking. Equal accuracy, unequal standing.

The proposal. I have been trying to write my own penalty function: Brier-shaped, but with a weight on each proposition that grows with how many further things it settles, and with a penalty that grows without bound as credence approaches 0 or 1 — though only for questions the agent has actually investigated, since I do not want an idle person charged for the confidence of an inquirer.

What I would like back:

(i) a precise statement of an accuracy measure, and of the accompanying norm governing what an agent should be trying to do with it, that captures as many of the ten verdicts as possible;

(ii) for each clause of that measure and norm, the verdict that motivated the clause;

(iii) its verdicts on two or three cases of your own construction that I have not listed, chosen to probe whether the measure is really doing what you claim;

(iv) a candid list of the verdicts it cannot accommodate, saying which of them you think I should abandon and which you think a better account could save; and

(v) a report on the technical properties: whether your measure is proper, strictly proper, bounded, and additive across a partition; whether the discontinuity I want in verdict 4 can be had without also making 0.999 expensive; and whether the weighting clause of my proposal survives the dominance arguments that motivate probabilism in the first place, or quietly wrecks them.
