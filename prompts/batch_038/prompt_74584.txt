I am a novelist, and for three years I have been at work on a book whose final movement turns on a question I cannot settle without help. I should warn you that the help I need is not literary; it is conceptual, and I want to be answered as precisely as you can manage.

Here is the situation. A critic whose judgment I trust read the chapter in which Marguerite stops resenting her husband for something he did eleven years earlier — a betrayal, ordinary in kind, ruinous in timing — and told me I had got it right, though for reasons he supplied rather than any I had in mind. His account, which he reaches for constantly and which he says he takes from the philosophers, is that resentment is a move in a conversation. On this picture, to resent someone is to address a demand to them; anger is speech awaiting an answer; to hold a person responsible is simply to keep that exchange open. Forgiveness closes it. And when the other party cannot or will not reply — because he is a child, or ill, or a psychopath, or merely unreachable — we stop talking *to* him and begin talking *about* him: we adopt what Strawson called the objective attitude, treating him as a case to be managed, cured, tolerated, or avoided rather than as someone to argue with.

He then drew a conclusion about my chapter that I have been unable to dislodge. His argument ran: Marguerite no longer resents; therefore the conversation is over; therefore she has taken up the objective attitude toward Elias; and since that attitude is precisely the withdrawal of ordinary human involvement, what I have written — whatever I intended by it — is a portrait of a woman who has left her marriage while remaining in the house. He added that no third position is available to me, because resentment is the only currency of the participant attitude, and that if I want Marguerite to stay I must give her her anger back.

The trouble is that what I wrote is this: she stays. She is kind. She makes his tea. She handles the money and the repairs and his mother. She has simply run out of things to say to him about the thing he did. She is not cold; she is calm, and in the last scene she laughs at something he says about a neighbour. My reader's analogy explains my own chapter to me better than I could have, and I find that unnerving rather than flattering — partly because it is tidy in a way that living people are not, and partly because I suspect it is doing work I never asked of it.

So I would like you to put the analogy under examination rather than merely admire or dismiss it.

First, give me the correspondence explicitly, and I do mean explicitly: which element of an actual conversation is standing in for which element of the moral relation. Who is the speaker, who the addressee, what is the utterance, what is its point, what counts as uptake, what counts as a reply, what is the shared language, whose turn is it, what does a breakdown look like, and what is the exit? A table is welcome if it keeps you honest.

Second, separate the disanalogies that bear on my reader's inference from those that merely decorate the picture. I have three candidates and I want to know whether each one cuts. Is resentment an act or a condition, and does the analogy depend on collapsing that difference — on turning a state one is in into a thing one does to someone? Are there reactive attitudes with no addressee at all: indignation on a stranger's behalf, at which no one present can be said to be addressed; resentment of the dead, who cannot reply; fury at an institution, a war, a diagnosis? And if some of the attitudes have no conversational shape, what becomes of the claim that resentment is the sole currency of participation?

Third, tell me what the analogy smuggles in from its source. Conversations presuppose a shared grammar and a willingness to be understood; they have turns; they can be declined, interrupted, or concluded by either party; they are entered voluntarily and left visibly. Which of those features is being used to establish that Marguerite's calm must be a *conclusion* — a closing of an exchange — rather than an exhaustion, a change of subject, or a refusal to open one that was never open? Note that Elias apologised twice, at length, and that she accepted both apologies without difficulty and yet the anger went anyway, some years later, without ceremony.

Fourth, adjudicate the question of the third position, and be blunt about the outcome. Are there ways of ceasing to resent that are not the objective attitude? Is that attitude in fact a wholesale withdrawal of involvement, or does it come in degrees and settings — a doctor with a patient, a trainer with a nervous dog, a parent with a toddler, all of whom remain deeply involved with the person in view? Can a person stay fully participant in the practical texture of a shared life while having left the address? If the theory admits such a position, describe it and tell me what it costs. If it does not, say so plainly, and I will rewrite the chapter.

Fifth — and this is where your answer will be most useful to me as a craftsman — if the conversational analogy cannot carry the weight my reader puts on it, propose a better one for what Marguerite has done, one that can distinguish her calm from coldness and her staying from staying on. Tell me what it maps, where it too will strain, and what it licenses me to write that the conversation picture forbids.
