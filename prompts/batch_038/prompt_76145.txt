So three years of running our third-year Theory of Knowledge seminar has finally landed me with the session nobody else in the department wanted: 80 minutes on the pramāṇas, 22 students, whiteboard only, no slides. They've all done intro epistemology so they know Gettier, they've done a week on testimony where the reading was Hume and Reid-ish responses, and not one of them has met any Indian philosophy before. The module descriptor calls this slot "non-Western epistemology," which means half the room will walk in expecting a culture segment and being nicely tolerant about it rather than expecting arguments. I don't want to plan the explanation myself, I want you to plan it for me — the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what, which single case should carry the whole session, the misconceptions and the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off, one closing question that separates people who got it from people who are just being polite, and what I should cut and say I'm cutting. No reading list, don't bother, I've got that sorted.

My half-formed hunch is that the thing worth teaching isn't the list of four at all, it's the reduction question: what is actually being disputed when one school says a putative pramāṇa is "just" another one. The Buddhists say two are enough and that everything else collapses into them; the Mīmāṃsakas say you need six because absence and postulation do work that neither perception nor inference can; Nyāya sits in between at four and refuses the extra two. Underneath all of it there seems to be a question my students can actually argue about — what makes two ways of coming to know the same capacity or two different ones, and is that a question with an answer or just a choice of taxonomy? I want the session to leave them thinking that question is hard, not that the ancient Indians had a charming list.

Two problems I can't solve on my own. First, the carrying example. I keep drifting to fat Devadatta — everyone agrees the guy doesn't eat by day and yet he's plump, so you come to know he eats at night, and the fight is over whether that cognition arises from needing to dissolve an inconsistency or is really a suppressed inference with a pervasion you never articulated. It's vivid and it stages the reduction question perfectly. But it does zero work for testimony, and testimony is precisely where these students are already prejudiced; they've been trained to think "authority" is the unphilosophical pramāṇa, the one you invoke when you've run out of reasons. So maybe the case should be the one where you were told in advance by a forester that the gavaya resembles a cow, and later, out in the forest, you spot something cow-like and it clicks what the word was hooked to — because then the question becomes whether that click is memory plus testimony plus perception or something over and above them, and Nyāya's reason for saying "over and above" is a reason about how memory can't itself generate anything new. Tell me which one to build on and what the case must have and must not have, because I suspect I'm trying to make one example do two jobs and it won't. Second: where do I put the bit I find most striking, which is that on the Nyāya picture you never witness the truth of your own cognitions — veridicality gets ascertained only afterwards, and the test is roughly whether acting on it works out, and in ordinary cases you don't even run the test, you just act unless something raises a doubt. It looks uncannily like externalism plus a default presumption of trust, and it's the thing that would most surprise them, but it seems to presuppose they already know what a pramā is, so I can't tell whether it's an opening hook or a closing payoff.

Also fold in these two, if they fit without derailing anything: the idea of a vitiating condition — wet fuel, say, as the thing that stops smoke tracking fire, defined structurally rather than as a list of things to check for, which I think is the closest thing here to what we'd call an undercutting defeater — and the bizarre perceptual machinery that gets invented so you can supposedly perceive a pervasion holding across instances you never visited, which is where I'll need to physically restrain them from saying "that's just Hume's problem of induction and they solved it." Misconceptions I'm already braced for: pramāṇa gets translated in their heads as "source of evidence" and the whole framework turns internalist; they decide the five-step schema is Aristotle with two redundant steps bolted on for ritual reasons; they take śabda to mean faith; they conclude the reduction debate is verbal quibbling about a list. Add whatever else you'd expect and tell me the order they'll arrive in, because I think some of them will form the internalist reading before I've even said the word pramāṇa. One constraint on the cut list: they're assessed by an essay in which they have to set one of these reduction disputes against a live debate in the analytic literature, so whatever survives has to leave them able to write that essay and not merely charmed, and I'd like you to tell me honestly whether putting the five-step inference schema on the board at all is a trap at this length.
