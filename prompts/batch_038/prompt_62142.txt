I lead a small equalities analysis team in a large English metropolitan council. Since the administration changed, every cabinet report with a distributional effect has to carry a short distributional statement from us: who gains, who loses, and how far the proposal narrows or widens inequality in the city. We have always produced that statement from a single measure — equivalised household net income, occasionally supplemented by a ward-level deprivation score. We are now being challenged from three directions at once. Disability and carer organisations say income is the wrong measure, because two households on the same income can do and be radically different things depending on impairment, unpaid care commitments and the cost of moving around an inaccessible city. Our public health colleagues say what actually matters is lived experience, and they want subjective wellbeing in the statement. And the scrutiny committee, advised by an academic, has asked why we are not counting wealth, housing security and access to services as resources in their own right, noting that a cash measure hides who holds what.

I need you to convert this debate about the metric of equality into a procedure my team can run on a proposal. The constraints are these: four hours per proposal; two analysts with no formal training in political philosophy beyond a first-year module; a final statement of no more than four pages for elected members who will not read a methodological argument; and the possibility that the statement is later read by lawyers, because several of these proposals touch our statutory equality duties.

Please give me the procedure itself, in order, as something I could hand to a new starter on their first day. For each step I want: the question asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, what a deficient answer triggers, and which step comes next. The procedure must state explicitly which metric is in play at each point and why that metric is licensed for the question being asked, so that we never smuggle a measure in silently. It needs a step where personal, social and environmental conversion factors are identified as such, rather than folded into a cost-of-living adjustment. It needs a rule for what to do when the three candidate metrics rank the proposal differently — and I do not want that rule to be an average or a weighted index, because the whole point of the disagreement is that they are measuring different things. It needs stopping points: places where the answer is a flat no, and places where the question turns out to be one of values rather than fact and the procedure must hand the decision to the members rather than dress a preference up as a finding. Mark clearly which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do, and name the kinds of proposal on which it will misfire.

Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography or citations. Refer to a theorist only where a step would be unintelligible without the name attached to it.

Run the procedure on three of our live cases, showing your working at each step rather than only the verdict:

- Case A: capital funding to bring forty neighbourhood playgrounds up to inclusive design standards (level-access surfacing, sensory equipment, an accessible toilet and changing facility at each site), with no change to fees or charges elsewhere.
- Case B: replacing our current means-tested council tax reduction scheme with a flat £150 annual payment to every household in the bottom two income bands, which is cheaper to administer and would take up far more of the entitlement than the existing scheme does. Carer organisations have already told us the payment is not adjusted for the additional costs of disability.
- Case C: a proposal my own team drafted and has been promoting for a year — free access to all council leisure centres and pools for every resident, paid for by ending a means-tested concessionary travel pass. Our internal monitoring suggests the largest gains in hours used would go to middle-income households with cars, and the pass being withdrawn is the one lowest-income users rely on to reach facilities at all.

I expect Case C to come out badly for us. If your procedure lets it pass comfortably, that is evidence the procedure is wrong, not that we were right; but if it refuses it, say so plainly and show which step did the work.

Write the whole response in British English. After the prose procedure and the three worked cases, give me a machine-readable summary as a single JSON object with one field, `case_verdicts`, holding an array of three objects in the order A, B, C. Each object must have exactly these fields: `case_id` (the letter); `metric_selected` (one of "welfare", "resources", "capabilities", "multiple"); `why_licensed` (one or two sentences naming the step that selected it); `step_at_which_stopped` (the step number and its title); `outcome` (one of "proceed", "proceed with conditions", "refer to members", "refuse"); `conditions` (an array of strings, empty if none); `divergence` (an object with the keys `welfare`, `resources`, `capabilities`, each taking one of "narrows inequality", "widens inequality", "no material change", "insufficient data"); `conversion_factors` (an array of the conversion factors the assessment turned on); `judgment_calls` (an array of strings, each naming a point where a person rather than the procedure decided, with one clause on why); `not_settled` (what the procedure could not resolve in this case); and `confidence` (one of "high", "moderate", "low").
