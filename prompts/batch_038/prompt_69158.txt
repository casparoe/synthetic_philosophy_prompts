The question: *State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as precisely as you can, and distinguish it from the impartial observer argument. Then explain what the theorem does and does not establish about utilitarianism, and address one standard objection to the utilitarian reading of it.*

The student's answer: "Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says the following. Suppose each individual has preferences over social prospects (lotteries over outcomes) satisfying the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, so each is represented by a utility function u_i. Suppose society also has preferences over those same prospects, satisfying the same axioms, represented by a social utility function W. And suppose society respects the Pareto principle: whenever every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them too. Then W is a weighted sum, W = a_1u_1 + ... + a_nu_n, with every a_i positive. So social evaluation is linear in individual utility. Two things are worth stressing. First, the theorem needs cardinal utility, so the u_i have to be measurable on a ratio scale with a real zero point, otherwise the weights wouldn't mean anything. That's also how Harsanyi solves the problem of interpersonal comparison: once everyone's utility is on the same cardinal scale, comparing people is just comparing numbers, and Arrow's impossibility result doesn't bite because Arrow was denying himself exactly that information. Harsanyi's is the stronger theorem for that reason. Second, the theorem on its own leaves the weights free, but the Pareto principle pins them down as equal, since no one's utility function gets a bigger coefficient than anyone else's, so we land on the classic utilitarian sum. That's why the theorem is normally taken to establish utilitarianism rather than merely some weighted variant of it. The same structure appears in Rawls: Rawls and Harsanyi agree that the person behind the veil doesn't know who she will be and treats all positions as equally likely, and they disagree only about the decision rule, maximin instead of expected utility. The standard objection is that the u_i are just representations of preferences over lotteries, not measures of well-being. But a welfarist already holds that well-being is preference satisfaction, so the objection begs the question. And it isn't needed for other purposes either: someone who wants to favour the worse off can stay inside the theorem simply by using unequal weights, so prioritarianism is a special case of it rather than a rival."
