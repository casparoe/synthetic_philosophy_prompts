My old boss is retiring at the end of the month and I've been volunteered to give the toast at her farewell dinner. She ran the behavioural insights unit inside a government department for eleven years — basically her entire job was defaults. An organ donor registration pilot she flipped from opt-in to opt-out, pensions auto-enrolment, the form for claiming a heating benefit cut from nine pages to two, the school meal ordering system rearranged so the thing you see first is the thing you take. About 70 people will be in the room: her old team, a couple of ministers, two or three academics who've written angry things about her, a civil liberties lawyer who fought her opt-out pilot in public for years and lost, and then her family — husband, two adult kids, and a grandson of about fifteen who has decided the whole profession is creepy and says so at dinner. I'm not a philosopher, I do programme evaluation now, and I've been arguing with a friend all week about whether any of this is defensible, which is probably why I'm overthinking the toast.

She had one rule, taped inside her desk drawer, and she made every one of us argue our work past it: if you'd be embarrassed to explain it to the person it happens to, don't ship it. Not a rule about outcomes — a rule about being sayable out loud, to the person it runs on, in words they'd accept. That's the thing I want the toast built out of, because its also the thing the lawyer in the room thinks she never actually honoured, and honestly the thing I'm not sure she always honoured either. So here's what makes this hard: the toast is itself a piece of choice architecture. It's going to shape how sixty-some people remember her and what the ministers think they're permitted to do next, and almost nobody in the room will notice it doing that. So it has to pass its own test — nothing in it she'd wince at hearing in front of her family, nothing that quietly settles an argument by leaving out the part that cuts against her, no sentimental inflation of what eleven years of form design actually amounted to.

Write it, roughly four minutes to say out loud. It needs one joke that works for both halves of the room — the people who know what a reference class is and the fifteen year old — and it has to get the "there's no such thing as a neutral default, so the only questions are which one and who picked it" point across without turning into a lecture. Don't use the word paternalism; she banned the word nudge in the office somewhere around year three, on the grounds that it made everyone feel clever, so let that ban do some work too. Handle the lawyer: concede what his objection gets right — something about how cheap a nudge is for the person doing it and how invisible it is to the person receiving it — rather than flattering her past him, but don't let the toast become about him. And end as affection, on something particular about her, not on a moral about public service.

Then, separately, in three or four sentences: tell me where you bent the truth to make it land, and which line in the speech her own rule would flag.
