I'm in year four of a philosophy PhD, nominally punishment theory, but I've drifted into a project about informal social sanction — deplatforming, public naming, the whole apparatus — and I want to pressure-test a proposal I cooked up before I hand it to my advisor, who I fully expect will hate it. I've been calling it the discharge model. That's my own coinage, I made it up for the paper, so don't go looking for prior art or attributing it to anybody. The idea is to treat a public sanction event the way bankruptcy law treats a debt: a clock, and then a hard stop. Concretely, three provisions. One: seven years after the sanction event, the incident is expunged — it can't be used in employment screening, professional licensing, or reinstatement decisions, and platforms have to restore accounts on request. Two: the clock only runs if the person hasn't repeated the conduct, but there is deliberately no contrition requirement, no mandated apology, no therapy box to tick, because compelled remorse is its own species of coercion. Three: it binds only institutions with formal decision procedures — employers, licensing boards, platforms — not individuals; your friends are still perfectly allowed to not want you around. My pitch is that informal sanction right now is punishment with no proportionality, no procedure, and no exit, which makes it look less like punishment and more like outlawry or civil death, and that a bound is the bare minimum for it to be permissible at all.

So: write me a dialogue between someone defending that and someone attacking it, where both are genuinely sharp and neither is a strawman. The defender argues roughly my line above. The attacker shouldn't be a free-speech absolutist or a scold about "accountability culture" — their view should be more interesting than that, something like: social sanction isn't punishment at all, it's a community continuously revising who it trusts, and bolting a punishment-shaped clock onto it misdescribes what's actually going on. Trust doesn't expire on a schedule, and a community that has to readmit someone on day 2,556 isn't forgiving them, it's being overruled by an institution that was never the injured party. And if you make informal sanction look like punishment in order to borrow punishment's legitimacy, you'd better be ready to hand over all of punishment's procedures, not just the sentence-length part. I want them to actually respond to each other's points and get dragged into the weeds, not deliver alternating op-eds.

Give them a case to fight over, since abstractions will let them talk past each other. D. was nineteen in 2014 and posted a run of genuinely vile stuff, not edgy-adjacent, actually targeted at a specific group. It resurfaced in 2023; he was fired from a mid-level nonprofit job, dropped by the platforms, and publicly named. Two complications I want the characters to have to handle rather than wave away: first, the viral thread that got him fired included an invented detail — something he never did — which was corrected months later in a place nobody read, so his record is partly false and uncorrectable in practice; second, he's spent the years since doing unglamorous, verifiable repair work, and the people most directly targeted by the original posts say clearly that they don't want him back and object to any clock whatsoever. Have the two characters argue about who, if anyone, gets to stop the clock, and about whether expungement is even the right remedy for a record that's false rather than merely old — because expunging a partly false record buries the correction along with the truth, and I want somebody to notice that.
