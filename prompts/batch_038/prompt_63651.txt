I direct a four-person, three-year research program on how institutions ought to relate to their own pasts — a university deciding what to teach and what to rename, a museum deciding what to conserve and what to let go, a learned society deciding what to do with the record of a discredited founder. Our partners keep asking us for guidance, and we keep stalling, because our shared working vocabulary has a hole in it. That vocabulary is deliberately Nietzschean: his second Untimely Meditation, on the use and disadvantage of history for life. We use his three modes — monumental history, which looks to past greatness for models and encouragement and risks flattening the past into an ideal that crushes the present; antiquarian history, which reverently preserves the soil one grew from and risks mummification; critical history, which drags the past to judgment and breaks with it, and risks mere self-assertion. We also lean on his notion of plastic power, the capacity to grow out of oneself, to incorporate and transform what one inherits instead of being turned by it into a passive arena of unresolved struggles, and on his list of symptoms of historical over-saturation: the sense of being a latecomer, an epigone; ironic detachment; a personality worn down by endless comparison; and a corroded sense of justice in someone trained to enter every standpoint and therefore unable to condemn any.

The team has produced the following candidate questions, and I need to convert them into an agenda.

1. The criterion problem. "Serves life" does all the normative work in the essay. Can it be specified so that it can be failed — so that some institutional posture toward the past can be shown to be pathological rather than merely contested — or is plastic power simply a name for the outcome we already admired?

2. The transfer problem. Plastic power is ascribed to a person, a people, and a culture. Our clients are institutions: charters, committees, staff turnover, no continuous psychology. What survives the transfer, and must the question always collapse into the attitudes of whoever currently holds office?

3. The sequencing problem. Can the three modes be combined in one project — an exhibition that is at once monumental and critical — or must Nietzsche's balance be temporal, with demolition, reconstruction, and consolidation taking turns? If temporal, who sets the clock, and how long is a healthy interval?

4. The antiquarian-detachment problem. Nietzsche ties antiquarian piety to one's own origins and locality. Almost everything our partners actually hold is neither grand nor founding: maintenance routines, mid-tier figures, the ordinary infrastructure of a discipline. Can the mode be pointed at that without ceasing to be Nietzsche's mode?

5. The critical-mode problem. Is critical history the cure for the corrosion of justice that historical training produces, or its most dangerous form — licensed anachronism? What would distinguish the critical historian's condemnation from a present that simply prefers itself?

6. The diagnosis problem. Could any of the symptoms be turned into an institutional diagnosis, with something that would count as evidence of improvement, or is the health-and-sickness idiom unusable outside polemic?

My own priors, which you should attack if you think them wrong: I believe (1) is upstream of everything else and that a program which cannot answer it will produce beautiful descriptions with no normative force; I suspect (6) is a trap dressed as a project, since two of my colleagues want it precisely because it sounds like something fundable, and one of the four of us has survey-methods training that makes it look tractable. One colleague holds the opposite view on both.

Give me a defended ordering, not a survey. Select the two or three questions the program should actually spend its three years on and rank them, stating the criteria you are using — how many of the other questions depend on the answer, how tractable each is for historians and philosophers rather than social scientists, how likely a good answer is to change what our partner institutions do, and how much each is already over-worked. Give a definite verdict on every one of the six: pursue, defer, or drop, with reasons. Say which look profound but are scholastic, and which look modest but would unlock the others. Then tell me what, at an eighteen-month review, should move a question up or down: what result, what failure to get traction, what change in the outside world. I am not after references, bibliography, or a list of who has written on this — I want your judgment, stated as a judgment, with the reasoning exposed so I can see exactly where you and I part company.
