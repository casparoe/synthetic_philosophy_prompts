I serve as executive director of a regional land trust governed by a nine-member volunteer board, and I am trying to resolve a procedural dispute that has left us unable to record a decision on a significant acquisition. I would value a careful analysis rather than a quick fix, because the board is now split over a question of principle that I do not think any of us is equipped to settle.

Last month we considered purchasing a conservation easement on two hundred acres of riverfront farmland. Our bylaws require a three-question roll call on every acquisition: (1) Is the parcel consistent with our stated conservation priorities? (2) Is the purchase likely to deliver the ecological benefits described in the staff report? (3) Can we absorb the cost without impairing the endowment? Each question is decided by simple majority, and the acquisition is approved only if all three carry.

The vote went as follows. Three trustees answered yes, yes, no: they accept the priority case and the ecological case but believe we cannot afford it this year. Three answered yes, no, yes: they are satisfied on priority and finances but doubt the habitat modeling in the staff report. Three answered no, yes, yes: they are persuaded by the science and the money but hold that riverfront farmland falls outside what the trust was founded to protect. Each of the three questions therefore carried, six to three. When the chair then put the overall motion to purchase, as our practice has always been, the vote was nine to zero against, since every trustee's own answers, taken together, implied rejection.

The minutes remain unwritten, and the argument has turned substantive. One trustee, a retired judge, says the premise votes are the only ones that expressed a judgment about anything and that the motion was never really put to a vote on the merits. Another says the bylaws are clear that approval requires all three questions to pass and that we are therefore obliged to buy the easement. A third says the nine-to-zero result is the only one that reflects what the board actually wants and that recording anything else would falsify our intentions. That same trustee has proposed re-running the motion now that everyone has seen the tabulation, and predicts it will pass, because two members of the first bloc have told him privately that they would rather the land be protected than win their argument about the endowment. Others call this an attempt to manufacture a result by holding a vote twice. A fourth trustee wants to amend the bylaws so that we vote only on the overall motion in future, on the grounds that this embarrassment would never have arisen otherwise; a fifth wants the opposite amendment.

We must also answer to outside parties. Our annual report to the state charitable regulator requires a single coherent statement of board action, one of our funders has asked individual trustees whether they personally support the purchase, and the trust attends a town meeting next month where residents will ask the same question of whoever from the board is present. I expect that a majority of our trustees, asked one at a time, would say no.

With that in mind:

1. Is it correct to say that the board holds a contradictory set of views — that it affirms each of three propositions and denies what follows from them — when no individual trustee affirms and denies anything? Or is that a loose way of speaking that dissolves on inspection?
2. Which vote should govern the minutes, and does the answer depend on what the vote is for? The trust acts through staff, signs a purchase agreement, and can be sued, so the question is not merely internal.
3. Is the proposed second vote on the motion a legitimate correction of an artifact of procedure, or a manipulation of the outcome? Does it matter that the trustees who would now vote yes say they are voting against what they actually think we should do?
4. Should we amend the bylaws to decide acquisitions by a single overall vote, by the three questions alone, or neither? One of our members suspects that no drafting solution exists — that any procedure we choose will expose us to the same embarrassment on some future parcel. Is she right, and if so, what should a small volunteer board do about it?
5. Finally, is there anything objectionable in the fact that the decision we would record is one that not a single trustee endorses, given that we will defend it publicly as the trust's own considered judgment?
