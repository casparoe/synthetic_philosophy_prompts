I'm running one of the sessions in our department's perception-and-cognition reading group next Thursday — about a dozen people, mostly psych grad students plus two philosophers who come for the arguments and stay for the free pizza. The topic is whether perception is cognitively penetrable, and my worry is that the whole hour collapses into the definitional food fight it always collapses into: what counts as "cognitive," what counts as "perception," whether attention is a loophole or a legitimate route in. My advisor's view is that once you pin the definitions down there's nothing substantive left to disagree about, which I find deflating and also probably wrong, and I want to walk in with material that carries weight no matter how you settle the terminology.

So I typed up five experimental setups. I described them all in the same flat voice with roughly the same amount of methodological detail, and — full disclosure — two of the five I made up out of whole cloth, because I wanted to see whether the ones that smell like famous published results get a free pass. Im not telling you which two and I'd rather you not play guess-the-paper, so please don't.

1. Subjects played a repeated investment game with a partner whose headshot sat in the corner of the screen. Afterwards, some were told, as part of the script, that the partner had quietly kept money that belonged to them; others were told the partner had played straight; a third group got no information at all. Then a size-matching task: a face flashed for about 120 ms followed by a pattern mask, and the subject adjusted the width of a grey oval until it looked as wide as the face had looked. Faces of "cheaters" were matched roughly 5% too wide relative to the straight-player faces, with no shift in the no-information group. The effect survived a concurrent syllable-repetition task, and there was no shift when the faces were replaced by scrambled-face images matched for spatial frequency. n = 84, within-subject, and the lab reports one replication with a different stimulus set.

2. Subjects learned two novel nonsense labels for two patches sitting next to each other on a green-to-blue continuum, while a different pair of patches the same physical distance apart in another region of the continuum got no labels. Training was short and the labels were never mentioned again. Test: match-to-sample — see one patch, wait, then pick the identical patch out of an array of six, with instructions stressing speed and accuracy and never asking for any verbal report. Discrimination across the labelled boundary was measurably sharper than across the equally-spaced unlabelled boundary, and the gap disappeared when subjects did articulatory suppression (repeating a string of syllables) during the delay. n = 40, between-subjects for the suppression manipulation, replicated with a second set of labels in a different colour region.

3. Half the subjects did 20 minutes on a stationary bike and were then handed a cup of water; half sat quietly and were handed nothing. Everyone then estimated, by walking blindfolded toward a table and stopping where they thought they'd arrived, the distance to three objects sitting 3 m away: a bottle of water, a stapler, and a mug. Thirsty subjects stopped about 12% short of the water bottle relative to controls, consistent with seeing it as nearer. The same pattern came out at about the same size for the stapler and the mug, and thirsty subjects also judged the hallway they'd just walked down as shorter. n = 30 per group.

4. Skiers at the top of a run estimated its steepness by tilting a hinged board until it matched the slope in front of them. Half had just been told by the experimenter that the run was graded expert; half were told nothing. The told group set the board about 4 degrees steeper. Two wrinkles: the study also collected the estimate again ten minutes later, from memory, and the paper reports those numbers rather then the on-site ones; and in a second phase the same subjects judged photographs of slopes they'd never seen, were told that some of the photos came from expert terrain, and showed a shift about the same size as on the real slope. No measure of expected effort, and no measure of how good a skier each subject took themselves to be. n = 32 per group.

5. Subjects were given the classic arrow-head versus feather-head figure, the one where two horizontal shafts look unequal in length but are physically identical, along with a steel ruler. They measured both shafts themselves, out loud, until they could state and demonstrate that the lengths were the same; the experimenter quizzed them and every subject passed. Immediately afterwards, still holding the ruler and still able to give the correct answer on demand, they did a perceptual matching task on the same figures — adjusting a comparison line until it looked as long as the shaft. The illusion came back at something like 90% of the size it showed in a baseline group that had never been handed a ruler. n = 60.

Sort these five by how much evidential weight each one carries for the claim that perceptual experience itself — not memory of it, not the judgment or report that comes after — is shaped by the content of some belief, desire, expectation, or label the subject already has. Give me exactly five numbered entries in your order, strongest first, and in each entry give me the case number, a score out of ten, and one or two sentences naming the single feature of the setup that does most of the work for or against it. No introduction, no wrap-up paragraph, no detour into how the definitions should be fixed — just the five entries. If one of them strikes you as belonging on a different list entirely, treat that as a finding and score it accordingly instead of parking it in the middle.
