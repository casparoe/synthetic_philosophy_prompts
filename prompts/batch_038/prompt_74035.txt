I am in the final year of an undergraduate degree in philosophy and economics, and the module I am taking on risk and rational choice has spent the last three weeks on the dispute between expected-value maximisation and the Kelly criterion. My seminar presentation this Thursday concerns the ergodicity argument: the claim that, because wealth typically evolves multiplicatively, the expectation value of wealth is an average taken across an ensemble of parallel histories of which the agent lives only one, whereas the quantity the agent can actually experience is the time-average growth rate, which for a favourable bet is recovered by maximising the logarithm of wealth. My tutor is broadly sympathetic to that line. A friend of mine who reads mathematics thinks it is log utility in a new coat, and that Samuelson's counter-objection — that a long-run growth criterion is of no use to an agent who must make a single decision and then die, and that an agent with a bounded utility function who declines one play of a bet will decline any finite number of plays — has never properly been answered. We have been arguing about it in the bar for a fortnight, and I should like to end my presentation with a joke that takes a side, or at the very least knows where the bodies are buried.

Please write that joke for me. I should like it in dialogue form, no longer than about a hundred and twenty words, and I should like the setup to be concrete rather than abstract: two agents offered the same repeated even-money wager on a coin that lands heads sixty per cent of the time, one of whom stakes his entire bankroll every round because that maximises his expected wealth, the other of whom stakes a fifth. The punchline must turn on the actual content of the two positions — the difference between an ensemble average and a time average, the way variance erodes the geometric mean relative to the arithmetic mean, or the fact that asymptotic growth-rate optimality is cold comfort to a single mortal player — and not merely on the fact that the characters are busy doing sums. Someone who knows what a Kelly bet is ought to find it funny for the right reason; someone who also knows the Samuelson objection ought to find it funnier still. Please do not pun on the name Kelly, avoid the stock opening in which several theorists walk into a bar unless you can genuinely subvert it, and above all do not append a gloss explaining the joke. The audience will be half philosophy students and half finance students, and both halves will resent being walked through it.

After the joke, add two or three sentences telling me precisely which piece of the theory the punchline is doing work with, so that I can check whether it is load-bearing or merely decorative, and tell me candidly whether it would still land for anyone in the room who has seen nothing more than the formula f = p − q for an even-money bet. If you conclude that the setup I have specified cannot carry a real punchline, say so and propose the nearest variant that can, rather than padding out a weak one.
