Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 words between a computationalist about mind and an anti-computationalist whose objection is not the usual syntax-versus-semantics one, but the triviality of implementation itself.

The computationalist holds that having a mind is a matter of one's physical states realizing the right abstract automaton, and that this is a substantive, falsifiable thesis about brains rather than a redescription of everything. The anti-computationalist's argument is that Putnam's noncyclical-behavior assumption licenses arbitrary regroupings: since a rock, a wall, or a puddle is in a distinct maximal state at each moment, you can chop its history into disjunctive state types that mirror the transition table of any finite automaton you please. If every system implements every automaton, "implements" is not a mind-independent property, and the computationalist's thesis is either vacuous or false.

Constraints on the exchange:

- The computationalist may not simply appeal to the "natural" or "intended" mapping. They must do work — for instance, insisting that a genuine implementation have to support counterfactuals about what the system would do given different inputs and interventions, with state groupings that are physically specifiable and causally robust rather than ad hoc disjunctions — and may press the point that the core theorem concerns inputless automata, so the live question is what happens once inputs are added, since inputs are precisely what such counterfactuals quantify over.
- The anti-computationalist must press on who selects the relevant counterfactuals and by what non-question-begging standard, arguing that counterfactual robustness already presupposes an individuation of physical states that is itself description-relative, and that the computationalist's escape therefore smuggles in the interests of the describer.
- Neither may invoke understanding, intentionality, or phenomenal consciousness as a premise. The dispute stays entirely about whether implementation ascriptions can be made non-arbitrary.

I have read enough versions of this argument in which the anti-computationalist is a cardboard cutout, so hold both sides at full strength: each should land at least one blow the other is forced to concede, and the dialogue should close by naming the crux the two now share and saying what would settle it, rather than by agreeing to disagree.
