so i'm helping run a small reading group on the philosophy of obligation at my institution and we've hit the promising week, and honestly the standard menu has started to blur for everyone — Hume's interest-based story, the rule/practice conventionalism, Scanlon's Principle F and its assurance-ish machinery, the second-personal stuff, and then the will-theory camp (Owens, Shiffrin, that family) where promising is a transfer of normative control over an action. My students can recite the labels but they can't tell you what would actually settle a hard case, and I think the reason is that the cases they get are all too clean: two living adults, a loan, a dinner, a ride to the airport. Everything interesting happens when one of the parties stops existing. So here's what I want you to write, and please put on your best promissory-obligation-specialist hat — you're someone who has actually thought about what distinguishes a promise from a mere prediction or a threat, not someone summarizing an encyclopedia entry. Think carefully before you start; jot yourself a few brainstorming bullets about which of the accounts genuinely diverge on the case (keep those to yourself or make them very short, I mainly want the dialogue).

The case, which is basically the Kafka/Brod situation with the serial numbers filed off. A novelist, call her Vera, is dying. Her closest friend and chosen literary executor, Ilan, visits her in the last weeks. She gives him the only complete draft of an unfinished second novel and tells him she wants it destroyed — not archived, not sealed in a vault for scholars, burned. Ilan promises. Then, because she can see him hesitating, she adds a fallback in the same breath: "if you find you can't do it, then lock it away for fifty years and let whoever's left decide." Then a third thing, which she makes him write down: "and I release you in advance from anything you find you can't bring yourself to do." Vera dies. Ilan doesn't burn the book. Twenty years later, Vera's nieces — the residuary beneficiaries of her estate, who were children when she died and have no memory of her — find out about the manuscript, find out it's good, and write Ilan a letter saying, in effect: we're the only people who now stand where Vera stood, we release you, publish it, the world will be better off and nobody is harmed. Ilan's daughter, a lawyer, tells him that's the end of the matter and he's free. He doesn't feel free. That's the whole setup.

Now write me a dialogue, roughly 1400–1800 words, between two philosophers who are consulting for Ilan and who flat-out disagree about why. Character A holds that a promise transfers authority over an action to the promisee, so the obligation is constituted by Vera's having been put in charge of what Ilan does with the book; A has to deal with the fact that the person in charge is dead, and with whether the right she acquired can be bequeathed to nieces who were toddlers at the time or whether it lapsed the moment she died, leaving Ilan bound to nobody in particular. Character B holds that promising matters because of what it does for the promisee's expectations and her ability to plan and settle things — an assurance-and-practice picture, maybe with a Humean or Scanlonian flavor — and has to deal with the fact that there is now no expectation left to frustrate, nobody who will ever learn what Ilan did, and no reliance loss at all. Both of them should be genuinely good: nobody gets to be a strawman, nobody gets to win by the other side making an obvious blunder, and they should actually respond to what the other just said rather than alternating set pieces. I want them to press on at least these, ideally in this order of escalation: (1) can a right created by a promise survive its holder, and if not, what is left — a free-floating duty nobody can enforce or waive, and is that even coherent? (2) is breaking a promise to a dead person a wrong *to her*, and what would have to be true about the dead for that to make sense? (3) does Vera's advance release clause do anything, given that she tried to waive a claim before it had any determinate content, and does the fifty-year fallback sever cleanly from the burning instruction or is it part of one indivisible deal? (4) the control question — if Vera had never extracted a promise and had only said "I hope you'll burn it," would Ilan's publishing be any less bad, and if it wouldn't, what work is the promise actually doing? Have one of them concede a real point somewhere in the middle, a point that costs them something, because otherwise it reads like two lectures. End without a tidy resolution: no handshake, no synthesis, but a sharpened crux, with each of them stating in a sentence or two what they now take the actual disagreement to be, and it should be clear to the reader that the two of them disagree about something deeper than this one manuscript. Named speakers, minimal stage directions, and let them sound like people who are a bit tired and a bit competitive, not like a textbook. Also if you can work in why Ilan's thier own feeling of not being free matters or doesn't matter to either theory, do — but don't let it turn into a psychology seminar, keep it about the obligation.
