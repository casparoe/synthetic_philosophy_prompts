I sit on the board of a small documentation center — basically an oral-history archive with two exhibition rooms bolted on — in a country about thirty years past the end of its dirty little civil conflict. I'm the only person on the board with any philosophy training (I teach at a community college, mostly intro ethics), so whenever a decision gets conceptually tangled everybody turns to me, which is flattering and also a bit of a trap. Here's the case for Thursday. A family has offered us the complete personal papers of a man who was a secondary-school teacher during the conflict and who, we learned from the papers themselves, was a paid informant for the security police. Two things in the donation matter. First: forty-odd hours of cassette interviews he recorded in the nineties with former political detainees, conducted as what he described as a private project — the interviewees were told nothing would ever be made public, and several have since died. Second: his diaries, which name in his own hand roughly two hundred people he reported on and, in a lot of cases, explain why. A victims' association has written demanding the diaries be published in full immediately, on the grounds that this is the only surviving list of its kind and that sealing it makes us complicit in a cover-up. The adult children of four of the interviewees have written seperately demanding the tapes be destroyed, or at minimum never digitized, because their parents' consent was conditional and the condition has been broken. The deed of gift says the tapes stay sealed until the death of the last-named interviewee and can then be released only with family consent, which in practice means never. And of course nobody wants to say out loud that we're also weighing the donation against the fact that we can't make payroll past March.

Our chair has been reading Margalit on the ethics of memory and keeps saying we have duties to the dead that don't expire; another member came back with Rieff's argument that the whole never-forget posture is itself a kind of sickness that keeps old wounds open, and he thinks we should hand the diaries over to the families named in them and destroy the tapes. Meanwhile I've been trying to sort out a distinction one of our volunteers came up with — or maybe borrowed, she's vague about it — that seems to me to be doing real work in the argument. She says we're running together two different things: 'testimonial debt', what we owe to specific people who trusted us enough to talk, and 'informational debt', what we owe to nobody in particular, just the accuracy and completeness of the record. Her claim is that the diaries and the tapes sit on opposite sides of that line and therefore can't be decided together. Nobody else on the board thinks the distinction is principled, but it keeps surfacing, and I suspect she's onto something and also suspect I like it because it lets us do less.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. I need a concrete recommendation on each of the two items — diaries and tapes, treated as separate questions — with conditions attached and a timeline the board could actually vote on, not a both-sides shrug. Take a position on whether the testimonial/informational distinction is real and load-bearing or just a way of feeling better about inaction, and if you keep it, tell me what it costs us in the cases where it points the uncomfortable way. Give the strongest version of the argument you reject — specifically the destroy-the-tapes position — in words its proponents would recognize as theirs. And since we'll get a variant of this case every year until the last witness dies, close with two or three criteria a future board could apply without me in the room. No bibliography and please don't invent sources or fake quotations, I'm not citing anything in a board memo, just argue. Two pages maximum, plain language, assume the reader is a retired dentist who cares a great deal but has never read any of this stuff. We meet in the back room of a bakery, so there is always pastry, which does not help anybody think clearly.
