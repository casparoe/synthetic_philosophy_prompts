I serve on the grants allocation committee of a mid-sized private foundation, and I should like your help drafting the written opinion I intend to circulate to my eleven colleagues before our June meeting. Some of them are philosophers; most are not, so the document must be readable by a lay trustee without becoming evasive about the technical difficulty at the center of the case.

The background is this. The foundation distributes a fixed annual pot, currently about nine million dollars, across roughly sixty funded programs. For three years it has ranked programs by a single cardinal score, "wellbeing points," obtained by asking each program director to state how many points a marginal dollar would purchase for the served population. Two consultants have now proposed replacing this self-report with what they call the Paired-Lottery Calibration. Each director completes a battery of choices between certain outcomes and probability mixtures of outcomes; the battery is fitted to a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function for that program; the zero point is then stipulated to be the status quo of the served population and the unit to be one statistical life-year; and the resulting functions are summed across the whole portfolio and the pot allocated to maximize the total. The consultants report that the fit is good, that the new ranking correlates only weakly with the current one, and that a small number of programs serving very severely affected but very small populations absorb a strikingly disproportionate share of the pot under the new rule.

The committee is split three ways. One faction holds that the incumbent self-report is transparently strategic and that the calibration is the first serious attempt to measure intensity of benefit at all. A second holds that a von Neumann–Morgenstern function is unique only up to a positive affine transformation for each person separately, so that the consultants' zero and unit are stipulations dressed up as measurements, and that the foundation has no warrant for making them on anyone's behalf. A third, smaller faction holds that declining to compare is itself a decision with identifiable victims, and that an ordinal ranking of sixty programs is merely an incoherent way of expressing the comparisons it pretends to avoid.

Please draft the opinion, of roughly twelve hundred words, recommending a concrete course of action the committee can vote on in June: adopt, reject, or adopt with specified amendments. The document should do at least the following. It should state plainly whether the affine-transformation objection actually bites against a protocol that fixes both a zero and a unit, and if it does not bite, explain what work the fixing is doing and whether the foundation is entitled to do it. It should say whether the lottery battery discloses the intensity of a benefit or the director's attitude to risk, and what follows for the weights if it is the latter. It should address the concentration of the pot, and recommend whether any cap, floor, or diminishing-weights schedule should be attached, giving a rule the staff could actually apply. It should consider that a calibration procedure known in advance can be performed to, and propose whatever remedy you think workable. And it should give the losing faction its strongest case in its own best terms, not a straw man, before explaining why the recommendation nonetheless goes the way it does. Close with what evidence, arriving before the next cycle, would change your recommendation. Reason from first principles; I do not want a bibliography or a list of authorities, and I do not want the opinion to depend on the reader having one.
