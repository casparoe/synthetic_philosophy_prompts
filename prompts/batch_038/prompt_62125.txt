I am a novelist, and the residential fellowship I have been awarded expects a piece of sustained argument alongside the fiction, so I need a proposal for the year's work. My novel in progress is a four-generation family saga, and while settling its architecture I ran into a historical-philosophical question I cannot get out of: the cyclical theorists of history and the directional ones may not be contradictories at all, but claims made at different resolutions of the same material. Ibn Khaldun's dynasty exhausts its solidarity within a few generations; Polybius's constitutions rotate on a comparably human clock; Spengler gives a culture a lifespan measured in a thousand years with seasons inside it; Toynbee's civilizations rise and fall on a clock of their own. A signal sampled coarsely trends, sampled finely it repeats, and sampled at just the wrong rate it can invent a cycle that was never there — so the choice of resolution may be doing all the work, and being made tacitly, by narrative interest.

Please turn this into a two-page proposal. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the four positions as they stand leave it open, including the possibility that they do make claims which conflict across resolutions rather than merely talking past one another; give my method, month by month; and specify what would count as failure — for instance, if the resolution turns out to be fixed by nothing but the shape of the story one wants to tell, which would make the whole dispute aesthetic. Add an honest note on what would make this too large for twelve months. I will supply the references myself. Close with a brief statement of how confident you are in this framing and which single claim in it you are least sure of.
