I TA a 200-person intro ethics lecture and every fall the exact same thing happens: we hit the week on honesty, I put up a slide asking whether lying is worse than merely misleading someone, and half the room is already certain the distinction collapses and the other half is certain it's the only distinction that matters, and nobody can say *why*. This year I want to open section with a sorting exercise instead of a lecture, so people commit to concrete cases before they ever hear the standard labels and the second half of the hour is an argument about their own answers rather than about definitions. Could you build me the instrument? I'm picturing a decision tree, or a taxonomy shaped like one, with roughly six branching questions and somewhere between seven and nine leaves. Each branch should be labeled with the claim that actually splits it — the thing people disagree about, not the thing they'd say they disagree about once it's been named — and each leaf should carry the standard name of the position if it has one, or a descriptive name plus a note that basically nobody defends it if it doesn't, plus one sentence on that leaf's most awkward commitment. That last part is what I'll actually use in section.

The whole thing has to be answerable by someone who's never seen the literature, so no question can be phrased at the level of principle — nothing like "does assertion carry a guarantee?" — it has to be a case you could describe in three sentences. Here are the cases I definitely want in the mix; sharpen them however you like, and add your own where there's a gap. A drug company's release says no clinical trial has found the side effect in question, when every trial it ran was too small to have detected it. A used-car seller, asked whether the brakes have ever failed, says he's never had a single complaint about the car, which is true. A candidate asked point blank whether he ever dipped into campaign money answers that every dollar of it went into the campaign, which is true of the invoices and false of what he got out of the vendors. A poker bluff, or the anchoring move in a salary negotiation, where everyone in the room knows the convention. A lie told to an abusive ex who has tracked your roommate down and is asking where she lives. And two more that I think do real work: a case where the lie is so clumsy that nobody believes it, sitting next to a case where the literally true statement lands perfectly and everybody does — I want the tree to make people choose between them — and a case where the misleading truth is the one that leaves a paper trail you'd have to defend later, so the difference isn't about the belief you implanted but about what you can be caught with. I'd also like you to build the branches out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names, which I take to mean sorting by which feature of the speech act you think carries the moral weight: the assurance that comes with asserting something, the speaker's intention, the hearer's entitlement to rely on it, whether the false belief actually got produced, the practice or institution that makes the words mean what they do, or the damage to general trust. Then tell me which combinations of answers your six questions permit that no position actually occupies, and for each of those say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it's genuinely underexplored — and flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate rather than pretending it can.

Please write it in American English, and don't bother with citations or philosopher names — I'm not going to be able to check them and my students definitely won't, so anything you can't stand behind should just be left out or described rather than attributed. Output can be a plain indented tree with the cases in full underneath each branch. Two things at the end, though: tell me how confident you are that your branches are actually independent, meaning that a person could consistently answer one way at an early node and another way at a later one without the tree breaking, and single out the one question you're least sure discriminates — the node where you suspect the collapse-the-distinction crowd and the distinction-is-everything crowd will both click the same answer, which would quietly defeat the point of the whole exercise.
