I spent thirty-eight years running a physical chemistry laboratory and retired two years ago. Philosophy has since become my principal occupation, and I find that I approach it with the habits of someone who used to write the instrument specification before ever switching the instrument on. I should like help drawing up a specification of that kind, in the philosophy of mathematics.

The subject is the dilemma Benacerraf pressed in the early 1970s: a semantics on which ordinary mathematical statements are straightforwardly true appears to commit us to objects standing outside space, time, and causal commerce, while an epistemology respectable enough for a naturalist appears to leave mathematical knowledge unaccounted for. I have worked through the familiar replies and I do not want a survey of them, nor a verdict on which is best. What I want first is the list of requirements any adequate theory of mathematical knowledge has to meet, so that I can afterwards judge candidates against a settled specification rather than against whichever single consideration each author happens to emphasize.

One item I want built into the specification, because I think it is generally left out, is a second explanandum. The literature concentrates almost wholly on reliability: why our mathematical beliefs should match the mathematical facts rather than be right by luck. In my working life mathematics had a second function, and it was not the function of a reliable belief-forming habit. When a derivation and a measurement disagreed, nobody concluded that the measurement had refuted the arithmetic; we went looking for the mislabelled reagent, the drifted calibration, the sign error. Mathematics operated as a constraint on which experimental outcomes counted as possible at all, and it carried an authority that no empirical generalization ever carried for me. An account that dissolves the reliability problem by deflating mathematical truth — treating it as a useful fiction, or a sediment of our practices, or truth-relative-to-a-structure-we-select — owes an explanation of why the deflated item retains the authority it so obviously exercised. Please state the two explananda separately, and tell me plainly whether you believe one theory can satisfy both. If you think their conjunction is unsatisfiable, I would far rather see that as a stated result with the argument behind it than as a hedged remark at the end.

Beyond that, here are candidate requirements I have collected and cannot rank; assess each, keep or discard it, and say on what grounds:

1. That mathematical knowledge be a species of the same genus as empirical knowledge, rather than a separate faculty requiring its own story.
2. That the account explain why mathematical disagreements are settled by proof and not by further measurement.
3. That it cover mathematics no one has verified or ever will — the idle regions, the enormous cardinals, the conjectures that outlive their authors — and not merely the working core.
4. That it explain the reliability of belief without the explanation itself being a cosmic coincidence, that is, without our having stumbled on a match between mind and abstract reality for no reason.
5. That it generalize to the other apparently non-empirical domains, logic at minimum, ethics if you think that belongs in the same class.
6. That it not contradict what the history and sociology of mathematics show about how results are actually found, guessed, checked, and sometimes gotten right by accident.

For each requirement I want the consideration or case that motivates it, and a mark saying whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I am especially interested in your judgement about which of these are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices dressed as criteria: requirement 1 in particular looks to me like a naturalist's article of faith, and requirement 5 looks like a demand for economy that may be doing nothing but flattering the person who makes it.

There is a further difficulty I cannot resolve on my own and which you should treat either as a requirement or as an objection to the entire enterprise of list-making, at your discretion. Must the specification be neutral among contested epistemologies? Some philosophers argue that if the reliability challenge is stated using a substantive theory of knowledge — tracking, sensitivity, safety — it can be discharged far too cheaply, simply by denying that theory; others answer that a challenge stated thinly enough to be neutral stops being worth worrying about. If every specification either smuggles in an epistemology or evaporates, that is itself something I need to know before I read another proposal.

Finally, two shorter things. Identify which single requirement on your list eliminates the most candidates, and say why that one has the widest reach. And then, in a paragraph apiece and no longer, name for each of the main strategies now on offer — platonism coupled with an evolutionary or selection story, fictionalism, structuralism, and the neo-Fregean or abstractionist route — which requirement it gives up. I am not asking you to choose a winner, and I have neither library access nor any appetite for references: I want your reasoning and your own formulation of the criteria.
