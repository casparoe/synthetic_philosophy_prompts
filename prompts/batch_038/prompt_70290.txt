I sit on the board of a nine-unit housing co-op and last night two other board members, Elena and Marcus, had a genuinely bad arguement that I was supposed to referee and couldnt. Here's the setup.

Our charter has two clauses nobody's ever had a problem with. The own-unit clause says that on any question about what a member does inside their own unit, the co-op's determination has to go the way that member prefers. The unanimity clause says the co-op can never rank one arrangement above another arrangement that every member prefers. Then there's a third bit: when two members' protected uses collide, the board has to issue a single ranked determination, because that ranking is what decides who gets the soundproofing grant.

The collision: Sofia wants to practice trumpet in her unit before 8am, Jae wants to keep two extremely loud parrots in his. Walls are paper. Both activities happen entirely inside their own units, so both are protected. I wrote down their rankings of the four arrangements from what each of them said at the meeting, so please check my arithmatic before anything else:

Sofia: trumpet-only > all-quiet > both > birds-only
Jae: birds-only > all-quiet > both > trumpet-only

Elena's and Marcus's moves, in order:

1. Elena says the charter is self-contradictory in this case and she can show it in three steps. The own-unit clause gives you trumpet-only above all-quiet (that's Sofia's call, since only her unit differs) and both above trumpet-only (that's Jae's call, since only his differs). Unanimity gives you all-quiet above both, because they both prefer it. Round and round. So the board can't issue the determination the charter requires, and one clause has to be deleted. She'd delete own-unit, on the grounds that unanimity at least tracks what's good for members, whereas a right that lands you in the arrangement you yourself rank third isn't doing you any favours.

2. Marcus says that's a category mistake. A right isn't a premise you feed into a ranking of worlds, it's a boundary on who gets to decide. Sofia decides her trumpet, Jae decides his birds, done. The board doesn't need an ordering over all four combinations, nobody actually asked for one, and the circle is an artefact of Elena pushing rights through an aggregation machine.

3. Elena says the charter does ask for one, that's the entire point of the grant clause. And on Marcus's reading the result is worse, not better: if each just decides their own unit, Sofia plays and Jae keeps the birds, and you land on both — which each of them ranks below all-quiet. So the boundary reading doesn't dissolve the problem, it produces the outcome both complainants are complaining about and then calls it freedom.

4. Marcus says fine, then they trade. Sofia practices at the music school, Jae moves the birds to the back room or agrees to a covered cage during quiet hours. Rights are alienable, that's completely ordinary, the board's job is to bless whatever deal they reach and otherwise stay out.

5. Elena says three things are wrong with that. (a) If the determination depends on whether a deal gets made, then the rights aren't determining anything, and Marcus has quietly abandoned his own reading. (b) There's no deal available: each says they'll only give in if the other gives in first, and each prefers to keep their own noise whatever the other one does — that's literally what thier rankings say. (c) And the escape hatch everybody reaches for, that this sort of paradox needs nosy preferences, needs people caring about what goes on inside somebody else's unit, doesn't apply here, because the circle only uses Sofia's view about her own trumpet, Jae's view about his own birds, and their agreement that all-quiet beats both. Nobody's meddling.

6. Marcus says (c) is exactly backwards. The unanimity step is the meddlesome one. All-quiet versus both differs in *both* members' protected choices at once, so that pair isn't inside either person's sphere, and letting a preference over such a pair override what two separate rights imply isn't treating rights as boundaries at all, it's treating them as one input among others. Keep the own-unit clause, restrict unanimity to comparisons that don't straddle two protected spheres.

7. Elena says that's arbitrary and self-defeating: if unanimity can never be applied to a pair straddling two spheres, the co-op can never prefer anything, because every comparison worth making involves at least two people's units. Marcus's unanimity clause is a dead letter and he hasn't noticed.

Two questions, in this order. First: does the circle actually go through on the rankings as I recorded them, or did I garble something at the meeting? Second: go through moves 1 to 7 and tell me who's right at each one — rate each move from clearly right to clearly wrong with a sentence or two of justification, rather than just giving me a tally at the end. Then say who's more right overall about what the charter should do here, and what the two of them are jointly missing.
