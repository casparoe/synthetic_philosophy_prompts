So our epistemology WIP group has this tradition where everyone writes a one-page list of what they actually believe about their topic and the rest of the group spends an hour trying to break it. Mine is in about ten days and I'm still drafting — third year, working on disagreement and higher-order evidence — and honestly this is the first time I've put my views down side by side like this, so I have no idea whether the package is even coherent. Here it is, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

(1) I'm a permissivist: for a fair range of questions the total evidence doesn't single out exactly one rational doxastic attitude, different standards or priors can be equally rational on the same evidence. (2) I'm an equal-weight conciliationist about acknowledged peer disagreement — when I learn that someone I take to be my epistemic peer on Q disagrees with me, I should give their conclusion the same weight as my own reasoning, which in the simple two-person case means going to about 0.5. (3) I think peerhood is genuinely common in philosophy, at least among people who've read the same stuff and argued about it for years; I don't want the "nobody's really a peer on the big questions" escape hatch, partly because it seems unfalsifiable-ish and partly because it smells like motivated reasoning. (4) I think the spinelessness/self-undermining objection fails, and my reason is this: when people disagree with me about conciliationism itself, that meta-disagreement isn't a second independent piece of evidence, its the same disagreement wearing a different hat, so conciliating on both double-counts. (5) Higher-order evidence can be misleading, and when it is I think it can leave you rationally incoherent — the hypoxia-style case where you have strong evidence your reasoning is impaired but your reasoning feels perfectly fine to you; I don't accept a blanket requirement that your beliefs line up with your beliefs about your beliefs. (6) That said I do want to keep something like: you shouldn't knowingly hold blatantly incoherent credences, and I'm some flavor of conditionalizer, so (5) is meant to be the exception rather than the rule. (7) Case verdicts: in the restaurant-check case where a friend and I both total up the bill, get different numbers, recheck, and still differ, I split; and I think the same goes when a friend and I have both gone over an argument in a paper very carefully and disagree about whether it's valid — I split there too. (8) But I don't split when I think the disagreement clearly traces to different values or upbringing rather than to different takes on shared evidence, e.g. me versus a smart friend from a very different background on whether some policy is unjust; there I think their belief tells me about them, not about the question. (9) I also happily ignore people with demonstrably awful track records on a question even when they're as smart and as well-read as I am, and that has never felt like special pleading to me. (10) And I don't think any of this makes me a skeptic or stops me doing philosophy — sitting at ~0.5 on most contested questions is compatible with carrying on arguing for views and writing papers.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture about the disagreement literature and definitely not citations, I'll fill those in myself. Go through and tell me which of these actually pull against each other and which only look bad because they're on the same page: rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each real one say what would have to give, what the fix costs me elsewhere in the list, and whether there's a cheaper revision I'm not seeing (I suspect some of these can be patched by restricting a thesis rather than dropping it, but I don't know which). Then tell me which single change would buy the most coherence overall. I genuinely don't know where the body is buried here, and I'm deliberately not telling you which commitment I'd rather sacrifice, partly because I don't have an answer to that yet.
