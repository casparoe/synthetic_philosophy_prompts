I am in the second year of a master's programme in philosophy and I co-convene our department's weekly decision-theory reading group: fourteen of us, mostly final-year undergraduates and a few postgraduates, meeting for seventy-five minutes on Thursday evenings. This week's session is on large-number dilemmas and superrationality, and we have all read Hofstadter's 1983 Scientific American column on dilemmas for superrational thinkers, later reprinted in *Metamagical Themas*, including his account of the Luring Lottery and of what actually happened when the magazine ran it — entrants submitting numbers so astronomically large that the prize ceased to be meaningful and the winner could not even be determined. I would rather the group arrive at that failure themselves than hear me describe it, so I want to run the lottery for real, in the room, and I would like you to design it.

The idea I want them to feel is not merely that the game is a trap. It is the premise that superrational reasoning quietly relies on — that the other agents are relevantly like me, so that whatever looks compelling to me looks compelling to them, and my decision is in some sense theirs as well — and how fragile that premise turns out to be once the group stops being homogeneous. Accordingly, please build a two-stage design rather than a single round. In the first stage, the fourteen of us should be as alike as the setting permits: identical stakes, identical information, no communication after the rules are read. In the second stage, break the resemblance in a way the participants can see coming before it happens — for instance by disclosing that a designated minority of us are playing for stakes several times everyone else's, or by giving three members information the rest lack — so that the group has to notice that the inference which felt available a few minutes ago no longer has the footing it seemed to have. I want the rules themselves to make that transition unavoidable, not my commentary.

Please specify: the materials and how entries are submitted and kept secret in a small room where everyone knows everyone; how the draw is conducted live and verifiably, with a running tally of the pool that participants can watch as it collapses the prize; a payoff scheme that costs us nothing but is not therefore weightless — we are a reading group, not a lecture hall, so please be concrete about what is actually being competed for and how it is settled within the session; the communication rules, before play and between the two stages; and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits inside seventy-five minutes including the debrief.

The debrief should map what happened in the room onto the formal structure: the individual best response, the runaway escalation it generates once others are expected to reason similarly, and the point at which the superrational recommendation stops being applicable. It should also anticipate what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. In particular, I expect someone to submit an absurdly large number as a joke, exactly as the magazine's real entrants did, and I genuinely do not know whether that destroys the exercise or is the most instructive thing that could happen in it — tell me which, and tell me what I should do in the moment rather than after. Finally, give me an honest note on which feature of the dilemma a fourteen-person parlour version cannot reproduce, and which I will therefore have to supply in discussion.

On how you should answer: I do not want a menu of variants with the trade-offs laid out for me to adjudicate. Commit to a design — one payoff scheme, one decision about pre-play communication, one way of handling the joker — and give me your reasons in a few sentences for each choice. Where your confidence is genuinely low, such as whether the second stage will land with a group that has just been burned by the first, say so plainly instead of hedging the whole thing.
