I write a science-and-ideas column and I've spent the last few weeks sitting with Henry Sidgwick's psychical research, which is a strange thing to sit with: the man who wrote The Methods of Ethics was also the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and the society's big census asked some 17,000 people whether they'd ever had an apparition-like experience. Its made me want to pin down what I actually think about evidence like that, going by my own verdicts rather than by my general attitude about spooks. So here they are:

- A woman sees her brother standing at the foot of her bed, soaked and miserable; next morning she learns he drowned that night a hundred miles away. One such case: nothing to me. Two dozen of them, collected independantly and screened for the obvious routes: now I lean toward something being there.
- A medium produces details about a dead relative nobody in the room could have known, and afterwards we find she went to school with the sitter's sister. Dismissed, no residue.
- Same seance, same kind of detail, but no information route can be found at all. Barely moves me — and I can't say why, because that's just the case above minus the debunk.
- A lab publishes a card-guessing result at better than one in a million, protocol posted in advance. I assume there's a flaw I can't see, and I'd take that bet against almost anybody.
- A huge physics collaboration reports neutrinos outrunning light at comparable significance. I assume a flaw there too — and was right, it turned out to be a loose cable.
- Two teams report in 1998 that distant supernovae imply the expansion of the universe is speeding up. Massively unwelcome, nothing in the received picture wanted it to be true — and I basically just accept it. That's the pair I can't reconcile with the two above. Same statistical heft, same "this would rewrite everything," completely opposite verdicts from me.
- My most sceptical friend swears she knew her father had died before the phone rang. I go back and forth on this one constantly.

Is there a principle behind these — precise enough that I could hand it to a fact-checker and she could apply it without me in the room? Tell me what each clause is doing and which case forced it in, which of my verdicts it has to overrule, and what it delivers on the two I left open: a well-designed psi study nobody can replicate (does my confidence in the original drop, and how far?), and whether Sidgwick's own census result should have moved him more than it apparently did. And be blunt about whether the 1998-versus-psi difference is a real asymmetry in the evidence or just my prejudices wearing a lab coat.
