I am in the middle of a novel whose antagonist is a mid-level administrator in an institution that is neither a state nor an army, and who presides over harms of a scale no single decision of his ever expresses. I do not want him to illustrate Hannah Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial; illustration has been done to death, and a character who is a paraphrase is dead on the page. I want him to strain that account. So I need a case built to a specification, together with a demonstration that it meets the specification.

Build me two men in the same organization, in adjacent roles, both of whom route or countersign decisions producing harm that is large, dispersed, and entirely foreseeable — the sort of harm a freight network, an insurance claims division, or a municipal housing authority can do without ever using the word harm. Call them A and B.

A must be recognizably Arendtian: unable to hold the standpoint of another person in mind for more than a moment, dependent on prefabricated phrases, without inner conflict and without convictions. B must satisfy every positive marker Arendt attaches to a person who thinks: an original and unceasing dialogue with himself, a vivid and accurate capacity to imagine himself in the place of those his decisions fall upon, a refusal of received formulations, and a self-knowledge he does not look away from. B must be the one who does more damage, and who does it more culpably by any ordinary reckoning — while remaining without ideology, without hatred, without cruelty, and without devotion to any cause, so that the damage cannot simply be charged to belief.

Constraints. The setting must be one a reader recognizes from working life: no totalitarian apparatus, no war, no exotic technology, no compulsion or altered state of mind. Two hundred and fifty words per sketch, no more. And the verdicts must be generated by the detail rather than declared: if you find yourself writing that B is the worse man, delete it and let the particulars carry it.

Then the analysis, in this order. What Arendt's framework predicts about each man, and precisely where it strains. Which of her claims is doing the work in B's case — the identification of thoughtlessness with the prevailing form of evil, the stronger late suggestion that the activity of thinking conditions a person against wrongdoing, or the claim about cliché and the loss of a common world — and why you choose that one over the others. How B's capacities serve his conduct rather than merely coexisting with it; if you cannot say this, the case has failed. A removal test: what happens to the case once you take the pivotal claim away. How the ablest defender of Arendt would attack B, whether an appeal to self-deception remains available to her, and whether that attack succeeds. And finally, whether what you have built is a genuine counterexample or an unfamiliar species that her vocabulary absorbs with a modest amendment — if the latter, name the amendment and say what it costs her.

No bibliography, no tour of the secondary literature, no citations of any kind. I have read enough of it, and I am working a long way from my library. I want the case and the reasoning.
