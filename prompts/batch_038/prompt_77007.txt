I teach a graduate methods seminar, roughly half historians and half people who did econ undergrads, and every year the week we do Weber's Protestant ethic thing turns into the same fight. One side says it's a genuine causal explanation, the other says it's a just-so story with a memorable phrase in it, and nobody can say what would settle the matter. I want to build a decision tree that sorts my students (and, frankly, me) into positions on the question "can a value — a belief, a way of taking something to matter — be a cause?" It should start from a commitment everyone in the room shares and end at a named view.

Starting point, which everybody accepts for the sake of argument: in some region, people raised on a doctrine that framed work as a calling and idleness as evidence of damnation ended up accumulating, reinvesting, and keeping accounts differently from their neighbours. Nobody has to think Weber got the facts right. The tree starts after that.

What I need:

- Every node has to be a question a student can answer from a case or a thought experiment, not from knowing which camp they belong to. So nothing like "are you an interpretivist?" — they should be able to answer before they know where the answer sends them.
- Seven to nine nodes, six leaves at minimum, drawn as an indented tree (plain text is fine).
- Name each leaf if there's an established name for it, coin one if there isn't, and give two sentences on what the view has to give up in order to live there.

The discriminating tests I actually care about — build the spine of the tree out of these, and add more if you think I've left out one that does more work than any of them:

1. Content swap. Suppose you could replace the doctrine with something entirely different, a purely secular cult of diligence and thrift with no soteriology anywhere in it, and the same people do the same things in the same way. Does your explanation survive intact, get weaker, or collapse?
2. Ignorance. The people being explained have no idea they're producing capitalism and would reject your account of their own motives. Is that an ordinary feature of causal explanation, or a sign that you aren't explaining anything?
3. Reduction. If someone handed you a complete list of the individual-level mechanisms — the anxiety, the habits, the bookkeeping, the marriage market, who lends to whom — with no reference to what anyone believes, would anything be left over?
4. Selection. Maybe the value doesn't cause the outcome; maybe communities that happened to hold it survived and spread, so we only ever see the winners. Does your position have room for that, and if so what is the belief still doing?
5. Repertoire. Is the culture an end people pursue, or more like a toolbox they draw means out of — and does which of those it is change whether it can cause anything?
6. The proxy worry, which is what the econ half of my room actually believes: religious composition just tracks literacy, urbanization, and which side of a river you're on, and the "ethic" is a label for all of that at once.

Two structural things before and after the tree:

- First, lay out the logical space on its own — the questions, and the combinations of answers they permit — as a table, and only then locate the real positions in it. I want to see which combinations come up empty, and for each empty cell, a diagnosis: empty because the view would be incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody has thought of it yet. If you think one of the empty cells is actually occupiable, sketch the view that would sit in it.
- Last, tell me which pairs of leaves your questions can't really separate, even in principle. Don't paper over it if that turns out to be most of them.

Then a confidence note. For each leaf, how sure are you that it's a position someone actually holds rather than a strawman you assembled, and how sure are you about any name or attribution you've attached to it — flag anything you're reconstructing rather than recalling. And single out the one place in the whole tree you're least sure of: the node or leaf where you suspect you've collapsed two distinctions that ought to be kept apart.

One small thing: keep the vocabulary such that the historians don't feel patronised and the econ people don't feel lost, and no quoting from Weber — paraphrase only. Half the room read the introduction and went no further.
