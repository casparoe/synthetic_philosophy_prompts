My flatmate Nadia signed up for one of those self-binding services back in January — 400 dollars a month goes straight out of her account into a locked pot, and she only gets it back if she uploads proof of twenty hours of work on her novel that week; miss a week and the money goes to a lobbying group she positively loathes. The thing does have an escape hatch: she can cancel on thirty days notice and forfeit a fifth of the pot. Two months ago, right after a bad stretch where she'd blown a couple of deadlines, she used it. Now she's calmed down, thinks it was a mistake, tried to re-enroll, and got told there's a twelve month waiting list. She and our friend Marcus have been circling this for weeks and I've honestly lost track of who's ahead.

(1) Marcus's opening move was that the cancellation is exactly the event the contract was built to stop — the whole point of a Ulysses arrangement is that you don't trust yourself in the moment, so her quitting at her weakest point doesn't undermine the device, it vindicates it. (2) Nadia said that's circular: on his logic any decision to exit is automatically proof that exiting was a bad decision, which means no later version of her could ever legitimately revise a plan she made earlier, no matter what she finds out in between. (3) Marcus said fine, but there's a difference between revising because you learned something and revising because you're temporarily not in the mood, and he reckons hers is squarely the second, since nothing about the novel or her situation changed, only her energy. (4) Nadia pushed back that something did change — she started seeing a therapist who thinks the deadline system is feeding her anxiety — and she says a precommitment is only legitimate against a weakness you can predict in advance, not against a considered change of view about what's good for you. (5) Marcus then went constitutional on her: he said this is precisely why entrenchment exists, that a bill of rights is a calm-moment instruction addressed to your panicking future self, and an exit clause you're allowed to pull *during* the panic is worth nothing, so if anything her contract was badly designed rather than wrongly honored. (6) Nadia said the analogy doesn't travel, because a constitution binds other people who never signed it and gets enforced against them, whereas hers was self-regarding and revocable by design; she added that Odysseus only made it past the island because the sailors were a third party who could physically hold him to his orders, and she was playing both roles at once, wich is why the whole thing fell apart.

Neither of them will give an inch and they've both started dragging me into it at dinner. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? I'd especially like to know whether (4) actually does the work she thinks it does, i.e. whether "new information" versus "just not in the mood right now" is a real line that someone can apply from the inside while they're in the middle of it, whether Marcus's constitutional comparison in (5) is load-bearing or just decoration, and what the two of them are both missing.
