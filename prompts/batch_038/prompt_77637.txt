I am giving a thirty-minute talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on generalization. The room will be mostly empirical machine-learning researchers — several of whom work on domain generalization, covariate shift, and out-of-distribution failure — plus two Bayesian statisticians and one philosopher of science whose specialty is confirmation theory. Almost none of them will have thought about Goodman since graduate school, and the machine-learning contingent thinks about generalization entirely in terms of hypothesis classes, priors, sample complexity, and distributional assumptions. I need to know what they will do to my argument, in their own idiom.

My thesis is that Goodman's new riddle of induction is not a puzzle about linguistic habit but a puzzle about which hypotheses are stable under intervention. I propose to define a predicate as projectible just in case the regularity it licenses is invariant across interventions on how the sample was produced: the hypothesis must survive not merely more of the same data but data gathered under deliberately altered conditions. Green passes, because emerald colour holds up across changes of lighting, mine, and examination date short of the cutoff; grue-ish predicates — emeralds examined before a fixed date are green, all others blue — fail, because the property they ascribe is invariant only under a sampling procedure that happens to have stopped before that date. The riddle bites, on my account, precisely because the two predicates are extensionally indistinguishable over all evidence available before the cutoff, so invariance can only be established by paying the cost of an intervention. That, I argue, is why induction looks underdetermined from inside the data. Entrenchment, on this view, is a useful heuristic proxy for invariance and does no explanatory work of its own.

I am willing to concede that invariance is itself assessed inductively; I intend to reply that it is assessed on a far narrower base — local, manipulable regularities — than the global projection the predicate licenses, so the circle is not vicious but hierarchical. I am not willing to concede that projectibility is merely a matter of practical convenience, and I want to resist the collapse of my criterion into a simplicity or Occam-style assumption about how properties are typed.

Please prepare a Q&A sheet for this talk, structured as follows. Give me exactly five numbered items, nothing else: begin with item 1 and end with item 5, with no introduction, no closing summary, no section headings, and no bibliography — refer to positions, theorems, and arguments descriptively rather than by citation, since I will supply references myself. Order the five by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, and make sure the fifth is the objection I am privately hoping nobody raises, together with a line on why that one in particular could sink the talk. Within each item, pack three things into at most two sentences: the objection as a member of this specific audience would actually phrase it, using their vocabulary rather than the philosopher-of-science vocabulary I have been using; the reply I can deliver in under ninety seconds; and the place where that reply is soft — the assumption it leans on that an opponent could push against. Avoid the stock grue responses I already know (that grue is disjunctive, time-indexed, non-qualitative, or gerrymandered) unless one of them is being wielded in a form this audience would characteristically reach for, such as a no-free-lunch or sample-complexity version of the point. At least one item should come from the philosopher of science in the room and should turn on the difference between a criterion for which hypotheses are reliably projectible and a criterion for which hypotheses are confirmed by their instances; at least one should come from the Bayesian statisticians; and at least one should challenge my account with a concrete learning-theoretic scenario in which a grue-ish hypothesis is in fact learnable from pre-cutoff data alone.
