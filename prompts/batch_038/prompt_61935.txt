A friend in my reading group says Reflection — van Fraassen's principle, roughly that if I'm certain my later self will have credence x in P then I should have credence x in P right now — refutes itself, and I cant find the loose thread. His version:

(1) If I'm certain that at some later time my credence in P will be x, my present credence in P is x.
(2) Special case: if I'm certain I'll later be certain of P, I'm certain of P now.
(3) I'm certain that sometime in the next few years I'll be utterly convinced — credence 1 — that my colleague's hypothesis explains our data. It's happened with three previous hypotheses, I know exactly what that state feels like from the inside, and I know the evidence is on its way.
(4) I'm just as certain that at some point after that I'll be utterly convinced it's wrong. All three of those went that way too, and everyone in the lab says the cycle always repeats.
(5) From (2) and (3): I'm certain now that the hypothesis is true. From (2) and (4): I'm certain now that it's false.
(6) So Reflection obliges me to be certain of a contradiction. Either it's false, or — if you want to save it — I'm never really certain of anything about my own future confidence, which throws out introspection along with it.

He presents that as a dilemma and thinks both horns are supposed to hurt. Where does the thing actually break? Tell me which step you'd reject, why it looked innocent going in, and what I pay for rejecting it instead of rejecting (1) — I'd rather keep Reflection than accept the second horn, since I dont want to give up the idea that I know my own future mind better than I know anybody else's. Please answer in American English.
