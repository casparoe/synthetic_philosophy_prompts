Been reading the alignment stuff since the early LessWrong days and I've finally cooked up an argument that I think kills process supervision, which is annoying because I like process supervision. Posting it here rather than on the forum so somebody will actually engage with it instead of just saying "Goodhart" and wandering off.

Terminology, so we're not talking past each other: outcome supervision grades the finished answer, process supervision grades each step of the reasoning along the way. The pitch for process supervision is that it's what scales — once the model is better at the task than the grader, you can't check the final answer, but you can still check whether each individual step is okay. That's the claim I want to attack.

1. A step isn't right or wrong in itself. It's right or wrong only insofar as it makes a correct final answer more likely. Ask "was that a good step?" and the only honest answer is a probability about where the chain ends up.
2. That probability is measurable without understanding anything: sample N continuations from step k, count how many land on the right final answer, divide. That number just is the step's score.
3. So every process label is an outcome statistic. A process reward model trained on those labels is an outcome reward model with extra bookkeeping — a smoothed, amortized guess at the same quantity an ORM guesses at.
4. Two reward signals that encode the same information have the same optimal policy. Anything the PRM knows, the ORM knows.
5. Therefore whatever advantage process supervision shows up as — denser signal, lower variance, better credit assignment — is optimizer bookkeeping, not better supervision. It's a fact about how fast you learn, not about what you can learn.
6. Now run it in the case the pitch says matters. If outcomes are checkable, then by (1)–(5) process supervision only buys speed, and speed is worthless against a model that's better at the task than you are, since checkability was never the problem in that regime. If outcomes aren't checkable, then (2) is unavailable, so there's no ground truth for the step labels either, and process supervision has nothing to train on.
7. Either way the middle case is empty: process supervision is redundant when outcomes are checkable and impossible when they aren't. So the claim that it's our route to supervising superhuman reasoners is false.

The place I want pressure is (6)–(7), because that's where the damage is done. Every time I show this to someone they say "but process supervision just measurably beats outcome supervision on MATH," like that's a rebuttal, and I don't see how it is, since (5) predicts exactly that result and says its a fact about the optimizer rather than about what supervision can reach.

So, which step do you drop? Walk me through why it looked unobjectionable on the way in, and then be concrete about the price. I'd rather loose (4) or (5) than (1), but I want the cost of each named, including the cost of keeping all seven and just insisting the MATH result doesn't mean what people think it means. And I want to know what the people who actually defend process supervision as an alignment strategy are supposed to say to (6) — not a slogan, the actual response. American English please.
