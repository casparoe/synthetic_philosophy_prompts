I am a first-year doctoral student in a programme that combines archaeological method with the philosophy of the historical sciences, and I am enrolled this term in a graduate seminar on evidence and inference about the unobservable past. In the fourth week each participant circulates a written statement of their own methodological commitments, and the seminar spends the session trying to break it. I would like a rehearsal of that session in advance of mine.

Here is my profile. I hold all of the following.

(1) The material record is static; past behaviour was dynamic. Any claim about what people did therefore requires a bridging argument, and the only respectable bridging arguments are middle-range ones — empirically grounded, independently testable generalisations about how particular activities and particular natural processes leave particular material traces, built up through ethnoarchaeology, experimental replication, and taphonomy.

(2) The warrant for such bridging arguments is actualist. What licenses an analogy between a documented case and an undocumented past one is that the same causal processes operate in both, linking the same conditions to the same outcomes. Formal resemblance between the cases is, on my view, evidentially worthless; only the relational kind of analogy carries any weight.

(3) Where a documented ethnographic or historic population can be shown to be continuous with the population responsible for an archaeological assemblage, one may reason backward from the documented case to the prehistoric one — the direct historical approach. Continuity is what does the work here, not similarity, and continuity is something I take to be an independent empirical finding rather than an assumption.

(4) The record is not a snapshot. I reject the picture of a site as a moment frozen in time: assemblages are palimpsests, accumulated over long spans, subject to reuse, disturbance, time-averaging, and post-depositional modification, and much of what looks like a coherent activity area is a mixture of unrelated episodes.

(5) Equifinality is pervasive. Materially near-identical patterns can be produced by quite different behaviours and by quite different formation histories. It follows, in my view, that no archaeological inference is deductively secure, and that the right model of inference is to the best explanation rather than subsumption under laws.

(6) Material culture is meaningfully constituted, not merely functionally adaptive. An object's form and deposition were partly fixed by what they signified to the people who made, used, and discarded them, and that significance was conventional, locally specific, and not reducible to ecology, subsistence, or technology. Reconstructing past belief, ritual, and symbolism is therefore a legitimate target of archaeological explanation, and not a counsellor of despair about the prehistoric mind.

(7) Ethnic, linguistic, and social groups are not readable off typological clusters of artefacts. Pots are not people, and style is not a map of population; shared material culture can cross-cut descent, language, and identity, and any of those can change while the material record stays put.

(8) Convergence is what makes an inference robust. Where several lines of evidence — built by different methods and drawing on different source materials — point to the same reconstruction, that reconstruction is warranted to a degree no single line could deliver, and this is what compensates for (5).

(9) Interpretation is situated. The questions we ask of the record, the analogies that strike us as natural, and the pasts we are willing to imagine are shaped by our own social and political position. This does not make me a relativist about the past: what happened is determinate, and some interpretations are simply false.

The inference I want this profile to license is a specific one, and it is the subject of my dissertation: that a class of patterned, bone-rich deposits in a Neolithic settlement represents communal feasting carried out with ritual or ancestral significance, rather than ordinary domestic refuse, or the by-product of butchery at scale, or the accumulated remains of many unrelated meals.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on which of these positions is true in the abstract. Specifically:

- Work through the set and tell me which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, ordered from most to least severe. For each, state precisely what the tension consists in — which claim, taken at face value, undermines which other claim, and by what route — and then say what I would have to give up or weaken in order to remove it, and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the profile.
- Tell me just as clearly which pairs only look as though they conflict, and what distinction dissolves the appearance. I would rather be told that two or three of my commitments are perfectly compatible than be handed nine undifferentiated complaints; part of what I need to know is where the real fault lines are.
- Then tell me whether, given the profile exactly as stated, the feasting-and-ritual inference is licensed. If it is licensed for one component of that claim and not another — if, say, the feasting goes through but the significance does not — draw the line explicitly and say which commitments do the work on each side of it.
- Finally, identify the single revision that would buy the most overall coherence, and describe what my profile would look like afterwards, including which of the remaining commitments would have to be restated to fit.

Please be concrete about the mechanism of each tension rather than about its mood. If at some pair the difficulty amounts to nothing more than the familiar observation that processual and interpretive temperaments pull in different directions, say so and move on; I am interested in the places where my own stated grounds for one commitment defeat another, including places where I have not noticed that I am relying on the same resource twice.
