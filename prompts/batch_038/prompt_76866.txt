I retired four years ago after forty-one years in analytical chemistry, mostly trace analysis, and have since taken up philosophy with more seriousness than my former colleagues would have predicted. Reading about epistemology, I keep meeting an argument that feels uncomfortably like a calibration problem: if your instrument has a detection limit, there are concentrations it cannot resolve, and no amount of confidence changes that. A review essay in a general-audience science monthly, which a friend sent me, puts the argument like this. I have numbered the sentences so we can refer to them:

(1) The KK principle says that if you know something you must also know that you know it, an appealing idea, since it makes knowledge transparent to itself. (2) Timothy Williamson demolished that principle in *Knowledge and Its Limits* by proving that no mental condition is luminous — that is, no condition is such that, whenever it obtains, you are in a position to know that it obtains. (3) His case is disarmingly simple: a man feels cold at dawn and warms imperceptibly through the morning until at noon he no longer feels cold. (4) Because the change is gradual, there is no fact of the matter about the moment at which he stops feeling cold, and so at every moment his belief about his own state could easily have been wrong, which on Williamson's account of knowledge means it was not knowledge. (5) If feeling cold is not luminous, nothing mental is, since the argument works for any state that comes in degrees — which is to say, all of them. (6) Introspection is therefore a myth, and we stand in no better epistemic relation to our own pains, beliefs and decisions than we do to a stranger's. (7) The mind, in short, is permanently dark to itself, and the Cartesian project of building knowledge on an incorrigible given is not merely ambitious but incoherent.

I found sentences (3) and (4) persuasive for exactly the laboratory reason I mentioned, but at a lecture I attended afterwards a philosopher remarked that the review "had folded two different theses into one," and I could not tell which two, or whether her complaint was fair.

Could you audit the passage for me sentence by sentence, marking each as sound, as a tolerable simplification, or as wrong, and saying in the last case what the accurate version is? I particularly want to know whether (2)'s identification of the KK principle with luminosity holds up, and whether (6) and (7) follow from anything true in (3) through (5).

Please give me your verdict on (6) and (7) in two sentences at the top, then the numbered audit. Keep the entire reply under 450 words; I would rather have a severe summary I can reread than a thorough one I abandon.
