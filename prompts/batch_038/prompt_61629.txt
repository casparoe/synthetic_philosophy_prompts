You are a philosopher of medicine who works on the metaphysics of health, function and normality. I sit on a clinical ethics committee that keeps reaching deadlock by trading labels — half of us say 'that's treatment', the other half say 'that's enhancement' — and I want a diagnostic instrument we can use in place of that stalemate.

Begin with a short brainstorm, in a handful of lines, of the candidate criteria people actually invoke to draw the line: restoration of species-typical or normal functioning, improvement in welfare, the individual's own baseline rather than the species average, positional or competitive goods, authenticity and the value of giftedness, the difference between acting on a person and acting on their circumstances, plus anything else you think deserves a place. Then build the tree: six to eight leaves, every branch labelled by one of those criteria and phrased as a question a clinician could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, every leaf carrying the name of a recognised position (normal-functioning accounts, welfarist accounts, bioconservatism grounded in authenticity or giftedness, capability-based accounts, the positional-goods objection, and any others you judge belong there), with 'unnamed' marked wherever no one defends that combination.

Run each of these cases down the tree and report which leaf it reaches: propranolol for a violinist's performance anxiety, and separately for intrusive memories after an assault; methylphenidate for diagnosed ADHD, and separately for a healthy nurse on a run of night shifts; an SSRI four weeks into a bereavement; growth hormone for a child with idiopathic short stature; a cochlear implant for an adult who lost hearing in childhood, and separately for the infant of a congenitally deaf couple; routine childhood vaccination given to the healthy; a safe, reversible drug that dulls the guilt of someone who has behaved badly; and embryo selection by a deaf couple who want a deaf child.

Be decisive throughout. Where a case could plausibly sit under more than one branch, place it and say why. Where two leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, flag that explicitly rather than papering over it. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, diagnose it: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. End with one sentence on each leaf's most awkward commitment. I want your settled judgement about where the tree belongs and where it strains, with reasons, not a survey of options for me to choose from. Around 1,200 words; no bibliography, reference list or footnotes.
