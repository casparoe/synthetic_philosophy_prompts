I'm putting together a talk for a mixed room — mostly psychiatrists, plus some philosophy grad students — and I need a case built to order rather then the usual one. The terrain is the fight between selected-effects (etiological) accounts of biological function and the causal-role/analytical account of the Cummins kind, and I want to run it through the question of when a harmful condition that is working exactly as designed still counts as a disorder.

Build me a single clinical case in which the two accounts come apart in one specific direction: on the selected-effects account the mechanism in question is doing its proper job, with no failure of designed function, so a Wakefield-style harmful-dysfunction analysis has to say "not a disorder" even though the patient is badly off; while on a causal-role account, with the explanandum fixed by the question the treating clinicians are actually asking, the same mechanism is failing to make the contribution the rest of the patient's system currently depends on. I want the reverse-direction case too, briefly, if you can do it without inventing a second scenario — otherwise just note why it doesn't fit.

Constraints, and please don't fudge them:

- Realistic. An outpatient clinic or GP practice, not a trauma bay, no sci-fi, no invented genes, no made-up syndromes. The patient should be a plausible person a clinician in the audience would recognize.
- Whatever selection history you lean on has to be something a biologist would sign off on without argument: a reflex, a hormonal or autonomic axis, an inflammatory or immune response, a fever response, a sensory adaptation. No just-so evolutionary psychology, and nothing that depends on a contested story about what some emotion or behavior was "for".
- The divergence must not be a plain ancestral-versus-modern mismatch case. That's the version everyone reaches for and my audience has already seen it. The trouble has to come from inside the patient's current functional organization — from what other parts of the system now need from the mechanism, not from the environment having changed.
- The case itself in two paragraphs maximum. Everything after that is the demonstration.
- Write the answer in British English.

Then show your work. Name the mechanism and the trait. State its selected-effects function and point to exactly which features of the case show that function being discharged competently. State its causal-role function relative to the explanandum you picked, and show where the contribution fails. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the separating work — the one I could not delete without losing the divergence.

After that, three follow-ups:

1. What happens if I do delete it? Does the case collapse into a mismatch story, into one where both accounts agree, or into one where they agree that there's no function talk to be had at all?
2. How would a defender of the selected-effects view attack my case? I'm expecting the reply that the causal-role "function" isn't a function in the relevant sense at all, or that I've put the trait at the wrong level of description, or that the real explanandum is the whole organism's fitness rather than the capacity the clinicians care about. Give the strongest version of that attack and tell me whether there's a repair to the case that survives it.
3. What do the clinicians actually do with this patient, and does the "not a disorder" verdict land them anywhere they'd find absurd — for instance in whether this is medicine's business at all, whether it gets a diagnostic code, or who pays?

Dont give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; I'll sort that out myself. And the thing that would count as cheating here is stipulating the verdicts rather than letting them fall out of the two accounts — so if you can't hit all these specs at once without forcing it, say so plainly and tell me which constraint I should drop first.
