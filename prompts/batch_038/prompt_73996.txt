I am a high-school senior, and for the past two years I have moderated a weekly online reading group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Our members are all sixteen to eighteen; we are not a course, though our faculty adviser reads over anything I produce, and several members are putting together applications to summer philosophy programs and have asked me to write them a practice paper. We have just finished a four-week unit on Turing's 1950 article on computing machinery and intelligence, and we read the whole thing, including the closing discussion of learning machines that almost every summary skips. I would like you to help me draft one essay question on that article, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who specializes in the philosophy of AI and has taught this paper many times, and who is practiced at writing examinations that separate students who have understood an argument from students who have memorized its labels. Before drafting anything, open with a short brainstorming passage: sketch three distinct candidate cruxes the question could turn on, then say in a sentence or two which one you are choosing and why, and why each of the other two could be answered largely by recitation.

The constraint that matters most to me is that the question should hinge on a distinction I do not believe is in Turing and that I have not seen stated anywhere else, so it cannot be looked up or recalled. I have been calling it the difference between mere plasticity and what I will term evaluative grip. A system has mere plasticity if experience can alter its dispositions; it has evaluative grip if it already has standing preferences among possible alterations of itself, so that a reward or a punishment can function as a reason to change rather than as a force that simply changes it. My reading of Turing's educational programme is that it quietly presupposes the second and not merely the first: if you build a child machine and then raise it with rewards and punishments, deliberately leaving a random element in the search and letting the teacher work without knowing anything about the machine's internal organization, then discipline is only possible for a learner that already has something to lose. I want the examination question to make our students decide whether that presupposition is a real commitment of the programme or an artifact of Turing's borrowing from the human case. Please write the stem so that it is self-contained: it should present a compact stipulated scenario — something like two child machines raised by the same architecture-blind teacher, one built so that its reward channel is the only thing it cares about and one that arrives at training with a set of independent projects of its own — and then ask which of the two, if either, has been educated rather than merely shaped, and what the answer implies for Turing's suggestion that teaching a machine differs little from teaching a child. The question must be answerable in forty-five minutes by a strong high-school student who has read the article once, it must not require any other text, and it must not be answerable by describing the structure of the imitation game or by listing the objections in the middle of the paper.

For the deliverables: give the question stem, a model answer of roughly five hundred words, and a marking scheme worth twenty marks that names the specific moves a top answer must make, states what a merely good answer is missing, and attaches a one-line note to each point describing the mistake that point is designed to catch. Please also include, after the marking scheme, a short paragraph predicting the single most likely way a bright but superficial student would lose marks. Two housekeeping requests. First, do not put anything in quotation marks as though it came from Turing's article or from any other source — none of us have the text in front of us while drafting, and I would much rather have careful paraphrase than a quotation I cannot check; describe the passage you have in mind instead of citing it. If you want to bring in a contemporary training method such as reinforcement learning from human feedback, do so by description and make explicit that it is my addition, not Turing's. Second, write the whole response in American English, and think carefully about whether the must-hit moves in your marking scheme are genuinely separable from one another; I do not want five points that turn out to be the same point wearing different clothes.
