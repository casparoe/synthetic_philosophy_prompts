I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to write a four-part series on one question: whether the states inside a trained machine can be about anything in the way my own states are about things, or whether they are about anything only derivatively, in virtue of how designers and users read them. My working spine is the old exchange between Searle and Dennett — the Chinese Room on one side, and on the other Dennett's two-bitser: a soft-drink machine built in the United States to accept and reject quarters which, shipped to Panama, is just as accurately described as detecting quarter-balboas, since the two coins are indistinguishable by weight, thickness, diameter, and composition though not by the stamping on them. The moral Dennett draws is that there is no fact about what the device's states represent apart from the interpretation imposed on it and the environment it happens to sit in, which is supposed to undercut the claim that human meaning is original while machine meaning is merely borrowed.

I have four installments of roughly 3,500 words each and a fixed budget of interviews and demonstrations. What I need is not a survey and not a reading list but a defended agenda: which open questions in this debate deserve the space, in what order, and which plausible-sounding questions I should cut. For each item you keep, give me (a) what depends on it — how far upstream it sits relative to the other questions, and whether it is the crux the rest of the argument quietly turns on; (b) how tractable it is for a journalist rather than a specialist, meaning whether I can actually do something with it that generates information — an interpretable-model demonstration, a designed failure case, an interview that pins someone down — or whether I could only restate positions at greater length; and (c) what a general reader would come away wrongly believing if I botched it.

One candidate I want you to evaluate on its merits rather than dismiss, because I have not seen it handled anywhere and I suspect it is either the most interesting thing in the series or a confusion on my part. Dennett's picture has meaning conferred on a physical state by an interpreter. But in a modern trained system there is a third party in the chain: the optimization process that produced the state. Gradient descent over a corpus and a reward signal selects internal features for their predictive and instrumental usefulness without anyone interpreting them, and the resulting features are then read, often imperfectly, by two separate channels — a decoder that an engineer trains to label the activations, and the model's own verbal reports about what it is doing, which need not agree with the decoder. So: is content conferred by selection a genuinely distinct kind from content conferred by interpretation, giving three categories instead of Dennett's two, or does it collapse into derived intentionality with extra steps? Relatedly, the Panama case has an obvious analogue in a model whose internal states were calibrated on one distribution and deployed in another, where what those states are about seems to drift with no change to the model at all. Rank these against the more familiar questions — whether the syntax/semantics distinction survives, whether the homunculus problem is dissolved or merely relocated, whether functional-role accounts can be made non-question-begging, whether the whole debate is stale relative to what these systems now actually do.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and mark each item accordingly: settled, live but narrowing, wide open, or arguably not a real question. I especially want to be warned wherever a contested position is routinely reported as the outcome — in either direction. I do not want to write that philosophers disposed of Searle decades ago, and I do not want to write that the Chinese Room is treated as decisive; tell me where each of those impressions comes from and what the actual distribution of views looks like. Finally, tell me what would change your ordering: which results or arguments over the next two or three years would move an item up or down the list, and which of my four installments you would write last because it depends on how the others turn out.
