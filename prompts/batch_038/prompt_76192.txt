I volunteer as chair of a five-person panel that decides whether posts come down from a community site, and I taught myself enough SQL to build the database that records our votes. Every case is a set of rows, one per panelist, with a boolean column for each premise and one for the conclusion. Our standing rule is: remove the post if and only if it is false and it caused measurable harm. Last month three of us judged the post false and four judged it harmful, so a majority taken column by column says remove; but only two of us actually voted to remove, so a majority taken on the conclusion column says leave it up. I have since learned this has a name — the discursive dilemma — and that a general impossibility result is supposed to sit behind it.

Restate the dilemma and that impossibility result in the vocabulary I work in daily: schemas and integrity constraints, aggregate functions and GROUP BY, views over base tables, transactions and atomicity, normalization. Use my panel as the running example, and do not strain the analogy to make the philosophy fit. Then give me the residue report — what translates cleanly, what you had to bend, and what has no column, constraint, or query in my language at all.

I want a straight verdict on a suspicion of mine. Either the theorem is repackaging the banal fact that aggregate functions do not commute with row-level predicates, which every query writer already lives with, or there is a piece of it my schema cannot represent. Say which; and if it is the second, point to the piece and tell me whether the gap is in my vocabulary or in their argument.
