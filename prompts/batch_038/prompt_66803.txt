For the past year or so I have been turning over, entirely on my own, a statistical situation that I believe most people's instincts get backwards. It is the case in which one course of action looks better than another when you tot up everybody, and worse than it in every subgroup you might sensibly sort those people into — so that two apparently equally careful people can look at the very same figures and reach opposite decisions, each convinced the other has been careless. What has interested me more than the trick itself is the question underneath it: when ought you to trust the lumped-together number, and when ought you to split? My own tentative answer, which I arrived at by thinking about hospital waiting lists and university admissions rather than by reading anything technical, is that it depends on whether the feature you are sorting people by is something the decision-maker could have acted upon, or is instead something brought about by the decision itself. I may well have this wrong or only half right, and I should like to be told plainly if so.

In a fortnight I am facilitating my regular evening discussion group, which meets in a room at our public library. We are twelve to sixteen people: two retired teachers, a district nurse, a couple of students, several warehouse and care shift-workers, and me. Nobody present, myself included, has studied mathematics, statistics or philosophy beyond school level. I have no degree and no teaching qualification; I learn these things privately from whatever I can get hold of and then bring them to the group. The room has tables, chairs and a flip-chart. There is no projector, no laptops for participants and no reliable signal for phones. Some people arrive five or ten minutes late, some have poor eyesight, and I cannot ask anyone to do arithmetic in their head beyond counting small piles of objects or comparing two fractions that share a denominator. I have about twenty pounds to spend on materials, and I can offer points, tokens, privileges or small forfeits as stakes, but not money.

I should like you to design a ninety-minute activity for this group that makes them run into the reversal themselves, and then into the harder question about when each way of looking is the right one. Please observe the following.

The group must be confidently wrong before anything is explained. Nobody may hear the name of the phenomenon, or be told that a puzzle is coming, until the debrief; and please do not reuse any well-known real dataset or its actual figures, since several of our members have seen the standard examples in newspapers and would recognise them. Invent the scenario and the numbers.

The figures should come into existence through the participants' own choices wherever this can be managed — cards dealt, tokens allocated, patients triaged by the players themselves — rather than arriving on a sheet I read out, so that nobody can suspect the setup was rigged. If that cannot be achieved, say so honestly and give me the best fallback.

Please build two played decision rounds whose correct answers point in opposite directions. In the first, the lumped number should mislead and the split should be right. In the second, the split should mislead and the lumped number should be right, because the division being made is itself produced by the choice under consideration rather than existing beforehand. The group must not be told which round is which, and having learnt the lesson of the first round they should walk straight into the second. I would also like the debrief to be honest about whether the second round really shows that splitting is wrong, or only that it answers a different question from the one the decision needs answered — I suspect the latter, and I would rather the activity did not overclaim.

Consider a third scenario in which the feature being sorted by is a social category rather than a clinical or practical one, so that which number you are entitled to use stops being a technical matter. Tell me whether this should be played or merely discussed, and justify that choice against the ninety minutes and the group's stamina.

The scoring must be such that the decision which maximises a player's points is the correct one, and such that both the lazy lumping move and the lazy splitting move cost something.

The debrief must map particular moments in the room onto particular points, land on a plain-language criterion for when to trust the aggregate and when to split, tell me whether my own tentative gloss above survives contact with the activity, and say what the facilitator has to supply because the game cannot deliver it — including the awkward fact that in real life one is not told the causal structure, and the risk that the evening's moral is heard as "statistics is useless, trust your eyes". If there is a well-known sibling puzzle, concerning two groups compared before and after an intervention, where the same question about adjusting or not adjusting arises, tell me whether it belongs in the debrief; if you are not certain of its name, describe it instead of naming it.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to recover, and make sure I am not required to compute anything live in front of the room.

Please give me your answer as a single JSON object using exactly the following schema, with British spelling throughout:
