Our faculty search committee is deadlocked between two colleagues who have argued for a year about which rule to use for shortlisting candidates, and I have been asked to produce the document the two of them should have written. One is an impossibilist: she reads the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem as showing that any deterministic, surjective rule over three or more alternatives with an unrestricted domain of strict preferences is either dictatorial or manipulable, and she holds that every escape route trades strategy-proofness for a defect at least as bad. The other thinks the theorem is irrelevant to their actual decision: he points out that committee preferences here look close to single-peaked along an ideological axis, that randomizing via random dictatorship is strategy-proof, and that locating a successful manipulation is computationally hard in any case.

Draft the joint statement they could both sign. First, a numbered list of claims both accept: precisely what the theorem assumes and what it rules out, and what each of the three escape routes costs — including whether a claim of single-peakedness can be checked from the ballots alone, and whether an ex ante strategy-proof lottery is still non-manipulable once the draw is known. Then a table of the residual disagreements, each phrased so that the colleague who holds it would accept the wording of their own side. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move one of them, with, for each, a concrete test, computation, or example both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I expect one crux to be whether single-peakedness can be established without the establishing of it being itself manipulable, and another to be whether computational difficulty of finding a manipulation counts as immunity; add any others you find. Close by identifying which of their disagreements turn out to be merely verbal, and give the wording both could adopt once that is clear.
