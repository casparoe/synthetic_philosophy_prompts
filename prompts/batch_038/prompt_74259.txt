I'm a defense attorney in Ohio, fifteen years in, mostly state court felonies, and last spring I burned a Saturday on a CLE seminar about neurolaw that I fully expected to be a waste of time. It wasn't, because of one hour on Libet. I took away this much: the slow buildup in the motor cortex — the readiness potential — starts something like half a second before the movement, while the subject's reported awareness of the urge to move shows up only around 200 milliseconds out, so the brain is visibly getting ready before the person experiences deciding; Libet then retreated to a veto, a narrow window in which consciousness can supposedly stop the movement it didn't start; later scanning work pushed the lead time out to several seconds; and then there's the reanalysis, which I think came out of a modeling group, arguing the ramp isn't a decision at all but just noisy neural activity drifting until it crosses a threshold, which if true makes the whole thing much less spooky. I've read enough about this to be dangerous and nowhere near enough to be usefull. I'm fluent — I live — in a completely different framework, and I want you to do the translation for me.

Here's what I'm after. Take the Libet result and the free-will argument that grew up around it, and restate the whole thing in the vocabulary I actually work in: voluntary act, automatism, the statutory list of bodily movements that don't count as acts (reflex, convulsion, movement during sleep or unconsciousness, things done under hypnosis, and the catch-all for movements that aren't a product of the actor's effort or determination, conscious or habitual), mens rea and its degrees, premeditation and deliberation, the withdrawal and abandonment doctrines, and the procedural side — who carries the burden, what gets presumed, how a jury is instructed. Do it as faithfully as you can, and please don't cheat by relabeling: I can smell it when someone quietly imports an assumption from the target side and calls it a translation, e.g. assuming that the law's voluntariness question and the neuroscientists' timing question are the same question. Then give me the residue report, sorted into three piles. Which claims carry over cleanly — does the veto have a real counterpart in abandonment or withdrawal, or is that a stretch, given that those doctrines ask about reasons and the veto as Libet describes it isn't reason-responsive? Which ones only fit with distortion? And which have no expression at all — specifically, what is the legal counterpart, if any, of "preparation began before awareness of deciding"? Is that automatism? Is it nothing, because the law's test is about absence of conscious control and external compulsion rather than about when anything began, so the law simply never asks the question neuroscience is answering? And be blunt about which side the gap indicts: is legal voluntariness missing a distinction it ought to have, or does the residue show that the philosophers' fight over free will is less substantive than it looks once you notice that what actually settles responsibility is a bundle of rules about proof and attribution rather than a claim about causation?

The reason I'm asking isn't academic, and this is the part I most want you to run through the same machine. I have a client with an implanted closed-loop stimulator — drug-resistant epilepsy, and the device sits there reading intracranial signal, and when it sees the pattern it doesn't like it pulses to abort it before the seizure goes clinical. He's charged with aggravated assault over an incident at a bar. He tells me two things I don't know what to do with. First, that sometimes he feels the device act ahead of him, that there's a moment where something gets switched off that he was in the middle of. Second, that after a programming adjustment last winter his wife described him as flatter for a couple of months, and he can't tell whether the urges he didn't act on during that stretch were his and suppressed, or were never generated because the machine got there first. So: redo the translation for an agent that is a person plus a controller in a feedback loop that closes inside his own skull, where the controller's settings are chosen by a third party at a clinic who is not in the courtroom. Where does "product of the effort or determination of the actor" fall when the output is jointly produced? Does the binary voluntary/involuntary distinction have any room for a hybrid like this, or does it force everyone into a false choice between automatism and full responsibility? Does the device break the framework, or does it expose that the framework never drew the boundary around a brain to begin with — that "the actor" was always a placeholder for a judgment about whose conduct to attribute, meaning the Libet residue and the stimulator residue turn out to be the same residue? And finally, tell me whose conduct to attribute here in a way I could actually stand up and argue — or tell me plainly that nothing in any of this is usable and that the right answer is that the legal question was never a causal one.
