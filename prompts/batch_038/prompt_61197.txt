Take the Olds and Milner self-stimulation work — the 1954 rat experiments, plus the later human case in the early seventies, the psychiatric patient who was given a pushbutton wired to a septal electrode and pressed it obsessively, well past what anyone expected, then complained when the session ended. Facts I'm working from, correct me if I'm off: the original discovery was accidental, since Olds was aiming at a different structure and noticed the rat kept returning to the spot where it had been zapped; the same people also found sites animals would work to switch off; and decades later the hedonic-impact crowd argued the electrode was driving wanting rather than pleasure, so that pressing rate is a bad read on how much an animal actually likes the stimulation.

Now analyze that whole arc as a test case for the move that does a lot of work in the AI-welfare debate: that a scalar reward signal just is valence, so an agent taking large negative reward or sustained prediction error is a candidate for suffering.

First, sort out what's actually being claimed, because at least three things get collapsed together: (a) the reward signal literally is the felt valence, so bad rewards are bad feels; (b) it's a reliable marker of a valenced state realized elsewhere in the system — the hedonic hotspots in the rat, possibly nowhere in an agent; (c) valence is nothing over and above the reinforce-and-avoid role, so anything with the role has it. For each, say what the 1954 and 1972 results predicted, what they actually showed, and how much survives the wanting/liking revision.

Then run it the other direction: does the episode license the inference from "this system optimizes a scalar" to "this system can suffer", or quietly undermine it — is the rat a wirehead, a hedonist, or neither? Where does the analogy to trained RL agents break (no homeostatic damping, weight changes vs transient states, the agent can't pick its own reward, no competing hunger to trade off against)? And finish by saying where the theory is simply silent here, and whether some other framing — maybe one that puts the moral weight on thwarted wanting instead of negative reward — handles the hundres-of-presses patient better.
