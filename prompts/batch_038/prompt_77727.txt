Been keeping a spreadsheet of my own self-control failures for about two years now (grad student, mostly the dissertation and the gym), and the pattern that fell out isn't the one I expected. I'm not after a diagnosis of me — I want to know whether what I've described already has a name.

Every Sunday I set the week: four writing blocks, four gym sessions. On Wednesday I skip one. But I dont experience that as caving, because by Wednesday the plan has already been revised — the deadline moved, my advidsor was slow with comments, I slept badly Monday and Tuesday, and anyway four blocks was badly calibrated and three is what a realistic week actually looks like. Once the plan says three, skipping the fourth isn't weakness, its compliance. Then Sunday rolls around and I set it back to four, because four is what I'd endorse on any given Sunday. Eleven downward revisions in two years, and every single one had a decent reason attached.

Same shape elsewhere: I've meant to bump up my retirement contribution since I started the job, but each quarter there's a real reason to wait — the rent went up, I'm about to travel, let me first see whether the raise comes through — and each quarter I separately decide that this quarter was never the right one.

So the structure is: the standard gets moved just before the action, and the move is locally defensible, so there's never a moment where I judge X better and do Y. Synchronically I've never been akratic once. Diachronically I'm a wreck. And it's not plain preference change either, since if you ask me on a random Wednesday whether four is the right target, I say yes.

I know the standard line that weakness of will has to go against your own better judgment at the time of action, and I know the discounting models with naive versus sophisticated procrastinators — but naive agents there just fail to predict their future slip-ups, they don't re-legislate the target every week. Closest I've got from folk vocabulary is "rationalization" and "moving the goalposts," but both of those sound like moves you make to win an argument, not something you do to yourself that leaves your own bookkeeping genuinely clean.

Does this have an established name or a literature? If not, coin one. But the label isn't the point — the carving is. Give me the three or four nearest existing concepts with one line each on exactly why mine isn't that, then your term with a real definition, then the edges: what counts as a case of it, and what's a nearby case that doesn't. Hard cap of 400 words for the whole thing; a tight list is fine, I don't need prose.
