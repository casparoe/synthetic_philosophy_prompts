I have no formal training in philosophy. I run a small business and have worked through these questions on my own over several years, mostly by reading. I would like a rigorous check on a set of views about possibility that I have written down, because I suspect at least one of them is unstable and I cannot tell which.

Here is the set, in my own words:

1. Only this world is real in the full sense. When people talk about other possible worlds, I take them to be talking about complete ways the whole of things could have been — blueprints, so to speak — and I do not believe any of those blueprints is somewhere, or that anything in them is made of anything. I came to this material through David Lewis; I rejected the many-concrete-worlds picture at once, but I kept a good deal of the rest of what he said.

2. Nothing is in two of these ways-things-could-have-been at once. When I say that I might have become a fisherman, I do not mean that some one person, namely me, is a fisherman elsewhere; there is no such stretching across.

3. What makes the claim true, then, is resemblance. The blueprint counts as one in which I become a fisherman because the person in it who resembles me most closely — in where he came from, in temperament, in the shape his life takes — becomes a fisherman.

4. In any given blueprint there is always exactly one person who resembles me most closely. If two candidates strike us as exactly equidistant, my view is that one of them is in fact a shade nearer and we are simply unable to detect it. I hold to this because I think that without a single determinate match, the claim stops being a claim about me.

5. I do not believe in differences that cannot be pointed to. If two things agree in every respect anyone could state, then there is no further fact about which of them is which; nothing is left over as a bare "but this one, really."

6. On persistence through time in this world: what I am is a series of stages held together by memory and character. In the kind of case where my brain is divided and two people wake up carrying my memories and my dispositions, I think neither of them is me. I want to be careful about how I put this: I do not think that is because we lack information. I think there is no fact of the matter.

7. Finally, the reason any of this matters to me is that the counterfactuals are mine. Regret about what I might have done, relief about what I did not, planning against what I might yet become — these attitudes seem to me to presuppose that the possibilities in question are possibilities for me.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Please go through the set and tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally serious. For each, say what would have to be given up or weakened in order to remove it, and what that revision costs — including what it costs the remaining items, since I expect the damage will not stay local. I have deliberately not said which of them I would be most willing to abandon, because I do not know, and I would rather your analysis not be steered by a hint from me.

Two further requests. First, tell me how confident you are in the verdicts you reach on each conflict, and single out the point in your own analysis where you are least sure of yourself. Second, put your bottom line on the very last line in a fixed form so that I can file it: that line should begin with "Verdict:" followed by the number of the single item that most needs to be revised, or by the word "none" if you judge the set to hang together as it stands.
