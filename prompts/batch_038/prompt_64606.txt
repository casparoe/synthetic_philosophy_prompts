**Question 3 (20 points).** In "The Methodology of Positive Economics" (1953), Milton Friedman argues that a positive theory is to be judged by how well it predicts the class of phenomena it is intended to explain, and not by whether its assumptions are descriptively realistic; he adds that the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be, while insisting that unrealisticness by itself does not make a theory significant. This position is generally known as the F-twist, and Alan Musgrave wrote a paper offering to untwist it. For this question you may take as given Musgrave's claim that the F-twist arises from a failure to distinguish three kinds of assumption, sorted by the job each one does: *negligibility assumptions*, which say that some factor has a negligible effect on the phenomenon under investigation; *domain assumptions*, which specify where the theory is meant to apply; and *heuristic assumptions*, which simplify the derivation of the theory without making a claim about the world. Musgrave argues that Friedman's dictum is false of all three kinds, and conjectures that under pressure from criticism an assumption may migrate from negligibility status to domain status to merely heuristic status, and that such migrations go unnoticed if the three kinds are not kept apart.

(a) Explain what the twist consists in, and what Musgrave's untwisting amounts to. (10 points)
(b) Once untwisted, is the quarrel between Friedman and his critics substantive or merely verbal? Defend your answer. (10 points)

---

Friedman's essay is usually summarized as holding that a theory is to be judged only by the accuracy of its predictions for the phenomena it is aimed at, not by whether its assumptions describe reality faithfully. His illustrations all have the same shape. Leaves are denser on the south side of a tree as if each leaf had worked out where the most sunlight falls. Expert billiard players make their shots as if they had solved the relevant trigonometry. Firms in competitive markets push each line of action to the point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost as if the managers were actually running that calculus, even though interviewing them turns up nothing of the sort. In every case the "as if" clause is doing the work: the assumption is not offered as a report of a mental process but as a device for getting to predictions, and the predictions are the only thing that can confirm or refute the theory. Friedman then goes a step further, and this is what got him in trouble. He says the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions, because significance comes from stripping away a mass of irrelevant detail. Critics have read that as a celebration of falsehood and as a license to insulate economic theory from every empirical complaint about its starting points.

Musgrave's untwisting is that the whole debate has been run with an undifferentiated idea of "assumption." He sorts assumptions by the job they do. Negligibility assumptions are substantive and testable, since they say a factor doesn't matter much. Domain assumptions say where the theory holds. Heuristic assumptions are pure devices and assert nothing about the world. With the three in hand you can see that they differ in how unrealistic they are: a heuristic assumption is the most unrealistic of the three because it commits to nothing, a domain assumption is less so, and a negligibility assumption is the least unrealistic, since it has to be roughly true for the theory to work at all. It follows that complaints about unrealistic assumptions can only ever bite on the third kind, and that the F-twist was a reasonable enough thing to say about the other two. That is why Musgrave's paper reads to me as conceding most of the ground to Friedman and relocating what is left of the quarrel into terminology. On that basis I'd answer (b) by saying the dispute is verbal: the two sides are using "unrealistic" in different senses, and once the senses are prised apart there is no disagreement left worth having.

There is one obvious worry about the typology, which is that classifying an assumption as a domain assumption seems to require already knowing where the theory works, and that is just what the debate is about. But Musgrave can reply that the domain is normally fixed by the theorist's own stated ambitions, so the classification isn't question-begging after all.

Finally, Musgrave's conjecture about migration helps here. Under criticism an assumption drifts from negligibility status into domain status and then into being a mere heuristic device, which describes a theory becoming progressively more realistic as it matures, since each migration trades a risky claim about the world for a safer one. So the critics' objection loses its grip over time rather than having to be answered.
