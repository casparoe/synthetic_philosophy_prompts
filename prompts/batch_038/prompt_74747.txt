I'm a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on agency and responsibility; we've spent the last three weeks on the free will problem, and the instructor has asked each of us to prepare and run one fifty-minute segment of the final review session. Mine will have sixteen students — mostly juniors and seniors who have done all the reading and can recite the positions, but who, in section, tend to state conclusions rather than feel the pressure of the arguments. I'd like to design an activity that makes them feel the force of a specific worry: whether an agent must have been able to do otherwise in order to be an appropriate target of blame, and what survives of blame when that ability is quietly removed. Crucially, I do not want to announce the topic. Nobody should hear the words free will, determinism, or compatibilism until the debrief, and I would rather the group stumble into the problem through what they do than be told what problem they are doing.

My rough idea, which you should feel free to restructure or replace: each participant first makes one small but genuine choice with a real payoff attached — something they actually care about, not a token, so that the choice is theirs in a way they cannot shrug off. Before they learn anything further about the setup, they record two things: whether they could have chosen otherwise, and whether they deserve credit or blame for what they chose. Only afterwards does the facilitator reveal that the session had been rigged in advance, and the class is split so that different groups learn different things about the very same choices — one group learns that a concealed arrangement would have overridden their decision had it gone the other way, so that in fact nothing else was open to them; another learns that the arrangement existed but never engaged, so that their decision was reached entirely on its own; a third learns that no arrangement existed at all. The point I want them to hit is that a single action can be described in ways that pull their intuitions about blame apart, and that some of them will keep blaming in the first case even after they agree nothing else was possible, while others will insist the arrangement changes everything.

Please write this up as a complete, runnable facilitator guide. I need: the exact choice to be made and how the payoff works without real money being exchanged; roles, if you want to use them (I am happy to have one or two students play the party who arranged things in advance); a list of materials, assuming a seminar room with a whiteboard and index cards and nothing else; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in fifty minutes including the debrief; the precise wording of the reveal scripts, since a careless phrasing here could contaminate the intuitions I'm trying to elicit; and the set of questions to ask at each stage, including how to structure the recording so that participants are committed to an answer before the reveal. For the debrief, I want a mapping from specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinctions they were enacting — the difference between an arrangement that would have intervened and one that actually shaped the decision, and the difference between being unable to do otherwise and being unable to do otherwise for the relevant reasons — and I want you to identify which of these the activity genuinely reproduces and which it merely gestures at. Tell me, too, what I should predict will go wrong the first time I run it: participants who try to outguess the setup and deliberately choose against the arrangement to prove a point, a reveal that lands flat, groups that converge on the same verdict so that no disagreement surfaces, anyone who feels manipulated or resentful about being observed without consent. Finally, be candid about the one feature of the underlying problem that a staged activity of this kind cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply by hand in the debrief.

One further constraint: I would like the segment to end with something I can hand in as evidence that it worked. Please propose two or three brief written questions to pose in the last five minutes whose answers would reveal whether participants' intuitions actually shifted, along with a short note on what pattern of responses would indicate that the activity failed to generate the tension I was after.
