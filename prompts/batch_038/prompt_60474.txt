I'm co-running a reading group this term with an unusually mixed room: two philosophy graduate students, several people who work in monitoring and evaluation at NGOs, and a pair of friends who came to effective altruism by way of software engineering. Our unit is on the epistemic problems for consequentialism, and the centerpiece is the cluelessness objection — the claim that we are so ignorant of the long-run and indirect effects of our actions that consequentialism cannot in practice tell us what to do, since the considerations that would settle the matter (the descendants of the person you saved, the conflict your donation helped avert or precipitate, the compounding ripples of a job offer accepted or declined) are precisely the ones we have no access to. I want the group to work through the standard moves on both sides: that subjective or expected value may remain action-guiding even under radical ignorance; that the objection may generalize to any moral view that treats consequences as mattering at all, rather than being a difficulty peculiar to the consequentialist; that anyone claiming to steer the deep future inherits the same blindness in an amplified form. The trouble is that two hours of straight exposition tends to leave the NGO contingent agreeing with one another that philosophy is just anxiety with footnotes, and I would rather close the session with something that makes them laugh first and argue second.

So please write me a Swiftian modest proposal of roughly 800 to 1,000 words, cast as a glossy white paper from an invented philanthropic consultancy — something appropriately managerial in name — announcing a new giving vehicle engineered to be immune to consequentialist cluelessness. The governing conceit should be that, because the long-run effects of no action can be known, the most defensible portfolio is one whose effects are designed to cancel: every intervention matched by an equal and opposite intervention, underwritten by a monitoring-and-evaluation framework that measures nothing in particular and a theory of change that terminates in an explicit shrug. Let that logic run remorselessly forward, past the point where the reader stops nodding and starts recoiling — the escalation should follow from the premises, not from added absurdity. The furniture I'd like to see, in whatever order you find natural: an executive summary; a cost-effectiveness table whose confidence interval runs from civilizational ruin to astronomical flourishing and which nonetheless yields a recommendation; a boxed set of frequently asked questions in which the answers are technically responsive and practically useless; a paragraph where the author slips into unapologetically act-consequentialist arithmetic and catches himself mid-sentence; and a dissenting appendix from a second reviewer who observes that the offset vehicle's own long-run effects are exactly as unknowable as anyone else's, and therefore cannot say whether the fund should be doubled, dissolved, or never mentioned again.

Two constraints matter more than the others. First, the humor has to come from getting the philosophy right: a reader who knows the debate should be able to tell that you understand what the objection claims, what its best replies are, and where those replies get uncomfortable, and should find the satire aimed at a style of discourse rather than at any real person or organization — please do not name living philosophers, actual charities, or existing evaluators, and do not include a bibliography, notes, or citations of any kind. Second, keep it legible: a member of my group who has never encountered the cluelessness objection should finish the piece with a coherent, accurate sense of what the objection is, and the deadpan should never break character or wink. The final lines should leave genuinely open whether this proposal is more or less defensible than ordinary, un-hedged giving — if the piece reads as though it has settled that question, I'll consider it a failure.
