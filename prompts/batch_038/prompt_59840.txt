I commission and write a monthly feature for the science-and-ideas desk of a general-interest magazine, and this month I would rather not file the two-hundredth column about large language models and inner experience. My subject is Frank Jackson's Mary — the colour scientist who knows every physical fact about vision and appears to learn something new when she finally leaves the black-and-white room — and my vehicle is Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun.

Here is the reading I want you to test, and to build out if it holds. Klara is almost always discussed as a candidate for consciousness, but I want her treated as a candidate for *knowledge*. Consider her epistemic situation: she learns nearly everything she knows about the world by watching it through the shop window, and later through Josie's bedroom window; she has no physics, no biology, no theory of disease or of gene-editing; and the one general theory she does hold — that the Sun is a person who distributes a special nourishment, who revived the beggar and his dog, who can be petitioned at the barn on the horizon, and who must be defended from the Cootings Machine she sabotages at cost to her own P-E-G Nine solution — is very probably false. Klara is therefore not Mary but Mary's inverse: a subject with radically incomplete and largely mistaken physical information who nonetheless seems to accumulate dense first-personal acquaintance with sunlight, weather, and human faces. My claim is that the novel dramatizes a question Jackson never poses: can phenomenal knowledge be acquired by way of a false physical theory, and if it can, does that reinforce the thought that phenomenal knowledge is knowledge of facts, or push us toward the ability and acquaintance readings and the view that Mary gains only an old fact under a new mode of presentation? I would also like your judgement on whether the reading survives Jackson's own later abandonment of the argument.

A second thread, and please treat this as a philosophical datum rather than a stylistic tic: when Klara's processing is strained, her visual field divides into panels — Josie's mother's face breaking into a grid of boxes is the instance I recall most clearly. Is that a claim about the structure of her experience, that is about phenomenology that is compositional and disunified, or a claim about her access and her reporting? Does the novel ever equip us to tell the difference, and if it never does, is that a flaw in the novel or the point of it?

Some requirements. Anchor the argument in identifiable scenes: the shop window, the beggar and the dog, the night walk to the barn, the sickroom when the clouds part, the exchange late in the book in which Josie's father presses Klara on whether there is anything in a person that could not be copied, and the closing scene in the scrapyard. Then say plainly where the novel resists — where Klara's unreliability makes the reading parasitic on a narrator we have no reason to trust, where her solar theology may simply be Ishiguro's way of depicting a machine's confabulation, and where the book's deliberate refusal to settle whether there is anything it is like to be Klara helps or hurts my case. I want the piece to end by stating what the novel contributes to the debate that argument alone could not deliver, and I want you to resist both failure modes: turning the book into an illustration, and bending the philosophy until it fits.

Formalities: roughly 1,200 words; no bibliography, no references, no list of further reading — I will source everything myself, so positions and arguments may be named but nothing needs to be cited. No verbatim quotation from the novel, since I check quoted lines against my copy and an accurate paraphrase is more useful to me. Where you are unsure of a detail of the text, mark it as uncertain rather than filling it in.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Name the argument or the consideration that would move you to the opposite verdict on whether this novel supports or subverts the knowledge argument, and say where you think my framing is most likely to be wrong — including whether the boxes sequence is carrying more philosophical weight than it can bear.
