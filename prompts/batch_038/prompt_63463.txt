I've somehow been talked into giving the remarks at the dedication of our new black-box theater in about three weeks. The donor made his money in streaming, which I'll let you sit with for a second; he expects to be thanked inside the first two minutes. The room will be roughly a third trustees and alumni, a third theater faculty and students, a third townspeople who have never read a word of mine and won't.

Here's my problem. The book I'm finishing is basically a long argument with Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the theater in Geneva, and I find his worry persuasive more often than I'd like to admit — the thought that a permanent playhouse in a small self-governing town quietly converts people who used to do things into people who watch things get done, and that a crowd in the dark is not the same animal as a crowd in the square. I don't want to hide that in the speech, and I don't want to fake-resolve it either. But I also can't give a dedication that lands as a rebuke to the people who paid for the building. And I'm not a Rousseauean puritan about it: his own alternative is the open-air festival with no stage, where the spectators are the spectacle and see themselves in each other — and a black box gets surprisingly close to that precisely because nothing in it is bolted down. That fact should do actual work in the ending, not get waved at.

So write me the speech, six to eight minutes. It thanks the donor honestly, it gets the spectatorship worry across in a form a townsperson follows on first hearing — Molière's Misanthrope is probably my in, the point that the upright man ends up being the funny one while the easygoing compromise wins our sympathy — and then it turns on the theater/festival distinction structurally rather than mentioning it.

Two requests. If you think the premise is off — if that's not really the argument Rousseau is making, or if you think a dedication speech that admits doubt about its own occasion can't possibly work and I'd be better off eating the cost and writing something warm — tell me that plainly up front instead of politely handing me something that quietly ignores what I asked for. And after the speech, give me a short note on where it stretches him: he's writing about Geneva, not about theater in general, and I don't want to turn him into a man who'd have preferred the building stay a parking lot.
