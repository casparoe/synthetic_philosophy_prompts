I have no training in philosophy or in the history of mathematics. For several years now I have been reading Hobbes on my own — Leviathan twice through — and I have recently been working through a popular book about his long feud with John Wallis and the Oxford mathematicians, whose central chapter is given over to his philosophy of geometry. I want to know whether that chapter can be trusted. I have no way of distinguishing careful exposition from confident error, and I have been filling pages of notes on the assumption that it is careful.

Here is the passage, as I wrote it out:

"Hobbes held that the only real knowledge is causal knowledge: to know a thing is to know how it comes to be. This is why he made geometry the model of the whole of science. On his account we are certain of geometry because its starting points are definitions that we ourselves impose — a definition cannot be false, only more or less useful — and once the meanings of circle, line, and angle have been settled, the rest of the science follows by reckoning alone, by adding and subtracting names, with nothing left to check against the world. Hobbes accordingly thought that the truths of geometry are known quite independently of any drawing, and he treated diagrams as a convenience for the slow: the geometer's reasoning would lose nothing if no one had ever set compass to paper. Natural philosophy is a poorer enterprise on this view, because we do not make bodies and so can never command the demonstration a geometer has; at best we reason backwards from what we see to what might have caused it. The same conviction explains the wreck of Hobbes's final decades. He could not tolerate the new infinitesimal methods, in which a curved figure is treated as made up of infinitely thin straight slices; and because he had already decided that certainty is a product of definitions rather than of constructions, he spent the remainder of his life publishing false squarings of the circle and attacking men who were simply better mathematicians than he. His hostility to algebra was of a piece with this: he mistrusted symbols because he could not handle them. What survives of all this is a startlingly modern idea — mathematics as a game played with signs of our own invention, owing its truths entirely to our stipulations and nothing at all to reality — which is why Hobbes deserves to be counted among the ancestors of the formalist picture of mathematics that most working mathematicians still hold by instinct."

I would like an accuracy audit I can act on. Please go through these claims in order and tell me which a scholar of early modern philosophy and mathematics would accept as they stand, which are defensible simplifications that a general reader can safely keep, and which are simply mistaken. Be explicit wherever the passage seems to be sliding rather than asserting something outright false. I am especially unsure whether the author is making one claim about definitions and certainty or two distinct ones, and whether Hobbes's nominalism about definitions really does the work the passage assigns to it. I also want to know whether the passage's explanation of the circle-squaring fiasco runs in the right direction, or whether it has the relationship between Hobbes's theory of definitions and his insistence on constructing figures exactly backwards.

The sentence about diagrams is the one that made me uneasy, because the same chapter goes on to describe Hobbes arguing at length about how a line comes into being through the motion of a point. I cannot reconcile those two things, and I would rather be told which of them to distrust. The closing claim, by contrast, appealed to me: I have long been inclined to think mathematics is something we make rather than something we find, and the suggestion that Hobbes anticipated this is part of why I enjoyed the book. So please tell me frankly whether that conclusion follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it leans on the parts that do not hold up — and whether the final sentence's remark about working mathematicians is even a claim about Hobbes anymore.

Finally, if correcting the summary changes how one ought to read the rest of his project — the materialism, the treatment of reasoning as computation — please say so, since I would sooner rebuild my notes than go on polishing a misreading. I do not want references or suggestions for further reading; I want your judgment of the passage itself.
