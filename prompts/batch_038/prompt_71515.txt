I edit a small quarterly that publishes fiction adjacent to philosophy of mind, and for our next issue — the theme is witnesses — one slot is still unfilled, and it is the one I care most about. Roughly half the submissions we get on machine minds turn out to be retreads of the Chinese Room with the room replaced by a chatbot, a warehouse, or a generation ship, which is not what I want. What I want is a story about derived intentionality, and specifically about the argument that there is no such thing as original intentionality for it to be derived from. Two thousand to two and a half thousand words, please. I came at this years ago through the old sequences and a footnote I have never been able to shake; a friend who does interpretability work now tells me the question is settled in practice and only philosophers still fight about it, which is precisely why I want the fiction, since the practice is where the argument lives. My sense is that a paper cannot do much with it, because the real question is what an interpretive practice looks like from the inside — what it feels like to be the person whose job it is to say what a machine's states were about, and who is paid for being right.

Here is a setting; change whatever you like, but keep the mechanism. In a near-future jurisdiction, logs from automated monitoring systems are admissible in civil proceedings provided a licensed practitioner — call her a content auditor — signs a certificate stating not what voltages a system was in but what those states represented. Your protagonist is such an auditor, methodical, quietly proud of the work, and she has been retained in a wrongful-death case at a cold-storage or grain-handling plant, where a two-state discriminator either did or did not change state at 03:14 on the night in question and someone died. The discriminator is trivially simple — the kind of device Dennett had in mind with his two-bitser, with few enough components that a competent engineer can survey the entire causal story from the inside — and it was built and calibrated to separate one pair of worldly conditions in the environment it was installed for. But the plant was retrofitted, the sensor relocated, the ambient chemistry drifted, and the opposing expert has noticed that the same device, with nothing about it altered, now tracks a different pair of conditions entirely, and can equally well be re-read as having been tracking that other pair all along. I want that re-mapping made concrete and visceral: a specific physical setup a reader can hold in their head, and a scene in which someone who was certain what a lamp or a log line meant is shown — without anything inside the machine changing — that it meant something else.

The hard part is fairness. Give the opposing view a character with something to lose: an expert witness, a former supervisor, a judge who once read philosophy, who maintains that the whole licensed profession is a category error with letterhead, that no amount of covariance plus an interested audience makes a state about anything, and that only minds have underived content while everything else is a courtesy we extend. Then make the auditor's answer cost her something instead of arriving as a clever rebuttal — she should come to see, or to admit she always half-suspected, that the facts fixing the content of her own perceptual states sit no more inside her head than the facts fixing the device's: her ancestry, her training, the community that reads her behavior and assigns it content. If the machine's aboutness is borrowed, so is hers, and the gap between them is one of degree and complication rather than of kind. Let the ending turn on a choice she must make about which mapping to certify, where the reading that best predicts the system's behavior is not the reading its builders intended, and where the two readings place liability on different parties; leave it genuinely open whether her signature records something or makes it so. A few craft rules: no philosophers named as characters or cited in dialogue, no scene that is a lecture with two people standing in it, apparatus legible to a reader who has never opened a journal, and at least one quiet scene with no argument in it that nonetheless bears on the question. I would rather the last line be an image than a verdict.
