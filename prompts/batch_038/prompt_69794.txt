Write a dialogue between two theorists disputing whether it can be rational to discount a future good merely because it lies further off.

Give the first speaker, A, this position: pure time preference is not a prejudice to be argued out of but a consequence of the other things B wants. If a ranking of infinite welfare streams is stationary — shifting both streams forward by a period leaves every comparison unchanged — and if the timing of benefits ever matters at all, then earlier must be preferred to later. Further, anonymity across dates, monotonicity, and continuity cannot all be satisfied at once, and the impartial orderings actually on offer are indifferent to any finite difference: on them, adding a thousand years of flourishing to a stream leaves it exactly as good as before.

Give the second speaker, B, this position: date of occurrence is morally arbitrary, and discounting for it — as distinct from discounting for the risk that the future people will not exist — is a failure of imagination wearing the costume of an axiom. B concedes A's results but denies that they vindicate impatience; they show only that one axiom must go, and B is willing to give up completeness, adopting an overtaking-style partial ordering that does register finite improvements even though some pairs of streams come out unranked.

Have them fight over a concrete crux rather than generalities: A produces a pair of long-run schemes whose cumulative advantages alternate forever, so that B's ordering must shrug, and asks whether a theory that cannot rank this pair is fit to advise anyone; B replies that A's axioms fix the sign of the discount rate but never its magnitude, so on A's side too the hard cases are settled by fiat. Both must be formidable, neither a strawman, and every turn must engage the point just made instead of restating a position. Begin with the dialogue itself, with no preface and no commentary.
