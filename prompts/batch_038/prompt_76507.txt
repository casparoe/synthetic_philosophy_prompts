Two people in an online anthropics seminar I help run have basically stopped listening to each other. One is a committed self-sampling-assumption person — she thinks you should reason as if you're a random sample from your reference class, takes the doomsday argument seriously, and holds that SIA is just a licence to believe absurd things about the size of the universe on no evidence at all. The other is a committed self-indication-assumption person — he thinks observer-moments should be weighted by how many of them a theory says exist, that this is what makes fine-tuning and multiverse confirmation work without any extra machinery, and that every SSA paradox dissolves once you admit SSA is wrong. They've both read the standard material and neither budges.

I want the document they should have produced: the joint statement from an adverserial collaboration between them. Assume I already know what SSA and SIA are and what the presumptuous philosopher case is — don't explain the background to me, just do the drafting.

To stop them retreating to the cases they've both memorised, both parties have to apply their view to a new setup, which I'll call the duplicate lottery:

A machine picks an integer k uniformly from 1 to 1000. It then creates exactly k people in identical sealed rooms. Each of them is told this whole protocol and nothing else. Exactly one of the k — chosen uniformly among them — is fitted with a red wristband; everyone else gets a plain grey one. You are one of them. You look down and see a grey band.

Three things follow from this that they have to address head on. First, a warm-up both should be able to sign: if instead k were guaranteed to be either 1 or 1000 with a 50/50 prior, seeing grey settles the question outright, becuase with k = 1 that single person must be the red one. Second, the real numbers: what credence does each camp assign to k > 500 (a) before you look at your wrist, and (b) after you see it's grey? I want actual figures, not hedges, and I want the conditioning written out. Third, and this is where I expect the interesting fight: does the grey band carry any information at all, and if so, which way does it push? Note that with k people exactly one is red, so the chance of being grey depends on k — I want to see whether either camp thinks that dependence is legitimate evidence, and if one of them thinks it isn't, what principle licenses them to ignore it.

The deliverable:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Claims both of them can sign, each short. If a claim is only signable because a word in it is doing double duty — say "probability" or "reference class" or "evidence" — mark it as such and say which reading each side has in mind. I'd rather have six honest claims than twenty padded ones.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the issue | the SSA camp's own formulation of it, in wording she'd sign | the SIA camp's own formulation, in wording he'd sign | whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal | one sentence of justification for that last call. Cover at least: what indexical uncertainty is uncertainty about, whether observer-count facts confirm the theories that predict them, what the reference class is fixed by, and whether the doomsday-style reasoning survives in some weaker form.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. For each: the case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Two candidates I'd like considered, plus any of your own: (i) suppose the lottery is run a thousand times and afterwards every created person files a report — the fraction of reports coming from high-k runs is a fact both camps agree on, so is that fact relevant to your credence, and does answering "no" cost the SSA camp anything? (ii) if we stipulate the betting setup precisely — payoff made once per person in the room versus payoff made once per run of the machine — do the two camps' recommendations converge, and if they do, how much of the original dispute was about words?

4. A short section on falsifiers: what each camp would accept as evidence against their own view, and an explicit answer to whether there is anything at all that both agree would move the debate. If the honest answer is "no, and here's why", write that.

Keep the whole thing tight — under about a thousand words. And end with exactly one line, with nothing after it, in this form:

\answer{<SSA camp's credence that k>500 after seeing grey> / <SIA camp's credence for the same question> / <genuine or verbal, for whichever disagreement you judge most fundamental>}
