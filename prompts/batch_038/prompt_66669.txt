I've spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums that my instincts about the transitional-justice literature are mechanism-design instincts, and I think they've led me somewhere slightly heretical. I'm drafting an essay and want to pressure-test a piece of jargon I invented for it against a play that seems to me to be about precisely the thing I'm groping at.

By a holdout detail I mean a feature of an event known to the victim and the perpetrator but withheld from everyone else, so that a confession which correctly supplies or corrects it carries information that could not have been scripted by the elicitor. Without any holdout, a confession is what I'll call scripted: it may be sincerely delivered, tearfully performed, and institutionally accepted, but every element of it could have been reconstructed from what the interrogator already said aloud, and so it carries close to zero bits. My working claim is that a truth commission running an amnesty-for-disclosure bargain has an acute scripted-confession problem — a perpetrator who needs amnesty will volunteer whatever is independently checkable plus a plausible margin, because marginal detail is free to him and expensive-looking to the commissioners — and that much of what these bodies certify as the truth about the past may be perpetrator-to-perpetrator corroboration wearing the costume of verification.

Dorfman's Death and the Maiden (I mean the play; flag it if Polanski's film diverges where it matters) is, I think, a working model of this. Correct me if I've garbled any of it. Paulina Salas was tortured and raped as a political prisoner by a doctor whose face she never saw, and who played Schubert's Death and the Maiden quartet while he did it. Years later, in the new democracy, her husband Gerardo, a lawyer, is being appointed to the government's investigative commission — whose mandate, as the play is careful to let Paulina point out, reaches only cases where the victim died, which puts her own suffering structurally outside the official record. Then she recognizes the voice of Dr. Roberto Miranda, ties him up, and holds a trial of her own. The device I can't stop thinking about: she tells Gerardo her story with small details deliberately altered, and the confession that Gerardo and Miranda then compose together reproduces the true details instead of the planted ones. That is why she is certain of his guilt — even though the confession was extracted at gunpoint, co-written by the man's own defence counsel, taped, signed, and yet worth nothing whatever to a third party. The last scene jumps ahead to a concert hall, Miranda maybe in the audience and maybe only in her head, and whether she killed him is never resolved.

Read the play through that lens, then. Specifically:

1. Does the holdout device actually carry the epistemic weight I'm putting on it, and what is the play saying about the difference between convincing the one person the evidence was designed for and producing a record that others can act on?
2. Is Paulina's private tribunal the play's answer to the commission's failure, or its indictment — does Dorfman think a truth that only the victim can certify is a truth at all?
3. Where does the play resist the reading? Tell me bluntly which parts of my framing flatten what the play is doing — what she wants from this particular man, what the coercion costs her, what Gerardo is for — and whether my jargon is doing unearned work.
4. The concert ending: is Dorfman's refusal to settle Miranda's guilt a claim about the limits of holdout verification or dramatic hedging, and does withholding the result place the audience in the commission's epistemic position rather than Paulina's?
5. Does the mechanism transfer? Of South Africa's individualized amnesty in exchange for full disclosure, Argentina's commission that could compile a record but not pardon, and Rwanda's gacaca, where confession and apology earned reduced sentences, which faces the worst scripted-confession problem and why — and does the play illuminate that case or actively mislead about it?

Give me exactly five numbered items, one per question above, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; I'll expand them into sections myself. Argue from the play and from the concepts rather than from who has said what — no bibliography, no roll call of scholars, and no quoting the script at me, since I'd have to check any quotation anyway.
