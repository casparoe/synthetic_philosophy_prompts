I cover AI and cognitive science for a general-interest magazine and I'm trying to write a piece on whether large language models mean anything by the words they produce. Two philosophers I interviewed both waved the question off with Putnam's old line about meaning not being in the head — Twin Earth, water versus XYZ, the whole thing — as if that settles it: a model trianed on text has no causal commerce with the world, so there's nothing to discuss. I'm pretty sure that's a non-sequitur but I cant say exactly where it breaks.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan can carry, running from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical? For each one I want to know what exactly is supposed to fail to be in the head — reference, extension, sense, narrow content, what the word picks out for the individual speaker as opposed to for the community — what argument or intuition actually backs it, whether it follows from what Twin Earth shows or is something extra, and whether someone could grant it without conceding anything about LLMs. Then tell me which readings my interviewees actually need for their dismissal, and which ones point the other way: I have a hunch that the stuff about the division of linguistic labor between experts and laypeople cuts against them, and I'd like to know whether that's right or whether I'm mangling it. Give it to me as a menu I can work from while reporting, with a verdict on each reading — platitude, contentious, false — rather than one long story about externalism. No bibliography needed.
