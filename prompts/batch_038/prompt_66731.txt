I fit age–period–cohort models for a living. My working vocabulary consists of age effects, period effects, and cohort effects treated as separable components; the exact linear dependency among the three, since cohort is period minus age, which leaves any decomposition unidentified until constraints are imported from outside the data; cohorts as diagonals moving through the age-by-period plane; and the standing suspicion that a striking cohort effect is really a mis-specified period effect or an artifact of whatever constraint I happened to impose. I have no philosophical training beyond a single undergraduate course, and I will notice if a translation smuggles in my own assumptions and presents them as the philosophers' conclusions.

There is a debate, in philosophy and in the history of ideas, over whether generations — Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z — are real kinds or useful fictions. Five claims from that debate are set out below. For each, restate the claim as exactly as you can in the vocabulary described above, and then say what failed to survive the restatement.

1. Generations are real social kinds rather than convenient labels: people born inside the same window of years share a common location in historical time, and that sharing grounds a distinctive outlook that would obtain whether or not anyone used the names.
2. Generational labels are retrospective constructions, applied by journalists and market researchers to what is really a continuum of birth years; there is no fact of the matter about where one generation stops and the next starts, so the boundaries are arbitrary and the groupings nominal.
3. Shared birth years create at most a potential generation, which becomes an actual one only when it splits into rival sub-groups that respond in opposed ways to the same events — so a single cohort can contain two generations in the substantive sense.
4. Once a generational label circulates widely, people revise their self-descriptions and conduct to match it, so the classification alters the population it purports merely to describe.
5. Explanations framed at the level of the generation — that Millennials postponed household formation, that Generation Z is more anxious — are legitimate and are neither reducible to, nor in competition with, explanations framed in terms of the individuals concerned.

Deliver exactly five numbered items, one per claim, and each item exactly two sentences: the first the restatement in my vocabulary, the second what the restatement loses, together with a verdict on whether that loss shows the original claim was less determinate than it sounded or shows that my framework has no way of expressing something true. Offer no introduction, no conclusion, no headings beyond the numbers, and no suggested readings. Where a claim can be rendered in my terms only by quietly assuming that cohort effects are constrained period effects in disguise, treat that as a failure of the translation and say so rather than presenting it as a success.
