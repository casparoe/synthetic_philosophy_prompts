Please take the role of a political theorist who reads Machiavelli closely and who has also sat through enough executive searches to know how boards actually reason about risk. I want you to think carefully through a decision I have to help settle this week, and I want the thinking laid out before the document, not buried in it.

The situation. I sit on the board of a mid-sized mutual insurer that writes residential windstorm and flood coverage along a single coastline. Our chief executive retires in April. Our bylaws contain a clause, 7(c), drafted years ago by a director who has since died, requiring that the search committee's recommendation to the full board state "the basis on which the board judged each candidate's capacity to lead the company through adverse conditions." Nobody has had to interpret it until now, and it turns out to be the whole fight.

Candidate A joined as claims director and ran operations through two named storms four years apart. In the first, she paid on photographs and sworn statements rather than waiting for adjusters to reach the parishes, contracted three hundred outside adjusters in nine days, and absorbed an underwriting loss that consumed most of that year's earnings; the company survived, kept roughly 94 percent of its policyholders, and drew a commendation from the state regulator. Between the storms she rebuilt reserves and pre-positioned reinsurance. The objection pressed against her is that neither storm struck the densely insured metro corridors; that a federal backstop facility absorbed a share of the first event the board had not counted on; and that the balance sheet she inherited after a decade of benign weather is what kept her improvisation from sinking the company. In short: that she was fortunate rather than able, and that a board reading her record as evidence of ability has merely been fooled by which counties the wind happened to cross.

Candidate B has twenty-two years in the industry, eleven with us, four as chief operating officer after four as chief financial officer. Combined ratio in the low nineties for nine consecutive years, and no adverse-event record at all, because the largest single event the company faced during his line authority cost less than half of one year's surplus. He is described by colleagues as procedural, slow to delegate, and unwilling to act without a pre-existing authority. He told the committee, I think sincerely, that the correct response to catastrophe is not improvisation but terms written down years earlier.

The committee is split 6–5 and my memo goes to the full board Thursday.

The argument. I spent most of Sunday on this with a friend who works on Renaissance political thought, and we did not resolve it. Her position, in three moves. First: Machiavelli's term for the quality we are groping after is not competence and not moral virtue but a capacity to meet necessity decisively when occasion presents itself, and it shows in the seizing of an occasion rather than in a tally of results — so any outcome-based evaluation is the wrong instrument. But she pressed the sting herself: if results cannot evidence it, then nothing a board could observe evidences it either, and the concept collapses for selection purposes. Second: Machiavelli's own remedy for the fragility of fortune is not better people but works built in quiet weather — the image being a river that devastates only where no dykes or banks were raised beforehand. On that reading, what 7(c) gestures at is a property of institutions, B is basically right, and boards keep rewarding the temperament most likely to leave nothing standing once its holder departs. Third, and the one I could not answer: Machiavelli holds that a person whose manner of proceeding is fixed succeeds only while the times agree with it and cannot change his manner even when he sees he should — his illustration being a cautious commander whose caution prospered precisely because the times favored caution. If that is right, past success is evidence against range rather than for it, and we should discount A's storms and B's calm by the same factor. She added that my proposed hedge, appointing A with a chief operating officer of the opposite temperament, fails for the same reason: natures do not compose, and you can change a situation but not a character.

My counter was that preparation undertaken when nothing is happening is exactly the visible form of the capacity, which is what makes 7(c) satisfiable at all — and that A has some of that record, not only the improvisation. She said I was describing good institutions again and calling it character.

The deliverable. First, before drafting anything, give me a short analytical section — under 250 words — working out what turns on each of her three moves and where you think Machiavelli's categories genuinely apply to a corporate selection and where importing them distorts the case. Say plainly if you think they distort it. Then draft the memo to the board: 900 to 1,200 words, formal, signed from the search committee, and actionable. It must (1) say what would count as evidence, for this board, of the capacity 7(c) gestures at, and whether either candidate supplies it; (2) address directly the "she was lucky" objection and the symmetric objection that B has simply never been tested; (3) settle whether the board should prefer the candidate whose manner suits present conditions given that conditions may not hold; (4) decide the pairing question on the merits rather than assume it away; and (5) end with a clear recommendation and a time frame — appoint, appoint with specified structural conditions, defer, or reopen the search — with what the board would need to do between now and April.

Constraints. The memo must be legible to directors who have never read Machiavelli, which means the concepts have to carry the argument without the Italian vocabulary or any references to chapters; you may use the term once if you must, and gloss it in the same sentence. Do not quote Machiavelli, do not attribute specific passages to him, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations — describe his reasoning in your own words only. Treat the dissenting directors' best case seriously enough that they could recognize it and reply to it, and tell me what would have to be true for that case to prevail. Invent no facts beyond what I have given you; if you need an assumption, flag it as one.
