I am writing a novel set during and after an invented war, and I need help keeping one character's mind consistent with itself. He is a retired judge advocate giving evidence to a parliamentary inquiry; the chairman keeps putting cases to him, and he has become, against his will, the book's moral authority. His verdicts so far, all delivered in earlier chapters:

1. A nineteen-year-old conscript on the aggressor's side, raised on the story that the invasion was a rescue of kin across the border, who killed three defending soldiers in open country: nothing he could be condemned for, and no complaint had he been killed doing it.
2. A career officer on the same side who knew from the first week that the war was a land grab and stayed for the promotion, killed by a sniper while directing a barrage: the judge dismissed his family's claim on the ground that he had forfeited it, and added that the sniper had done nothing wrong.
3. A farmer on the defending side with no uniform, no commander and no orders, who shot a soldier: a combatant, not a murderer.
4. An unarmed surgeon attached to the aggressor's forces who shot a soldier in the act of executing his patients: permissible.
5. A conscript of the aggressor who deserted after being told his parents would be shot if he stayed: not to be punished, and whatever the two men he killed before deserting were owed, he owed nothing.
6. A drone operator on the defending side, four thousand kilometres away, who destroyed a fuel tanker moving petrol to the aggressor's bomber groups the night before a sortie: permissible.

The chapter I cannot finish turns on the case the judge reserved. It concerns a captain in the aggressor's air force logistics directorate, posted in the capital, four hundred kilometres from any front, unarmed and never likely to fire a shot, who schedules the rail movements that keep the bomber groups flying. He is not deceived about what the bombers do. He could transfer to a training school or take early release into the state railways at some cost to his pension and no cost to his safety. A partisan cell proposes to shoot him on his way home.

Tell me what the judge should rule if he is to be consistent with himself. Specifically: the two or three doctrines that accommodate verdicts 1 through 6 equally well; what each of them says about this captain, and about the partisan who would kill him; which doctrine you think he is actually running and why; one further case of your own devising that would separate the doctrines that happen to agree about him; and, last, the two or three sentences the judge would say aloud in the inquiry room — plain enough for the public gallery, exact enough that a reader who knows this literature would recognise precisely what he has just committed himself to.
