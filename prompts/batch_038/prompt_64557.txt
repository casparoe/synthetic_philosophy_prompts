I am contributing a chapter to a collected volume on aesthetic moralism, and the editor has asked each of us to open with a compact statement of the position we intend to defend, so that the other contributors have something definite to attack. My own position is a secularised Tolstoyanism, arrived at over some years of teaching and, I should add, of resisting both of the standard reactions to *What Is Art?* — the dismissal of Tolstoy as a philistine and the pious defence of him as a misunderstood moralist. Here is the package as I currently have it:

(1) Art is the deliberate transmission, by means of external signs, of a feeling the artist has himself experienced, in such a way that the receiver is infected by that feeling and thereby joined to the artist and to everyone else who is infected by the same work. Nothing further is constitutive of arthood: not beauty, not the giving of pleasure, not formal achievement, not the expression of a worldview.

(2) Infection is therefore the mark of the artistic, and it is binary — an object either infects or it does not. What admits of degrees is excellence, measured by the strength of the infection; and that strength depends on the individuality of the feeling transmitted, on the clarity of its transmission, and above all on the sincerity of the artist, understood as his actually feeling what he transmits.

(3) Infection is unmediated and effortless on the side of the receiver. If a work can move me only once I have acquired the conventions, the background, or the interpretive apparatus that lets me follow it, then what has occurred is a cognitive accomplishment of mine rather than an infection of me, and the object is not, for me, a work of art. This is the clause I rely on when I explain to students why Tolstoy's verdicts on Shakespeare are not simply the boorishness they are usually taken to be.

(4) The value of a work, as distinct from its arthood, is fixed by the moral quality of the feeling it transmits. Here I part company with Tolstoy: I do not ground that appraisal in the religious perception of the age, nor in any teleology of humanity's movement toward perfection. I ground it in an epistemic-moral good — art acquaints a person with feelings she could not otherwise have had, and thereby enlarges the range of her sympathies.

(5) I nonetheless keep Tolstoy's conclusion about accessibility, and take myself to be entitled to it without his theology: art that can be received only by the specially trained fails its vocation, difficulty is a defect rather than a distinction, and the narrowness of the audience a work could in principle reach counts against its value.

(6) I also hold that the responses of the prepared reader are not to be discounted. My own experience of the late James — several flat readings, and then, somewhere around the fifth, an overwhelming one — is evidence about that work's value which my theory is obliged to accommodate rather than explain away. And I hold, with Tolstoy, that the professional critic and the credentialed connoisseur are obstacles to art rather than aids to it; my own teaching, which prepares students to receive difficult works, is not in conflict with that.

What I want from you is an audit of this set, not instruction in it. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordered by severity, and for each say precisely what would have to give and what giving it would cost me; tell me which pairs only look like tensions and dissolve on inspection, and why they look like tensions in the first place; tell me where, if anywhere, I am retaining a conclusion whose only warrant was a premise I have since discarded; and tell me which single revision would buy the most consistency for the least damage — including, if that is your verdict, that the package is coherent as it stands and my worry is unfounded. Please conduct the audit on my commitments as stated rather than on Tolstoy's text: if a tension can be dissolved only by an interpretive claim about what he really held, say so and mark the item as exegesis-dependent instead of settling it. Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence — whether the package hangs together and, if it does not, which commitment has to go — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my question.
