ok so — no philosophy background here, I run a small print shop and I've been on my condo board for three years, and this started at a board meeting in the spring and has been rattling around in my head ever since. Could you act as a philosopher of technology who's patient with non-specialists? Please actually think it through carefully, and maybe brainstorm out loud a bit — list the three or four places you think the argument could be breaking, then pick — before you commit to a diagnosis. I'd rather see the working than a verdict.

The background: our building has a side door off the alley that the cleaning crew and the delivery people use. In April the board put a badge reader on it. Stated reason was insurance, and it wasn't a fake reason — some guy had walked in off the street and taken a bike out of the lobby. Everyone who lives there got a badge. The cleaning company didn't, because they're a contractor and the whole thing was set up as a resident amenity, and nobody thought about it until about two weeks later when the crew started having to buzz the front desk and wait for someone to let them in, sometimes ten minutes, in the rain. One of the cleaners, a woman who's been coming here for six years, asked if they could get badges. The property manager said the insurer requires badges to be issued to "residents and their guests." So now the people who work in the building every single day come into it as guests. That part genuinely bothers me and I want to be able to say why.

A friend of mine who's in a grad program lent me an essay to read about this kind of thing, by Langdon Winner, on whether artifacts have politics — the famous example is Robert Moses building the parkway overpasses on Long Island too low for buses to get under, so the beach was effectively for the people who could drive there. That case I get, it's vivid and I buy it. (My friend also mentioned some researcher went and checked the record and argues the bridge story is overblown, which I found interesting and also completely useless to me at a board meeting.) The problem is that when I tried to write up my own version of the point for the newsletter, I ended up building an argument that proves way too much, and my friend — who is supposedly the expert — couldn't tell me where it goes wrong.

Here it is. (1) The badge reader has politics in the same sense as those bridges: it's a physical feature that settles who moves freely and who waits, and the line it draws tracks a social division, resident versus worker, rather than anybody's needs. (2) But the reader doesn't do that by itself; it's the badge policy that does it, and a policy is made of people's decisions, so the reader has its politics only derivatively, by being plugged into a rule. (3) Nearly every physical feature of the building can get plugged into a rule like that. The side door faces the alley and not the street. The lobby has a desk somebody sits behind. The bike room takes a key. The stairwell door won't open from the inside except by pushing a bar. Each of those, under some policy or other, sorts people into groups that line up with who has money or a job here or the right paperwork. (4) So if the reader is political, so is the direction the door faces, the height of the service counter, and the size of the type we print the notices in. (5) A phrase that applies to which way a door faces applies to everything, and "everything is political" tells the board nothing, because whatever we decide we'll be doing politics, so it can't be a reason to decide one way. (6) So either the badge reader isn't political after all, or "artifacts have politics" is empty — and I really don't want it to be empty. (7) Therefore artifacts don't have politics; politics belongs to people's choices and institutions, and the hardware is just hardware.

I do not believe (7), not for one second. I still feel like the badge reader case is different from the which-way-the-door-faces case, and I want to be able to say what the difference is without rigging the game in my own favor. So: which step do I reject? Walk me through the ones that look innocent but aren't. And whatever you name as the flaw, price it for me — if I hold onto (1) I seem stuck with (4), and if I dump (4) I need some principle that separates the reader from the orientation of the door, and I don't know what that principle would be or whether it's the kind of thing that can be stated rather than pointed at. Also: does it matter that the insurance reason was real and not a cover? One guy on the board keeps saying that if the motive was safety and not exclusion then there's no politics in it at all, full stop, and I can't work out whether he's making a decent point or just changing the subject on me.
