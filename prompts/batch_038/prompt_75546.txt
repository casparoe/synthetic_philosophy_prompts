I serve on a small-town historical commission, and over the last year I have worked out a position on monuments on my own, with no training in philosophy. Before I put it in writing for the board, I would like it audited. Here is what I hold.

(1) A monument honors; it is not neutral record-keeping. That is why I reject "just leave it up, it is only history."

(2) What a monument says was settled the day it went up. The people who raised ours in 1909 meant something by it, and whoever walks past afterward does not get to change that.

(3) Even so, ours should come down. It stands across from the school that the doctor on the pedestal, as a school board member, helped keep segregated, and what it tells the families walking past today is that this town still stands behind that admiration.

(4) When deciding whether anyone deserves a statue, I weigh the whole life rather than the worst single thing; nobody survives the other test. But some acts rule out a statue regardless of everything else the person did.

(5) Removal does not erase history. I would move ours to the county museum with a full account of why it was moved.

(6) The decision belongs to the people who live here now, by vote — not to historians, and not to the commission I sit on.

Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which only look like they do? Please order the real ones by severity rather than reporting everything as mildly in tension with everything else, and for each say what would have to give. I have not decided what I would be willing to abandon, so do not assume a favorite. Then tell me the single revision that would settle the most of the set at once, and what it costs me — what I would no longer be able to say once I make it.
