I write novels, mostly family stuff, so I spend my working life inside dialogue, and there's a move people keep making in dialogue that I've never been able to find a word for.

Two friends at the door at the end of a dinner. One says "we have to do this again soon, properly," the other says "yes — a whole weekend, let's take the houses in Portugal, all of us, the kids too." Neither will do any of it. But it isn't a lie in the interesting sense, because she doesn't want him to believe it. If he actually booked the houses she'd be appalled. And if he said "no we won't, and we both know we won't," he'd be the one who behaved badly — the literal reader is the offender here, not the victim. Stranger still: the more extravagant the plan, the warmer the exchange, as though the implausibility is the thing being handed over, and the effort of composing it is the message.

Same shape elsewhere. My aunt telling my uncle he looks well when the two of them have just come back from the oncologist and he says "I know, I feel it." A guest praising a ruined casserole to a host who ruined it and knows it, and who accepts the praise with a small modest wave that everyone at the table recognises as a wave.

So: is there an established concept for this? Is it tatemae, or that general Japanese arrangement? Is it a white lie — except a white lie wants to be believed and this one seems to fail on purpose, and would be spoiled by succeeding? Nearest neighbours and how mine differs from each matter more then the label to me, because I have to know when a character has stepped outside it — what breaks the thing, what counts as a betrayal, whether acknowledging it is always the wrong move. And whether it's more or less honest than a straight white lie, given nobody is actually deceived. If nothing fits, coin a term and tell me where its edges are.

Plain prose please, no headings or bullets or bold or tables — like a letter.
