I am a fourth-year philosophy student, and this term I am taking a seminar on formal epistemology whose last three weeks have been devoted to updating on uncertain evidence. Alongside the seminar I work about ten hours a week in a perception lab, where participants are shown colour chips drawn from a smooth continuum and asked to report which of several named categories each chip belongs to, together with a graded confidence rating. My interest is in what a Bayesian should say about data of that kind, and I have run into a difficulty I cannot resolve, which I would like to make the subject of an eight-week summer project funded by my department's undergraduate research award. The application requires a proposal of roughly fifteen hundred words, which would then become the first chapter of the thesis I hope to write next year.

The difficulty is this. Probability kinematics, as I understand it, takes as its input a partition together with a new distribution over its cells, and holds fixed the conditional probability of everything else on each cell; the rule is silent about where the partition comes from, and the usual gloss is that the evidence must determine one. In the colour experiment there is no obvious candidate. The chips form a continuum, so any partition is a carving; the categories our participants use have boundaries they themselves cannot locate reliably, so I am not confident whether a given chip falls in the red cell or the orange cell; and granularity is a free parameter, since the continuum can be cut as finely as one likes. These seem to me to strain three different assumptions at once. If I am unsure which cell a chip falls into, I cannot supply the new distribution over cells that the rule requires as input, because that input already presupposes the carving. If I do choose a granularity, then two agents who carve differently but receive the same report can arrive at different posteriors for a downstream question — whether the participant's colour space is normal, say — and the difference is not traceable to any difference in their evidence. I know of two standard repairs and neither looks satisfying: redescribing the evidence as the proposition that this subject had an experience as of a chip in this region and then ordinary conditionalizing, which seems to relocate the vagueness into the description of the experience rather than remove it; or adopting a family of probability functions, one per admissible carving, and updating on all of them, which concedes that the output is not a single posterior and leaves me, as far as I can tell, without sharp predictions and without a principled account of which carvings count as admissible.

Could you turn this into the proposal? I would like it to contain: a precise statement of the research question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than amounting to the observation that vagueness is hard; an explicit account of how the question differs from the familiar complaint that the rule does not tell you which partition to use, and from the problem of old evidence, since a reader will assume I have merely rediscovered one of those, together with a candid judgment about whether anything genuinely open remains once they are set aside; a description of the method, including what the formal setup would look like and what I would actually do week by week across the eight weeks — I expect this to involve proving some small results about updating under boundary uncertainty in a toy model rather than surveying positions; a statement of what a successful result would look like (a rule, an impossibility theorem, or a dissolution of the question) and what would count as failure; the reasons for preferring this approach to the two repairs I described; a short paragraph anticipating the committee's objection that this is a solution in search of a problem, since actual agents rarely need posteriors over colour continua; and an honest assessment of scope, saying where I should cut if the project proves too large for one summer. Please do not include a bibliography or a list of references — I will assemble those myself from the seminar reading list — though you are welcome to refer to positions and arguments by description.
