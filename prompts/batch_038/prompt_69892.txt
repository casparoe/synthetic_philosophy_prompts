I lead a small backend team at a digital-health company, and one of the things we maintain is the service that decides, case by case, whether a user's interaction with a screen counts as consent to a particular processing of their data. In practice this means our product has to answer questions that our lawyers and our privacy office answer with vocabulary I cannot operationalise: they will say that a certain flow leaves an agreement merely voidable rather than void, or that a user "acquiesced" rather than consented, or that a disclosure was technically accurate yet the consent was still not real, and I have no way of turning any of that into a branch in a state machine. I have no formal philosophy training — I read a little around the edges — and the material I have managed to find tends either to assume the reader already knows the terminology, or to collapse everything into the single test of whether the user was adequately informed and acted freely, which is exactly the test that fails to discriminate between the tickets landing in our queue. So I would like you to build me a decision tree, and I would like it built out of the logical space of the questions first: give me the branch conditions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then attach labels to the resulting cells, rather than starting from a list of named doctrines and reverse-engineering questions for them.

The branch nodes must be phrased so that an engineer holding only a written description of a case could answer them without knowing where each answer leads, and no node may simply ask whether the consent "was real". At minimum the tree should separate: deception about what the act fundamentally is from deception about its incidental terms or consequences; a threat, a hard but genuine offer, and the mere absence of attractive alternatives — including the case where the absence of alternatives is produced by background circumstances nobody in the transaction created; competence treated as an all-or-nothing property versus a threshold that moves with the stakes and the irreversibility of the decision; scope and bundling, where a single affirmative gesture is taken to cover purposes the user was never separately asked about; revocability, and whether the possibility of withdrawal is a condition of the consent's ever having been valid or only a condition of our being permitted to keep relying on it; and interface-level shaping of preference, where the user chose voluntarily in every narrow sense but our own flow supplied the reasons they chose for. The leaves should carry whatever label the law or the literature actually uses — valid, void from the outset, voidable at the user's option, mere acquiescence or submission that was never consent, consent that is procedurally sound yet cannot authorise the act because of what it does to someone who never agreed to it, and consent that is valid and yet was obtained in a way that wrongs the user anyway. Where a leaf has no standard name, say so and coin one. For every leaf, add one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the case it gets obviously wrong, or the cost it imposes on us as a designer. Then list the combinations of answers that the tree permits but that no leaf occupies, and diagnose each as incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate, and say what further information about a case would separate them.

To keep this honest, here are seven anonymised tickets from the last quarter; after the tree, tell me which leaf each one lands on, and whether that verdict matches the intuition you think most readers would have, and if the two diverge, which of them is more likely to be wrong. (1) A seventeen-year-old who fully understands what a registry involves and is not being pushed by anyone, in a context where we have set no age rule at all. (2) A patient mid-appointment, whose clinic tablet shows the data-sharing toggle already selected and a single button marked Continue; the alternative path takes three taps and a call to a support line that closes at five. (3) A user offered a £200 gift card to join a study carrying a small risk of disclosure to an insurer — the risk was described accurately, in plain language, and a follow-up quiz suggests the user understood it. (4) A user who agreed to share genomic data with "research partners"; nothing on the screen was false, but we had already signed an exclusive arrangement with a firm whose product the user would object to on conscientious grounds, and the possibility simply never occurred to them. (5) A user whose employer pays for a wellness app and can see the enrolment list, though not individual results. (6) A terminally ill patient on high-dose opioids who gives an unpressured, lucid, well-informed yes to a data donation at three in the morning through a chatbot. (7) A user who consented after an onboarding flow containing three testimonials about how sharing helps other patients — every one of them true, none of them mentioning risk. Two further requests: note anywhere the tree sorts a case differently than a court probably would, and say which of the two you think is tracking the morally relevant facts; and please write the whole response in British English, including spellings and date formats, since it will go into our internal documentation.
