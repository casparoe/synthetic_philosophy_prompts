Longtime lurker, first-time asker-sort-of. I've been on the mod team of a big online community for about eleven years — wiki plus forums plus a Discord, ~200k registered accounts, maybe 3–4k of whom post in any given month — and I got into all this governance stuff via the coordination posts on the rationalist blogs, then a long argument with a friend who does union organizing, who keeps telling me my problem is just a bargaining problem in disguise. I don't think she's right and I can't articulate why, which is part of what I'm asking about here.

The practical problem: people constantly show up with a claim about what "the community" wants, and we have no principled way to decide whether that claim licenses us doing anything. Last quarter alone we had a petition with 4,200 signatures demanding we reverse a moderation policy; a poll we ran ourselves that got 900 responses and pointed the other way; a voice town hall where about 60 people, mostly the loudest regulars, backed something else entirely; and a post with 800 upvotes asserting that everyone already agrees. We did what felt least bad and then wrote an announcement saying the decision reflected the community's view, which probly wasn't true, and it got quoted back at us for months. Please write me an actual decision procedure my team can run on a claim of the form "the community wants X," before we either act on it or repeat it in public. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure terminates in "no — that's a faction, not a community" or "this isn't up for a vote at all," and where it has to hand the decision to a human because what's left is a values question rather than a factual one. Constraints: it has to be runnable by volunteers with no stats training in under an hour, and the output has to be text I can paste into an announcement and defend in front of a ten-person mod council where at least three people already think I'm wrong.

The cases I specifically need it to survive, because this is where our intuitions turn to mush: who's actually inside "the community" — the 200k registered, the 3k active, the lurkers who'd leave if we get it wrong, the people we banned last year, the ones who only materialize when there's a fight, and the people who'd join under a different policy; whether 4,200 signatures beat 900 poll responses or whether that comparison is just nonsense; how much weight intensity gets versus raw headcount, and how you'd even measure intensity without it collapsing into "whoever shouts loudest"; what to do when the ballot options were chosen by an interested party, since I increasingly suspect "the community wants X" isn't a report about a pre-existing fact at all but a move someone is making, with the will getting manufactured by whoever wrote the question; and what happens when a majority of the people who bothered to show up want something that would drive out a minority, i.e. the things that shouldn't be aggregable in the first place. Then run it on three cases — the petition, our own poll, and the town hall — and make sure at least one of them lands somewhere I personally will hate (I'm fairly sure the honest answer is that our poll was worthless, and I defended it publicly). Close with a blunt list of what the procedure can't settle and where it will predictably misfire, and be honest about which steps are just judgment calls wearing a flowchart costume.

Don't hedge and don't hand me a menu of options to choose between — commit to the version you think is right and give me your reasons, including where you're making a call that someone else could reasonably make differently. No bibliography or reading list; if you want to gesture at a theory, gesture at the idea, not the citation, because I'm not going to go look anything up. American English, please.
