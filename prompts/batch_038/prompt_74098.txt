Sixteen months ago I resigned from a position in municipal social services and moved back to the town where I grew up to become my mother's primary caregiver. Her dementia has advanced considerably in that time. In the long stretches of the night I have been reading in the ethics of care, and partly to keep myself honest, partly because a hospice organization here has asked me to speak to its family-caregiver support group in the spring and I want to be clear about what I am actually telling them, I have written down the set of positions I seem to have arrived at. I would like a rigorous audit of whether they hang together as a package. Please write in American English.

My commitments:

1. Care is a practice before it is a sentiment, and the ethics of care is best understood as a standing criticism of moral theories that begin from general principles and derive judgments, rather than as a rival theory offering better principles.
2. Dependency is not a tragic interruption of the normal human condition but that condition itself: everyone has been dependent, is dependent, or will be, and our institutions are built on a pretense otherwise.
3. Because of (2), the provisioning of care is a matter of justice and ought to be organized collectively and publicly, rather than left to the accident of who happens to love whom.
4. Yet the value of care lies in the particular relationship. The person who cares for my mother is not interchangeable with any equally competent stranger, and I do not think this is a bias I owe anyone an apology for.
5. Attentiveness — noticing what this person needs, which requires knowing her across time — is the first and most important moral achievement in caring, and most downstream failures turn out to be failures to have noticed.
6. Self-sacrifice is not a virtue. A caregiver who is depleted, or who quietly resents the person she is caring for, is not thereby caring well, and any moral outlook that reads her exhaustion as proof of her goodness is using her.
7. Conversely, a caregiver who feels no warmth at all but does the work attentively and reliably is caring well; the presence or absence of affection is morally secondary.
8. A person in the advanced stages of dementia remains fully within the scope of care and does not fall out of it because she can no longer recognize anyone, reciprocate anything, or carry a narrative of her own life.

And two verdicts about my own situation:

A. About a year in, I began hiring aides for overnight and weekend coverage so that I could return to part-time work. I think this was not merely permissible but correct, and I do not think it made the care less mine.

B. Over the last few months my mother no longer recognizes me, speaks very little, and what I do has become almost entirely physical: feeding, bathing, repositioning, sitting with her. I find myself thinking that the relationship, in the sense that gave it its value, has come to an end; that what remains is skilled custodial work; and that this is precisely the portion a well-trained aide could perform as well as I could, or better. I think it would be defensible, and perhaps better, for me to step back and let that happen.

What I want from you is an audit rather than an exposition of the field. Tell me which of these pull against each other and which merely look as though they do. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list. For each, identify the commitment that would have to be given up or qualified, say what giving it up would cost, and note where a single revision would resolve more than one tension at once — and then tell me which single revision buys the most. Be equally explicit about the pairs that only appear inconsistent, and explain why they survive scrutiny, because I expect several of them do. Parts of this list feel shaky to me and other parts feel obvious, but I have no reliable sense of which is which, so please do not take my intuitions about the location of the problem as data, and do not assume I am more attached to any one item than to another: treat the whole list as negotiable.
