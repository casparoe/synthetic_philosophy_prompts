I'm a history doctoral student, and there's a move in narrative history I can't place. A historian explains why a particular cabinet fell in a particular week by telling the story forward, and every step is singular: a minister's vanity, a rumor that leaked on a Tuesday, a backbencher home sick with influenza. Pressed for a generalization, the historian offers none — instead they point to a collapse three centuries earlier, in another country, with a recognizably similar shape, and remark that it is the same kind of thing. The generality lives entirely in the pairing; no conditional with an antecedent and a consequent appears anywhere. Yet the pairing does real work: readers treat the second case as showing that the explanation is not merely local, and when the pairing is weak the explanation is judged weak, even though no generalization was ever stated.

Is there an established concept for this — explanation whose generality is carried by a matched pair of narratives rather than by a law? I know about ideal types, explanation sketches, and the demand that a narrative be followable, but none of them seems to fit: an ideal type is supposed to be an articulated construct you can state, a sketch is supposed to be elliptically convertible into a law, and followability concerns a single story taken on its own. If there is no name, coin and define one, and tell me where the line falls between a bad pairing and a merely strained one.
