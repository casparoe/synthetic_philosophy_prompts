I am preparing a handout for a formal-epistemology working group. We can all recite the hypotheses of Aumann's agreement theorem, yet in discussion we keep sliding past one another about what the result is supposed to establish. Build me a decision tree that sorts a participant from bare acceptance of the theorem's setup to a named position on whether, and when, rational agents may disagree.

Run the tree through a single concrete case: two careful hydrologists who have read the same historical record, have repeatedly disclosed their probability estimates to each other, and still differ on whether a particular dam fails within fifty years. Every branch node must be phrased as a plain question about that case, answerable without knowing which leaf the answer leads to. Among whatever else you think belongs, the nodes must cover whether the two hydrologists genuinely share a prior or merely share evidence; whether what each knows of the other's estimate is common knowledge or only mutual knowledge to some finite depth; whether learning that an equally careful peer reports a different number is itself evidence bearing on one's own; and whether ideal Bayesian updating is a standard agents can be held to at all.

The leaves must be positions rather than names: at minimum the view that the theorem is normative rather than descriptive, the view that common knowledge is unattainable and so the theorem is idle, the subjectivist rejection of the common prior assumption, the differing-evidence reply, an escape via imprecise credences or sets of priors, and a bounded-rationality account. For each leaf, give one sentence on the most uncomfortable commitment it inherits from the case.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and identify at least one combination of answers that seems logically available but that nobody defends, saying whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored. Please write the answer in American English.
