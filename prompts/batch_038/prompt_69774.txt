My brother works as a staffer for a city councillor where I live, and a couple months back the councillor killed a bus route that mostly served the east side of town in order to lock in the votes for a much bigger affordable-housing package. My brother thinks that was clearly the right call, and he's been saying "the ends justify the means, that's just politics" at every family dinner since. But he also insists — really insists — that the councillor is a good guy who ought to feel awful about it, and that feeling awful is part of what makes him a good guy rather than a monster. Those two positions don't seem to fit together and I can't articulate why, which is annoying because he keeps treating my objection as squeamishness.

I took exactly one political theory course, and in it we read that Walzer thing about dirty hands, and I remember the professor saying the whole point was that the politican is still guilty even when he did the right thing all things considered — which sounds like pretty much the opposite of "the ends justify the means". So I've got a vague sense that the slogan is doing something slippery: sometimes people say it to mean nothing was wrong at all, and sometimes they say it while conceding something was wrong, and those can't be the same claim.

Can you lay out the different precise things a person could be asserting when they say "the ends justify the means"? I want an actual menu, not one gloss. Vary the pieces that matter: what the "ends" have to be (any end? a good enough end? the agent's own end or someone else's?), what "justify" is supposed to cover (permission, or also praise, or also the disappearance of any moral remainder?), whether it needs the ends to actually come off or just to have been reasonably expected, whether it's a thesis about all action or a special one about political roles specifically, and whether it's being offered normatively or as a bit of cynical description of how politics works. For each reading say whether it's trivially true, substantive but defensible, or just false, and then tell me which one or two my brother was actually leaning on and whether any of them let him keep both halves of his story — that the trade was right, and that the councillor owes the east side something more than a shrug. If some of the readings are flat-out inconsistent with each other, flag that explicitly, because I suspect he's been sliding between two of them without noticing. (And don't bother with who coined the phrase, he says Machiavelli and I don't care, I care what it's claiming.)
