So, context: I'm TA-ing a big intro epistemology lecture for the first time this year, about 40 kids, and the skepticism unit got scheduled right before break, which turned out to be the worst possible timing. The discussion sections split cleanly in two — half of them walked out thinking the whole problem is a verbal trick (nobody actually doubts the external world, so there's nothing to solve), and the other half got genuinely spooked; one of them emailed me at like 1am asking whether he knows his mom exists. Then we spent the last two sections on the On Certainty excerpts: Moore's hand, the riverbed image where some propositions harden and act as channels for the fluid ones that shift around, the idea that what has to be accepted is forms of life. Now every student has a slightly different Wittgenstein and is convinced theirs is the obvious one, and my own take keeps sliding depending on which section I'm in. My roommate, who is not a philosopher, listened to me rant about this at dinner and said I sound like someone who thinks nobody can be wrong, which is its own interesting data point.

Next Tuesday is the last section of the semester and instead of more lecture I want to run a sorting exercise — basically a "which reading of On Certainty do you actually hold?" decision tree on a one-page handout I can walk them through live in 50 minutes. So: seven to nine nodes, each one a concrete little case or a question about a case that a student can answer without knowing where the answer sends them (no "do you think hinge commitments are doxastic?" — that just sorts them by which label they picked up). Leaves should at minimum cover: the view that hinges aren't beliefs at all, so the skeptic's question about them is malformed; the view that they are beliefs, split into whether they're actually known or merely something you're entitled to trust without evidence; the quietist/dissolution reading where there's nothing to answer; the deflationist reading where hinges are just extremely well-confirmed ordinary beliefs and On Certainty adds nothing; the externalist or animal-knowledge reading where you can know them without being able to justify them; and the framework-relativist reading where rival hinges can't be criticized from outside. Each leaf should get the name it goes by in the literature if it has one, plus one honest sentence about what it costs you — the most awkward commitment it forces.

Three things I specifically want you to build in, because they're what my sections actually fought about. First, a node on whether a hinge can be adopted on purpose — can someone decide to treat a claim as beyond question, or do these only get acquired? If nobody in the literature occupies that branch, say so and put it in the unoccupied-column with a guess about why (incoherent, just unattractive, or nobody's bothered). Second, a node on whether the framework is one per person, one per community, or nested layers — and then tell me which of your leaves collapse into each other if a student answers "nested," because I think at least two of them will. Third, a revisability case: my grandmother treated "the people in charge know what they're doing" as beyond question and then found out otherwise at 60, so does the tree say that was never a hinge, or that hinges can be lost? And flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually separate — I suspect quietism and deflationism give identical answers everywhere — and either say what extra question would tell them apart or admit it can't be done on a handout. Finally, since I'm going to hand this to forty people who will trust it: tell me how confident you are that these joints are the literature's joints and not just your own tidying, and point at the single branch or leaf you're least sure about, where a specialist would probably say I've mislabeled something or invented a position nobody defends. Please write the whole thing in American English, spelling and idiom, since it's getting printed here.
