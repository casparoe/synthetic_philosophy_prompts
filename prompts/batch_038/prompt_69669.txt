I'm trying to get my head around Dutch book arguments and I'd like a dialogue written out that actually gets into the weeds instead of the textbook version.

Two characters. The first is a hardline Bayesian: if a bookie can assemble a package of bets that you'd each accept on your own terms and that leaves you guaranteed to lose money, then your degrees of belief are simply irrational — vulnerability to sure loss is what incoherence consists in, not a mere symptom of it. The second thinks that's much too quick, and here's the line of resistance I want them to press, because I never see it properly worked through: the agent in the case has credences that obey every probability axiom you like, but evaluates each bet against whatever their situation happens to be at the moment the bet is offered, weighting losses more heavily than equal-sized gains. The bookie then walks off with their money anyway, by stringing together a handful of individually attractive bets. So you get the sure loss with no incoherence anywhere in the credences.

Build the example concretely and make the arithmetic checkable — I want to follow along and confirm each bet really is one the agent would take when it's put to them, and that the sequence really does net out to a loss for them. If it's cleaner to run it as a cycle of options the agent ranks intransitively because the ranking shifts with the reference point, do that instead, whatever actually works.

Then have them fight over what the case proves. Does it kill the Dutch book argument as a test of rationality? Or does it just show the test was never about credences alone but about credences plus a way of valuing outcomes, in which case the argument is fine and just narrower than advertised? Or is the loss-averse agent the irrational one after all, and the Bayesian right to say so? Give the Bayesian the good move — that the agent could sidestep the whole mess by precommiting to evaluate bets as a package — and give the other one a real answer, e.g. that precommitment is exactly the global calculation the sequential setup is engineered to prevent, so demanding it is question-begging.

Neither of them should be a strawman and neither should win by default. I want them responding to each others actual moves, conceding where it's warranted and pressing where it isn't. If they don't converge, fine — but by the end it should be clear what the residual disagreement is and what would settle it.
