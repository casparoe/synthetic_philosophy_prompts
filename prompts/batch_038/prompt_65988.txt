I'm working on a paper about the naturalist/normativist debate over the concept of disease, and the main secondary source I have used so far is a chapter in a medical humanities reader. Its account of Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory runs roughly as follows:

"Boorse's biostatistical theory is the standard statement of naturalism about disease. Health is normal functioning, and normality, on Boorse's account, is a statistical notion: a condition is a disease just when some part of the body functions at a statistically abnormal level, measured against the reference class of people of your age and sex. Because statistical facts involve no evaluation, the theory is meant to take values out of medicine altogether — disease becomes a matter of biology and nothing else, and the long-running dispute over whether disease is value-laden is settled by definition rather than by argument. The theory has a notorious upshot that Boorse is willing to live with: rare conditions are not diseases, since what is rare is not statistically normal. His critics have pressed a more damaging point. There is no non-arbitrary way to fix the reference class, because any sorting of people into groups reflects the interests of whoever does the sorting; narrow the class enough and everyone in it is perfectly normal. Since the reference class is chosen rather than discovered, the theory is value-laden after all, and naturalism collapses back into the normativism it was designed to displace."

I found the closing argument persuasive and had begun building my paper on it, but a classmate who has read more of the primary literature says the passage is a mess and that I would be better off not relying on it. I am not in a position to tell which of us is right, so I would like an audit of the passage rather than a defense or an attack on the theory itself. Please work through it and tell me:

1. Which claims a careful defender of the biostatistical theory would accept as fair description — and which important components of the theory the passage simply omits, especially anything that does real work in fixing what counts as a function or what the relevant goals of the organism are supposed to be.

2. Whether the theory is statistical in the way the passage assumes. In particular, is statistical abnormality sufficient on Boorse's account, or is something about the direction of the deviation doing work that the passage drops? And is the claim about rare conditions actually a consequence Boorse draws, or does it rest on a conflation of two different things that could be statistical — the frequency of a condition in a population, and the level of performance of a part relative to what is typical for a group?

3. Whether the reference-class objection does the work the passage asks of it. It is not obvious to me that showing the choice of reference class to be interest-relative is the same as showing it to be value-laden in the specific sense the normativist needs, and I would like to know whether the final inference is a real result or a slide between two different notions. If it is a slide, say precisely where the slide occurs and what the objection would have to establish in order to go through.

4. Whether the passage treats the scope of Boorse's claim correctly. It seems to slide between a thesis about disease and a thesis about medicine or about being ill more generally, and I have the impression that the theory says something more modest about one of these than about the other. Tell me what the scope actually is and which sentences of the passage overstate it.

5. Whether naturalism and normativism are really the only two positions in the dispute, or whether the binary framing silently rules out hybrid accounts that concede part of each side. If there are such accounts, describe the shape of the compromise rather than listing names.

Where the passage attributes a thesis or a concession to Boorse, please say whether the attribution is correct, incorrect, or something he would insist on qualifying. I am less interested in where I should come down in the debate than in knowing which sentences of the summary I could safely repeat in my own paper as accurate, and which ones I would have to rewrite.
