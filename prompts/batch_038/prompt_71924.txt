At a roundtable on frontier-model governance last month, a panellist dismissed a proposed industry agreement to hold safety-testing standards steady with the remark that this was a race to the bottom and that whoever slowed down first would lose. Several of us objected but could not say what we were objecting to, because the phrase seemed to be doing at least three jobs at once: describing a game-theoretic structure, forecasting a trend, and excusing the people caught inside it.

Could you lay out, as a menu I can circulate before our next session, the distinct precise claims that 'race to the bottom' might be making? I would like the readings to be separated along several axes: what the race is a race between and over what resource; whose bottom is at issue, the competitors' own payoffs or those of outsiders; whether the claim is about a single bad equilibrium or about a dynamic of successive erosion; whether the participants are said to be compelled or merely to be misjudging things; and whether the situation is really the prisoner's-dilemma shape the phrase suggests or something else entirely, such as an assurance game in which nobody wants to move first but everyone would move together.

For each reading, say whether it is near-analytic, substantive and defensible, or plainly false. Then identify which reading the panellist's argument actually needed, and what would have to be true of the situation for that reading to hold. Please write in British English.
