I have a very small podcast about consciousness — recorded at my kitchen table, maybe three hundred regular listeners, most of them friends of friends. I never went anywhere near a philosophy department; I read Dennett's Consciousness Explained when I was nineteen and it kind of wrecked me, and then over the last year I've been working my way through the people who think he's cheating: the zombie people, the phenomenologists, the ones who say you can do rigorous first-person work without giving up on science.

Next month I'm interviewing a philosopher of mind who has spent his whole career defending heterophenomenology as the method for a science of consciousness. I don't want to spend the hour asking him what it is, because I know what it is and my listeners could find that out in five minutes anywhere. Can you write me eight to ten questions that actually put pressure on the method itself?

Things I want covered, at least one question each:

- The neutrality claim. He says the method doesn't take a side on whether experience is real, it's just how you gather the data. But treating somebody's report as a text describing a world that may or may not exist feels to me like its already putting the burden on the experience to show up. Ask him what a heterophenomenological result would look like that counts as evidence *for* there being something it is like, as opposed to evidence for the report.
- Misreporting. When a subject is flatly wrong — change blindness, the split-brain cases, people confidently describing things that weren't there — the method just says that's what's true in their heterophenomenological world. Doesn't the pile-up of cases like that end up doing all the real arguing for the eliminativist side? That would mean it isn't neutral, it's a slow-drip argument in a lab coat.
- The Husserl business. As far as I can tell Husserl suspended belief about the outside world so he could take the appearing seriously, and heterophenomenology suspends belief about the appearing itself. Am I mangling that, or is that a real inversion he should answer for? And he sets the whole thing up against a strawman first-person method where reports are automatically true — does anybody actually hold that view?
- Agreement. If two people's texts line up in fine detail about the same stimulus, over and over, isn't the boring explanation that they're both picking something up? What does the method do with that?
- People who can't talk. Babies, animals, severe aphasia, locked-in patients. Does heterophenomenology put them outside the science of consciousness, or does a pointing response count as a text?
- My own one, tell me if this is silly: I could sit down with a chatbot, ask it how things seem to it, and get a transcript richer than anything a human gives me. Does it get a heterophenomenological world too? If not, which part of the method rules it out, and can he say that without smuggling in the metaphysics he claims to be staying out of?
- Concreteness: one study where using this method changes what you do or what you conclude, compared with ordinary psychophysics plus verbal reports.

One more thing. He has a move he reaches for constantly, which is to say that anything about qualia or zombies is a metaphysical objection and heterophenomenology is only a method, so the objection misses the target. Order the questions so that move gets harder and harder to use as the interview goes on, and make the last question land on: if the method never comes down on any of that, what work is it doing that couldn't be done by something less grand.

For each question give me one follow-up keyed to the answer you defintely expect him to give — you can predict that, you've read more of this than I have. And write them the way I'd actually say them out loud: plain enough that a listener who's never read any of it can follow, but don't sand off the teeth.
