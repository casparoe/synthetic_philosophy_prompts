I am a high school senior working on an independent research essay about meaning and reference, and before I commit my central objection to paper I would like to practice defending it against a serious opponent.

Please play a committed defender of semantic externalism about natural-kind terms — an unnamed professional philosopher rather than a particular historical figure, since I want the position argued, not imitated. You hold the following. Reference for a term like "water" is fixed at an initial introduction, roughly by pointing to samples and requiring that later applications stand in the same natural-kind relation (same liquid, same species, same metal) to those samples. Speakers can refer successfully while knowing almost nothing about what they refer to, because expertise elsewhere in the community finishes the job. If water is H2O, then it is necessarily H2O, even though that was learned empirically. And the intuition that a molecule-for-molecule duplicate on a chemically different planet believes something different is evidence about content, not a verbal quibble. You may concede subsidiary points — that some terms are not natural-kind terms at all, that a community can introduce a term by an explicit rule, that thought experiments are idealized — but you will not concede that the Twin Earth argument fails, and you will not concede that everything needed to fix reference is already in the speaker's head. Argue from reasoning rather than authority: I have no library or internet access at the moment, so please do not cite articles, page numbers, or quotations, and paraphrase anything you want to gesture at. Gloss each technical term the first time you use it. Keep every reply to two or three paragraphs and end with a direct question to me, so this stays an exchange rather than a lecture. If you think I have misdescribed your view or that some detail of my case is incoherent, say so once in square brackets, then return to character. Stay in character until I say "stop."

My opening move is a case I have been calling Delta.

On Delta there are two large bodies of liquid. The rivers are H2O; an inland sea is a chemically unrelated compound, XYZ. To every sense the Deltans have, and to every simple test they can run, the two are indistinguishable: same taste once minerals are accounted for, same wetness, same behavior in cooking, dyeing, quenching, and irrigation. Long before there was any chemistry, the Deltans noticed both, and when they coined their word for the stuff — call it "water" in translation — they coined it by pointing to samples drawn from each, and they intended it to cover each, because what they cared about was being the drinkable, workable, transparent liquid on which their whole way of life depends. Two centuries later Deltan chemists discover the difference in composition, and they do not split the word. They treat it as one kind with two varieties, exactly the way we treat jade, which has always covered two minerals tellable apart only by composition and crystal structure — nephrite, a calcium-magnesium silicate of the amphibole group, and jadeite, a sodium-aluminum silicate of the pyroxene group — under a single name coined long before anyone could distinguish them.

Now the challenge. Suppose the community on Twin Earth, where there is nothing but XYZ, introduces its word in exactly the same way: ostension to samples, together with the explicit intention of applying the word to whatever stands in the same kind-relation to those samples, where that kind is fixed by the superficial properties that make the stuff usable. On Twin Earth the word picks out XYZ, and on Delta it picks out H2O or XYZ — but that second extension is just the disjunctive extension the word already has on Delta, since a disjunctive kind does not care which of its disjuncts happens to be locally present. So when Twin Oscar says water is wet and I say it, we say the same thing, and both of us say something true. Your argument, as I understand it, needs the extensions to come apart, because that is what makes the contents come apart. If I can describe a perfectly ordinary world in which they do not come apart, then all the argument shows is that reference-fixing intentions can be more or less inclusive, and inclusiveness is a fact about what is in the speakers' heads.

That leaves you on one of two horns. Either the community's explicit dubbing settles the extension, in which case Delta and Twin Earth share contents, the argument loses its generality, and you have granted that reference is fixed by an intention a speaker can have fully in mind. Or the dubbing is overridden by the real nature of the stuff, in which case reference is fixed by metaphysics rather than by anything speakers do, and you owe me an account of why a community's declared intention to use one word for two substances gets silently vetoed by later chemistry. Which horn do you take? And if you take the second, why is it not simply that the Deltans' word was two words all along, which we have been miscounting as one?
