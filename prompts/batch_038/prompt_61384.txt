Acting as a philosopher of science who has spent an unhealthy amount of time reading ML interpretability papers — that's the role I want you in. Take your time with this, and feel free to brainstorm a few angles on scratch paper before you settle on an answer.

Here's the practice thats bugging me. Mechanistic interpretability people train a sparse autoencoder on top of a transformer's activations, then judge it with basically two numbers: how much of the model's loss you recover when you swap in the autoencoder's reconstruction, and how many of the autoencoder's latents fire on a typical token (they call this the L0). You crank the sparsity penalty up or down until you like where you've landed on that trade-off curve, and then you announce that this layer has on the order of tens of millions of features and start naming them — there's the Golden Gate Bridge one, there's a "make the model respond in French" one, there's one for deception in some benchmark setting. And the arbiter of whether a feature is genuine is causal: you inject the direction, clamp it, or ablate it, and if the model's behaviour moves the way you predicted, the feature gets treated as real rather than as a useful fiction.

So what metaphysics of representation is all this resting on? Some specific things I'd like you to work through:

- When someone reports one number for how many features a layer has, are they committed to there being a fact of the matter, independent of anyone's choices? The number slides around as you slide along the trade-off curve and nobody seems troubled by that. Is the curve tracking something in the model, or is it mainly reporting how much reconstruction error you were willing to pay for sparsity?
- Does the causal intervention step actually buy you realism, or is it perfectly compatible with a thoroughgoing instrumentalism — the patch is just another predictive test, and showing that a direction changes outputs doesn't get you from it works to it exists?
- The same field constantly says features live in superposition, that most directions are mixtures, that monosemanticity is something you have to manufacture with the autoencoder rather than something you found lying around. Is that consistent with the patching-is-proof norm, or does one of the two have to give way?
- Which rival positions does the practice quietly rule out — instrumentalism about ascriptions in the manner of the intentional stance, pluralism on which many decompositions are equally legitimate, eliminativism about features altogether? And is there a realism about representations that survives, for instance one on which features are genuinely real but not unique, like a coordinate system that's informative without being the only correct one?
- Finally: if the community adopted an avowedly instrumentalist stance, what would change on the ground? Would any result get retracted, any metric retired, any training run configured differently? If the answer is nothing much, say so — please don't manufacture a difference just to make the debate look substantive.

Throughout, keep separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, since I suspect there's a fair amount of underdetermination here. British English, please.
