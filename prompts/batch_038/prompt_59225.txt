Six weeks ago I took up a new post as responsible sourcing lead at a mid-sized British food manufacturer. My first substantial task is to write the group's first animal welfare policy, because the board has approved a line of products built on insect protein — mealworm larvae and house crickets, from two farms in the Netherlands and Belgium — to sit alongside the chicken, pork and farmed prawns we already buy. I have spent the last month going through the legal and assurance scaffolding on which such a policy is supposed to hang, and I have found that I cannot make sense of it. I should like help working out what it is committed to, before I commit my employer to it.

Here is what I have found.

The Animal Welfare Act 2006, which carries the welfare offences, the licensing regime and the codes of practice, defines an animal as a vertebrate other than man. It contains a power to extend that definition to invertebrates of any description, but that power may be exercised only if the national authority is satisfied, "on the basis of scientific evidence", that the animals concerned are "capable of experiencing pain or suffering". So far as I can establish, it has never been used for insects.

The Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 defines "animal" as any vertebrate other than homo sapiens, any cephalopod mollusc and any decapod crustacean; octopuses, lobsters and crabs were added after the government commissioned a scientific review of the evidence for their sentience. That Act imposes no duties on businesses like mine. It creates an Animal Sentience Committee, which may report on whether ministers have had "all due regard" to the ways in which policy might adversely affect the welfare of animals as sentient beings, requires the committee's recommendations to respect provisions and customs relating to religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage, and obliges the Secretary of State to lay a response before Parliament within three months.

Pesticide authorisation does assess effects on bees, but as pollinators: the endpoints concern whether colonies survive, develop and keep foraging, not how individual bees fare.

In my own supply chain, the prawn line requires third-party welfare assurance and there exist recognised recommendations on how crustaceans should be stunned and killed; the insect suppliers are subject to no welfare standard whatsoever, and they kill by chilling the insects until they stop moving and then freezing them — a method defended in the trade literature on the grounds that insects are cold-blooded.

Meanwhile the same state whose statute book this is appraises flood defences by multiplying the probability of an event by the damage it would do; expresses the acceptability of industrial hazards as an annual probability of death; and in its Treasury appraisal guidance insists that low-probability, high-impact risks be taken into account even where the probabilities cannot be estimated with any confidence.

What I cannot get past is the difference in shape. For nearly everything else the machinery is probabilistic: a small chance of a large harm counts as a reason to act, and we are prepared to spend money against risks we cannot quantify. For the question of who counts, the machinery is categorical: standing is switched on when an authority is satisfied on scientific evidence, and until that moment it is simply off, however large the numbers waiting on the far side of the switch. A single insect facility houses more individual animals than the entire population of every protected vertebrate in this country; the number of insects killed each year by agricultural insecticide is larger still, and no part of the framework registers it as a cost. If sentience is a matter of degree or of confidence rather than a fact to be established, the arithmetic looks as though it ought to dominate everything else in my policy — and yet there is nowhere to enter it.

So I should like a reconstruction of the implicit theory, as carefully as you can manage.

What view of moral standing does this practice presuppose — that standing is binary and either present or absent, that it attaches to phylogenetic position rather than to evidence assessed case by case, that it is the sort of thing that must be known before it can obligate? What view of evidence, and what view of how to act under moral uncertainty, does the "satisfied on the basis of scientific evidence" gate embody? Does the practice require that the probability of insect sentience be treated as zero, or can it be read as requiring something weaker — that obligations be grounded only in settled findings, whatever one's private credence?

Which rival positions does it quietly rule out: that sentience comes in degrees; that moral uncertainty should be handled by expected-value reasoning; that the precautionary principle applies to the infliction of harm as readily as to its prevention; that numbers themselves carry weight; that standing need not be established before it is respected? Please say which of these are excluded by the practice as it stands, and which are merely unfavoured by it.

Is the framework internally consistent? In particular: the probabilistic treatment of catastrophic risk alongside the categorical treatment of standing; the protection of bees as an ecosystem service alongside the protection of crustaceans as welfare subjects; the committee's duty to have due regard to sentience alongside its instruction to defer to cultural traditions; and, in my own document, a requirement of assurance for prawns with nothing at all for crickets. Do these parts presuppose the same theory of value, or two different ones operating under one roof?

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. I suspect that a good deal of it is philosophically underdetermined — explicable by tractability, by which animals people already care about, by the administrative convenience of a list, or by the political economy of a small committee reporting to a minister — and I want to know which features of the framework, if any, could only be licensed by a substantive theory of moral status, as against several equally good ones.

Finally: what would have to change if the threshold view were replaced by an explicitly probabilistic one? Take the definition of "animal" in the 2006 Act, the remit of the sentience committee, the endpoints of pesticide assessment, and my own supplier code and killing-method requirements in turn. And tell me honestly whether such a framework can be made workable — whether weighting by capacity, by intensity, or by confidence provides any principled stopping point, or whether the arithmetic, once admitted, simply swamps the rest of the policy and shows that the categorical gate was doing something other than tracking the truth.
