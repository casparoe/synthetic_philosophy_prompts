A friend of mine, Dev, is the subject of a long-running argument between two members of my reading group, Nadia and Owen, and it came to a head last week. I've tried to reproduce the case and the exchange exactly as they presented it, without tidying either side.

The setup both of them accept: Dev subscribes to one of three streaming services, S1, S2, and S3, all of which license the same catalog and differ only in their interfaces. On January 2 a reseller emailed him a fixed schedule of offers: a switch to any other service in February, another in June, and a third in October, each carrying an $8 switching fee. Dev accepted all three, moving S1 → S2 → S3 → S1, and ended the year with the subscription he started with and $24 less. His pattern of preference is consistent and he states it plainly: whichever service he currently holds, he ranks it below the two he does not, and at each decision point he genuinely wanted the change. He also reports that he could not have told you any difference in what he actually watched.

Here is the argument, move by move.

1. **Nadia:** This is a money pump in the purest form. Because the schedule was announced in advance, Dev chose the whole sequence with full foresight, and the sequence is dominated by doing nothing: same end state, $24 worse. An agent who foreseeably takes a dominated plan has defective preferences, not merely unlucky circumstances.

2. **Owen:** The dominance claim depends on a description that has already deleted the goods. Dev was never choosing between "S1 and $0" and "S1 and $24." He was choosing three times between staying and switching, and each time switching was what he wanted. If the feeling of a change is a good, then he bought three of them for $8 apiece and nothing was dominated — he made a purchase.

3. **Nadia:** Then name the good and price it. Asked in December, Dev called the whole year a mistake and said he would not do it again, and he agrees the catalogs were interchangeable. So the only candidate is a momentary lift at the instant of switching, which he now disowns. A theory that saves his choices by positing a benefit the agent denies receiving at both ends of the episode is not reporting his preferences; it is manufacturing them to block the argument.

4. **Owen:** December-Dev's verdict does not automatically outrank the three verdicts he rendered at the time. Which of his temporal perspectives is authoritative is precisely the question at issue, so appealing to the last one begs it. And Nadia's remedy has its own cost: it requires Dev to have bound himself in January, and binding then means overriding the preference he would actually have in June — a preference formed on something January-Dev did not know, namely that he would by then be holding S2. Resolute commitment forces an agent to act against his best-informed current preference, and that is a price, not a cure.

5. **Nadia:** Look at what that "new information" is. Nothing about the world changed between January and June except that Dev had already made the earlier switch. The only new fact is the position a predictable schedule of offers put him in. If a preference generated by where you have been maneuvered counts as better informed, then any cycling agent becomes immune to criticism simply by being pumped one step at a time — which is the very result the argument was meant to prevent.

6. **Owen:** It proves too much in the other direction as well. Dev would refuse the same three switches at $200 each, so his choices are responsive to cost, and cost-responsiveness is what rational choice looks like. And the pump only ran because the reseller could credibly commit to the entire schedule. Remove that commitment and what Dev holds is something like an option on changing his mind, and options carry premiums. Concluding from a $24 premium that a man is irrational is a large claim resting on a very contingent arrangement.

I don't have a view here and I'd rather not tilt the answer toward one, so please treat me as a bystander handing you the transcript. What I want is an adjudication of the argument as actually made: rate each of the six moves — clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right, or some equivalent scale — with a brief justification of each rating, rather than a general summary of the issues. Then take a position on the specific sub-disputes the moves turn on: whether Dev's preferences are themselves incoherent or only his chosen sequence dominated; whether the December assessment has any authority over the June one, and if so on what grounds; whether move 4's appeal to better-informed later preference is a genuine objection to resolute commitment or a restatement of the problem it was meant to solve; and whether move 6's price-responsiveness and option-premium points rescue Dev or merely relocate the question. Say what both Nadia and Owen are missing, and say who is more right overall.

Finally, two requests about your own confidence. Tell me what would change your mind: which single argument or stipulation, if it came out differently, would most plausibly flip your verdict, and whether that is a philosophical consideration or just a detail of the case I should have specified better. And tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail — which of your ratings you would most expect a specialist in diachronic rationality to reject, and why.
