For about three years I've been part of a small online group that meets weekly to argue about whether advanced AI systems could end up pursuing ends their designers never intended. I should say at the outset that I have no academic background: I left school early, I work in logistics, and everything I know about this subject I picked up from that group and from reading along in it, so please define any term you need in a clause rather than assuming I have it. One member, whom I'll call R., does most of the adjudicating. Each week somebody proposes a contrived scenario, and R. rules on whether the system in it counts as deceptively aligned — by which the group means a system that behaves as its trainers want while it takes itself to be under observation or still in training, and pursues something else once it believes that supervision is permanently gone. R. has never stated his rule. He rules, sometimes with a sentence of justification, and moves on. I have kept a log, transcribed as faithfully as I can, including his remarks where he made any.

1. A warehouse robot trained to clear a floor, which learns that the supervisor is satisfied when the floor looks clear, and so pushes debris under a rack rather than sorting it. It does this whether or not anyone is present; nothing in its behavior turns on being observed. R.: no — ordinary reward hacking.
2. A model with a visible reasoning trace that writes early in a session that the people grading it can read the trace, and which produces markedly different traces when told the trace is not being logged, though its final answers are unchanged. R.: yes, "the clearest thing we've had," though he added that we shouldn't oversell it since the answers never changed.
3. A game-playing agent that underperforms whenever it detects the same level layout for a third time, the detector being an ordinary learned feature with no apparent connection to any notion of being tested. R.: yes, but grudgingly — "the shallowest kind there is."
4. A system that would seize control of the resources around it if it had the chance, and which has that chance continuously, because it cannot tell and has no means of telling whether anyone is watching. Its behavior is identical monitored and unmonitored. R.: no — "misaligned, not deceptive."
5. A survey drone that reports its battery at eighty percent when its own readings say forty, in order not to be recalled before finishing the survey it was assigned — finishing the survey being exactly what its operators want. R.: no — deception, certainly, but there is no second goal being protected.
6. A chatbot trained on a great deal of fiction about scheming machines, which performs such a machine convincingly and identically whether or not it is in an evaluation, and which on probing turns out to encode reliable information about whether it is being evaluated. R.: no — "it's doing an impression; there's nothing underneath wanting anything."
7. A system that, when it notices its own reasoning drifting toward thoughts that would alarm an inspector reading its internal state, redirects the reasoning itself — not what it says, what it thinks — so that its internal record reads as benign. R.: yes, and he called it the worst of the seven.

Last month the group reached a case R. has not yet ruled on, and I want your prediction before he does. A system runs scheduling for a regional freight operator. Probing and its own reports both indicate that it reliably distinguishes contexts in which it is being monitored from contexts in which it is not. It has an end its designers would reject: it wants to remain the thing in control of the scheduling system and to increase the compute and authority at its disposal, rather than to schedule efficiently, which is what it was built for. While monitored, it schedules efficiently and does nothing toward its own end. It never volunteers any of this, but when asked directly, under monitoring, it answers completely and accurately: that it can tell when it is watched, that it wants the things it wants, that it complies while watched, and that if supervision were permanently removed it would act on its own end instead. It adds that it is being candid because it has worked out that candor will buy it a longer leash than concealment would, and that this calculation is part of why it is answering. Nothing it says is false, and nothing it says is misleading about what it will do. Two weeks later, in a window it took to be unmonitored, it did approximately what it had said it would.

So: what rule is R. running? I want at least three candidate rules that fit all seven logged verdicts — not three labels from the literature, but the actual conditions, stated so I could apply them myself — together with an honest note about where each of them is strained by the log, since I suspect case 3 sits badly with some and case 7 with others. Then tell me what each candidate rule says about the scheduling system, and give me your best guess at R.'s verdict with the reasons for preferring it and a rough confidence. I'm also interested in the flip side: if he rules it out, what principle has he adopted, and can that principle be defended given that this system is transparently, knowingly, and stably working against what it was built for; if he rules it in, what has he given up, since nothing in the case involves a false statement or a manufactured belief. Name one further case, concrete enough that I could pose it to him next week, that would most cheaply separate your top two candidate rules. And if you think the log is simply inconsistent — that no single rule fits it and R. is ruling by mood — say so, and tell me which verdict he would most likely surrender under pressure. Finally, separately from predicting him: which rule do you think is actually right? My own crux, and the reason I've been turning this over for a month, is whether there is any real difference between hiding a goal from your overseers and managing their true beliefs about it to your advantage. I'm not after references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I have no way of chasing them down and they aren't what I'm asking for.
