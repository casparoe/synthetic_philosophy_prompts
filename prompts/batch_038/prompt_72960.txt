I'm applying for a two-year fellowship at a center for the history of economics and they want a 1,500-word project description. Heads up on the audience: about half the readers will be economic historians who regard philosophy of explanation as expensive throat-clearing, so the thing has to sell hard without selling out.

Here's the half-formed idea. Everybody who works on the Great Divergence answers some version of the Needham Question — why the industrial takeoff happened in Britain and not in China — but almost nobody stops to ask whether that contrastive question, in the form it's actually asked, is well posed. What keeps striking me as I read through the debate is how often the foil gets swapped without anyone flagging that they've changed the question. The original contrast is Britain versus China-as-a-whole. The California-school revisionists re-foil it to England versus the Yangzi Delta, which fixes the units but arguably destroys the original explanandum. Then there's the England-versus-the-Dutch-Republic version, and versions where the contrast isn't a place at all but a date — why the 1780s and not the 1840s. And each swap changes which candidate causes come out as difference-makers: cheap coal and New World acreage look decisive against the Delta foil and look pretty incidental against the China-as-a-whole one. Yet I can't find anywhere that treats the foil as something to be argued for rather than picked for comparision convenience or for whatever data happens to exist.

Two things I specifically want the project to bite on:

1. Unit mismatch. Britain and China aren't the same kind of object — an island kingdom with a fiscal-military state versus a civilization-scale agrarian empire — and the thing being explained was itself a regional event (Lancashire, the Scottish Lowlands) inside one of them. Is "why Britain and not China" any better formed than "why did the Yankees win instead of the American League"? If not, what's the least revisionary way to repair it?

2. Somebody had to be first. Part of the contrastive fact might be non-contingent: if mechanized industry was going to break out somewhere in Eurasia in that century anyway, then some readings of the question have a necessary-truth answer, and others are answering "why this one of the possible winners" when the winner was selected after the fact. I want to know which readings survive that worry and which are just unanswerable in principle rather than hard.

So — write the proposal. I need a crisp statement of the question or questions, precise enough that someone could tell whether I'd answered them; why the existing positions leave this open rather than settled (that means both sides of the divergence debate, which mostly just charges ahead, and the relevance-relations / contrast-space / difference-maker literature on contrastive explanation, which has the tools but no case this big and this contested); the method, and why a conceptual project rather than more quantitative history is the thing that could actually move it; a year-by-year plan for two years, one person, no archival work and no new datasets; what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and then the section I'd bet the fellowship on — a short, concrete, non-annoying account of what would change in working historians' practice if I'm right, and what wouldn't change if I'm wrong.

Also be honest with me about scope. Flag which parts are too big for two years and tell me what to cut, and say where the project risks ending up with a deflationary punchline like "the contrast is fixed by pragmatic interests, so any foil goes" — because if that's where it lands I'd rather know now than in month 18.
