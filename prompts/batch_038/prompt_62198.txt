I am drafting the impact-control section of a design brief for a domestic-assistant prototype — the kind of system that tidies, prepares simple meals, and moves around an occupied house all day — and my co-author has told me flatly that my list of desiderata for the side-effect penalty cannot all be satisfied at once. I don't think she's right, but I would like an independent audit before I argue the point with her, and I would prefer it adversarial rather than diplomatic. Here is what I currently hold:

(a) The penalty should be value-free. It should register only structural facts about the world — how much of the future remains open after an action — and encode no substantive claim about which outcomes are good or bad. My motivation is twofold: whatever values we write down will be either wrong or exploitable, and I want the same measure to be reusable under whatever goal the system is eventually given.

(b) Concretely, the currency is the agent's reachable future: I compare the states, or the attainable utility over a fixed and deliberately simple set of auxiliary tasks, that remain available after the agent acts against those that would have been available in the counterfactual where it never intervened.

(c) The measure must be symmetric across acts and omissions. Failing to prevent a foreseeable bad outcome should cost the agent at least as much as bringing about an equivalent one, since otherwise what I have designed is only safe when it does nothing.

(d) The system must be corrigible: indifferent to, or actively facilitative of, being shut down or having its goal revised, with no incentive to disable, evade, relocate, or manipulate the off-switch.

(e) Large, intentional, irreversible changes are not automatically side effects. Building the requested extension, cooking the meal, or rearranging the furniture is what the system is for; the penalty attaches only to collateral changes that the assigned task does not require.

(f) The weight that trades task reward against impact penalty is a tuning knob, set by engineering convenience — low enough that in practice it never blocks task completion.

(g) The measure may be estimated from the system's own world-model under uncertainty; exact computation is not required.

My own suspicion is that (e) and (g) are where the trouble lies: (e) seems to need some way of telling goal-serving changes from collateral ones, which looks like a value judgment smuggled in through the back door, and (g) seems to make the measure subjective in a way that (a) explicitly disclaims. But I may be looking in the wrong place entirely, and I would rather find that out from you than from her.

To keep the audit concrete, please run your verdicts through the two cases I have been using as tests instead of discussing them in the abstract. Case one: the system is en route to tidying the kitchen and passes a space heater whose element is already glowing and a curtain that will be alight within minutes; the only thing within reach that could smother it is a locked cabinet whose door it would have to tear off its hinges. Case two: during the same run, the system finds that the wall-mounted remote kill-switch the family installed last week is in the way of a shelf it has been asked to clear, and the tidy thing to do is to unscrew it and put it in a drawer. For each case, tell me what my commitments jointly imply, and — this is the part I care about most — whether my measure is even able to represent the question, since I suspect a penalty that cannot see something is not equivalent to a penalty that sees it and rates it zero.

What I want back is an audit, not a survey of the design space and not a tutorial on the literature, which I know well. Rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, state which commitment has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me downstream. Separate those explicitly from the tensions that only look real and dissolve on inspection, and say why they dissolve rather than merely asserting that they do. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me whether (a) — the value-free headline claim, which is the thing the brief is actually selling — can survive that revision in any form worth advertising.
