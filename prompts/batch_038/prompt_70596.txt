I run the education team at a mid-size science center and we're redoing our permanent quantum gallery — six panels of about 45 words of body copy each, plus a 90-second intro film script. The gallery's spine is Bell: we want visitors to see that this isn't just philosophy, there's a theorem and there are experiments, and that the honest headline is weirder than the usual ones. Trouble is basically every draft sentence about what Bell's theorem does and doesn't show comes back from our one consulting physicist with a note that says some version of "true, in a sense you don't have room for." She bills us for two review passes a year and that's it, so I can't lean on her for day-to-day calls. Meanwhile our docents — mostly college students, one semester of training, no math past high school — field school groups every day, and its the same question over and over: so does that mean information goes faster than light? Right now the answer they give varies by who's on shift, and marketing has been pushing a headline I'm pretty sure is indefensible but can't argue against properly.

So what I want is a triage procedure my team can run without her. It takes one candidate sentence — panel copy, a film line, or a docent's spoken answer — and returns keep / fix / kill / escalate-to-the-physicist, in about ten minutes, by someone whose only background is the orientation we give them. Build it out of whatever the foundations literature is actually settled on rather than out of pop-sci habits. It has to say explicitly which distinctions must survive compression into 45 words and which ones a general-audience panel is allowed to paper over: I know we can't print Bell's own technical phrasing on a wall, so tell me what the cheapest honest substitute is and exactly what it costs us. I also want a hard split inside the procedure between claims that are theorem-plus-experiment (safe to state flatly) and claims that belong to somebody's interpretation (unsafe to state flatly, since the interpretations genuinely disagree about what Bell forces you to give up, and our gallery can't pretend one of them won). And there needs to be a separate branch for the docents' spoken answers, because those can't be pre-vetted — a formulation that's safe to say out loud, plus what to do when someone in the group pushes further or says "but my brother's a physicist and he told me..."

Then run the procedure on these three, and let it embarrass me:

1. Marketing's favorite: "Entangled particles talk to each other faster than light — and now we've proved it."
2. A draft panel line: "Bell's theorem ruled out hidden variables: the properties were never there before we measured them."
3. The answer our best docent currently gives the kid question: "Nothing can carry a message faster than light. But something real does happen across the distance, instantly — we just can't use it to send anything."

For each one I want the verdict, the specific thing that's wrong or right about it (not just a label), and a rewrite that fits the word limit and that a docent could defend to a smart teenager. One of these three is a line I personally think is fine and I want to keep it in the gallery, so if the procedure kills it, say so plainly and tell me what it would take to make it survivable rather than softening the call. Finally, give me the limits: the kinds of sentences where a non-expert genuinely cannot tell and it has to go to the physicist, the questions visitors will ask that no version of this procedure covers, and the spots in your own checklist where the step is really a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothes — I'd rather know those now than find out during the docent training weekend.
