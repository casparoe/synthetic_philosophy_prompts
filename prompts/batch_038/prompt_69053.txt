My friend does Roman history and she's always telling the rest of us that we're citing the wrong part of the fall. Not Sulla at the gates, not Caesar at the river — the army. Her version goes: once Marius opened the legions to men who owned nothing, recruits had no farm to go back to and no stake in what the Senate decided, so they looked to their commander for the land grants at discharge; the legions turned into personal clienteles; and after that every constitutional device — tribunes, vetoes, the annual magistracies — was just furniture. So her punchline is that the Gracchi, Sulla and Caesar are all downstream of one economic change, and that anyone proposing institutional fixes today (norms, term limits, tighter rules on emergency powers) is rearranging the furniture too, because the real lever is making a soldier's post-service livelihood depend on the state rather than on his commanding officer. She wheels this out in basically every arguement we have, including one last week where she said a large professional volunteer force drawn from the bottom of the income distribution is structurally the same thing as the post-Marian legion. Audit it for me: what maps onto what (the propertyless recruit, the general's land grant, the Senate's refusal to fund it, the tribunes), and where does the mapping snap? What is she quietly importing from Rome that modern states just don't have? And does the conclusion about institutional fixes survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If it doesn't hold, what would be a better source domain for the same worry?
