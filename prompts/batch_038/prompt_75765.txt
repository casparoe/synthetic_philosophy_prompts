I am preparing a graduate seminar on scientific realism and its discontents, and I should be grateful for your assistance with an argument that one of my doctoral students and I have been developing together — or, rather, with an argument that has developed in a direction neither of us intended. For what follows, please regard yourself as a professional philosopher with specialist expertise in the theory of verisimilitude and in formal epistemology generally: someone entirely at home with consequence sets, content measures and possible-world semantics, and willing to work slowly and carefully through a technical example rather than reaching for a stock response.

The setting is a toy language containing two independent atomic questions about an exoplanet — whether it has an atmosphere (a), and whether it has liquid water (w) — and let us suppose, for the sake of the example, that the whole truth about the matter is a ∧ w.

(1) Realism about an inquiry holds that truth is its aim; and a fallibilist realist, who concedes that our theories are false, can regard the history of that inquiry as progressive only if some false theories are closer to the truth than the theories they replaced.
(2) Closeness to the truth is a matter of consequences and of nothing else, since a proposition is exhausted by what follows from it. So h′ is at least as close to the truth as h just in case every true consequence of h is a consequence of h′, and every false consequence of h′ is a consequence of h; and h′ is strictly closer just in case at least one of these two inclusions is proper. This is in effect Popper's original definition.
(3) Almost all of our best scientific theories are false.
(4) Yet on the definition in (2) no false theory is ever strictly closer to the truth than another false theory. The point is not merely formal; it can be seen at once in the toy language. Let h1 be "there is neither an atmosphere nor liquid water", and h2 be "there is an atmosphere but no liquid water". Plainly h2 is the better theory: it gets one of the two questions right, and h1 gets neither right. Nevertheless h1 entails a truth that h2 does not — that there is liquid water if and only if there is an atmosphere — while h2 entails a truth that h1 does not, namely that there is an atmosphere; and symmetrically, h1 entails a falsehood that h2 does not (that there is no atmosphere), while h2 entails a falsehood that h1 does not (that there is an atmosphere if and only if there is no liquid water). Each theory therefore fails both clauses of (2) with respect to the other, and the definition declines to rank them. That this is general, and not an artefact of my example — that the definition cannot order false theories at all — was established in 1974 in three short papers appearing in the same issue of the British Journal for the Philosophy of Science, and Popper conceded the point immediately.
(5) The natural repair is to restrict the comparison: to rank theories not by all their consequences but only by those consequences that bear on the questions the theories are addressed to, or only by their conjunctive parts once the theories are put into some privileged normal form. But such a restriction renders closeness to the truth relative to a choice of problem-set and of vocabulary, whereas realism requires an objective relation obtaining between theories whether or not anyone has carved up the questions in the manner that makes the comparison come out right; and in so far as the restriction is tuned to reproduce our intuitions in just such cases as h1 and h2, it is ad hoc.
(6) The alternative repair is to abandon consequences altogether and to measure the similarity between the worlds a theory permits and the actual world. Such metrics, however, are gerrymanderable: for any two theories whatever, and any verdict one might wish to reach, a metric can be devised that delivers it. A relation whose every verdict can be manufactured at will is not an objective relation.
(7) So there is no objective relation of closeness to the truth that applies to false theories: on the only account that takes consequences seriously it is empty, by (4); on relevance-restricted accounts it is interest-relative or ad hoc, by (5); and on likeness accounts it is indeterminate, by (6).
(8) Hence (1) and (3) cannot both be maintained. Either truth is not the aim of scientific inquiry, or it is not an aim we can be said to approach; and the realist must retreat into instrumentalism, or into a purely negative falsificationism that speaks of eliminating error but never of getting nearer the truth.

I cannot accept (8). I have spent most of my working life defending a broadly realist philosophy of science, and (8) is the conclusion I should most dislike swallowing; yet I have been round the argument several times with my student and neither of us can find the exit. Each step looks defensible when considered on its own, and (4) in particular is not a matter of philosophical taste but a proved theorem, which makes it awkward simply to declare the argument unsound and move on. My own hunch, such as it is, is that the trouble lies somewhere in the passage from (4) to (7); my student, who is more radical than I am, thinks the rot sets in already at (2), and that a definition demanding unanimity across the whole of a theory's consequence set was never a candidate for capturing anything we care about. I am not in a position to adjudicate between us, and I do not wish to prejudge the matter for you.

I should like a careful diagnosis, and I should like you to think the problem through rather than to answer quickly. Begin with a short brainstorming passage, before you commit to anything, listing the steps at which the argument might plausibly break — including steps you ultimately judge innocent — and then work through the argument in order. Tell me whether you take it to be valid with a false premise or invalid with a bad inference; identify the step or steps you reject; explain why each of them looked unobjectionable in the first place; and price the rejection explicitly and comparatively. What does one pay for rejecting (2), for resisting the necessity claim embedded in (5), for resisting (6), for denying that (8) follows from (7), and for biting the bullet and accepting (8) after all? Among other things, please address whether (4) tells us anything about the intuitive notion of closeness to the truth or only about one explication of it; whether the generalisation from "this definition collapses" to "no such definition can work" is licensed; whether the trilemma in (7) is exhaustive; and whether a realist can keep (1) and (3) while conceding that no global ordering of theories by truthlikeness is available — by relying on local, case-by-case comparisons, say, or by explicating progress in some quite different register. Check my toy example as you go, and tell me if you think I have mis-stated it. Do not close on a non-committal note: say which single move you would make in my position, and which of my commitments you think I ought to sacrifice. You may describe the families of response (consequence-based, relevance-restricted, likeness-based, and so on) in your own terms, but please do not attempt a bibliography, a reference list or page citations; I am interested in the reasoning rather than the scholarship. Kindly write your answer in American English.
