Our family foundation's endowment is roughly $1.9 billion, and I serve as vice-chair of the investment committee, which has to rule on an unusual conveyance before its meeting on the 14th. A donor-advised fund has offered to transfer to the endowment, at no cost and with no premium, a structured note whose payoff is fixed by a coin-toss protocol administered each year by an independent auditor using a published random-bit source. The note terminates on the first anniversary n on which the coin lands heads; if n is odd the counterparty bank pays the foundation 2^n/n units, and if n is even the foundation pays the bank 2^n/n units, where one unit is one million dollars. So the first anniversary pays us $2 million, the second costs us $2 million, the third pays $2.67 million, the fourth costs $4 million, the eighth costs $32 million, and the schedule continues in both directions with no cap. Our general counsel has separately raised a collateral question about how an uncapped, undated contingent liability would be carried on the books, but the committee has asked me to circulate a memo on the valuation and policy issues first, since several trustees now say they will vote no on principle and I would rather they vote on reasons.

The committee is split three ways, and the split is not along the usual lines. Our head of quantitative research has produced a two-page valuation: written in anniversary order, the probability-weighted payoffs give 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + … , which converges to ln 2, so the note is worth about $693,000 to us, our investment policy statement directs the committee to allocate so as to maximize expected long-run value without qualification, and therefore we are obliged to accept — indeed, he argues, obliged to accept every instrument of this shape we are ever offered. A mathematician who holds one of the rotating academic seats replies that the valuation is not well-defined at all: the series converges only conditionally, so listing the very same outcomes in a different order makes it converge to whatever number one pleases, or diverge outright, and nothing in the term sheet privileges anniversary order over any other. A third member, a trustee who made his money in trading, says both are fussing over nothing — this is just the St. Petersburg lottery with a sign attached, we passed on something similar in 2019, and the real point is that our reserves are finite while the liability is not: there is a one-in-32,768 chance the note survives to its sixteenth anniversary or beyond, at which point we would owe $4.1 billion at n = 16 and $14.6 billion at n = 18, each far more than the endowment.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee, about 900 to 1,200 words, written for trustees without mathematics but not condescending toward the quant. It should do four things. First, state a clear recommendation — accept, decline, or accept only on renegotiated terms you specify — together with the concrete steps and the deadline that follow from it. Second, explain why the quarrel between the quant and the mathematician is not a technicality that can be waved away, and say plainly what the committee should conclude about whether this note has an expected value, including what is and is not different about it compared with the 2019 offer the trader has in mind. Third, propose replacement language for the policy statement's expected-value clause, so that a future offer of this kind can be handled without reopening the entire question; the language should make explicit whether the foundation treats the value of additional money as bounded or unbounded, and should state what follows from that commitment for instruments whose payoffs are unbounded in either direction. Fourth, give the strongest honest case for the recommendation you are not making — in particular, if you advise declining, address the worry that a foundation pledged to maximize expected value cannot refuse a free instrument with a positive expected return without abandoning its own stated principle, and that whatever reasoning defeats this note would also oblige us to divest a good many ordinary holdings we currently consider unremarkable.
