ok, six weeks ago I got moved off modeling onto post-market surveillance at a mid-size device company — infusion pumps, class II, the unglamorous kind. My actual job is watching adverse event reports and deciding what gets escalated.

Here's what's eating me. My director has a line he uses in every review, and it's now the load-bearing wall under a proposal to cut complaint-intake headcount by a third. The line is: a week with no reports is the same kind of evidence as checking another room of the house for your keys and not finding them. Each empty room narrows down where the keys could be, so each clean week narrows down where the problem could be — the silence is doing work, it's accumulating, and eventually you don't need as many people listening. He's not being stupid on purpose, he's a smart guy, and every time I push back I come out sounding like I'm just defending my team's budget.

I took a philosophy of science class years ago and the one thing I still remember clearly is the raven thing — you want to confirm all ravens are black, and a white shoe technically counts as evidence for it, because a white shoe is a non-black non-raven and the hypothesis is equivalent to everything non-black being a non-raven. I have this strong feeling that's exactly the trap he's walked into, but I cant make the argument tight enough to survive a room full of people with spreadsheets.

So: audit the keys analogy for me, both directions.

First the correspondence table. What's playing the keys, the house, the rooms, the act of looking, the act of finding? Then which of those mappings actually hold up in a passive reporting system and which ones are quietly doing work they havent earned. Specifically:

- is his each-empty-room-narrows-it-down move the same move as each-white-shoe-confirms-all-ravens-are-black, or are they different moves wearing the same coat? where exactly do they come apart?
- I vaguely remember the defusing reply being that sure, the shoe confirms, but the amount of confirmation scales with how big the class you're sampling from is — a shoe is negligible because non-ravens are basically everything, whereas an empty room matters because there are only so many rooms. If that's the right shape of reply, does it actually rescue him? We have something like four million units in the field and maybe 200 reports a week. Is our inbox the room or is it the shoe?
- there's also some rejoinder — I think it involves background knowledge, maybe two possible worlds — to the effect that seeing your first black raven can actually count slightly AGAINST all ravens being black. Does anything like that have an analog on my side? Like, does the first clean week behave differently from the fortieth, and does the direction of the evidence ever flip once you know things about how these devices fail and how people report?
- what would have to be true of our intake pipeline for the keys analogy to actually hold? I keep circling the difference between going and looking versus sitting and receiving — under-reporting, the fact that a report only exists if someone decided to file it, the fact that some failures are invisible by design. Does that break the analogy or just weaken it, and how would I tell which?

And then the part I actually need: tell me what survives. I suspect there's a defensible version of his claim hiding in there — something like silence from a targeted, high-coverage, actively-searched system is not the same sentence as silence from ours — and if so I want it stated cleanly, because I'd rather hand him the correct version of his own argument than win a cheap one. Give me the strongest honest case that he's right and I'm wrong, too. If that's the verdict, say it plainly; I'd much rather find out from you than from an auditor.

Don't bother with references or a reading list, I have no way to look anything up at work anyway — I just want the reasoning laid out where I can check it myself.
