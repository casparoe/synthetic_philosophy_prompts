I am Group Head of Internal Audit at a mid-sized industrial company with operations in Western Europe, the Gulf and South-East Asia. I am entirely fluent in the vocabulary of my profession — control environments and the components of internal control, entity-level versus process-level controls, segregation of duties, related-party transactions, materiality, design effectiveness as against operating effectiveness, tone at the top, the three lines of defence, the audit committee's appetite for residual risk — and, honestly, I think almost exclusively in it.

Our group code of conduct takes the standard line: corruption is the abuse of an entrusted public or corporate role for private gain; gifts from suppliers above a modest monetary threshold must be declared or refused; cash and cash equivalents are prohibited outright; hospitality must be proportionate, infrequent and entered in a gifts and hospitality register. Our country teams push back, and their pushback has a shape I recognise from the anthropological and political-science literature rather than from any audit manual. They say the policy is calibrated to a world in which the boundary between the public and the private sphere is sharply drawn, and in which a gift is a discrete transaction rather than the opening move in a relationship. They say that in their settings a refusal is itself a signal — sometimes an insult, sometimes the rupture of the relationship on which the business depends. And they say that what headquarters classifies as a bribe and what their counterparts understand as the discharge of an obligation of reciprocity are not one act described in two moral vocabularies, but two different social facts. Behind these objections sits, as I understand it, the literature on gift exchange and obligatory reciprocity, together with the criticism that the standard definition of corruption presupposes a public/private distinction that many societies have never drawn and that was in some places imposed by colonial administration rather than inherited — with the consequence that what presents itself as a universal standard is in fact a particular norm in universal clothing.

I want to test this properly rather than dismiss it as special pleading. So: restate the whole of that objection — the gift/bribe distinction, the claim about the cultural specificity of the public/private boundary, the claim that reciprocity constitutes a bond rather than purchases a decision, and the further claim that Western anti-corruption standards are therefore not universal — in the vocabulary of internal control and audit, as faithfully and as precisely as you can manage. Where the source material uses a term with no genuine counterpart in my framework, say so plainly rather than reaching for the nearest available word and letting the substitution pass unremarked.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. For each component of the objection, tell me: which claims translate cleanly, and what they become once translated; which translate only with distortion, and exactly where the distortion enters; and which cannot be expressed at all. Is there anything these critics are asserting for which my framework has no term, no category and no line item — something for which there is no form to fill in and no control objective to write it against?

Attend particularly to a suspicion I have been unable to resolve. It seems to me that a faithful translation may force me to treat an ongoing reciprocal relationship between a buyer and a supplier as a conflict of interest by definition, since that is precisely what my framework says such a relationship is. If so, the translation has smuggled in the assumption under dispute and demonstrated nothing. Tell me whether that is in fact what happens; and if it is, tell me which of two diagnoses is correct. Either the fault lies in the target framework — internal control is constitutionally incapable of representing a legitimate obligation of reciprocity, and would have to be extended, perhaps by admitting a category of disclosed and bounded relationship that is neither arm's-length nor corrupt — or else the objection, once one tries to state it without borrowing my categories, proves less determinate than its advocates suppose: an account of what acts mean that never quite reaches the point of saying which acts should be permitted, which decisions should be reviewable, by whom, and what the register should record.

Please write the whole answer in British English. Do not supply references, a bibliography or a reading list — I am not after sources and have no means of checking them. Work from the concepts themselves.
