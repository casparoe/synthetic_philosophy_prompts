I serve on my state's sentencing commission, which the legislature has directed to review the purposes clause of our sentencing code — the provision that tells judges what a sentence is *for*. The current clause ranks four purposes, and the first of them is that the sanction imposed should be proportionate both to the seriousness of the offense and to what the offender deserves for having committed it. Two competing amendments are on the agenda for next month's vote.

Draft A deletes the desert language outright. It restates the purposes of sentencing as protecting the public, rehabilitating the offender, and repairing the harm caused, and it provides that the length of a custodial term shall be set by an assessment of the offender's continuing risk and reviewed periodically by a panel, in no case exceeding the existing statutory maximum for the offense. Draft B leaves the desert language where it is and adds only a necessity constraint: no sentence may be more severe than is required to achieve the clause's remaining purposes.

The sponsors of the two drafts are, I think, re-enacting a philosophical dispute rather than a merely practical one. Draft B's defenders are closer to Daniel Dennett: the capacities we actually care about — recognizing reasons, anticipating consequences, getting a grip on one's own impulses — are real, gradable, and precisely what makes someone an apt subject of blame; holding people responsible both presupposes and cultivates those capacities, so a code that stops addressing offenders as answerable agents may dissolve the very competence it depends on. Draft A's defenders are closer to Gregg Caruso: if a person's conduct is the joint product of a genetic endowment, an upbringing, and a nervous system none of which they selected, then desert in the basic sense — suffering owed simply because of what one did — cannot be made out, and confinement is legitimate only on protective grounds, on something like the rationale for quarantining the carrier of a dangerous disease.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the commission beforehand, between 1,200 and 1,600 words. It must reach a clear bottom line — adopt Draft A, adopt Draft B, or adopt a modified third option whose operative language you spell out — and it must handle three points that have deadlocked us:

1. Asymmetry. Under Draft A, a person convicted of a low-level property offense but assessed as high-risk could be confined longer than a person convicted of a violent offense assessed as low-risk. The sponsor answers that the statutory maximum prevents this; opponents answer that a maximum caps a class rather than limiting within it, and that risk instruments cannot do the work proportionality was doing. Is there a principled internal limit available to a purely forward-looking rationale, or does Draft A merely exchange a desert ceiling for an actuarial one?

2. Retroactivity. Roughly 1,400 people are serving terms imposed under the current clause. Draft A bars any review that would lengthen a term; some of us suspect that bar is a concession to secure passage rather than a principle. Say what the commission should do about the petitioner whom the new criteria would entitle to more time.

3. Address. Victim representatives on the commission argue that if terms are set by scores, the process stops asking anything of the offender — no acknowledgment, apology, or restitution, only management — and that the documented demographic skew of these instruments will fall hardest on those the reform is meant to help.

Beyond this, the opinion should reconstruct both positions for commissioners with no philosophy, saying what each takes to be the *injustice* in the other's rationale rather than its imprudence; and it should be precise about two things that both camps get wrong in debate — that Dennett is not a desert-maximizer, and that Caruso's quarantine analogy is meant to import constraints (necessity, least restrictive means, restoration, release when the danger passes) rather than to license indefinite containment. Include at least three criteria a review panel could actually apply to an individual petition, a phased timetable running across the next two legislative sessions, and a candid statement of the strongest reason to reject your recommendation together with why it does not prevail. Argue the case out; do not defer to authority, and do not append a reading list.
