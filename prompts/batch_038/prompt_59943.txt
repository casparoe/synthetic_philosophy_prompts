I'm a process safety engineer at a mid-size chemical site — about forty of us, three shift crews, and I'm the only one whos job is the safety instrumented functions. Every month or two somebody brings me a protective device or a loop and asks the same question in different words: does this thing still actually do what it's supposed to do when the bad thing happens, or are we just collecting green ticks from tests that prove nothing? We've started calling the second one a 'paper disposition' around here, which is meant as a joke but its also the real problem.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run on any proposed protective function — relief valve, gas detector, triplicated shutdown loop, deluge system — that sorts out the different ways a device can look disposed to protect and not be. There seem to be at least three distinct failures: one where the triggering event itself destroys whatever it was that made the device able to respond; one where the device is still perfectly able to respond but something sitting between it and the event blocks the response; and one where the device does respond, but for reasons that have nothing to do with any capability it actually has. I vaguely remember from a class years ago that there are names for the first two, something about finking and masking, but don't hold me to that. Give me the names and plain-language definitions a controls tech will follow, and tell me which of these distinctions I can safely collapse for field use and which one I absolutely cannot, because collapsing it is how people get hurt.

Then the procedure: questions in order, what a passing answer looks like for each, where it stops with 'no, this does not protect you', and where it hands off to a human because the remaining question is about how much risk we're willing to carry rather than a fact about the hardware. Ten to fifteen minutes per device, no philosophy background assumed, site manager signs off at the end.

Run it on these three, taking my descriptions as given:
1. Gas detectors in the compressor house, bump-tested weekly with a bottle of cal gas. They always alarm. The cal gas leaves a film that slowly coats the sensor, and a detector keeps passing its weekly test right up until the week it stops responding for good.
2. An emergency isolation valve stroked quarterly, with the control system automatically putting the loop in manual during the test so we don't trip the unit. Last spring one loop sat in manual for eleven days with four passing strokes in the log.
3. The new shutdown function — three sensors, two-out-of-three voting, cost a fortune, everybody loves it.

Be straight with me on #3. I expect the procedure to say something I don't want to hear, and if it genuinely doesn't, say why not instead of manufacturing a problem.

Last thing, and this is the part I can't work out myself. All those philosopher cases seem to be about one object and its own intrinsic properties — this glass, this wire. But a loop's ability to protect isn't located in any component: it's spread across the sensor, the logic, the final element, and what state the whole unit happens to be in at the time. So does the fink/mask distinction even apply to something like that, or do I need a fourth category, something like 'the device that would have saved us was fine but the plant wasn't in a condition where it could be reached'? Tell me how you'd build the answer into the procedure rather than arguing it in the abstract.

No references or reading list please — I'm not chasing down papers and neither are my techs.
