Been trying to build a case that the moral equality of combatants is actually true, and it's now proving way to much. Here's the argument:

1. Whether you may kill someone turns on whether they're liable to be killed, and liability turns on facts about the justice of their cause and their own responsibility for the threat they pose.
2. Individual soldiers can't know those facts. Which side has a just cause is contested at the top level of governments and by scholars decades later, and whether the guy downrange is a responsible threatener or a coerced conscript or a reservist who thinks his country is being invaded is invisible from a rifle scope.
3. You may do something only if you're in a position to know it's permitted — anything else is just gambling with someone's life.
4. So no soldier is ever in a position to know his targets are liable, and no soldier may permissibly kill.
5. Notice that this is symmetric: the prohibition falls identically on both sides, which is exactly what the moral equality of combatants claims. Both sides' soldiers stand in the same moral relation to each other. So the revisionists who say unjust combatants are all murderers owe us an explanation of why a standard nobody can apply in the fog of war is the right account of the deep morality.
6. But (3) applies just as much to ordinary self-defense. Someone grabs you in a parking garage and you cannot know whether he's culpable, psychotic, mistaken about who you are, or himself an innocent threat.
7. Killing in self-defense is sometimes clearly permissible.
8. So something's broken.

If I have to choose I'd keep (2) and (7). Where does it break, and what does each of the three moves — Walzer-style conventionalism, the revisionist liability account, and splitting the law of war off from the deep morality — have to pay to block it?
