I run the ethics slot of an in-house CPD day at a fertility unit — about fourteen people, mostly embryologists and fertility nurses, one consultant, and the GP who does their counselling referrals. Ninety minutes, at the end of the day, they'll be tired. No pre-reading and please don't hand me a reading list either, they wont read anything.

The session is nominally about procreative beneficence — the "should we be selecting the best embryo" question. But what I actually want them to leave understanding is the non-identity problem, because in my experience every objection this audience gives to procreative beneficence is the same objection in a different costume, and most of them either dissolve or at least change shape once you can see the structure underneath. So the real thing I need is a teaching plan for that idea, with procreative beneficence as the frame they think they signed up for.

Don't explain the non-identity problem to me, I know it. Plan how I explain it to *them*.

- **The order.** What has to be in place before the paradox can even register for them? I think the person-affecting restriction has to be surfaced and named before I can suspend it, but I don't know how to get them to see they're holding it without sounding like I'm accusing them of holding it. Give me the sequence, what depends on what, and roughly where the ninety minutes goes.
- **The one case that carries it.** Not a menu of three — pick one and tell me why. Say what it has to have (the alternative child never existing at all, a life that's plainly worth living) and what it has to leave out. And be careful here: these people will translate anything I say into embryo selection because that's their job, and "the embryo we don't transfer" reads to them as a death rather than a non-event. Either the case survives that translation intact, or the plan needs to name the translation problem at the moment it happens.
- **The misconceptions**, in the order they'll actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I get ahead of each one rather than fielding it afterwards. I'm expecting: "but the child is harmed by it"; "so you're saying we should have disabled children"; "then nothing we do to future people can be wrong"; "this is just eugenics with extra steps"; and the sneakiest one — "we're not doing anything *to* the embryo, so there's nothing that needs justifying." Tell me which of those I've got wrong and which ones I'm missing.
- **One question** near the end that would genuinely tell me whether it landed, plus what a right answer sounds like versus a fluent-but-wrong one.
- **What I should refuse to cover** at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing it.

Be committed: one case, one sequence, one closing question, and flag the single step in the plan your least confident about with this particular audience and what I do if it goes flat. And if you think my diagnosis is off — that the non-identity problem is the wrong thing to teach these people because it'll land as a word game and they'll switch off, or that it can't be separated cleanly from the embryo question in ninety minutes — say that plainly rather than building me a careful session on a bad premise.
