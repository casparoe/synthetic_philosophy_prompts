Spent 34 years in a renal physiology lab, and since I retired our regional hospital has had me sitting on its clinical ethics panel as the "science" member. It's got me hooked on the philosophy end of the work, but it's also made me realize I can't tell which principle the panel has actually been applying when it declares somebody dead. Here's the record as best I can reconstruct it from the minutes:

1. Ventilator patient, no brainstem reflexes, no spontaneous breath on two apnea tests 24 hours apart, no flow on the angio — declared dead. The written reason was that the body "had stopped holding itself together": pressors climbing, temperature drifting, pressure gone by day two.
2. Anencephalic newborn, breathing on her own, gagging, pupils reactive — not dead (withdrawal of support was handled as a separate question).
3. Man six years into a vegetative state, breathing unaided, brainstem fully working — not dead.
4. Donation-after-circulatory-death case: we required five minutes of no circulation with no autoresuscitation before declaring death, and turned down a proposal to cut it to two, on the grounds that at two minutes the heart can still restart itself so you can't call the loss irreversible.

New case. Twenty-six-year-old, motorcycle crash. Every test we run says the whole brain, brainstem included, is destroyed, so on precedent 1 she'd be dead today. Except she's 18 weeks pregnant, and the body is doing astonishingly well — on a ventilator and hormone replacement but off pressors, holding its own temperature, healing a surgical wound normally, cleared a line infection, gaining lean mass, fetus growing fine. Family wants support continued to delivery, roughly 17 more weeks, and someone has to say whether we'd be maintaining a dead woman or treating a living one.

What should we decide if we want to stay consistent with our own record? Which of the four is the closest analog? And if the record genuinely doesn't settle it, spell out what principle we'd be signing on to with each choice, plus one hypothetical case that would tell the candidate principles apart. I've deliberately kept my own leanings out of the write-up.
