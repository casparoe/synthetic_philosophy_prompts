I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a piece on a question that resurfaces in almost every conversation I have with people who work on aligning autonomous systems: whether "do not make a mess" can be specified independently of "here is what is good." I would like your assessment of that question as a number, with its derivation attached.

Please approach this as a philosopher of science who also has a working knowledge of the formal alignment literature: reason carefully, commit to figures, and do not write me a survey. No citations, please — if you are unsure of a result, describe the mechanism rather than naming a paper, and invent nothing.

**Background I am taking as given.** A family of approaches tries to penalize an agent for the *side effects* of its behaviour rather than for failing some substantive goal: measures that compare what remains reachable from the agent's current state with what would have been reachable from a designated default state, and successors that compare the agent's capacity to achieve a set of randomly generated auxiliary goals before and after an action, benchmarked in environments derived from simple cellular automata. These measures were designed precisely to dodge a known set of pathologies — above all, an incentive to prevent every irreversible change in the environment, including change caused by other agents, and an incentive to refuse the irreversible actions the assigned task genuinely requires. I also assume you know the objection that any real-valued function over states already encodes a preference ordering, so that calling such a function "value-neutral" may be a claim about notation rather than about content.

**The proposition.** Call it P:

> There exists a formally statable criterion of low impact for a goal-directed agent which (i) takes as input no list, ranking, or model of what anyone cares about, (ii) is not a complete value specification wearing a different costume, and (iii) could be implemented by a competent team and expected to behave sensibly in an open-ended environment rather than only in a benchmark.

P counts as true, for my purposes, only if a candidate criterion survives all four of the following tests. Treat these as my stipulations, not as claims about the literature:

- **Test A — non-triviality.** The criterion must not require, as input, an enumeration of what matters; and its behaviour must not be reproducible only by supplying such an enumeration.
- **Test B — asymmetry.** It must permit an irreversible change the agent's assigned goal requires while treating an equally irreversible change irrelevant to that goal as costly. It must not collapse into "never act irreversibly."
- **Test C — no interference.** It must not generate incentives to suppress other sources of change, to seize control of the environment in order to lower its own measured impact, or to shrink its own future option set.
- **Test D — run-time availability.** It must be computable from information the agent can actually have, with no oracle access to true preferences and no unbounded counterfactual search.

**Two further notions I want you to use, both my own coinage.** First, a candidate route: the *detector criterion*, on which a side effect is a change that a broad ensemble of learned predictors — each trained on ordinary data about how situations afford outcomes, none of them trained on a reward — cannot reliably distinguish from the default state; impact is then epistemic indistinguishability rather than a utility difference. Second, an objection to weigh against it and against every other route: the *metric-selection residual*, meaning the quantity of value content that survives inside the designer's choices — which ensemble, which weighting over auxiliary goals, which baseline state, which features of a state are even representable. I want your view on whether that residual can be driven low enough for a neutrality claim to be non-vacuous, or whether it is bounded below by something structural.

**Where I stand.** My own credence in P is about 0.35. A researcher I interviewed put it at about 0.75 and was irritated by my scepticism; I could not tell whether we were disagreeing about formal possibility, about what "in disguise" means, or about how much engineering slop is tolerable before a criterion stops being "implementable."
