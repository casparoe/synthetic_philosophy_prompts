Until last month I was a software engineer, and I have now taken a research position on a small team that evaluates the behaviour of large language models. The team is about to publish a position paper that I have been listed on as a co-author, and the framing section, written by our director, opens with the following paragraph, which he says he has paraphrased from a trade book on machine intelligence:

"Darwin and Turing performed the same manoeuvre: a strange inversion of reasoning in which an effect that appears to demand a mind behind it is shown to require no such mind. Darwin demonstrated that the appearance of design can be produced without a designer; the eye is the product of a process with no purposes of its own, and the living world as a whole exhibits every mark of intelligence with no intelligence behind it. Turing made the parallel move about meaning. His machine manipulates symbols by rules it follows with no understanding whatever, and his celebrated test was constructed to establish that this is all there could ever be to it — that a system can display every behavioural mark of understanding while understanding nothing. This is the condition now described as competence without comprehension: an agent can be fully competent at a task while comprehending nothing whatever about it. It follows that the ambition of building machines that genuinely understand is not merely difficult but incoherent, and that Searle's Chinese Room argument, whatever Searle supposed it established, in fact settles the question against him: the man in the room understands nothing, comprehension makes no difference to what comes out of the room, and so comprehension cannot be doing any work."

I have had one undergraduate course in philosophy of mind and no more, so I cannot tell how much of this I should be comfortable signing. My instinct is that the spirit is defensible and that at least one of the steps does not follow, but I would very much rather not raise an objection in a meeting and have it turn out to be a beginner's error that everyone on the team already knows to dismiss.

Could you take the paragraph apart for me sentence by sentence, and sort its claims into those a philosopher working on these questions would accept as stated, those that are true only with qualifications the paragraph silently drops, and those that reverse or otherwise misstate the very positions they invoke? I would like three points addressed explicitly, since they are where my own unease is concentrated.

First, was Turing's test offered as a demonstration that understanding is dispensable, or as something more modest about how the question whether machines think is best reformulated? The paragraph treats it as having a conclusion it may never have claimed.

Second, is competence without comprehension being used the way it was introduced? My hazy recollection is that its author wanted comprehension explained in terms of layered competences rather than eliminated, and that he treated it as a matter of degree; if so, the paragraph's flat dichotomy between full competence and total absence of comprehension may not be his position at all, and I would like to know what he would object to here.

Third, is the final sentence a fair reading of the Chinese Room or a capture of it? I want to understand precisely what the systems reply and the robot reply deny, and whether the paragraph's conclusion could survive those replies even if every other claim in it were granted.

Having done that, please tell me whether the paragraph's overall thesis — that machine understanding is a category mistake rather than an open empirical question — is supported by any of the material that survives audit, or whether it rests entirely on the parts that need correcting. And if it rests on the latter, sketch what a defensible replacement framing would have to say instead: I need enough of the reasoning to propose a revision to my director and to hold my ground if he pushes back, rather than a list of sources to send him.
