I chair my department's spring colloquium series, and next week's speaker is a Kierkegaard scholar whose interpretation of Fear and Trembling departs from both readings my graduate students will arrive with. She rejects the divine-command reading, on which the teleological suspension licenses Abraham because God has commanded something ethics cannot command; and she rejects the existentialist reading, on which the act is a groundless leap that simply demonstrates the primacy of decision. Her own thesis, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: the ethical, in Johannes de silentio's sense of the universal, is by construction a system of general descriptions, whereas Abraham's obligation to Isaac is a relation between two particulars that no general description captures; the suspension is therefore not the overriding of a duty by a higher authority but the coming into view of something that lay all along outside the expressive range of the ethical. On this account ethics is not overruled, it is shown to have been silent; Abraham requires no command in order to be unintelligible; and the absurd names not the content of a promise but the fact that the particular cannot be reconstructed from the universal.

I am moderating the Q&A, and I would rather not simply let her deliver the reading a second time. Please draft eight to ten questions for the discussion period, each followed by a short note identifying the reply she is most likely to give and the follow-up keyed to that reply, so that I can work from the page rather than improvise. The questions should turn on the content of her position and press where the literature presses. At minimum, I want:

- a question on the obvious collapse worry: if the claim is that no system of general rules can capture a concrete relation between two persons, what distinctively survives of faith, of God, and of the absolute duty to God — and is Problema II not simply surplus to her interpretation?
- a question on silence: de silentio insists that Abraham cannot speak and that what he would have to say could not be understood. Does her reading explain the silence or merely relocate it — is Abraham silent because his ground is inexpressible in principle, or because he happens to lack an adequate audience?
- a question on the criterion: if the act is not licensed by a command, what separates Abraham's faith from a monstrous exercise of self-authorization? Anticipate her likeliest answer — that from outside there is no such mark, and that this is just what makes faith a paradox — and give me a follow-up that makes that answer cost her something, for instance about whether she can still describe his act as a response to anything, and about the Exordium's four retellings, in which Abraham remains willing to obey and yet faith is lost: on her reading, what goes wrong in those variants, if obedience was never the point?
- a question on the three tragic heroes of Problema I (Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus): her reading seems to make the difference between Abraham and the tragic hero one of description rather than of authority, so ask what continues to do the separating work, and whether the tragic hero too might not be said to act on grounds the universal cannot express.
- a question on the double movement in the Preliminary Expectoration: resignation and then faith by virtue of the absurd, receiving Isaac back. Does her reading have room for the second movement, or does it reduce faith to a thesis about the limits of ethical language?
- a methodological question: de silentio presents himself as unable to comprehend faith and as something of a poet. Can her interpretation be true of the text while remaining unavailable to its pseudonymous author, and what does she make of that gap?
- as the final question, one asking what textual feature would count against her reading.

Please observe a few constraints. No generic questions — nothing about her path into the field, nothing about whether Kierkegaard remains relevant. Cite locations by Problema number and section name rather than by page, since her edition and mine differ. And keep each question short enough to be delivered aloud in a single breath, with the pressure carried by the follow-up rather than by the initial formulation.
