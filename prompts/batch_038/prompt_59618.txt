i teach a first-year history of science seminar and next week is the newton week, and i'd rather close it with a poem than another discussion question. the thing i keep failing to get across in lecture is that newton the mathematician and newton the heretic are the same person, so i want the poem to do that structurally instead of just asserting it.

the conceit: two voices interleaved, like a printed page and its marginalia. the public voice is the General Scholium — the thing he actually printed, appended to the 1713 second edition of the Principia that his young editor put together — and it runs in heroic couplets, regular iambic pentameter, full natural-theology confidence. it should actually make his argument rather than gesture at it: bodies can persevere in their orbits by the mere laws of gravity, but those laws could never have given them the arrangement they have; comets cut across every vortex with total freedom; so that most beautiful system proceeds from "the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful being." then the move i most want landed precisely — his god is not a world-soul and not a substance but a lord, deity just is dominion, which is why he calls him Pantokrator, universal ruler, and why he can write "He is not Eternity and Infinity, but Eternal and Infinite." dominion, not essence.

the private voice is everything he kept in the trunk. unrhymed, shorter-breathed, indented or otherwise set off so it reads as annotation on the other column. same man: he'd decided by about the time he turned thirty that the trinity was a fourth-century insertion and showed that the two epistle texts used to prop it up were interpolations, and published none of it because it would have cost him everything; he got a royal dispensation so the Lucasian chair wouldn't require holy orders he could not have taken in conscience; he left more words on Daniel, the Apocalypse, alchemy, and the dimensions of Solomon's temple as a key to when the whole thing ends than on the whole of his published natural philosophy; and the papers sat in a family trunk until an auction in the 1930s, when the economist who bought the bulk of them concluded newton wasn't the first man of the age of reason but the last of the magicians.

constraints. for as long as the public voice holds, the couplets stay perfect — full stops on the rhyme. the private voice breaks them: spills over the line ends, cuts in mid-couplet, and by the last stanza the public column is down to a fragment or a half-line while the private one has the page. but here's the part that matters most: dont write it so the private voice is the truth about him and the public voice is the mask. thats the cheap version and it's also wrong. the god of the scholium and the god of the trunk are one god — a sovereign who acts by will and not by essence, substantially present everywhere, space something like his sensory organ (in the queries he tacked onto the Opticks, and the phrase that handed Leibniz a way to accuse him of giving god an organ), which is also why gravity isn't occult, it's will working through secondary causes. if the reader comes away feeling that the anti-trinitarianism and the pantokrator fall out of a single conviction about what it is to be god, the poem has done what i want.

40ish lines. a newton scholar should read it and not catch a single falsehood; a student who's never heard of the general scholium should get a good poem and not a lecture.
