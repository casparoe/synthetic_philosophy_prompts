I spent thirty-five years in a national metrology laboratory and retired four years ago. Philosophy is a late acquisition; I have been reading it properly only since then, and I have recently come to a halt in Aristotle on money — the treatment of reciprocity and exchange in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, and the account of household management and wealth-getting in Book I of the Politics. Before I read any further I want the specification of the problem laid out, and I want it thought through rather than recited to me.

My difficulty is a measuring difficulty. In my trade a unit has to be homogeneous with the quantity it measures: you cannot take a temperature with a length, and a measure is worth nothing unless what is measured is a magnitude of the same kind. Aristotle appears to say close to the opposite. As I read him, he holds that exchange requires commensurability, that money was introduced to supply it, and that what money really stands in for is demand or need, which is what holds an association together. He says money exists by convention rather than by nature and can be devalued or rendered useless at will. He concedes that in the strict sense things as different as a house and a pair of shoes cannot be made commensurable, yet maintains that demand supplies a common measure accurate enough for practical purposes. He notes that money's own purchasing power fluctuates over time, though more slowly than that of other commodities. He describes how metal first passed by size and weight and was later stamped, the stamp existing to relieve people of the trouble of measuring the quantity — which is precisely what a certification mark does, replacing a measurement with a claim of authority. And in the Politics he argues that taking interest is of all forms of wealth-getting the most contrary to nature, because it sets money to breeding money when money was invented only for the sake of exchange.

So I should like the desiderata: the requirements an account of money has to meet if Aristotle's charges are to have a target at all. The standard of adequacy I have in mind is deliberately narrow — an account good enough to support a criticism of money, good enough that when Aristotle says a certain use of it is contrary to nature there is something there for him to be right or wrong about. I am not after a theory that explains how payments clear.

Specifically:

1. The requirements list itself. For each requirement, give the consideration or the case that generates it — Aristotle's own where he has one — and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a commitment peculiar to a school. Please do not simply hand me the three textbook functions of money; tell me where they belong in the list, and whether they are requirements at all or merely descriptions of what money happens to do.

2. Which subsets of the list cannot be jointly satisfied. I suspect at least one triple is already in trouble: money as a measure of value, money as a thing whose own value is set by agreement and can be altered, and money as a standing guarantee that exchange will be possible later when a need arises. If a subset is genuinely unsatisfiable, say so flatly. I regard that as the interesting result rather than a defect in the list.

3. Which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.

4. A separate section on measurement. Does calling money a measure commit one to value's being a scalar magnitude? Work through three answers — that a real scalar underlies exchange, labour being the usual candidate; that there is only ordinal ranking, with no comparison between persons; and that the measuring talk does no measuring, money merely fixing an arbitrary unit of account against which everything else is quoted — and for each, say which of the requirements it can no longer meet. Here my day job makes me suspicious in a way I cannot resolve alone: a single scalar can summarize a multi-dimensional comparison only once weights and a base have been chosen, and different choices yield different numbers.

5. A brief scoring of four contenders against the list: Aristotle's own account; the account on which money grows out of barter to solve the problem of finding two parties who each want what the other has; the account on which money is constituted by a state's designation of what settles a debt; and the account on which money is a transferable record of an obligation. For each, name the requirement it fails, or the one it is forced to reinterpret.

6. Whether the charge about interest is moral or conceptual — that is, whether Aristotle thinks lending at interest is unjust, or thinks it is a category mistake, the employment of a measuring device to generate more of the device. Tell me which reading makes the argument better and which makes it worse.

Two ground rules about how you answer. First, commit. I do not want a survey that arranges the options and leaves me to choose among them; where a requirement or a reading is contested, take a side, give your reasons, and then mark separately the places where you are relying on a disputed interpretation of Aristotle rather than on something he plainly says. If you think a requirement I have gestured at is not a requirement but a prejudice of my profession, say so and argue it. Second, no apparatus: no bibliography, no reference list, no citations, and please paraphrase rather than quote him. I am working offline with one shelf of philosophy in the house, and a citation I cannot look up is worse to me than none. A numbered list with a short justifying paragraph under each requirement is what I want for section 1; prose for the rest, and as long as it needs to be.
