I am a novelist, and the book I am working on turns on a self-deceiving narrator: a memoirist who has rewritten the same chapter — her brother's death — eleven times and kept every draft, so that she is surrounded by the evidence of what she has been doing and is nevertheless, on the page and off it, wholly convinced by the eleventh. A philosopher friend insists the character cannot exist, and gave me this:

(1) Self-deception is intentional deception in which one person occupies both roles.
(2) An intentional deception is executed by a plan whose content the deceiver must keep in view while she carries it out.
(3) In self-deception the content of the plan is that not-p, where the deceiver knows p.
(4) Keeping that content in view is exactly what would defeat the belief it is meant to produce.
(5) So no one can intentionally deceive herself.
(6) The repair is to divide the mind — Freud's censor, Davidson's partition — with a boundary keeping what one compartment knows from reaching the other.
(7) But such a boundary can be posited whenever a subject's attitudes conflict, so on this account nobody ever holds a contradictory pair, and akrasia, denial, and wishful thinking are all re-described as locally coherent compartments.
(8) Worse, a compartment cut off from the knowledge that manufactured its belief is not deceived at all; it is merely uninformed, and there is no one the deception is happening to.
(9) So the partition, invoked to save self-deception, abolishes it — along with the conflict my narrator is supposed to be made of.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I must choose, I would rather lose (1) than lose her, but I suspect I am not seeing what (7) and (8) are really doing. And tell me what would change your verdict: which consideration or counterexample would move you to a different step, and where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to fail.
