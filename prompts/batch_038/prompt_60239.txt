I am one of three members of the Animal Welfare and Product Integrity Advisory Panel at a mid-sized agri-ingredients company. We rear black soldier fly larvae at industrial scale for feed protein, and we separately operate a controlled-environment greenhouse business whose substrate supply chain is, unavoidably, insecticide-intensive. The panel has no decision authority; it writes recommendations to the executive team, and ours is due in ten days, ahead of an audit by a European feed customer who has asked in writing whether our harvest process is welfare-assured. The concrete question before us is whether to adopt a standard that (a) requires larvae to be chilled into torpor before being killed, rather than direct freezing, blanching, or maceration; (b) requires the chilling step to be logged and spot-verified; and (c) commits us not to use any killing method for which there is no evidence at all of reduced aversiveness. Engineering puts the cost at roughly an eight percent throughput loss on that line, which handles several hundred million individuals a year — expensive but absorbable.

The panel is split, and not about the money. Our chair, who comes from operations, wants an explicit decision rule so that this petition and the next one get the same treatment. Her proposal is that we score each candidate mitigation as (individuals affected per year) × (our credence that the species is sentient) × (an intensity-and-duration factor between zero and one), and adopt anything whose product clears a fixed threshold. One of my colleagues, a philosopher we retain part-time, thinks the rule is either vacuous or self-defeating: at hundreds of millions of individuals, any credence above rounding error clears the threshold, so the rule collapses into an instruction to adopt every mitigation anyone ever proposes; but if credences are set honestly low, as he thinks the evidence on insect nociception warrants, the same rule licenses doing nothing, and its output is largely a restatement of whichever number we put in. He also raises what I take to be the harder objection. The behavioral criteria our own standards documents rely on — flexible avoidance of noxious stimuli, learning from injury, trade-offs between foraging and self-protection, tending to wounded body parts — are, he says, satisfied by things nobody on this panel thinks have interests, including the adaptive controller running our greenhouse and, with a little generosity, a modern thermostat that learns occupancy patterns. Either the criteria are tracking the wrong property, or we are committed to a conclusion we find absurd, and he wants the memo to say which. There is also a scope worry nobody has put in writing: if the standard applies to larvae, our substrate is full of mites and springtails, and the grain we buy is produced with broad-spectrum insecticides that kill vastly more arthropods than we ever rear. Meanwhile our marketing department has already used the phrase welfare-assured in a draft brochure, which may mean part of this decision has been made for us.

Please draft the recommendation I will circulate to the executive team before the panel votes. I need a clear bottom line — adopt, decline, or adopt with specified modifications — and not a survey of the considerations. Beyond that: give us a decision rule that survives my colleague's objection, show it applied to the larvae case with the arithmetic stated rather than assumed, and explain why this rule rather than a simple threshold on sentience credence or a simple prohibition list. Resolve the reductio explicitly: either say why the criteria do pick out a genuine property that our greenhouse controller and thermostat lack, or concede that they do not and explain what, if anything, still justifies treating a larva differently from a thermostat — and if your answer is that moral status comes in degrees rather than being on/off, say what that changes operationally for this petition instead of leaving it as a slogan. Give a defensible answer on scope, including where you would draw the line between the larvae line, the substrate fauna, and the field insects killed upstream, and be candid if the line you draw is partly arbitrary. State the strongest consideration against your own recommendation in wording its advocate on this panel would accept, and explain why it does not carry the day. Close with a timeline separating what must be settled before the customer audit from what can be deferred to the next cycle. Roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words. No references or citations — I will add anything we need myself — and please do not end with a call for more research unless you specify exactly which finding would flip the recommendation.
