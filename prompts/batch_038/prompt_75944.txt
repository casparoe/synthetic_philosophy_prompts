For thirty-eight years I was a marine microbiologist, the last fifteen of them directing a laboratory concerned with microbial physiology in anoxic sediments. I retired four years ago and have since been reading philosophy of science with some seriousness, which I ought to have done decades earlier. I have now been persuaded to chair the screening committee for a private foundation that awards a substantial prize, every second year, for work judged to have produced or demonstrated a novel form of life. The committee consists of three people: myself, a synthetic chemist who knows nothing of biology beyond school level, and a lay trustee appointed by the donor. We receive between thirty and fifty submissions per cycle, ranging from serious laboratory work to outright crankery, and we meet once, by video conference, for a single day. Each file gets roughly ten minutes.

Our statutes define life in one sentence, borrowed long ago from the astrobiology literature, to the effect that a living thing is a chemical system which sustains itself and is capable of Darwinian evolution. I have come to think that sentence does no work at all, and last cycle proved it: we argued for four hours over a single submission and discovered afterwards that the three of us had been using the word in three different senses. I should like to repair this before the next cycle.

Please act as a philosopher of biology who has also sat on enough real panels to know what can survive a ten-minute review and what cannot. Take the time to think this through properly; I would rather have a well-reasoned procedure than a prompt one. Work in the following order.

1. Begin with an analytic preamble of no more than four hundred words. Brainstorm there, before drafting anything: what conceptual choices must a workable procedure make, which distinctions it must preserve, and which familiar refinements it may safely discard for a committee of three non-philosophers. Be explicit about anything you are setting aside and why.

2. Then give the procedure itself: an ordered sequence of questions, in the order we are to ask them, each accompanied by (a) what we would have to find in a submission for an affirmative answer to count as sufficient, (b) what an evasive or insufficient answer looks like in practice, and (c) whether the question is one of empirical fact, which the chemist and I can settle from the file, or a conceptual choice which the committee must make deliberately and record in the minutes. Mark clearly the points at which the procedure terminates in a rejection, and the points at which it hands the matter to the three of us because it is not a question of fact but a decision we are entitled to make; for the latter, state what we would have to agree upon in order to proceed at all.

3. Tell me how the answers are to be combined. My reading suggests that the properties associated with living organisms — bounded individuality, self-maintenance, repair, reproduction with heritable variation, cumulative adaptation over generations, responsiveness — come apart in practice rather than clustering neatly, so I suspect that a checklist with a threshold score is the wrong instrument. If you agree, say what to use instead and justify it; if you disagree, defend the score.

4. The procedure must handle, explicitly and not in a footnote: dormant or preserved material, since submissions routinely concern cultures, spores, or seeds that are at present doing nothing at all, and we need to know whether "not alive just now" differs from "not alive at all," or whether that distinction simply cannot be made from a document; the individual–lineage problem, since reproduction may be demanded of the submitted entity or of the lineage to which it belongs, and our files include sterile hybrids, worker castes, cultured cell lines that no longer exist anywhere in nature, and bacteria whose genomes have been so reduced by their symbiotic way of life that they cannot survive outside a host; the substrate problem, since we receive digital submissions and I want the procedure to declare whether it assumes the substrate to be irrelevant, on what grounds, and what it says to a group that insists upon it; and heredity without templating, where what is passed on is a composition rather than a sequence. Finally, it must take seriously the inductive predicament: we have encountered exactly one kind of life, so criteria read off it may be local prejudice, and a procedure that simply demands the familiar machinery is designed to exclude the very thing the prize exists to find. I want this worry built into the instrument, not waved at.

5. Run the procedure on three submissions, step by step, showing where it terminates or hands out, stating the verdict, and identifying which steps in that run were judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

   Submission A. A group reports lipid vesicles in a flow reactor which grow by incorporating amphiphiles from the medium, divide when the flow shears them, and pass on a characteristic mixture of entrapped catalysts; populations can then be selected for a rate. No molecule in the system is templated. The group calls the entrapped mixture a compositional genome.

   Submission B. An artificial-life group reports a long-running simulation in which self-replicating programs mutate and are selected for solving a sorting task. They supply source code, logs, and a documentary film. Their claim is that the system evolves cumulatively and has lineages with a record of descent, and is therefore alive; that it happens to run on silicon is, they say, a fact about their hardware rather than about their organisms.

   Submission C. A tissue-engineering group reports constructs about a millimetre across, assembled from differentiated amphibian cells, which move coherently, close wounds in themselves, and persist for two or three weeks on stored yolk reserves. They do not grow, do not feed, and do not reproduce; they are discarded at the end of each experiment. The group is candid about all of this and asks to be judged as the designer of a novel organism rather than the builder of a device. The donor has dined with the principal investigator and is enthusiastic. I do not expect our answer to please him, and I would far rather know what the procedure says now than discover it in the meeting.

6. Identify three or four situations, drawn from plausible submissions, in which you expect the procedure to fail — to give an answer that is wrong, or indefensible, or merely an artefact of its own wording — and say what the committee should do when it recognises that it is in one of them.

7. Close with a draft of about three hundred and fifty words for the foundation's report to the donor, in plain language, explaining how the threshold question is decided. It must not say that philosophers disagree, and it must not pretend that our criteria are nature's own joints.

Two further constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I have the literature and want your reasoning instead. And do not invent experiments, taxa, instruments, or attributions: if you need a scientific detail you are not certain of, flag it as uncertain rather than supplying something plausible. Throughout, distinguish what is a matter of empirical fact about a submission from what is a decision the committee is entitled to make, and where you are genuinely unsure, say so and tell me what would settle the question.
