I am assembling a problem set for an advanced seminar on the interpretations of probability, and I want a purpose-built case rather than one of the standard exhibits: no die-and-coin catechism, no lottery, no Sleeping Beauty, no branching universes, no inverse-conditional puzzle about backward probabilities. Here is the commission.

The case is meant to isolate a question I think the usual three-way taxonomy obscures: what is a probability claim *answerable to*? On a von Mises-style reading, to relative frequencies in a collective generated by a repeatable experiment; on a single-case propensity reading of the Popper/Mellor sort, to a physical disposition of a situation; on a subjective Bayesian reading, to an agent's evidence. My working hypothesis is that the three can be made to agree numerically while disagreeing about what would falsify them, and that such a case is a sharper teaching instrument than one in which the numbers merely differ. Build me that case to the following specification.

**Requirements on the scenario.**

1. The event whose probability is at issue, call it e, is the outcome of a single run of a mechanical randomizer. The randomizer is a unique, hand-built device, operated exactly once and then dismantled or destroyed, such that no other token of its construction has ever existed and none ever will. Note that this uniqueness is there for the narrative and for one of the removal tests below; it is *not* intended to silence the frequentist, and I expect you to explain why it does not.
2. A bias must be concealed in the actual situation: something absent from the device's documentation, physically external to the randomizer as documented (installed downstream of it, and removable without altering the device itself), and empirically calibrated. There must exist a documented series of runs of a bench rig sharing the relevant geometry, in which the favored outcome occurred in some exact proportion strictly between one half and one; you choose the count and the proportion. That series must be unknown to the agent at the time of the run and discovered afterward in an archive.
3. The device as documented must be genuinely and measurably symmetric: the dossier records tolerances and a release mechanism that imparts no preferred direction. Consequently the best propensity attribution to the documented device is exactly one half, and the agent's evidence licenses exactly one half. Those two halves must be generated by *different* facts, and your analysis must say which facts generate each.
4. Exactly one named human agent, with an exhaustively specified evidence set at the moment of the run: list in at most five bullets what the dossier contains and what it omits. The single run must decide something that agent or their institution actually cares about, so that standing at one half while the truth is higher has consequences for someone.
5. State the actual outcome of the run, and choose the outcome that the bias disfavors, so that the single datum does not quietly vindicate the higher number.
6. Realism: an ordinary laboratory, workshop, hospital, or municipal setting; classical mechanics plus ordinary electromagnetism; chaotic sensitivity is permitted, quantum indeterminism is unnecessary and I would rather you left it out; no demons, oracles, perfect predictors, or time travel. Every quantity must be one a competent technician could in principle have measured.
7. The scenario must be inert. A purely descriptive paragraph of at most 250 words, followed by the evidence bullets. It may not contain the words probability, chance, propensity, disposition, likelihood, or credence; it may not name any interpretation, theorist, or school; and it may not report any number as a probability. All numbers in it must be measured quantities, tolerances, or raw counts. Every interpretive verdict must be derived in your analysis from the described facts, not visible in the wording of the scenario. A case that stipulates its verdicts rather than generating them is a failed case.

**The pattern the case must generate.** The frequentist must have a determinate number for e strictly between one half and one, taken from the documented bench series, and you must address both the gap between an observed sample proportion and a collective's limit and the question of why that series rather than some other class into which the run also falls. The propensity theorist must land on exactly one half for e, attributed to the documented device, with the bias treated as a mask on that device's disposition rather than as part of the situation whose propensity is at issue; defend that choice of relatum, then price the alternative of attributing the propensity to the total situation, saying what propensity theory gains or loses under each. The subjective Bayesian must land on exactly one half for the named agent at the moment of the run, and on a specified revised credence once the archive is opened.

**Deliverables, in this order.** (i) The scenario and the evidence bullets. (ii) Three derivations: for each interpretation, the number, the reasoning that produces it from the described facts, and what that number is answerable to. (iii) The perturbation: the archive is opened. Give the new value for each of the three and explain why one of the two pre-discovery halves moves and the other does not. (iv) A design audit: name the single feature doing the work; run three removal tests — the bias was never installed, the archive is never found, the device is one of a mass-produced batch rather than a unique token — and report how each verdict changes; and tell me candidly whether the case begs the question against the frequentist by building in which collective is the relevant one. (v) The counterattack: how a defender of whichever interpretation your case treats worst would break it, and whether the break succeeds.

I want a committed response, not a survey. Close by taking a definite position on which of the three interpretations the case as you have built it best supports, with reasons, and do not hand the choice back to me. In particular, tell me which interpretation's pre-discovery verdict for e your case shows to have been *simply incorrect* — as distinct from rational but uninformed, or licensed only relative to a description that has since been superseded. Pick exactly one, or argue that none qualifies, and defend the pick.

No references, bibliography, or reading list; name a theorist only where it clarifies a view. Work from first principles.

Your very last line must be exactly:

\answer{F=<x>, P=<y>, S=<z>, S*=<w>, W=<label>}

where x, y, z are the pre-discovery values under frequentism, propensity theory, and subjective Bayesianism respectively, w is the post-discovery credence of the named agent, and label is F, P, S, or NONE, recording your answer to the incorrectness question. No text after that line.
