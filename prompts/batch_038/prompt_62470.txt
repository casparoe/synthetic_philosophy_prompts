Retired seven years ago from a state school where I did comparative anatomy and alot of fieldwork on freshwater snails. Since then I've been reading philosophy in the evenings and I've hit a wall: on adaptation, everybody I pick up either assumes an answer or assumes the whole question is a rhetorical trick, and I still don't know what a good answer would even look like. So before I read another one, give me the spec.

Write out the desiderata for any account of a claim of the form "trait T is an adaptation for doing F" — what such an account has to deliver before a careful biologist should sign the claim. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, not just a label. The ones I especially want worked through: what the account owes on origin versus maintenance, since a thing can start as a side effect of something else and later be kept because it pays, and the reverse also happens; how it handles traits that can't be varied independently of their neighbours because development and shared growth processes tie them together; what it says about a structure that's useful now but was never selected for, and one that was selected for something and is useful for nothing now; and what it owes me when two different stories fit the same comparative data equally well — is "no worse than the alternatives" enough, or does the account have to say which data would have come out differently? Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable, in the sense that dropping them makes the claim stop meaning anything, and which are just the house style of one camp. My hunch is that demanding a tight causal story about the origin is one of the latter, but I can't tell hunch from prejudice yet.

Use my own case as the test bed. Thirty years on a little snail with a calcified ridge running along the inside of the last whorl. I told students it stiffened the shell against crayfish crushing. Then it turned out the ridge is laid down by a fold of mantle that also secretes the dark band on the outside, and one population has lost the ridge but kept the band and has, if anything, a thicker shell. I honestly dont know whether the ridge was ever for stiffening or whether it's just where that fold happens to sit. An adequate theory should be able to say something about my ridge other than that it's complicated.

One thing I bring with me, which I want you to take seriously and then attack: I'm an experimentalist by training, so my instinct is a deletion test. Take the trait out, drop the organism back into the setting its ancestors actually lived in, run the lineage forward, and see if it holds on; whatever it fails to do without the trait is what the trait was for. Say whether that's a genuine desideratum any account should meet or just my lab-bench prejudice dressed up as a criterion — and note what it does to a byproduct structure, since deleting one of those usually wrecks the thing next to it, which looks suspiciously like the deletion test assigning functions to spandrels.

Flag any pair of requirements you think can't both be satisfied. If some subset of the list is jointly impossible, name the subset and say which requirement I'd have to give up — that's the part I most want. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of the approaches people currently push. Keep the scorecard short, two or three sentences each on the main contenders, because the criteria are the deliverable and I'll do my own judging.

Purpose, so you know what "adequate" means here: good enough that a non-specialist can write "this is for that" in print without lying to the reader. Not good enough to settle a metaphysics debate — I gave up on that ambition around page forty of the last one I tried.
