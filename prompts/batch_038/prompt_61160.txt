I've been reading this stuff since the Overcoming Bias days and there's one argument I've now watched go around in circles for something like fifteen years, on blogs, in comment threads, in Discords, in my own reading group: what is intelligence, and is it one thing. The camps never move. You've got the psychometric side saying g is about as real as anything in psychology, it predicts job performance and health outcomes and training success and basically everything else, and the machine-intelligence side pointing at something like the Legg-Hutter move — intelligence as expected performance across all environments weighted by how simple the environment is, which is supposed to make "general" precise instead of vibes. Then you've got the no-free-lunch side saying any measure like that is only defined once you've fixed a distribution over tasks, so a truly domain-general score is a category error and what looks like g is just a lucky match between the task distribution humans happen to care about and the biases the system happens to have. And you've got the situated/embodied side saying capability is a property of an agent-plus-environment-plus-scaffolding, so trying to factor out the agent's contribution is like trying to weigh a person by weighing the room. Plus the deflationists who say intelligence just is whatever the tests measure and everything past that is reification.

Here's the local version, which is the part I actually care about, because it's my friends and they've been at it for two years. One guy wants to define the intelligence of a system as how many bits it can shave off a description of its own environment per unit of compute, measured against a fixed reference compressor — he likes it because its objective and doesn't need a task list. Another says the only non-circular measure is sample efficiency at picking up a new skill in a domain you weren't trained on, holding compute and data fixed, and that compression-by-itself is worthless because a lookup table of past observations "compresses" fine and isn't intelligent at all. A third says both are doomed for the same reason: they're single-agent measures, and the thing we actually want to know about is the loop, not the thing sitting in the loop — including the tools, the training pipeline, the people correcting it. Each of them thinks the other two are talking past them. None of them can say exactly where.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to attack rather than nod along with: this isn't a verbal dispute about the word "intelligence," and it isn't a factual dispute either. It's a dispute about what kind of object the measure is supposed to be — a property of the agent, a property of the agent-world pair, or a property of a whole feedback loop — and once you see that, the three positions aren't rival answers to one question, they're answers to three different questions that all get filed under the same heading. So the impasse is a quantifier-scope problem dressed up as a definition problem.

Is that right, or am I flattering myself? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement. Tell me which parts are genuinely substantive, which are verbal, which are hidden empirical questions in disguise (like: is there a fact of the matter about whether capability transfers across domains in a way that makes one scalar number predictive, and would we know it if we saw it), and which are bedrock intuitions that nobody on any side can argue for. Give me reasons drawn from what the camps actually say, not a taxonomy of theories of intelligence.

Two specific things I want you to take a swing at. First: there's a standard reply to the compressor guy — that swapping your reference machine or your universal prior only rescales the measure by a bounded constant, so the arbitrariness doesn't bite. Does that actually help him, or does a constant factor matter a lot when what you want out of a metric is a ranking of agents, and when the environments you care about are all high-complexity ones that the simplicity weighting squashes toward zero? Second: the sample-efficiency person and the loop person look like they're making competing proposals, but I suspect one of them is making a claim about measurement and the other is making a claim about what exists, which would explain why they never connect. If that's right, say so and show me.

And then: given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress? A distinction, an experiment, a formal impossibility result, a change of question, or nothing at all — in which case tell me why nobody should expect movement and what the people in my reading group should do with their two years. No reading list, no bibliography, no survey. Just the diagnosis and what would break the tie.
