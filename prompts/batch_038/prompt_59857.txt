I just started as the evaluation lead on a patient-safety program run across three regional hospitals, and I've inherited an argument that's been going for well over a year between the two senior people I report to. One is a philosopher-turned-consultant who thinks entirely in terms of Lewisian conventions: for him, a regularity persists because it's near-universally followed, everyone expects everyone else to follow it, everyone conditionally prefers that any given person follow it *provided the others do*, some alternative regularity would serve the same purpose just as well, and all of that is common knowledge. No sanctions required — if you find yourself needing sanctions, you've misdescribed the situation. The other is an evolutionary social scientist who thinks that framework is hopelessly narrow: she says what actually holds up the norms that matter is third-party punishment plus normative expectations, people comply because others will disapprove and act on that disapproval, and because the compliance is conditional on expecting both that others do it *and* that others expect it of you. Her line is that his account explains which side of the corridor you walk down and nothing much else. The grant renewal is due in three weeks and the funder wants a single theory of change that both of them will sign, which is how I ended up having to draft the document their adversarial collaboration should have produced a year ago.

The live case is our speaking-up intervention. Baseline: across the three theatres, junior scrub staff and anaesthetists essentially never interrupt a senior surgeon who is about to breach a sterile field or skip a checklist step, even though in post-incident interviews almost all of them say they noticed and wanted to say something. The package we're proposing has four parts — (a) every team member signs a commitment card that gets posted in the theatre; (b) a scripted "hard stop" phrase and a physical badge anyone can hold up, with a rule that the operation pauses when it's raised; (c) a rotating "safety lead" role, so on any given list somebody has it in their job description to be the one who interrupts; (d) monthly public reporting of how many stops were called and how many were later judged correct. My boss says this works purely as equilibrium selection: speaking up is in everyone's conditional interest once they expect the others to do it too, and the badge and the script are just focal points that make the new equilibrium common knowledge. My other boss says it will fail unless silence itself becomes punishable — the team has to sanction the colleague who watched and said nothing, and the badge only matters if there's a cost to not using it. I suspect one crux is whether this situation is a coordination problem at all or something with asymmetric payoffs, and another is whether third-party punishment of the non-punishers can itself be an equilibrium rather than just being pushed one level up. But tell me where I've got it wrong.

Here's what I need, in this order — please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I want the concepts argued out, not the sources named:

1. **Bottom line** — four or five sentences: the single biggest substantive disagreement, and the one prediction about the ward they'd each happily bet money on.
2. **Claims both sides can sign** — a numbered list, roughly ten items, each one or two sentences. These have to be real commitments, not "both sides agree that norms exist." Include at least a couple about what the Lewisian conditions actually require and a couple about what punishment can and can't do on its own.
3. **Where they still differ** — a table, six to eight rows, columns: topic / the convention-first statement in wording that side would sign / the enforcement-first statement in wording that side would sign / verbal or substantive. If a row is verbal, say what the underlying shared claim is. Be careful not to slant either column — if I can tell which side wrote it, you've failed.
4. **Cruxes** — three of them (mine or better ones), each about 250 words: the precise sub-question, the case or evidence both sides agree bears on it, what each side predicts *before* seeing the data, and what would make each side move. Footbinding-style coordinated-pledge collapses are fair game as a historical crux case if you think they help, but I'd rather have at least one crux that only our ward data can settle.
5. **The ward, run through both accounts** — around 500 words, plus a small prediction table: what the stop-counts, badge usage, and staff survey answers should look like at six months and at two years under each analysis, and which specific observation would falsify each one.
6. **What each side gives up if it loses** — 150 words or so, phrased as concessions each could sign today.
7. **What we are not claiming** — under 100 words, to keep the agreement section honest.

Total maybe 1800–2200 words. Casual register is fine, it's going to two people who argue over email anyway, but the formulations need to be tight enough that I could hand each of them their own column and they'd say "yes, that's my view."
