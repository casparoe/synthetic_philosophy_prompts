I wrote up the following argument about the absent-minded driver for a reading group, intending it to dissolve the puzzle; it now proves far too much. Setup: the driver must exit at the second of two indistinguishable intersections. Exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 1. Since he cannot tell where he is, he continues with probability p at each intersection, and ex ante p = 2/3 maximizes expected payoff, at 4/3.

(1) At an intersection, if he is following that plan, his probability of being at the second intersection is 2/5.
(2) But these are not two decisions: because he cannot tell the intersections apart, choosing at one is, for deliberative purposes, choosing a policy that governs both.
(3) Evaluating the options as policies, the plan is stable — reasoning at the intersection, the driver finds no alternative to p = 2/3 that he prefers — so planning-stage and action-stage reasoning coincide.
(4) The appearance of dynamic inconsistency in the driver case therefore came entirely from treating one repeated decision as two independent ones.
(5) The same error generates every temptation case. An agent who resolved in the morning to skip dessert confronts, in the evening, a choice he cannot distinguish from the choice he will confront at every future occasion that looks exactly like this one.
(6) So a rational agent evaluates that evening choice as a policy over the whole class of indistinguishable occasions, and doing so reproduces his earlier optimum.
(7) Therefore no agent ever knowingly acts against his own prior plan: weakness of will, precommitment, Ulysses contracts, and hyperbolic discounting all rest on the error named in (4).
(8) (7) is plainly false.
(9) So (2) is false — yet (2) is what earns the driver his 4/3, and without it I seem to have no account whatsoever of what he should do at the intersection.

Which step actually fails, and why did it look sound? Then price the exits: what does giving up (2) cost, and is the final clause of (9) true — does rejecting (2) really leave the action stage with nothing to say? If you would rather reject some other step, explain what (2)'s success in the driver case does and does not show about how far it generalizes.
