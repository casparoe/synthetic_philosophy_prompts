For a dozen years I have taught a graduate seminar in the philosophy of measurement, and my own research sits squarely in the representational tradition: extensive and intensive quantities, the axiomatics of additive conjoint measurement, uniqueness theorems, Stevens's scale typology, the family of admissible transformations associated with each scale type, and the criterion on which a statement about a measured quantity is meaningful only if its truth value is invariant across every representation that the uniqueness theorem permits. I am fluent in all of this and would like an answer pitched at that level; please do not define terms such as extensive quantity, order-preserving transformation, or positive affine transformation for me. I am not an economist, and my command of decision theory does not extend much beyond the standard expected-utility framework, so the welfare-economics material will need to be introduced to me rather than assumed.

What I want is a systematic translation of the debate over interpersonal comparisons of utility into that measurement-theoretic vocabulary, together with a rigorous account of what the translation fails to carry.

Here are the positions I would like rendered, one at a time.

First, Robbins's dismissal of interpersonal comparison as unscientific, and the positivist-flavored claim that there is no basis for such comparisons in observable behavior.

Second, Harsanyi's extended-sympathy and impartial-observer argument, along with the associated result that a social evaluation satisfying the expected-utility axioms together with a Pareto indifference condition must take the form of a weighted sum of individual utility functions, and the further step on which the weights are set equal.

Third, Sen's objection that Harsanyi's device pushes the veil of ignorance so far that the persons behind it are treated as identical, so that the interpersonal question is dissolved rather than answered; and Sen's related distinction between sympathy and commitment, where commitment involves choices that depart from an ordering of the agent's own well-being.

Fourth, the reading of Arrow's impossibility theorem as an information-restriction result: the claim that much of the theorem's force comes from admitting only ordinal profiles as arguments to the social functional.

For each of the four, I want three things stated explicitly. Give me the closest available formulation in measurement-theoretic terms. Then say what had to be bent to get it there. Then say what has no formulation at all — where the source claim depends on a notion for which my vocabulary provides no expression.

Beyond the item-by-item work, please address the following questions, which are the ones I cannot settle on my own.

Is Robbins's refusal best read as a claim about the absence of an empirical relational system whose ordering relations span two persons, so that no representation theorem licenses a cross-person comparison — or does it additionally import a semantic thesis about meaningless statements that representational theory would supply independently of any economic argument?

Does Harsanyi's device amount, in my terms, to a normalization convention that fixes a common origin and a common unit across individuals, and is the equal-weighting step a convention or a substantive hypothesis? Put in invariance language: is the claim that cross-person utility differences are invariant under the full family of admissible transformations, or only under a restricted subfamily — and which subfamily? Please distinguish carefully between comparability of levels and comparability of units, and say which of the two each argument actually requires.

Does Sen's charge against the impartial observer translate as the assertion that comparability has been manufactured by stipulating that all individual representations are one and the same function up to scale, rather than derived from any ordering relation that could be empirically interrogated?

Can representational measurement theory accommodate a pattern of choice that is not the representation of any preference ordering over consequences, which is what commitment seems to require, or does such a pattern register in my framework only as an axiom violation?

Is the gloss on Arrow's theorem accurate — that restricting the social functional to ordinal information is an invariance constraint, permitting the functional to depend only on features preserved by the admissible transformations — or does that gloss quietly strengthen the original claim?

As a test case for whatever you conclude, apply the translation to the practice of summing quality-adjusted life years across different people in cost-effectiveness analysis. That practice appears to presume both a common zero point, in some health state identified with death, and a common unit, in a year of full health. Are those presumptions empirical measurement claims, normative stipulations, or something my vocabulary cannot classify? And does the objection that cross-person summation here is meaningless survive the translation, or does it reduce to a complaint about which normalization has been adopted?

Two constraints on how you proceed. Do not smuggle the presuppositions of measurement theory into the economics and present the result as translation; in particular, do not assume without argument that utility is a measured quantity, that only empirically grounded orderings count, or that invariance is a criterion of legitimacy rather than a criterion of intelligibility. Where the economists are doing something other than measuring, say so and describe what they are doing instead.

Then give me the residue report as a genuine verdict, not a list. Sort the recalcitrant material into two heaps: items that indicate a limitation of representational measurement theory, such as a concept my framework has no place for, and items that suggest the original dispute was less substantive than it appeared, such as a disagreement that turns out to be a choice among normalizations. Where you assign something to the second heap, say what the underlying substantive disagreement, if any, really was. I am especially interested in whether measurement theory can even address the question of what the measurand is — the same axioms and the same uniqueness theorem would be satisfied whether the quantity represented is pleasure, desire satisfaction, or the realization of items on an objective list — and whether that silence is itself the largest piece of residue.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or citations; where you are uncertain about the provenance of a view, characterize the argument instead of naming a text. Please write the response in American English, in continuous prose with a modest amount of structure, and let the residue rather than the successful translations carry the weight of the discussion.
