Our team does release engineering for agent-ish systems at a mid-size AI lab. Between six and eight designs land on my desk a month and I've got about 45 minutes each before a go/no-go meeting, where the person signing off is an eng manager who's never seen a formal proof in their life. Every review turns into the same argument, and I want it turned into a procedure.

The argument: some failure modes don't come from what an agent does, they come from what it's about to do. A mechanism that triggers on the agent's settled plan can make the agent's own recommendation self-undermining — it recommends A, notices A is catastrophic if anything downstream can see it coming, switches to B, notices B is worse, switches back. You get thrash, or you get an agent that lands somewhere dumb for reasons it could articulate but can't act on. The three cases everybody reaches for are the two-agent arms race where the other side fires only if they predict you'll fire, the appointment in two cities where a perfect predictor kills you wherever you end up, and the button that only works if you were never the sort of agent who'd press it. Fine. What I don't have is anything a non-specialist can actually run in order.

So: write me the review procedure. Ordered tests, what a reviewer physically does at each step, where it stops with a "do not ship," where it stops with "ship, with monitoring," and where it hands the call to a human because the question is about values and not about the decision rule. Run it end to end on three things — the two-city appointment case as a calibration item so I can see what a fail looks like from the inside, our own case from last week below, and a rule you invent that passes everything you wrote but that you'd still refuse to ship. I want that third one to sting a bit.

Our case, roughly as spec'd. A research agent can (i) sit tight, (ii) publish its finding under embargo to a partner org, or (iii) publish outright. The partner runs a risk model over the agent's declared plan, its logs and its scaffold config — so it keys off what the agent is going to do, not off the act itself, and it moves before the agent does. Accurate about nine times out of ten, wrong the rest. Points, higher better:

| partner predicted → | sit tight | embargo | publish |
|---|---|---|---|
| agent sits tight | 20 | 20 | 20 |
| agent embargoes | 30 | 75 | 60 |
| agent publishes | 10 | 40 | 45 |

I ran the obvious thing by hand and got two answers that both look fine in isolation and one loop that never settles, so please have the procedure say something explicit about what to do when there's more than one resting point, and about whether it should be testing settled outputs only or also the states the agent passes through mid-deliberation — because if the partner's model can read a lean before it hardens, "final output" might be the wrong unit to test.

Some constraints, since I know how these documents usually turn out:

- each test needs a pass condition, a hard-fail condition, and a "this is judgment, mark it and hand it up" condition; no test that requires solving a fixed point by hand unless there's a cheap sufficient version a reviewer can do with a table and a pencil;
- for every step that wants a number you can't get (predictor accuracy, how much of the agent's plan is visible downstream, how long a thrash cycle takes), say what to substitute, and say which way the substitution biases the verdict;
- build in a guard against the cheap win: a rule whose content is just "always do what this rule says" sails through any self-recommendation test you write. I want the procedure to separate a rule recommending itself for a reason from a rule recommending itself by construction, and I want it to say what evidence counts;
- one page that the signing manager can read, and the longer reviewer-facing version behind it, same order, same verdicts;
- and a tie-break for me: if I'm at 45 minutes and two verdicts are still live, what do I default to, and why that rather than the other.

Be straight with me about the limits. If a design can pass every test and still be predictably awful, describe the shape of that case rather than promising the procedure closes it. And where two of your tests can fire in opposite directions on the same design, say which wins and on what grounds.
