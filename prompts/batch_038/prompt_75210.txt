I am a second-year chemistry undergraduate in the United Kingdom, currently taking statistical thermodynamics, and last week's problem class on the Gibbs paradox ended in a tangle I have not been able to untangle since. We were discussing whether exchanging two identical molecules in a box gives a genuinely new microstate, and hence what actually justifies the factor of 1/N! in the partition function. A friend of mine on the course cut the discussion short with what has become his stock line: "if you can't tell them apart, they're the same thing." He then put the sentence to work three times over. First, to insist that permuting two identical particles is not a distinct arrangement, so the factorial is simply obviously correct and anyone still puzzling over it has missed the point. Second, to say that the practice of labelling the molecules 1 and 2 in our derivations is a harmless fiction, since the labels never corresponded to anything. Third, and most aggressively, to dismiss the residual question of whether the two molecules are nevertheless two numerically distinct individuals: on his view that question "has no answer", and pressing it is just metaphysical noise. I suspect one sentence cannot support all three claims, and that the three uses may not even rest on the same proposition.

Could you separate out the distinct, precisely statable theses that "if you can't tell them apart, they're the same thing" might be expressing? I want a menu rather than a single favoured interpretation. I would expect it to include something like: an epistemic claim about what measurement could in principle reveal; a semantic or logical claim about what a language of properties can express concerning objects; the metaphysical claim that qualitative indiscernibility entails numerical identity; a verificationist claim about the meaningfulness of talk of bare individuality; a pragmatic or conventional claim about how one ought to count states for bookkeeping purposes; and, separately, the converse principle — that identicals are indiscernible — which I gather is uncontroversial but probably inert here, in which case please say so explicitly and explain why it cannot do any of the work my friend wanted from it. For each reading, tell me how strong it is (trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false), what would have to hold of the world or of our conceptual scheme for it to be true, and — this is the part I most want — whether it actually delivers the goods: does it license dividing by N!, does it license treating particle labels as fiction, and does it license declaring the individuality question answerless? Please also say where the classic counterexample bites, the thought experiment I half-remember from a first-year philosophy elective in which a universe contains nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres (Max Black's, I think), and which readings survive it. And if the move I have heard described as discerning objects by relations, even when no property singles any of them out, is genuinely available in this setting, explain what it does to the menu — which readings it rescues, which it renders unnecessary, and whether it undercuts the claim that the question about individuality has no answer. Finally, if it turns out that no reading of the sentence supports all three uses, say so plainly, and say what has to be supplied in its place for each use.

Please write the answer in British English, both in spelling and in idiom, and organise it so that each candidate reading is clearly delimited and its verdict visible at a glance. I am not after a reading list or any references — I have no access to a library this term, so what I need is the analysis itself, argued through rather than sourced.
