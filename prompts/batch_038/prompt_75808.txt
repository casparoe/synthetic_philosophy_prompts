I'm a novelist, about three quarters of the way through a book in which one of the main characters is a thinly disguised version of someone I knew well — different name, different city, same marriage, same habit of phoning people at midnight to talk about her marriage. Nothing scandalous happens to her in the novel. She objects anyway, on the grounds that the marriage stuff was told to me in confidence, and I want to take the objection seriously rather then wave it away as vanity.

So: rank these five accounts of what makes privacy worth having by how well each one explains what she's actually objecting to. Not by how true they are in the abstract — by their grip on this case.

1. Privacy as control over information about yourself.
2. Privacy as limited access — secrecy, solitude, anonymity — treated as a resource someone can be deprived of.
3. Privacy as what makes love, friendship and trust possible at all, since intimacy depends on being able to give some people things you withhold from others.
4. The reductionist line: there's no such thing as a right to privacy, just a bundle of other claims (property, bodily security, promises kept) that happen to look like one right from a distance.
5. Privacy as the fit between a piece of information and the context it's moving through — what's appropriate to circulate among friends isn't appropriate to circulate to a paying public, whether or not anyone consented or was hurt.

Give each a score out of 10 and one sentence on why it lands there, best to worst, and then re-score whichever ones move if we stipulate she never reads the book and never finds out it exists. Tell me whether that reshuffle means anything about the accounts themselves or is just an artefact of how I've set the case up.

Under 300 words for the whole thing, scores included. No bibliography, I'm not writing an essay, I'm writing a novel.
