ok so I'm trying to work out what I actually think about whether a legally valid but grossly unjust rule is still law, going case by case, and my verdicts have turned into a mess. Here they are, roughly in the order I came up with them.

1. A woman in an authoritarian state in the 1940s turns her husband in for remarks critical of the regime, knowing full well he'll be imprisoned or worse. There's a statute making those remarks criminal, it was properly enacted by that regime's own criteria, published, and applied by its own courts. I think what she did was monstrous. But I also think that at the moment she acted, the statute was law. Both of those, no tension.

2. Same woman, same statute. The regime falls, and she's prosecuted for the imprisonment her denunciation caused. Now I think that prosecution is wrong, and I can't explain why. If the statute was law, she was in the clear. If it wasn't law, then the new regime is convicting her retroactively, which I think is also wrong. I want to keep both intuitions and I don't have the machinery to do it.

3. A judge under that same regime has a case where the statute, honestly read, requires a death sentence for stealing food, and there's a narrow reading available that would let the defendant go. I think the judge should take the narrow reading, and I don't think he's just lying about what the law is when he does. This one bothers me: I let the judge read the statute in a way he knows the regime would reject, but I wouldn't let the wife in case 1 treat the statute as not-law.

4. A city ordinance says vehicles are banned from the park. Ambulance driving in for a heart attack: fine, obviously. A tank sitting on a pedestal as a war memorial at the entrance: fine. A six-year-old on a battery-powered toy jeep: not fine, and I feel strongly about that, but I genuinely cannot articulate how it differs from the ambulance. A delivery drone that lands in the park to drop off insulin: not fine as well, but only barely, and I suspect my verdict there is tracking my feelings about drones rather than anything in the ordinance.

5. A health ministry regulation, squarely within the delegation the legislature gave it and duly published, requires that a scarce life-saving drug be allocated by lottery rather than by ability to pay. Wealthy patients can still buy the same drug in another country. I think it's valid law and I think it's bad policy, and those two thoughts never touch.

6. A regulation from the same ministry, also within the same delegation, requires hospitals to turn away anyone who can't prepay, including in emergencies, and there's a bureaucracy that was built for the express purpose of making it stick. Here I want to say it isn't law, or at least that a judge may decline to apply it as law. And the only difference between 5 and 6 that I can actually name is how much I hate the outcome.

7. Someone tells me that a legal system which routinely does that sort of thing isn't a legal system at all. I think that's confused — the regime in 1–3 was a legal system, an awful one. But if the rules were applied so arbitrarily that nobody could predict what the courts would do, I'd say the word stops being usefull. So there's a threshold in my thinking and I can't locate it.

Is there a principle — ideally two, one about what makes a rule legally valid and a seperate one about what validity obliges officials and citizens to do — that delivers all seven, or nearly all? State it precisely enough that I could run a case through it that I haven't thought of yet, and then run one through it that I haven't: a present-day agency enforcing a rule no legislature ever voted on, drafted by staff lawyers, published in a register nobody reads, twice upheld by the courts, and unjust in a way I'd be hard pressed to spell out. Say what the principle says about my 3 and my 6 and where it comes apart.

Then tell me which of the seven I have to give up. Don't just hand me the two standard camps — I've read enough of the positivist/natural-law back and forth to know each side has a line for every case, and my actual problem is that I find both lines partly convincing. If your principle needs an extra clause to save a verdict, tell me what the clause costs and whether it's doing real work or just recording my preferences.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
