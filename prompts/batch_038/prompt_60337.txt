> "Indian philosophy doesn't frame knowledge the way we do; the nearest equivalent is the theory of the pramanas, the instruments of knowledge. Nyāya is the generous school here. It accepts four — perception, inference, comparison, and the word of a reliable speaker — where the Buddhists accept only the first two and the materialists only perception. Perception is the load-bearing one: a cognition produced by contact between a sense faculty and its object, non-erroneous and not yet put into words. Because the whole system is built on that contact, Nyāya is at bottom an empiricism — even testimony only counts as knowledge because you can eventually check the speaker against what you perceive. Comparison is the odd one on the list: it's what happens when someone tells you a gavaya is like a cow and you then spot one in the forest and think 'that's the thing I was told about.' Nyāya commentators mostly treat it as a variety of inference in any case, and they keep it on the list chiefly to pad their count against the Mīmāṃsakas. Inference is a five-step argument — thesis, reason, example, application, conclusion — which is Aristotle's syllogism with two extra steps bolted on for the slow students. The upshot is that Nyāya invented reliabilism two thousand years early: a cognition is knowledge just in case it comes out of a reliable process."
