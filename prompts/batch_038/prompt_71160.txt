I would like a philosophical reconstruction of an existing legal practice rather than an account of the case law, and I would rather you not give me a doctrinal history or any references to consult. Assume I know the facts set out below and want to know what they commit us to.

The practice is the American rule against federal commandeering, together with the doctrines that surround it. Congress may legislate directly to private persons and firms, and it may occupy a field outright, displacing state law entirely. It may also buy state cooperation: under *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987) it may attach conditions to federal grants requiring states to adopt policies it could not impose directly, subject to the limit recognized in *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) on conditions so coercive that they cease to be voluntary. Under *Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority* (1985) it may regulate state and local governments in their capacity as employers, imposing the same wage and hour duties on them as on private ones. State courts of general jurisdiction may be obliged to adjudicate federal claims. What Congress may not do is issue an order to a state's own political branches: *New York v. United States* (1992) holds that it cannot direct a legislature to enact a statute, *Printz v. United States* (1997) holds that it cannot conscript county chief law enforcement officers into administering the federal background-check regime, and *Murphy v. NCAA* (2018) extends the prohibition to federal statutes that direct states not to legislate. In each of those cases the Court acknowledged that Congress could have created the program itself, staffed it with federal officers, and preempted all contrary state law.

So the operative pattern is this: the federal government may take the field, may regulate the state as an employer, may bind its judges, and may purchase its executive's labor on terms the executive accepts — but it may not simply tell that executive what to do. The line does not track the degree of federal control over state policy, since preemption leaves a state with no policy at all; nor the burdens imposed on state budgets, since conditional grants and employer regulation can be heavier; nor the experience of the citizen, who in the *Printz* scenario faced the same requirement whichever instrument Congress had chosen.

Now add a comparison, because it is what makes me suspect the practice presupposes something more specific than it claims. Under the German Basic Law the ordinary rule is that the Länder execute federal statutes through their own administrations, with federal supervision limited to checking that execution is lawful; but where a statute is executed on federal commission, the Land authorities remain subject to instruction from the competent supreme federal authority, and federal supervision then extends to the expediency of execution as well as its legality — a federal ministry may direct how a Land office should decide a matter, not merely whether it decided it in accordance with law. In Switzerland the execution of federal law is assigned to the cantons, and the Confederation maintains almost no field administration of its own. Arrangements that the American doctrine treats as violations of a state's sovereign integrity are, in those systems, the everyday machinery of federal government.

With that in view, I want the following:

1. Reconstruct the theory of the state, and of what federalism is for, that the American practice presupposes. State precisely what conception of a member state it requires — is the state a subject with an integrity or standing of its own, distinct from the aggregated interests of its citizens, such that conscription and negotiation differ morally even when the resulting distribution of costs is identical? Or is the operative commitment rather about political accountability, about citizens being able to identify who decided something? Or about preserving a class of officials whose allegiance is not owed to the federation? Or about the form of federal action — that the federation may act only on persons and never on governments?

2. Say which rival theories the practice rules out, and how far it actually goes without them. In particular, does the practice require a non-instrumental view of federalism, or is it consistent with a purely functional account on which the only questions are which level of government can deliver which service at what cost, with mandates, grants, and preemption being tools to be chosen by efficiency? If it is consistent, why has the American system generated a prohibition that the functional view sees no reason to generate?

3. Distinguish carefully, throughout, what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably. I expect several candidate theories to license the same rules, and I want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where, that is, the doctrinal line could be endorsed from premises that disagree with each other about what a state is.

4. Test the reconstruction against the internal tensions in the pattern. Why may Congress bind a state judge to apply federal law but not a state sheriff to enforce it? Why may it regulate the state as employer but not as administrator? Why is a condition attached to a grant acceptable while a statutory command is not, when the state that refuses the grant may face losses far larger than the cost of complying with the command? For each tension, tell me whether your preferred reconstruction resolves it, absorbs it as a compromise with non-philosophical considerations, or must concede that the practice is inconsistent.

5. Consider four routes to the same on-the-ground outcome for the handgun purchaser: Congress funds the sheriff's office to run the checks and the state accepts; Congress preempts all state law and staffs a federal office in each county; Congress leaves state law untouched but conditions the legality of the sale on clearance by a federal officer; or Congress orders the sheriff to run the checks, as in *Printz*. Which of your candidate theories can see a difference among these four, and which cannot? If a theory sees no difference among them, what does that show about it?

6. Take up the German and Swiss contrast explicitly. Does it demonstrate that the American rule cannot be resting on a general truth about sovereign states, since equally federal systems function with the opposite arrangement and regard it as unremarkable? If so, identify the further, more local premise the American practice must add — something about how the union was formed, about who is taken to be the primary bearer of allegiance, or about the role of courts in policing the arrangement — and say whether that premise is one a theory of federalism can defend or merely a historical contingency that the doctrine has since been made to look principled.

7. Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforwardly instrumental one. Suppose a constitutional system accepted that the only legitimate questions about intergovernmental direction are about cost, capacity, and the clarity of responsibility to voters. Which features of current practice would survive intact, which would disappear, and what would have to be invented to replace the work the prohibition is now doing — for instance, disclosure requirements fixing who is responsible for a service delivered by state officers at federal direction, or constraints on the conditions attachable to grants. Be concrete about what the instrumental system would lose, if anything, that the prohibition currently secures.
