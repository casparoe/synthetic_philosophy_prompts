i'm TAing an ethics seminar and the last week is on whether real moral dilemmas are possible — one guy in the class is a Kantian type who insists conflicts only ever happen between grounds of obligation, never between the oughts themselves, so one of them just wins and nothing is left over. i want to open the session with a poem instead of an argument.

write me a pantoum: quatrains where the second and fourth lines of each stanza come back as the first and third of the next, and the poem's own first line returns as its very last line. five stanzas, no rhyme pressure. the case should be concrete — someone who has to either keep a promise made to a person who's now dead or break it to do something a living person desperately needs, with no third option and no way to do both.

the form has to carry the philosophy: the recycled lines should change jobs on the way round, so a sentence that read as a demand in stanza two arrives in stanza four as guilt, or as a debt that can't be paid — the claim that got overridden doesn't vanish, it comes back in a new position. i want someone who knows the debate to be able to tell from the poem that its siding with "both oughts are genuinely there" rather than "you just don't know which one applies yet," without ever announcing it. no jargon at all: don't use deontic, incommensurable, or residue, and don't name any philosophers. and don't resolve it — the closing line should feel like the circle snapping shut on the speaker, not like an answer.
