I work in the general counsel's office of a regional water district in the American West: eleven counties, three of them across a state line, plus two tribal nations that participate in decisions through a negotiated compact rather than as members of the electorate. A surprisingly large share of my time goes into questions that turn out to be about who gets a vote on what. In the autumn I've been asked to give a forty-minute talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on shared water governance — roughly half the room is political theorists, half is hydrologists and water engineers, and the engineers tend to assume the membership question is just a technical one about who bears the externality. I want the argumentative core of that talk written as a self-contained essay, and I'd like you to write it.

The controversy, as far as I've been able to reconstruct it, runs as follows. Any democratic decision about who constitutes the people already presupposes a people entitled to make that decision, so the boundaries of the demos cannot themselves be fixed by democratic procedure without circularity — the difficulty Dahl described as that of specifying the unit, the association within which democratic processes are supposed to operate. The two candidate principles that get offered in response each fail under pressure. The all-subjected principle, roughly that everyone subject to a body's binding and coercive rules is entitled to a say in making them, is stable, administrable, and tracks something real about the reach of state power; but it excludes precisely those outsiders on whom the body's decisions land hardest, and it makes the franchise depend on a contingent fact about who happens to fall inside a jurisdiction rather than on anyone's stakes. The all-affected-interests principle repairs that, but at a price: causal chains make nearly everyone affected by nearly everything, the relevant set shifts with every agenda item and can be reshaped by deciding what to decide about, the principle seems to enfranchise unborn people and possibly animals and ecosystems, and it gives someone with a trivial interest in a matter the same standing as someone whose life turns on it.

The proposal I want the essay to test came out of our own district practice rather than out of the literature, and I'm not sure it survives contact with theory. Call it the dial view: treat franchise as a dial rather than a switch. There is no single demos; for each decision there is a decision-specific electorate, and each member of that electorate holds a vote whose weight is a function of the depth and specificity of their stake in that particular decision — so a downstream irrigation district that draws on the same aquifer gets a fractional vote on a pumping-limit ordinance and no vote at all on our internal budget, while residents inside the district get a full vote on the budget and a smaller one on pumping. To avoid an infinite regress of electorates, the allocation rule itself — the metering rule, in our internal jargon — is fixed at the constitutional level, promulgated once, and not revisable by the very bodies whose composition it determines. The obvious objection is that this merely relocates the boundary problem: the metering rule is itself a decision with affected parties, so we are back to needing a demos to authorise it, one level up. The reply I find myself making is that the regress might be benign if, at some level, the content of the rule stops being sensitive to who is affected — but I cannot say whether there is such a level, or whether the whole scheme is really the all-affected principle wearing an administrator's uniform.

So: write an essay that weighs these options against one another and reaches a defended verdict on a sharply posed question — is the boundary problem a genuine threat to the claim that democratic decisions are legitimate, or a pseudo-problem generated by the assumption that the demos must be a fixed set? In particular, does something like the dial view dissolve the circularity, or only move it upstairs, and if the latter, is a relocated regress enough for legitimacy, or does democracy at bottom always rest on a pre-democratic act of exclusion — a founding, a border, a nation? Commit to an answer rather than closing on a non-committal note; a conditional verdict is acceptable provided you say what the condition is and why it cuts where you say it does, and I'd want the essay to identify which kinds of decisions are safe for the dial view and which are not. Work through at least one concrete case at some length; you may use our district, or substitute something better — a cross-border aquifer, a metropolitan congestion charge whose revenues are spent inside the city but whose burdens fall on commuters with no vote there, a special economic zone, an algorithmically administered benefits system. Argue rather than cite: no bibliography, no reading list, and please don't attribute positions to named theorists unless you are confident the attribution is right, since I will not be able to check it.

Finally, end with a short section set apart from the essay's own rhetoric in which you tell me what would change your mind. Name the single argument or consideration that, if it were sound, would push you to the opposite verdict; say where you think your own reasoning is most likely to give way — a slippery account of what counts as being affected, an over-optimistic assumption that a metering rule stays fixed once promulgated, a conflation of legitimacy with justice, a hidden premise about what democratic authority is for. And if you think my framing is itself confused, say so plainly instead of arguing politely inside it: if the circularity is not vicious, or if the dial view collapses into the all-affected principle on inspection, or if the problem dissolves once legitimacy and inclusion are pried apart, I would much rather have the frame broken than flattered.
