I am in the fourth year of a PhD in sociology, and for the past two academic years I have held a half-time appointment in my university's Office of Institutional Research, where I have become the person to whom deans and department chairs send contingency tables. Roughly once a month the request takes the same shape: here is a comparison in aggregate (pass rates under two course formats, retention under two advising models, grant success under two internal review processes, six-year completion under two bridge programs), here is the same comparison broken down by some third variable, and the two point in opposite directions. The requester wants to know which table to put in front of the provost. My office has no written answer, and different analysts have been giving different answers to structurally identical requests, which is now a reputational problem rather than a curiosity.

Please write that answer: a decision procedure, in the form of a document I can hand to a colleague who has taken one regression course and no causal-inference training and who will have to run it under time pressure. The constraints it must survive are these. The analyst gets an hour per case. The inputs are a contingency table, a short written account of how the rows were generated, and the requester's own statement of what they intend to do about the finding. There is no software; a calculator is assumed. A statistician may be consulted only at one designated escalation point, which the procedure must define rather than leave implicit. The output is a single page naming which comparison is decision-relevant, why, what the number does not license, and what the requester must be told before they quote it. The office director co-signs, and she is not a statistician, so the reasoning has to be legible to her.

The procedure has to carry the conceptual content rather than the slogan. I do not want "check for confounders." Specifically, it should force the analyst to settle, in a fixed order:

1. What intervention or decision the requester actually has in mind, and whether it would be applied unit by unit or to the population as a whole — including the questions to ask a dean who does not know why this matters and would not use those words, and what to do when the requester turns out not to have one.
2. What role the stratifying variable plays: a common cause of exposure and outcome, something the exposure itself brings about, a proxy for who was at risk, a consequence of the outcome, or an artifact of how the rows were assembled. None of this is readable off the table, so the procedure has to say what the analyst is supposed to go and find out, from whom, and what to do when nobody in the building knows.
3. Whether the phenomenon in front of us is a reversal of an association or the failure of an association to track anything about the world, and which of those two the requester is worried about; I suspect these come apart more often than my colleagues assume, and I want the procedure to treat them separately rather than as one worry.
4. Whether the engine of the paradox is unequal denominators, differing composition across strata, or the implicit weighting of strata — and, if the requester insists on one summary number regardless, how to produce it, how to choose the weighting, and how to disclose on the face of the document that the choice was made by a human being.
5. Where the procedure stops. There should be an explicit class of cases in which the correct output is not a number but a refusal plus a statement of what would resolve it, and the procedure should say who signs that refusal.

I also want the seams showing. Some of these steps can be executed mechanically and some are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume; my director will ask which is which, and I would rather the document answered her than be asked. Include a short section on the failure modes — the kinds of table and the kinds of request that will make a conscientious analyst following these instructions perfectly arrive at a confident wrong answer.

Then run the procedure end to end on three cases. Show the arithmetic; do not just assert which table wins.

Case A. External grant success, by school and by career band, one fiscal year. Established band: School A, 90 of 100 funded; School B, 190 of 200. Early band: School A, 20 of 200; School B, 70 of 600. School A's submissions are overwhelmingly from established investigators; School B's are overwhelmingly early career. Career band is fixed before either school's internal review process touches the proposal. The provost has asked for a single figure comparing the two schools' review processes and expects it on Friday.

Case B. Pass rates in a required gateway course, by format and by declared track. Quantitative track: new format, 350 of 400; traditional, 90 of 100. Non-quantitative track: new format, 100 of 200; traditional, 55 of 100. The traditional cells are small because the format is being phased out. Here is the part that will matter: the new format includes a week-two exercise that actively encourages students to re-declare into the quantitative track, so track membership is partly produced by the format itself. For two years my office has published a disaggregated report on this course, and I have defended it in committee on the grounds that within every track the traditional format looks better. The registrar now wants to know whether the university should finish the phase-out for everyone. I expect the procedure to vindicate my report. If it does not, say so plainly and tell me what I should have been reporting instead.

Case C. Six-year completion, by bridge program. Program P: 400 of 500. Program Q: 300 of 400. Within every band of entering high-school GPA, Program Q looks better; in aggregate, Program P looks better. When asked how the table was built, the analyst who compiled it says it was restricted to students who had declared a major by the end of the second year, because the completion data are messy otherwise. Program P requires declaration by the end of the second year. Program Q does not require it at all. Nobody at either program can say how many students in the other program's cohort would have met the filter. The dean of undergraduate studies wants the comparison for an accreditation self-study due in three weeks and has told my office that "no finding" is not an acceptable line item.

Finally, a request about how you handle the contested parts. Distinguish, throughout, what is settled from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this seriously. My own sense — which may be wrong, and which I do not want you to flatter — is that the causal-graph tradition, the potential-outcomes tradition, and a more purely evidential or associational way of talking do not describe these cases in the same vocabulary, and that a competent statistician or philosopher of science in one of those traditions might object to particular steps I have asked you to include. Wherever a step rests on such a commitment, flag it, name the commitment in a sentence, and sketch what the alternative step would look like for someone who rejects it. Do not present one school's framing as the consensus view, and do not soften the whole document by hedging every line either: I need something my colleagues can actually follow on a Tuesday. Relatedly, please do not append a bibliography or a reading list; I cannot chase it and neither can they. Name a technical concept only if you are also willing to say in one sentence what accepting it commits us to. Skip the familiar textbook illustrations of this puzzle — admissions at a large public university, kidney stones, batting averages. My analysts have all seen them, and the difficulty in my office is not in recognizing the pattern but in knowing what to do once you have.
