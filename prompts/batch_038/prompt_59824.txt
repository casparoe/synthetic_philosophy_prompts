I'm in a seminar on the interpretation of probability and am drafting a short paper on Bertrand's paradox. The chord problem is usually defused by blaming the continuum — infinitely many chords, no canonical measure — so I want a finite case that displays the description-relativity of the principle of indifference without any of that machinery. Please design a chance setup meeting every one of these specifications: finitely many possible outcomes, few enough to enumerate explicitly; no continuous parameters, limits, or measure-theoretic idealizations; two descriptions of one and the same physical arrangement, each complete and neither smuggling in extra information about how the arrangement arose; applying the principle of indifference to whichever alternatives each description makes salient yields different answers to a single, unambiguous question about the setup; and the divergence cannot be dissolved by conditionalizing on evidence stated in the case, by showing that one description misstates the arrangement, or by noticing that the two descriptions are really addressing different questions. Keep it non-anthropic as well: no self-location, no indexicals, and no structure in which naive indifference delivers an answer that correct reasoning simply overrules.

Then demonstrate that the design works. Show the two answers by explicit counting or arithmetic, identify the one feature of the case that generates the divergence and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and give the objection from someone who thinks consistency requirements of the transformation-group or maximum-entropy sort pin down a unique prior — along with the strongest reply available to me as the designer.

Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the setup and the question, (2) the first description and its answer, (3) the second description and its answer, (4) the feature doing the work and what removing it changes, (5) the objection and the reply.
