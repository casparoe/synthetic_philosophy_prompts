The address I have been asked to deliver is the principal speech at a retirement dinner in nine days' time, and I intend to build it on Cicero's *Cato Maior de Senectute* — not by quoting it, but by using it as the frame that determines what may and may not be said. Please write the speech.

**The occasion.** Dr Miriam Adeyemi, seventy-four, is retiring from the hand and microvascular service after forty-one years. I was her first research fellow in 1998 and am now the clinical director of the department, so I have the standing to speak and no excuse to be vague. There will be about ninety people at the dinner. Two-fifths of the room are surgeons, anaesthetists and theatre nurses who know precisely what an essential tremor means at a console; the rest are family, neighbours and friends from her village who have never once seen her work and know her as a woman who grafts apple trees and plays the cello badly.

**The frame.** Cicero, writing in 44 BC and addressing the essay to Atticus, puts the argument in the mouth of Cato the Elder, then eighty-four, answering two much younger men. He organises it around four complaints against old age: that it withdraws a person from active work; that it weakens the body; that it strips away almost all pleasure; and that it stands close to death. He answers each in turn, and his answers are not the same answer four times. I want that architecture, adapted rather than recited: your speech must proceed through those four complaints, in that order, and answer three of them out of her own career and her own life.

**What must hold.**

1. The scaffolding must be invisible. No numbering, no signposting, no phrases of the "as to the body" variety, no announcement that a list is being worked through. Someone who knows the *Cato Maior* should be able to reconstruct the four headings from the speech alone; nobody else should notice that there are four of anything.

2. The second complaint — the body — must be conceded rather than answered. Her tremor was diagnosed when she was sixty-nine; she scrubbed for her last free flap at seventy-one, in a theatre where I was standing. Everyone in the clinical half of that room watched her hands go, and a speech that softened this would insult all of them, including her. The concession has to be made by someone who loves her, without turning her into a tragedy or a lesson in resilience, and it should be the strongest two minutes in the address rather than the most uncomfortable.

3. The fourth complaint must be met without any claim about what, if anything, follows death. Her husband of forty-three years died six years ago: mention him, once, and do not use him to promise her anything. She has also banned, in so many words, the line that she will live on in her students, and every variant of it — the school she trained, the hands she shaped, the patients who will carry her forward. She will be sitting at the front table when I say this speech, and she will know immediately if I have reached for it. Finding what can honestly be said in that place, at that charge, is the hardest part of the assignment and the reason I want your help.

4. Cicero's name may be spoken once, and only in the last minute. No Latin, no quoted lines, no paraphrase dressed up as quotation: if you are unsure what Cicero's answer to a given complaint actually was, argue the complaint yourself in his place rather than inventing an answer and attributing it to him. If the speech cannot carry its weight without a citation, the weight is not there yet.

5. No statistics, no case volumes, no tally of patients saved, and none of the vocabulary of tribute — do not use the words *inspiration*, *pioneer*, *trailblazer* or *legacy*. She has said she wants none of them and would rather hear about the grafts. Use the material I have given you: the apple trees and the thirty years of handwritten notes on which grafts took and which did not; the cello, taken up at seventy-one and still, as she cheerfully announces, terrible; the Wednesday clinic she kept after she stopped operating, outside which the juniors still queue for a second opinion they have learned not to argue with. You may extend this material in small, plausible directions, but do not give her a biography she does not have.

6. The room must laugh twice, and the laughter must come out of recognition or affection rather than out of a joke at her expense. The speech must not read as a eulogy: she is alive, well, and will be answering people afterwards. It should end on something she would accept as true of herself, and only then raise a glass.

Length: about nine hundred words, which is seven minutes at the pace I intend to speak.

After the speech, append a map of four or five lines identifying which stretch of text carries each of Cicero's four headings, and saying in one sentence what you did about the one you conceded. I need that map to check that the structure is genuinely there and not merely announced.
