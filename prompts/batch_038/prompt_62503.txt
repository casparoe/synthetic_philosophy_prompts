I am preparing for a seminar session on the epistemology of thought experiments, and I would like help disambiguating a dismissive one-liner that has done a great deal of unearned work in our discussions: "It's only a thought experiment."

**Context.** Our group is divided between those who maintain that a thought experiment is nothing over and above an argument in picturesque dress, so that its epistemic force is exactly the force of its premises, and those who maintain that at least some thought experiments afford an insight into nature that no argument we could write down captures. Last week the first camp's most vocal member invoked the slogan to reject the conclusion of the tied-bodies argument we usually attribute to Galileo. Here is the version I put on the board:

1. Suppose, for reductio, that in a given medium a body's speed of fall increases with its weight.
2. Let H be a heavy body and L a light one, so that H falls faster than L, and tie them together with a cord.
3. L retards H and H draws L along, so the tied pair falls faster than L alone but slower than H alone.
4. But the tied pair is heavier than H, so by the supposition it ought to fall faster than H.
5. Contradiction; therefore speed of fall does not increase with weight — within the same material in the same medium, all bodies fall alike.

He said two things. First, "it's only a thought experiment, so it cannot tell you anything about how bodies actually fall." Second, pressed on step 3, he offered what I take to be a sharper worry: if H really does fall faster than L, then once they are tied the cord simply goes slack, L trails behind, and nothing retards H at all; the contradiction arises only if we assume from the outset that corded bodies must share a single speed, which looks suspiciously close to assuming the conclusion. Notably, he does not deploy the slogan against the freely falling elevator, whose conclusion — that an observer in free fall is locally unable to distinguish his situation from that of an observer drifting far from any mass — he accepts without complaint.

**What I want from you:**

1. A menu of the distinct claims that "it's only a thought experiment" might be making, ordered from the platitudinous to the radical. I expect these to include claims about the absence of new empirical input, about idealization, about the trustworthiness of our verdicts concerning merely imagined cases, about the gap between conceivability and actuality, about dispensability in favour of a discursive argument, and about whether the conclusion is conceptual rather than worldly — but do not treat that list as exhaustive or as correct. State each as a single sentence that could be true or false, and attach a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, contentious, false, or question-begging, with one or two sentences of justification.

2. For each reading, whether it would actually license rejecting the tied-bodies argument, and what supplementary premises it would require to get there. Flag in particular any reading that would equally license rejecting reductio arguments in mathematics; if our objector accepts reductio elsewhere, that reading is not available to him.

3. Whether any reading can accommodate the asymmetry between the two cases. A reading that dismisses the tied bodies ought, other things equal, to dismiss the elevator as well, unless it turns on some feature present in one and absent in the other. Identify the feature or features such a reading would have to appeal to, and judge whether the appeal is principled or ad hoc.

4. Whether the slack-cord worry is captured by any reading on your menu, or whether it is something of a different kind — a substantive objection to a premise that the slogan merely clothes. If the latter, say what the auxiliary assumption about coupling in step 3 amounts to, and whether the charge of begging the question against the supposition in step 1 succeeds.

5. I have drafted a principle I suspect sits behind the slogan and would like it assessed: a thought experiment can yield knowledge only if we could in principle have predicted the outcome of the imagined case without performing it, in which case the thought experiment does no more than render explicit what we already knew. State this precisely, say whether it is true, and say whether it dissolves the disagreement between our two camps or merely restates it in more technical language.

6. Two or three short questions I can put to him that would force a commitment to one reading rather than allow him to drift among several.

I am not after references or a reading list; I want the analysis itself. Please format the answer as a numbered menu, each item carrying its verdict, followed by short sections addressing points 2 through 6.
