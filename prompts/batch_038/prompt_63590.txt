Can you walk me through St. Paul's 2021 rent control episode as a test of the standard price-ceiling model? Facts I'm working from, correct me if I've garbled them: voters approved a 3% cap on annual rent increases in November 2021, with no exemption for new construction and no vacancy decontrol; developers and a lot of economists warned it would kill building; applications for multifamily permits reportedly fell by something on the order of 80% over the next few months; the ordinance took effect in May 2022; and by September 2022 the city council had amended it, exempting new construction for 20 years and loosening the between-tenancy rules.

Three things I want, in order. First, the textbook mapping, done carefully: who the players are, what's actually binding here and what isnt (was a 3% ceiling even binding given where inflation was running?), what the model predicts separately for existing units versus new supply, and who gains and who loses on each side. Second, and this is the part I care most about: the static ceiling model has no obvious place for the fact that the rollback came in under a year, so I'd like you to redo the analysis with a real-options lens. If the developer's decision is *when* to build rather than whether, then policy uncertainty plus the option to wait means a ceiling that's temporary — or widely expected to be repealed — can suppress investment as much or more than a permanent one, since what kills the project is the variance in possible future caps, not their level. Does that lens do better on the permit numbers, what does it say should have happened after September 2022, and how would you separate that from the interest rate shock hitting construction at the same time? Point out where it's just silent. Third, the political economy and ethics: the usual story is that controls persist because the beneficiaries are concentrated and organized while the costs are diffuse, but here the opposite happened almost immediately — I want an account of why, and what that says about whose interests a price ceiling really serves. Don't force the theory to speak where it can't.
