Nine months from now my extended family holds its reunion, and next spring the company my great-grandfather founded turns one hundred. I have inherited, by default, the job of making something out of the fourteen boxes of his papers that came to me when my grandfather died: ledgers running from 1919 to 1954, some nine hundred letters, a shoebox of photographs, and a diary covering 1931 to 1933 that a local historical society transcribed for me because the shorthand was beyond me.

He arrived with nothing in 1911 and built a coal-and-ice delivery business in a mill town, which became a heating-oil company, which is still in the family. My cousin Dale runs it now, and roughly half the people coming to the reunion work for it or worked for it.

Two things in the boxes complicate the story everyone tells. During the mill strike of 1932 his diary records, without any distress, that he agreed to extend credit only on a cash basis to men whose names the company had sent him, and the ledgers bear this out across eighteen months. And the estate papers show that when his brother died in the 1918 flu he took the larger share of what remained while his sister received almost nothing; her descendants are the poor branch of the family, and three of them are coming to the reunion.

My relatives have each settled on an approach, and the approaches are not compatible.

- My aunt Ruth, who is 81, wants everything catalogued and deposited with the county historical society untouched. Her position is that we are not qualified to judge, that selection is how families begin lying to themselves, and that these boxes have already outlasted everyone who made them.
- Dale wants a twenty-minute presentation at the reunion: the arrival, the horse-drawn wagons, the first truck, the building. He says the strike business is labor history, not family history; that it happened a hundred years ago; and that handing three of our cousins a grievance at a party would be cruel.
- My sister Nora, who teaches a labor history course at a community college, wants the diary and ledgers transcribed and posted with an introduction that names the blacklist, and wants the reunion to include a public acknowledgment to the sister's descendants. Her position is that a family that cannot say what it did will keep doing it.
- My own inclination, which I am somewhat ashamed of, is to deliver the boxes to Ruth's historical society and disappear from the whole thing.

You should also know that I have read everything twice, that I have not been able to work on the project in six weeks, that I sleep badly, and that I have begun to find the boxes themselves unbearable to be near. I cannot tell whether that means I should stop, or that I am the only one of us taking this seriously.

What should I actually do in the next nine months? Tell me which of the four positions is closest to right, where you would overrule it, and what each of them costs if adopted on its own. And there is one fact that all three of my relatives press into service: that my great-grandfather is the reason I exist, the reason I had money for graduate school, and the reason I own a house. Nora says that is precisely why I have to be the one to say it out loud. Dale says it is precisely why I have no standing to. Ruth says it is precisely why I should keep quiet and preserve the paper. Is there anything to choose between those three uses of the same fact, or is it only rhetoric?
