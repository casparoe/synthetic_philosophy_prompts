Last Saturday our department held its end-of-term dinner, and the argument I want you to settle was not mine. It belonged to two other guests: Nell, who has been a vegetarian since she was about twelve, and Peter, who gave up industrial chicken three years ago and now buys a quarter of a pasture-raised steer from a farm two counties over. I spent most of the evening at the other end of the table and have no horse in the race, though I have been a vegetarian for a decade and kept quiet about it; I wrote the exchanges down in my notebook afterwards because I could not decide which of them was doing the better reasoning, and both of them were sharper than the summaries I have seen of this debate. Here is the argument as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

(1) Nell opened with the arithmetic of animal numbers. On Peter's own account, he eats roughly two thousand broilers a year, or the feed-and-flesh equivalent of them, whereas the steer he buys represents a single death every other year. One life extinguished against two thousand, she said, is not a close comparison, and even granting him everything about how bad a broiler's six weeks are, two thousand lifetimes of misery cannot be outweighed by one afternoon in a stun box. (2) Peter said she was counting the wrong things. The harm to a broiler, on his view, lies almost entirely in its life, which is so wretched that death arrives as an improvement; the harm to a steer on good pasture lies almost entirely in its death, since the animal is flourishing right up to the final minute. A practice that kills two thousand animals each of whose lives runs a deficit, he said, is not obviously worse than one that ends the single life of an animal whose ledger has been in the black for two years, and Nell's comparison only goes through if deaths and lives land in the same moral category, which is precisely what he denies. (3) Nell called this a convenience. If a life bad enough makes killing a mercy, she said, then the same is true of a human infant born into misery, and Peter will not accept that; so he is helping himself to an asymmetry between ending a life and declining to start one that his own premises do not deliver, and the argument he actually needs is unavailable to him. (4) Peter said the asymmetry was beside the point, because Nell's own diet is soaked in killing that nobody counts. Wheat, soy, and alfalfa are grown on land worked by machinery: tillage, planting, harvest, and baling run over voles, field mice, ground-nesting birds, and the occasional fawn, and storage and transport finish off more. A bird needs about four and a half pounds of grain to reach slaughter weight, so his two thousand birds imply perhaps two and a half hectares of cropland a year; put even ten small animals killed per hectare per season on it, which he insisted was modest, and Nell's year accounts for twenty-odd deaths she never thinks about, while his beef comes off permanent pasture that has not been tilled in forty years and is harvested by an animal rather than by a tractor. (5) Nell made two replies. Empirically, she said, the per-hectare figures are conjecture assembled from a handful of harvest studies and extrapolated across continents, and once Peter adds the alfalfa and hay his cattle eat over winter, the mowing of that hay, and the fact that pasture has to be established somewhere, his clean contrast between a killing field and an innocent meadow dissolves. Morally, she said, the two are not equivalent in any case: the broiler is bred, confined, and slaughtered as a means to his dinner, whereas the vole under the combine is nobody's means and dies as an unwanted side effect, and if that difference carries no weight at all then he has no ground from which to object to anything. (6) Peter answered that the difference she is invoking sits entirely in his head and not in the vole. Death by header is exactly as bad for a mouse as death by captive-bolt, he said, and a distinction that changes nothing for the victim but only the state of mind of the person paying for it is the wrong kind of distinction to be doing the heavy lifting in an argument about suffering. Nell said that on his own principles he should not want more animals out there at all, since most wild lives end in starvation, parasitism, or being eaten alive, with hundreds of offspring produced for every one adult who survives; so when he praises pasture for sheltering wildlife he is really praising the multiplication of misery, and if he is not, then his position is that more animals is better no matter how bad each of their lives is, which is a view she has read about and finds repellent. Peter said that if wild lives really are that bad, as Nell has just told him, then converting habitat into grazed grassland and cutting wild numbers is a mercy by her own logic, and that her objection is not to his view but to the total view of value she is quietly relying on whenever she adds up deaths. Nell said he was proving her point about consistency rather than answering it: if reducing the number of miserable animals is the goal, he ought to be in favour of paving over the countryside, yet he donates to a land trust and keeps a cat that by his own estimate kills several hundred small animals a year in her garden. Peter said the cat is a separate matter, and Nell said that if he needs it to be a separate matter, he has conceded that her principle does not have to do all the work by itself.

Neither of them would yield after about six rounds, and the evening moved on to cheese. Please take the exchanges in order and tell me who is right at each stage and why, saying where an argument is sound, where it is sound but proves less than its author thinks, and where it simply fails; where a move turns on an estimate, tell me whether the inference survives the estimate being wrong by a factor of five or ten in either direction, since neither of them needs to be believed as stated. Then tell me what Nell and Peter both took for granted that neither of them should have: I suspect at least two things went unexamined that bear on the whole dispute, and I want them named and explained rather than hinted at. Finally, say what the single disagreement underneath all six exchanges is, the one that would have to be settled before any amount of further data about hectares, feed conversion, or field mortality could decide which of them should change his or her diet, and say which of them was closer to being right overall.
