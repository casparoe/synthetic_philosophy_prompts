Build me a thought experiment for a seminar on the cluelessness objection to consequentialism. The case should reverse the usual division of labor: a single decision carries an epistemic externality — it changes how much evidence will later exist about the effects of choices like it — with the result that a Lenman-style cluelessness skeptic ends up holding a determinate verdict against performing it, while an expected-value longtermist must rank it no worse than the alternative. Constraints: realistic institutional setting (a public health agency, a foundation's grants committee, a research consortium); one paragraph of scenario; no time travel, no superintelligence, no reliably accurate predictors; the externality must operate purely through the future availability of evidence rather than as a direct harm to anyone; and the ordinary long-run consequences of both options must remain genuinely opaque, so that the skeptic's verdict is a surprise rather than a setup. The purpose is to test whether cluelessness can ever rank options instead of merely paralyzing, so both verdicts have to fall out of the positions as normally stated — stipulating them in the description of the case is cheating. After the scenario, work through each side's reasoning step by step, identify the feature of the case that does the work, say what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed, and close with the skeptic's best reply to the charge that their view has quietly become outcome-committal.
