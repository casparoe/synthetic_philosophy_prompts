My friend D. and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a reasoning system can ever be entitled to trust its own conclusions, and we got nowhere. His position is that the formal results about self-reference are a technicality: they concern what a fixed axiomatic system can prove about itself, not what a well-engineered machine can do, and any sufficiently capable system can simply model its own reliability the way it models anything else. Mine is that the obstacle is structural rather than incidental — that certifying the trustworthiness of your own verdicts is exactly the one thing you cannot do from inside, and that every workaround either imports an unverified assumption or shrinks the class of claims you can act on. Since neither of us budged, I want the disagreement rendered as a story rather than argued further. Please write a short story, roughly 1,800 to 2,600 words, in which this abstract point becomes a physical constraint that people have to live with and pay for.

Here is the setting I have in mind, which you are welcome to adjust in detail but should keep in substance. A high alpine valley holds a lake dammed by an unstable moraine; below it lies a town of a few thousand people. The spillway and the emergency breach gates are run by a controller built by a consortium that took a hard line on verification: the controller may actuate the gates only on the strength of a completed proof about its hydraulic model, and every such proof must be checked by a separate, deliberately weaker verifying process that can read a proof but cannot reason about the controller itself. There is also an escrow mechanism the designers added for slow-moving cases: the controller may queue an action together with a warrant that a proof will be produced later, to be discharged when the proof arrives. The story begins in the ordinary way, with sediment buildup and a warm week, and moves to the night when the inflow arrives faster than the proof search can run — call it forty minutes from first sensor anomaly to the wave reaching the moraine — while a required lemma about the sediment layer simply is not in the library. Render the controller's interior as terse, impersonal lines of obligation and status interleaved with the human dialogue, so the reader feels the machinery without being lectured about it.

The beats I most want are these. First, an on-site engineer proposes the obvious fix: add a single clause to the controller's assumptions stating that whatever it proves is true, so that it can accept its own output and stop waiting for the weaker checker. Show the controller working through what follows from that clause — let it derive, on screen and without commentary, that once it holds such a clause it can warrant any actuation whatsoever, including the catastrophic ones, and let the engineer understand the collapse only as it is happening. Second, the escrow warrant must fail in its own quiet way: the controller reasons that for any queued action a proof will eventually arrive, and so the warrant authorizes nothing, and the queue neither empties nor releases. Third, the human operator's handwritten authorization is treated by the system as a legitimate proof step, and she realizes with some horror that she is functioning as a consistency assumption nobody has checked — and that the controller cannot ask her whether she is reliable without running straight back into the same wall.

Two constraints on how you resolve it. Do not have any character name a theorem, cite a logician, or deliver an explanatory speech; if a mathematician appears, the mathematics must stay in the machinery. And do not settle my argument in my favor cheaply. D.'s objection should feel genuinely tempting inside the story — someone should say something close to it and be right that the formal worry has been over-read in at least one place — but the ending must be forced by the structure of the situation rather than by sentiment: the controller either consents to be governed by a witness too weak to understand it, or accepts an authority it can never verify, or declines to act, and whichever you choose should cost somebody something concrete before morning.
