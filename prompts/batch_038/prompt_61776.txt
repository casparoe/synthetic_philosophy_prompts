I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years in radiochemistry, roughly half of them at a German national laboratory and half at an American one, and I now chair the six-member Operating Review Panel at a jointly run isotope-production reactor. I have also, rather late in life, taken up reading philosophy, and I hope you will indulge the amateurishness of vocabulary that results.

The case: during a night shift last month, a coolant-flow interlock tripped. Our written procedure requires an immediate manual shutdown and then a wait for the shift supervisor's authorization before any restart. That night the supervisor could not be reached for forty minutes, and the operator judged that following the procedure to the letter would allow a secondary loop to overheat and damage a heat exchanger. She therefore bypassed the interlock in a manner the procedure does not contemplate, restored flow, and brought the reactor down under controlled conditions an hour later. The engineering review has since concluded that her technical judgment was correct: the failure mode she anticipated was credible, there was no release, and no one received any dose. She wrote up the deviation herself before the end of her shift and submitted it unprompted the following morning. Two complications. First, that interlock is designated safety-significant in our licensing basis, and the regulator requires any unapproved bypass to be reported as a non-compliance event on the public docket regardless of outcome; whether to file is not ours to decide. Second, the audit that followed her self-report turned up three unreported deviations from the same procedure over the previous two years, none of them disclosed at the time.

The panel is split, and the split tracks training rather than argument. Our two German-trained members want the deviation reported with a formal reprimand and no mitigating finding: the authority of a procedure cannot depend on how the night turns out, and exceptions are precisely what hollow out a practice, the three silent deviations being in their view evidence that this is already happening. Our two American-trained members want the event reported as required, but with a panel finding that her action was substantively justified, a commendation for self-reporting, and a directive to rewrite the procedure: a system that punishes correct judgment under pressure will simply stop hearing about it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It must recommend one disposition from among: (i) report with reprimand and no mitigating finding; (ii) report with a justification finding, commendation for the self-report, and a directive to revise the procedure; (iii) report with no personnel action of any kind; or (iv) some other course you can defend. Declining to report is not available to us. Give reasons that a member on the losing side could actually answer, rather than a restatement of the conclusion.

Two things I want the memo to do beyond the disposition. First, take the cultural contrast seriously rather than as decoration. I have read that German moral thinking puts a premium on order and on the binding force of rules while American thinking prizes the individual conscience that overrides them, but I suspect the caricature is wrong in an instructive way, since postwar German law is anything but naive about obedience: I have come across Radbruch's argument that statutes which are grossly unjust forfeit their character as law, the constitutional right of resistance written into the Basic Law in the late 1960s, and the civil-service practice of remonstration, whereby an official who believes an instruction unlawful must object to his immediate superior, escalate if the objection is dismissed, and is thereafter protected once responsibility has moved up the chain. Tell me what the German position actually commits one to in this case, what the American position commits one to, and which of them has the better of it here — or whether the disagreement is in the end an empirical one about which incentive structure produces safer operators, in which case say what evidence would settle it and whether we have any. Second, assess honestly whether a remonstration-style channel could work in a control room, where an objection must be acted on in minutes rather than days, and where I understand the German channel is in practice seldom used because nobody wishes to be known as a troublemaker.

Finally, tell me where you think your own recommendation is most likely to fail. I spent a career asking where an instrument's calibration breaks down and which measurement would expose it; I want the same of this argument. Say which consideration, if it came out otherwise, would move you to a different disposition, and which single step in your reasoning you regard as weakest.
