Last night I had a long and unproductive argument with a friend, and I now think the disagreement was never really about the cases at all: it was about a proverb that my friend kept deploying as though it settled them. The proverb is "when in Rome, do as the Romans do" — traditionally traced, I believe, to advice Ambrose is said to have given Augustine and Monica about whether to fast on a Saturday, since Rome fasted and Milan did not. My friend has lived in several countries and holds that this is a moral requirement on visitors rather than a piece of travel advice. Three cases were in play, and I am not convinced they are cases of the same kind.

The first was tipping. My friend's view is that a visitor to a city where restaurant staff are paid less than a living wage and depend on gratuities is obliged to tip at the local rate even if the visitor thinks the arrangement exploitative, on the grounds that the visitor has voluntarily taken up the benefits of a scheme and cannot refuse its burdens. The second was jaywalking. My friend maintained that a visitor who waits at a red signal at an empty intersection, while every local crosses, is also at fault — not exactly immoral, but guilty of a failure of respect, a sort of tourist's moral preening, because the practice has settled what counts as prudent and safe there and the visitor is treating local judgement as beneath them. The third was mine, and I think it unsettled things. In a city I visited recently, the practice at a busy counter is an apparently formless scrum in which precedence is tracked entirely by everybody's memory of who arrived and by subtle acknowledgements; there is no visible line. When I attempted to form one, the scrum collapsed into shoving and several people lost their places. My friend's response was that I had destroyed a working coordination solution and thereby harmed people, which is precisely what the proverb warns against. I said that a regularity with no queue in it cannot oblige me to join one, and that a scrum is not a scheme whose benefits I have accepted; my friend said I was splitting hairs.

What I want, therefore, is the proverb taken apart rather than explained. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that this single sentence could be making. I expect there to be several, and I expect them to differ along lines that matter: is the claim epistemic (locals know things about local risk, cost and meaning that a visitor cannot acquire in a week); is it about fairness to a cooperative scheme one has joined; is it about consent, and if so, what kind of presence could constitute it; is it about the expressive content of deviation, so that non-conformity communicates contempt whether or not one feels any; is it about deference to a community's right to settle its own terms of interaction, which would make the visitor's objection a kind of imperialism of judgement; or is it merely prudential advice about blending in, with no normative content at all? For each reading, please state the conditions that would have to hold for it to be true, and say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or straightforwardly false. Distinguish throughout between readings that generate a content-independent obligation to conform and readings that give only a reason, which may be outweighed, and note whether the duty in question is special to visitors or falls on locals equally.

Then apply the menu to the three cases: which readings support my friend on tipping, which support them on jaywalking, which support them on the scrum, and whether any single reading supports all three without qualification. Please also address the scope of "Rome" — how long a stay, how repugnant a practice, whether it reaches practices that harm only third parties, and whether the proverb is self-undermining in places where the local practice is precisely to expect something different of visitors, so that doing as the Romans do means doing what the Romans do not do themselves. Finally, tell me which reading my friend was most likely invoking at each point, and whether, on any of them, my objection that the cases are disanalogous really is hair-splitting.

Please argue from the concepts rather than from citations: no bibliography, no reading list, and no invented references, though you may name a position or its adherents where that genuinely clarifies. Write the whole response in British English.
