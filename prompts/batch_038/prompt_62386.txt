I report on science and ideas for a national general-interest magazine, and this term I am one of two staff members sitting on our Editorial Standards Panel: five people in all, being the managing editor, a senior editor, a retired foreign correspondent, an outside ethicist who teaches journalism ethics, and me. The panel adjudicates contested publication decisions and, once a year, sets standing policy. Both jobs have landed on the same agenda, which is why I am writing to you rather than to a lawyer.

The case concerns a staff reporter's story alleging that a well-known behavioral-science laboratory falsified data across three published papers. Here is what we actually hold. A single anonymous source, a former postdoc in that lab, supplied a partial set of raw data files in which the same improbable values recur across participants in a pattern that a data analyst we retained describes as consistent with copy-paste imputation. The source will not go on the record or sign a statement, on the grounds that their work authorization runs through the institution and is up for renewal in eleven weeks. Two other former lab members, approached on background, declined to speak; one offered only that the allegation did not surprise them. The principal investigator, queried in writing, returned a detailed and internally coherent methodological account of the duplicated values, involving an imputation step during preprocessing. Of the two outside statisticians we consulted, one judged that this account would fully explain the pattern; the other said he could not assess it without the original un-imputed files, which the PI has not provided. The story is slated to run in nine days, and I travel to a conference on replication methods in three weeks, so I would like this settled before I go. The panel currently stands two for publication, two against, with the chair inclined to defer; she has indicated she would consider a conditional recommendation if the conditions were checkable.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate ahead of the meeting, on the order of 700 words. It should close on an actionable bottom line — run it, kill it, or run it subject to named conditions — with a deadline and an explicit statement of which new fact, arriving before that deadline, would flip the call. And because the panel is simultaneously being asked to adopt a standing policy on anonymous and unverifiable testimony, the memo should say what general rule this case ought to set: whether a source's account carries a presumption in its favor until we can articulate concrete reasons for doubt, or whether we may count it as evidence only when we can point to positive independent grounds — a demonstrated track record, convergent material, an explanation that survives hostile scrutiny. The outside ethicist will recognize the shape of a very old argument sitting behind that choice; you may name the two positions if it clarifies things, but I would rather you describe them than attribute them, and I want no reading list. The reasoning must be usable by working editors, not by specialists.

Two complications the memo should engage rather than flatten. First, the source's history with our reporter: two earlier tips that were confirmed and ran, and one that was partly wrong about a timeline and had to be quietly amended. Second, the source's incentives cut both ways, and the panel will expect both readings — accusing the PI jeopardizes a renewal the source needs, which suggests they would not fabricate it; but their contract was not extended this spring, which supplies a grievance. Note also that the asymmetry of harm is severe in each direction: a false fabrication charge ends a career and no correction undoes it, while suppression lets a practice continue across papers nobody has examined and leaves our source exposed to being discredited later by someone better resourced than we are. Give the losing side its strongest form; I have no use for a memo that reads as though the case were easy.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in your own recommendation — a rough probability that it is the right call given exactly what I have described, not given some better-informed version of the facts — and single out the one step in your reasoning you are least sure of, and say why it is shaky.
