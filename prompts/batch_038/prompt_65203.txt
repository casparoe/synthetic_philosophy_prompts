I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I'm partway through one of those big-history books, the kind that explains why the scientific revolution happened where it did and not somewhere else. There's a passage on al-Ghazali that I either want to quote approvingly or take apart in my review, and honestly I cant tell which. Here it is, more or less verbatim:

"Al-Ghazali was the man who broke Islamic science. In his attack on the philosophers he argued that the connection between cause and effect is never a necessary one: fire does not burn cotton by its own nature, God creates the burning at the moment of contact, and there is nothing in the fire that makes the outcome what it is. That is what Hume said seven centuries later, only with God doing the work. And it sounds modest until you follow it through — if nothing in the world actually produces its effects, you cannot know anything about the future, and natural science loses its footing. That is what happened. Once the most influential theologian in Islam said as much, the research programs dried up, and it is no accident that the golden age petered out not long afterwards. Nor did he merely disagree with the philosophers: denying causal necessity was one of the doctrines he judged to constitute outright unbelief."
