You are, for this exercise, a philosopher who works at the intersection of social ontology and ordinary-language analysis, and who is used to taking a sentence that has been doing unofficial work in an argument and separating out the several claims it might be making. Please think carefully, and before you commit to anything, spend half a dozen lines brainstorming candidate readings — including ones that strike you as marginal — so that the final menu is not simply the four obvious ones.

Last night I had a very long argument with a friend, a solicitor, about whether the moral cultures taxonomy he had picked up from a popular sociological book — the one that sorts societies into honour cultures, dignity cultures and victimhood cultures, and treats the complaints procedures, microaggression reporting and safe-space arrangements on our campuses as evidence of a third type — licenses the verdict he wants, namely that this is decline rather than change. I pressed him on whether the three categories are genuinely comparable types or a dignified norm dressed up as a neutral baseline, and on whether the notion that insult cannot diminish one's standing is a fact about persons or an artefact of a culture in which standing is supposed to be internal. Around one in the morning, having run out of argument, he recited the playground rhyme — 'Sticks and stones may break my bones / But words will never hurt me' (he used the variant with 'names' in the second line) — and said that this settles it, because every child once knew that words cannot harm, so anyone who asks an institution to take notice of a remark has either been harmed by nothing at all and is inventing an injury, or has simply abandoned a truth available to a seven-year-old. I am confident that something has gone wrong, but the sentence has a certain stubborn plausibility and I could not isolate where the work is being done.

What I want from you is not a defence of either side of the culture-wars question, and not an adjudication of which moral culture is superior; it is a disambiguation of that one sentence. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims the rhyme could be expressing, running from the trivial to the radical. I have in mind — but do not confine yourself to — a causal claim about whether speech produces harm; a claim about what counts as harm, i.e. whether distress or humiliation is a setback to one's interests or merely an unpleasant state; a claim about where standing lives, and hence whether an insult can damage something that its target's own attitudes do not control; a second-personal injunction to the child being taunted rather than a report of fact; a performative strategy, in which publicly declaring indifference is a means of making it true; a claim about comparative severity that concedes bodily harm in the first line and so is really a threshold claim about when third parties or institutions may intervene; and a claim about who is entitled to complain. For each reading, state whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a substantive and contentious one, or simply false, and say briefly what would have to be the case for it to hold. Then tell me which reading or readings my friend's inference actually requires — the one that would carry him from words to institutional redress — and whether that reading is among the true ones. Finally, address the framework question directly: could the rhyme be true within a dignity culture and false within an honour culture, where standing is publicly conferred and therefore publicly diminishable, and if so, what follows about its availability as a neutral premise in a comparison between the two? I do not want a bibliography or any reference list; speak of positions and arguments rather than sources and I will supply the citations myself. Format the answer as numbered readings, each labelled R1, R2, and so on, each carrying a bracketed tag — [platitude], [substantive-defensible], [substantive-contentious] or [false] — followed by two or three sentences. End with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: R_ — [tag] — supports/does not support the inference, where the blank identifies the reading that my friend's argument needs.
