I'm a fourth-year in philosophy of science and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at our department's work-in-progress seminar. The thesis: the pessimistic meta-induction has been run on the wrong target. Instead of totting up which theoretical posits got thrown out (the usual Laudan-style list of dead entities), I run the induction over the explanatory work a false theory's predictive success actually rested on. The punchline is that the realist's answer — success is explained by approximate truth — requires knowing which parts of a theory are the load-bearing ones, and that's only ever determinable retrospectively. So the selective realist's move, keep the working parts and dump the rest, can't be made at the time it needs to be made, which is now, for the theory in front of us. That's the whole paper.

The room: about fifteen people. Two faculty who are selective realists and will be irritated. One structural realist who thinks he solved my problem decades ago and may well be right. A historian of chemistry who thinks philosophers' use of phlositon and caloric as counterexamples is anachronistic garbage. A hard instrumentalist grad student who'll say the whole fight is pointless and we both lose. Another grad student working on underdetermination who will want to know why I'm not just reinventing that argument. Nobody else does history of physics.

Give me a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the view. I want the objections this particular room will raise, in their own vocabulary — not generic "critics of realism say" phrasing — ordered by how much damage each does if I fumble it. For each one: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a blunt note on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or quietly concedes the point. Then tell me what to concede up front in the opening so I'm not conceding it under fire, and name the one question I should be praying nobody asks — I have a feeling it comes out of the structural realist's corner, but I can't see what my answer would be. Finally, if you think there's a hole in the thesis that none of these people are positioned to find, say so.
