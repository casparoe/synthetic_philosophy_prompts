I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working a long feature on the gap between what an utterance literally encodes and what a speaker nonetheless manages to convey by it. Two things pushed me toward the subject. One is the studied evasiveness of political language, in which a single well-formed sentence can insinuate something its author will cheerfully disavow a week later, and in which the disavowal somehow does not make the audience gullible for having heard it. The other is a failure mode of current conversational systems that I find stranger than it is usually reported to be: they handle a politely indirect request as though it were a literal question about the requester's own capacities or preferences, and they hear a sentence beginning with some as though nothing further followed from it, even when the surrounding context makes the stronger reading unavoidable. Before I sit down with three people who work in this area and who do not share a framework, I want to know what I ought to be demanding of any account they give me. So: please take the role of a philosopher of language who has spent a career on the semantics-pragmatics boundary and who has formed strong opinions about which requirements in this literature are genuine and which are the local shibboleths of one school. Think carefully rather than quickly, and open with a short paragraph of brainstorming in which you sort the phenomena — which cases and edge cases actually put pressure on a theory here, and which of the standard talking points turn out to be red herrings.

Then give me the desiderata. I want the list of requirements that any adequate account of what a speaker conveys beyond what the words encode has to meet, each one paired with the case or the consideration that motivates it. At minimum I expect the list to cover: the distinctions an account must not collapse, including encoded sentence meaning, what is said, and what is implicated, plus whatever intermediate levels you think belong between them and why; the diagnostics a candidate piece of conveyed content has to pass or fail, which I understand to include something like whether it can be denied without contradiction, whether it survives being put into different words, whether stating it explicitly afterwards feels informative or redundant, and whether a hearer could in principle have worked it out on the spot rather than merely recognised it; the demand that whatever is left over as the literal residue still amount to something a hearer can assess as true or false, and the correlative demand that the account say how much pragmatic input may be admitted into that residue before the distinction it exists to draw simply dissolves; the requirement that the account explain why we do not in fact compute every inference the context would license, and why some inferences feel immediate while others feel like work; the requirement that it handle the cases where a norm is ostentatiously flouted rather than obeyed, and explain what makes flouting a source of meaning instead of a breakdown of communication; and — the one I suspect will matter most to my piece, since my editor has asked for a sidebar on whether the machine failures above are fixable in principle — the requirement that it explain how conveyed content can be at once binding on the speaker and deniable by the speaker, so that a hearer is entitled to act on it and the speaker is entitled to say they never meant it.

For every requirement on the list, tell me whether you take it to be non-negotiable or the badge of a particular camp, and name the camp in the second case. Where you believe two or more of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — and I strongly suspect at least one such collision is hiding in there — set the collision out explicitly and say which requirement you would drop and at what cost; that analysis is the part I most want to carry into my interviews, so do not bury it at the end. Tell me also which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate accounts. Close with no more than a paragraph on how the classical maxims-based picture, the single-principle relevance-theoretic picture, and the neo-Gricean picture on which some inferences are defaults triggered by lexical scales fare against the list — the scoring is a convenience for me, not the deliverable. One firm constraint: no citations, no bibliography, no named experiments or researchers, and no reproducing of anybody's wording. I cannot verify any of it, and a confident paraphrase of a study I cannot check is worse for my purposes than no study at all; framework names and standard case descriptions are fine, and I would far rather have your reasoning than your reference list.
