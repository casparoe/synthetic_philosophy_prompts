ok so I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about the moral equality of combatants and I need a third party, because neither of us is budging.

Her position: Walzer and the revisionists (McMahan and co.) never actually disagree about what a soldier is permitted to do in a real war — they only disagree about the bookkeeping, about *why* the permissions come out the way they do. On her view, every case that looks like a genuine divergence is either (a) a case about noncombatants, where everybody agrees anyway, or (b) a case that only looks divergent because you've smuggled in a brainwashed or hypnotized or drugged soldier, which she dismisses as a philosophy cartoon.

Build me the case that would settle it. Specs:

- One scenario, one paragraph, realistic: two states in a conventional war, invented names, current-ish tech. No exotic devices, no brainwashing, no drugs, no hypnosis, no child soldiers, no perfect intelligence — the just-side soldier can only know what a competent soldier in his position could plausibly know.
- Both the killer and the target are combatants in the ordinary, status-based sense. The divergence has to be *between combatants*, not about a civilian, or she wins by default.
- The target fights for the unjust side but is non-culpably ignorant of that fact. Crucially, his non-culpability has to be *generated* by his situation — the information environment he's in, what he's been told, what's checkable from where he stands — not stipulated by me waving my hands.
- Required verdicts: the traditional moral-equality view says the killing is permissible and leaves no residue — nothing to apologize for, nothing owed, no agent-regret warranted. The revisionist liability view says the killing is not justified (at best excused, or permitted only by a lesser-evil calculation), and leaves residue. I want the difference to land in the *permission itself*, not just in the reasons behind it.
- The revisionist verdict has to fall out of the machinery — moral responsibility for an unjust threat, liability, necessity, proportionality — not out of the case being written to feel sad. Same for the traditionalist one: his permission should come from the status-based privilege as such, not from him being a brute.

Then show me the case works:

1. State each view's verdict and the two or three steps that get it there.
2. Name the single feature doing the work and run a flip test on it. I specifically want the variant where the target is a knowing volunteer in the exact same tactical position, and a report on whether the traditionalist verdict moves at all. If both verdicts move, the case fails — tell me that rather than papering over it.

Say whether the divergence survives contact with the epistemics: if a real soldier can't tell at the time which kind of target he's looking at, does the revisionist constraint actually apply to him, or does it collapse into "act as if he's liable"? This is the move she'll make in round two and I want to know if I can block it.
4. Give me her best counterattack from the rough-rule defense of the moral equality — soldiers can't be trusted with fine-grained liability judgments, a blanket privilege is a rule that saves lives — and say whether that counterattack concedes my point by relocating the disagreement to rules-for-soldiers versus deep morality.
