The question, as it appeared on the sheet:

> **Q3 (20 points).** (a) State the Ship of Theseus puzzle as it arises for someone who holds that ordinary objects are wholly present at each moment of their existence, and say what name that view goes by. Explain what the reassembly variant — the one where the discarded timbers are kept and put back together into a second vessel — adds to the gradual-replacement case that gradual replacement on its own does not already raise. (b) Explain the perdurantist's answer to the puzzle. (c) Say whether you think that answer resolves the puzzle or merely restates it in a new vocabulary, and give at least one reason.

The student's answer, reproduced in full with their paragraph breaks:

> (a) The view that an object is wholly present at each moment of its history is four-dimensionalism, aka endurantism, and the puzzle assumes it. Theseus's ship leaves the harbor and over thirty years every plank, rope and sail is replaced as it rots, one at a time, so that at no moment does anything happen that looks like destruction. The endurantist is then stuck: either the ship sitting in the harbor at the end is numerically identical to the one that sailed out, though it shares no matter with it, or it isn't, in which case the ship ceased to exist at some particular moment and there is no principled way to say which one. Plutarch already gives us both halves of the case, since he has the Athenians keep the old timbers as they're pulled out and rebuild a second ship from them. That second version is the real puzzle. With replacement alone you can bite the bullet and insist the ship survives, because continuity was never interrupted. But now there are two candidates in the same harbor and each has a claim — one has the unbroken history, one has the original matter — and they cannot both be identical to the original, since they're not identical to each other.
>
> Leibniz's Law says that two things are identical just in case they have all the same properties. The rebuilt ship has the property of being made out of the original timbers. The repaired ship does not have that property. So neither of them is the original ship, and identity has broken down.
>
> (b) The perdurantist denies that objects are wholly present at a moment. A ship is a four-dimensional worm made of temporal parts, one for each instant of its career, in the same way a road is made of spatial parts. Persistence is not strict identity through time but the having of temporal parts at different times. So there's no contradiction: the original ship is the whole worm, and the stages in the harbor are parts of it. Gradual replacement is unproblematic because successive stages stand in the right immanent-causal relation, and the two candidate ships are simply two distinct worms that happen to share their earlier stages.
>
> (c) I think this restates the puzzle. The perdurantist still owes an account of which aggregate of temporal parts the name picks out, and there are many aggregates with equally good claims — one that stops at the last original plank, one that continues into the repaired vessel, one that jumps across to the rebuilt one. Choosing one of these as the ship is exactly the endurantist's problem relocated from objects to sums. So four-dimensionalism buys logical consistency at the cost of conceding that which sum a name picks out is not settled by the metaphysics itself.
