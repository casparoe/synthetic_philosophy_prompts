QUESTION: In the Philosophy of Right Hegel says that war has an "ethical moment." Explain (a) what war does, on Hegel's account, for the state and for the individual citizen; (b) how his account of the relations between states bears on Kant's proposal for a league of nations; and (c) whether the resulting position is best described as realism about interstate relations, a moral endorsement of war, or something else. Distinguish carefully between the steps in Hegel's argument that are conceptual (i.e. that follow from his account of sovereignty and of the state as an individual) and the steps that are empirical or historical.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Hegel's claim about the "ethical moment" in war is developed in the part of the Philosophy of Right on the state's sovereignty vis-à-vis foreign states, around §321–324. The state is for Hegel an actual individual, and individuality means being a one to the exclusion of other ones; so a state is essentially a state among other states, each autonomous in relation to the rest, and war is where this shows up in the world. In war the state's "absolute power against everything individual and particular, against life, property, and their rights" is exercised, and the nullity of those finite things is brought home to consciousness. That is the ethical moment: property and life, which inside civil society look like the whole point of the arrangement, are revealed as finite and as moments of a larger substantial order, and the citizen's readiness to risk them is what Hegel calls a substantive duty. He is explicit that the state cannot be understood as a device for securing life and property, since that security could not possibly be bought by sacrificing the very things it is meant to secure.

The passage everyone quotes is the comparison in the Addition to §324, where Hegel says that "the blowing of the winds preserves the sea from the foulness which would be the result of a prolonged calm," and that corruption in nations would be the product of a prolonged, let alone perpetual, peace. I want to stress that this is not decoration or an afterthought; its the conceptual core of the whole discussion. It follows from the concept of sovereignty that a state which never had to assert its independence against another would stop being a state at all, because its members would come to treat private satisfaction as ultimate. So Hegel's anti-pacifism is an a priori result, not a conjecture about how history happens to go.

This is also where Kant comes in. Kant wanted a federation of free states that would secure perpetual peace by adjudicating disputes between them. Hegel agrees that this is a noble ideal and that it would be desirable if realized, but denies that it can be realized, because human beings are naturally contentious and because states will not in practice subordinate their interests to a shared tribunal. Hegel is therefore a realist in the familiar sense: interstate relations are governed by interest and force rather than by law, and war is a morally neutral instrument that states deploy in pursuit of their interests. What makes him distinctive is only that he adds the health-of-the-sea point on top — perpetual peace would be bad for us even if we could have it. It is also why he needs the later claim that world history is the world's court of judgment: since no tribunal exists above states, the judgment gets carried out through which peoples succeed and which founder.
