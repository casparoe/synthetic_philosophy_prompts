I'm doing a seminar this term on Buddhist philosophy of mind as part of my philosophy degree, and we've been on personal identity for a few weeks now — anatta, and how it's supposed to square with rebirth and karma. Our text this week was the Yamaka Sutta (SN 22.85) in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation, and there's a stretch of it I keep going around in circles on. Sariputta has already got Yamaka to agree that none of the five agggregates is properly regarded as mine, as self, or as what I am, and then he runs what looks like an exhaustive list of ways you might construe the Tathagata:

> "How do you construe this, my friend Yamaka: Do you regard form as the Tathagata?" ... "What do you think: Do you regard the Tathagata as being in form?... Elsewhere than form?..." ... "Do you regard the Tathagata as form-feeling-perception-fabrications-consciousness?" "Do you regard the Tathagata as that which is without form, without feeling, without perception, without fabrications, without consciousness?"

and then the line I can't parse:

> "And so, my friend Yamaka — when you can't pin down the Tathagata as a truth or reality even in the present life — is it proper for you to declare, 'As I understand the Teaching explained by the Blessed One, a monk with no more effluents, on the break-up of the body, is annihilated, perishes, & does not exist after death'?"

A bit earlier in the same passage it also says, "With release, there is the knowledge, 'Released.'" So here's what I'm stuck on. My hunch, which I want you to test rather than just confirm, is that Sariputta is doing something like a Russell-style presupposition failure move: "the Tathagata" is a description that picks out nothing, so Yamaka's annihilation claim isn't false, it's malformed — the sutta is refusing to let a question get off the ground rather than answering it. But the "even in the present life" bit seems to make this much stronger than Parfit, who says the identity-over-time question is empty while still happily allowing that persons exist; here the failure to pin down is supposed to hold now, not just across death. So does this collapse into a Parfitian reductionism (persons are real conventionally, identity facts are empty), or does it go all the way to eliminativism about persons (there is no such thing as a Tathagata, or a person, at all)? And if it's the latter, how is anyone released, and who has the knowledge "Released"? Is that a straightforward inconsistency, or does the text just switch registers between conventional and ultimate ways of talking?

Two more specific things I'd like you to deal with, because they're the ones my tutor will jump on. First, is the list actually exhaustive, and what is the logical shape of it — identity with an aggregate, being located in one, being located apart from one, being the whole set, being stripped of the set? Does the shape matter, or would the argument work just as well with two options instead of five? Second, and this is the one I think is seperate from the metaphysics: "Tathagata" is a title for an awakened being, not an ordinary word for a person. Does the passage only show something special about arahants — that they're unlocatable in a way you and I aren't — or is the move meant to generalise to any person whatsoever? If it doesn't generalise, then it can't be doing the work anatta needs it to do in an argument about persons.
