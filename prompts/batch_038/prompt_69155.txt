So, context: I run the grants program at a small family foundation — about $9M a year, four board meetings, I write the recommendation memos and there are usually 30-40 finalists per cycle. The board is five people, none of them with any philosophy training (one of them took an ethics course in 1994 and brings it up constantly). We score finalists on a weighted rubric, five criteria, points out of 100, and the weights were set at some point in the late 2000s and have never been revisited. I'll admit up front that we sometimes pick the weights that make the answer come out the way we already wanted and then let the rubric take the credit, so I don't have a lot of standing to complain about fake precision — but I do want to stop doing it by accident. What's actually been bugging me is a pattern I've started calling the tiebreaker tell. Somebody on staff will say two finalists are incommensurable, different kinds of good, you can't compare them — and then within ten minutes someone proposes a totally arbitrary tiebreaker (which executive director we like more, whichever one we funded last cycle, one time literally a coin flip) and the room just accepts it. Nobody winces. If the values really couldn't be compared, settling it with a coin flip should feel like a category error, and it never does. My hunch is that most of our incommensurability talk is a label for something else and I can't tell which something else it is. For calibration on where I'm coming from: I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the sequences era, so my reflex is that everything is comparable and this is a modeling failure. But I've also read enough of the other side — Raz on incommensurability, Chang on parity and that move where you improve one option slightly and see whether it now clearly wins, Broome saying the whole thing is really just vagueness, Anderson arguing that cost-benefit is a normative mistake rather than a technical one — to know that "just pick a metric" is not obviously right. If I've garbled any of those, tell me rather than working around it.

What I want is a triage procedure my staff can run on a contested comparison before we try to resolve it. The deliverable is an ordered set of tests, in the form a non-philosopher can actually execute in a 90 minute meeting: what you ask, what a sufficient answer looks like, and what the answer tells you about which kind of hard choice you're in. It needs to sort cases into at least the buckets I suspect exist — genuinely incomparable, comparable but on a par, just vague about where the boundary is, comparable in principle but not measurable precisely enough to rank, and comparable but where measuring would itself be the wrong move — and I want you to be explicit about which of those distinctions survives the translation into procedure and which ones I should throw out because nobody on my team will apply them consistently. Each bucket should end somewhere different: rank them and stop pretending it's hard; no ranking is available, hand it to the board as a values call and here's what the memo should say; or the rubric's numbers are the wrong instrument but a comparison is still available and here's how to make it without inventing points. I especially want a probe for the tiebreaker tell — does accepting an arbitrary tiebreaker actually count as evidence against parity, or can a real parity case absorb one without anything going wrong? My guess is the second, and I think that's where I'm wrong, but show me the argument either way. And flag every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume, including the ones you can't help but include.

Then run it on three live cases. (a) An eviction-defense legal clinic in our county that keeps roughly 400 households housed a year, run by three lawyers, no scaling story whatsoever, versus a program in a neighboring state that trains high schoolers to monitor and restore streams — maybe 120 kids a year, small direct numbers, but the program officer insists the alumni go into the field and it compounds; staff keeps calling these different kinds of good and refusing to rank them. (b) Two grants in the same disease area, same intervention type, two different countries, cost-effectiveness from the same model with wide overlapping intervals and the better one about 1.4x on the point estimate — I want to know if this is a hard choice at all or just noise we're treating as sacred. (c) A local arts-and-outreach program the board chair's late mother founded, $150k a year, funding it is a standing expectation nobody wants to be the one to break, versus a much duller workforce program that is clearly better on expected value per dollar and clearly worse in every way anyone in the room can articulate. For each: which bucket, which test decided it, what the memo says, what the board does next. At least one of these has to come out against what I want — my prior is that (c) is my real incommensurability case and that the procedure should tell me it isn't, and if you think my prior is wrong, argue with it instead of flattering it. Finish with what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire in practice, and with how I keep the chair's insistence on a ranked list with a dollar score attached from turning back into fake precision. No reading list, please — I've got the names, what I don't have is the operational version.
