I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a magazine piece about how much moral difference the justice of a war makes to the individual soldier. For background I've been talking to a staff-college ethicist — a retired field officer — who defends the traditional symmetrism by analogy to the formal duel under a code of honor. His version is unusually detailed: both principals enter voluntarily; their seconds settle the weapons and terms before anyone is shot; the honor community recognizes the code and enforces it, so refusing a lawful challenge costs you standing; the affair can end at first blood or an apology rather than a corpse; the seconds and the surgeon stand by but do not fight and are not targets, because they are not principals; and afterward the community judges only whether the duel was properly conducted, never whether the quarrel was worth having. From this he draws three claims: that soldiers on both sides are moral equals, so the justice of the cause has no bearing on the permissibility of shooting; that noncombatant immunity falls out exactly as the immunity of the seconds and the surgeon does; and that we should no more blame a career or conscripted soldier for fighting than call a duelist a murderer for accepting a challenge his society insisted upon.

My editor is skeptical on one point: a duelist helps write the code he is judged by and opts into the risk, whereas an invading army's private has done neither, and the people he shoots have accepted nothing.

Audit the analogy for me. Map it feature by feature — which element of the duel is doing the work for each of his three claims — then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten those claims from the merely decorative ones, and name what the duel picture quietly imports that war lacks: consent, a shared authority that recognizes the institution, a stopping rule, honor as compensation for risk, the absence of bystanders. Tell me whether each claim survives, and if some better analogy captures what is genuinely right in his intuition, give it and say what it costs. I'd rather not get a balanced tour of the debate: commit to which of the three claims the analogy can carry and which it cannot, and give your reasons.
