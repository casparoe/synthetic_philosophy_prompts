I'm on a small applied-RL team and for the last year we've had this running argument that started as a joke and stopped being one: could the agents we train ever be in a state that deserves the word suffering? I know there's a whole adjacent debate about the moral status of AI systems and whether the labs should be worrying about it, but the hinge that keeps coming up for us is narrower and more technical. It's the claim that the reward signal in reinforcement learning plays the same functional role that hedonic valence plays in animals, so an agent that reliably avoids, escapes, and pays costs to prevent negative reward is doing something structuraly like suffering — and the reply that reward is just an engineered instrumental quantity, a designer's grading rubric, so the whole thing is a pun on the word reward. Both sides have been making their case for a while now and nobody moves, and when I read them side by side the arguments don't even seem to be touching.

Here's roughly what each camp actually says, as far as I can reconstruct it. The valence side points out that animal welfare science already treats functional markers as evidence — aversive conditioning that generalizes, escape and avoidance, trading a bad outcome off against hunger or safety, suppression of unrelated behavior — and that if an artificial system shows the same profile there's no principled reason the substrate should matter; they also note that in deep RL the reward isn't purely external to the agent, since it gets compiled into the value function that drives the policy at every step. The skeptic side says reward is dense and specified by us, often only entering through advantage estimates so the policy never receives anything reward-shaped directly; that reward hacking shows agents will sit in absurd loops farming a signal in ways no hedonic system would, which suggests the analogy is broken at the root; that the "optimal reward" line of work (Singh, Lewis and Barto's Where Do Rewards Come From?, 2009) already made the point that the reward function which best serves an organism's actual success need not look anything like the task's own payoff, so even in biology reward is better read as a designer's instrument than as a read-out of what's good; and that all of this leaves the explanatory gap untouched, since functional role tells you nothing about whether there's experience at all, let alone negative experience. Notice that the two sides agree on essentially every empirical fact about what RL agents do, and I think they'd both agree that if a system genuinely experienced something bad, that would matter. So it's not a factual dispute and it's not a dispute about whether bad experience would matter.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than endorse: "suffering" is being made to answer at least three separate questions — whether there is phenomenal experience at all, whether the system has interests or a good of its own that can be harmed (which some people think doesn't require consciousness), and what we as engineers should actually do about training setups, shutdowns, and which objectives we pick — and the camps slide between them constantly, so a skeptic who has only answered the first question sounds like they've dismissed the third. Layered on top of that, I suspect the deeper disagreement is about evidential status: whether a functional profile is constitutive of or reliably diagnostic of valence, or whether it's fully explained away by the training objective and therefore non-diagnostic — and neither side argues for its stance on that independently, they just assume it and it does all the work. Tell me whether that's right, wrong, or only half the story, and if you think the real fault line is somewhere else — a hidden empirical question, a disagreement about what counts as data, or a question that's just ill-posed — say so with reasons drawn from what each side actually commits to. Don't survey the consciousness literature and don't give me any references, reading lists, or bibliography; I know you can't look things up and I don't want invented ones.
