I TA a 12-person upper-level seminar on probability and minds, one 90-minute session. They all know basic Bayes (priors, conditioning, the Dutch book argument) and none of them have ever heard of logical uncertainty. I want an in-class betting game that makes them feel why "just be a good Bayesian" comes apart when the thing you're uncertain about is a mathematical fact that nobody in the room has time to work out.

Here's the thing I want them to run into by playing, and it must NOT get named until after: there are two different ways to get drained. One is the familiar one — your prices violate the probability axioms, so you can be booked with no information at all. The other is weirder: your prices are perfectly coherent, you've honestly said 0.5 because you can't compute the answer, and you still lose steadily to anyone who spent ten more minutes with a calculator than you did. And the punchline I want them to leave with, which I've been reading about in the logical induction stuff, is that the second kind of drain isn't a bug you can just fix, because fixing it would require being logically omniscient, which takes unbounded compute. So the criterion gets weakened to "don't be exploitable by a trader who is fast." Merely slow is survivable. Slow in a predictable direction is not.

So design the actual game. I need: the batch of statements they bet on (with an answer key I can generate and check the night before, or a way to resolve them live), a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, a market maker or trader role that has strictly more compute than the room, a compute budget so that actually working something out costs them something, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the two-kinds-of-drain distinction.

Two hard constraints. First, it has to work for the students who are bad at math — the point is about honesty under a compute limit, not about being quick at arithmetic — so build in claims that aren't math-flavored at all, and make it possible to contribute without being a human calculator. Second, include a few pairs of statements that are secretly the same claim, or secretly linked in a way that isn't obvious, because I think that's where the best moments come from; but tell me how to keep that from feeling like a gotcha.

Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I assume the two math kids will just solve everything while everyone else checks out, and I assume I'll be way too slow running the trader role live — handle both. And be frank about which feature of the view the game can't reproduce. My worry is that a facilitator with a laptop is basically a logically omniscient oracle, which is exactly the standard the theory throws out, so what they feel might be the wrong feeling. Tell me if that's right and what I should say in the debrief about it.
