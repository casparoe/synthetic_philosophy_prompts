Had a two-hour argument with a mate about Germany and privacy and neither of us could land it, so help me out.

The thing I keep coming back to: Germany is the country that treats data protection as a constitutional dignity matter, that had a mass boycott of its own census in the eighties, that keeps the police and the domestic intelligence services formally separated so they can't just pool what they hold, and that deliberately leaves its population registers choped up at municipal level so there's no single big searchable database. And yet in the same country a private stranger can go to the registration office, name you, pay about ten euros, and be handed your current home address. No reason required, apparently.

So what picture of privacy makes that coherent? Is the protected interest specifically against the state, against aggregation as such, or against being profiled rather than being located? My mate says the address rule survives only because it's old, dull and administrative, not because it's principled — I think that's too cheap but I can't say why.

Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it nicely, mark where it's philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change, in law or in reasoning, if you swapped in a blunt "nobody gets to assemble a picture of me" principle instead. British English, please.
