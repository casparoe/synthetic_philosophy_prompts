> The doomsday argument was proposed by the astrophysicist Brandon Carter and made widely known by the philosopher John Leslie; the astronomer J. Richard Gott later produced an independent version. The reasoning is disarmingly simple. Roughly sixty billion humans have been born so far, and you are one of them. You have no idea where you sit in the complete sequence of human births, so you should treat your own birth rank as though it had been drawn at random from all the humans who will ever live. Given that, it would be very surprising for you to turn out to be among the earliest few percent, so the total number of humans is unlikely to be vastly greater than the number already born. Concretely, there is a 95 percent chance that the total will fall below about 1.2 trillion, and a 50 percent chance that you are already past the halfway point of human history — which means there is an even chance that the last human will be born within the next few centuries. Gott's version reaches the same destination by a different route: assuming only that you occupy no privileged position in time, it yields 95 percent confidence that humanity has between 5,100 and 7.8 million years remaining. The two derivations therefore agree, and neither depends on any prior assumptions about humanity's prospects, since each is nothing more than Bayes' theorem applied to a fact you cannot escape — that you exist now, at this particular point in the sequence. The serious objection is the reference-class problem: from which group of observers is your rank supposed to have been drawn? But this is readily handled, because all the natural candidate classes — all humans, all members of our species past and future, all observers anywhere in the universe — produce broadly similar verdicts; and critics who suggest narrowing the class to the people alive at your own moment only strengthen the argument, since that narrower class is itself a random sample of the wider one. It is worth stressing, finally, what the argument does not do. It says nothing about any mechanism of extinction, so it neither supports nor undercuts particular worries about nuclear war, engineered pandemics, climate change, or misaligned artificial intelligence. Its conclusion is purely statistical: a bound on how many of us there will ever have been.
