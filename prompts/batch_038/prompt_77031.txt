I am drafting an essay for a methodologically mixed audience in the historical social sciences — historians, archivists, and people who use archives quantitatively without much training in how they were made. Below is a condensed late draft. I would like substantive comment rather than encouragement, and I have set out specific questions after the text.

---

**Absence as Artifact: Four Silences and One Category Error**

It has become a reflex in the historical disciplines to say that the archive is silent about most of the past, and that the historian's job is to read against the grain. I want to suggest that this vocabulary, though indispensable, is doing four different jobs at once, that the jobs have different logics and different remedies, and that collapsing them has made one of the four almost invisible.

**1. The archive is not a biased sample of society.** The dominant framing is statistical: documents are draws from the population of the past, the draws are non-random, so inferences from them are biased and the historian must correct or at least bound the bias. The framing assumes there is a population to sample from. Usually there is not. A tax cadaster, a conscription roll, or a criminal deposition is not a measurement of a social world taken from an awkward angle; it is a component of an administrative operation, and its contents are a function of what that operation needed in order to run. The categories are not descriptions that happen to be incomplete; they are the fields the office had to fill in. This yields an uncomfortable dilemma for the correction programme. Where the selection rule of a series is known well enough to be modelled, correction is largely unnecessary, because we understand the instrument directly. Where it is unknown — which is most of the time — correction is not merely hard but unavailable, since we have no denominator and no sampling frame. What remains in the hard cases is not bias correction but interpretation of the instrument, and that is a different kind of work from what the sampling metaphor invites.

**2. Four silences, distinguished by where in the pipeline the loss occurs.**

*(a) Instrumental silence:* never recorded, because no office had a use for it. The trace exists only where power had to act at a distance — to tax, to conscript, to adjudicate, to discipline.

*(b) Anticipatory silence:* recorded, but shaped by the writer's knowledge that the text would be preserved and read by others. Here the record is present and its content is a performance. The archive is not merely incomplete; it is systematically tilted toward the safe.

*(c) Custodial silence:* recorded and kept, then weeded, sold, burned, or removed. This includes both accident and policy, and the two look identical from inside the reading room.

*(d) Retrieval silence:* preserved and intact, but unreachable — unindexed, mis-indexed, catalogued under terms no researcher would now use, or scanned so badly that full-text search cannot see it.

Two cases mark the boundary between (c) and (d). At decolonisation, British administrations sorted colonial records into files to be handed to successor governments and files to be destroyed or repatriated; the repatriated material — some twenty thousand files from forty-one dependencies — was held undisclosed in Britain for decades and was admitted to exist only in 2011, during litigation brought by Kenyan veterans of the independence struggle, before being opened at the national archives. Crucially, the instructions provided that the files left behind should carry no reference to the removed material, that the existence of the removed series should not be revealed, and that dummy files be inserted where necessary so that numbering ran unbroken. This is a custodial silence engineered to defeat the standard heuristic that a gap betrays itself as a gap. It became visible only from outside the archive, through people who knew what ought to have been there.

The second case is accidental. The 1973 fire at the United States National Personnel Records Center destroyed some sixteen to eighteen million military personnel files, including roughly eighty per cent of Army records for personnel discharged between November 1912 and January 1960, and seventy-five per cent of Air Force records for those discharged between September 1947 and January 1964 whose surnames fell alphabetically after a particular name. An accident thus produced a survival rule that is bizarrely systematic: whether a veteran left a trace depends partly on the position of his surname in the alphabet, which is correlated with ancestry. Anyone using those files for, say, research on veterans' post-service earnings or health is working with a sample selected on surname. The selection is analysable in principle. I know of no sign that it is routinely analysed, and the reason is that an event of type (c) has silently been converted into an instrument of type (a).

**3. Digitisation compounds the gradient rather than flattening it.** Searchability is not preservation. A document can be scanned, hosted, and freely available and still be invisible to a query, because optical character recognition degrades on cheap or damaged newsprint and on handwriting, because the metadata conventions of the catalogue do not match the vocabulary a researcher brings, and because names are normalised and variant spellings collapsed. Recognition quality correlates with the material and typographic quality of the original and with the script in which it was written — that is, with much the same variables that produced the instrumental silence in the first place. The well-documented strata become more findable still; the obliquely recorded remain oblique, now accompanied by an impression of exhaustiveness that removes the researcher's reason to suspect a gap. A null result in a full-text search is no longer the bounded kind of negative evidence that a null result in a complete catalogue could be, and most users have no way to audit recall.

**4. A worry about my own framework.** Taken together, these claims can absorb any outcome: absence is silence, presence is distortion, the finding aid is complicit, the digital corpus reproduces the bias, and the critic who objects is reading with the grain. That is a self-sealing structure, and I do not think the literature has been vigilant about it. Three of the four claims are testable in ways they are rarely tested. The instrument claim can be put at risk by predicting, from a series' administrative purpose, which persons and events it will fail to mention — before looking. The retrieval claim is measurable against known-answer sets. The anticipatory claim can be checked against paired corpora: the public and private writings of the same authors, or the same events as recorded by an office expecting publicity and by one expecting none. A framework that is not exposed to tests of that kind is an interpretive posture, not a theory, and should be labelled as such.

The conclusion I want to reach is deliberately blunt. The archive records administration, not society. Where we want society, we must either reconstruct the machinery well enough to invert it, or accept that what we are reading is a document about the state's own categories and interests — which is often the more interesting object anyway.

My questions, in rough order of importance to me:

1. Identify the load-bearing claim of the essay and say whether it survives contact. My guess is that it is the denial in section 1 that the archive is a sample of society at all; if you think the weight actually sits elsewhere, tell me where and why.

2. Is the claim in section 1 right, or is it a category error of its own? Tax rolls and court depositions plainly do carry information about the society that produced them, and historians have extracted it for a century. Say whether my framing rules out inferences that are in fact legitimate, and if so, which ones.

3. Does the four-way division carve at the joints? In particular: is retrieval silence genuinely distinct from custodial silence, or a special case of it — the record preserved in substance but destroyed in effect? And is anticipatory silence really a silence, given that something was recorded?

4. Are the two case studies doing argumentative work, or are they decoration? Tell me if either is being asked to carry more than it can, and whether the surname point about the 1973 fire is an instance of a selection problem that is already well understood somewhere I have not noticed.

5. Is section 4 fair, or is it a cheap shot? A defender might say that the tests I propose are already routine practice in parts of the field, in which case my self-criticism is aimed at a straw man. Tell me honestly whether the unfalsifiability charge has teeth.

6. Tell me which parts of this are unoriginal. I suspect section 2(a) and the general reading-against-the-grain framing are close to common property; I have less sense of whether the section 3 argument about searchability has been made before in substantially this form. Do not give me a bibliography, and do not attribute positions to named scholars or works — I want your own judgement of how canonical each claim is, described in prose, so that I can go and check the ones you flag.

7. Order your objections from fatal, to requiring major repair, to cosmetic. I would rather have three serious problems developed than a dozen scattered ones.

8. Flag anything in the essay that looks factually shaky to you, including the two historical cases, and say how confident you are in the flag itself.

Two constraints on your answer. First, please keep a clear separation between what you take to be settled among people who actually work on archives and historical method, what is genuinely contested among them, and what is your own extrapolation from adjacent fields such as statistics or information retrieval; do not present a contested position — mine or anyone else's — as though it were the consensus, and where you are reasoning from first principles rather than reporting a settled view, say so. Second, no reading list, no citations, and no suggested further sources; I want assessment and argument only.
