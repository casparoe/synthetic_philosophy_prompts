Three months ago I was appointed to the external panel of our national museum's provenance committee, and I have just been asked to write the consistency memo for a claim on which I must recuse myself: I did my doctoral fieldwork in the claimant country and still correspond with several of the claimants' staff. I therefore want the analysis to be as impersonal as the record allows.

Our committee's decisions so far. We returned a group of funerary urns carried off by a military expedition in the 1890s, on the claim of a national museum rather than of any descendant community, and we did so even though the claimant had no adequate storage at the time and could only promise to build some. We declined a claim for architectural sculpture removed under a nineteenth-century permit whose validity historians dispute, citing the unresolved permit, the absence of any practice the removal had interrupted, and offering instead a long-term loan and a joint excavation. We returned a set of masks still worn in ceremonies by a living community, although our own acquisition in 1968 was legally impeccable and the export lawful. We retained a collection of ceremonial textiles sought by the same sort of community, on the ground that they would not survive local conditions, substituting conservation training, facsimiles, and an indefinite loan. And when a mixed claim arrived, we returned the pieces taken in a raid and retained those bought from a dealer with clean paperwork, over the objection that the group was indivisible.

The new claim: a cast-metal royal figure and a set of palace doors taken in a punitive expedition at the end of the nineteenth century, with no treaty behind it, though the colonial administration purported to authorize the removal and the successor state later repudiated that authorization. The claimants are joint — the reigning royal house, whose court ceremonies lapsed two generations ago, and the national museum, which opened a climate-controlled gallery last year and has an excellent record. The claim also sweeps in thirty smaller pieces acquired from a dealer in the 1950s with impeccable provenance and no connection to the expedition. Our statute bars outright disposal, so anything we do must be structured as a transfer, gift, or loan.

What decision coheres best with our own precedents? I can see at least four candidate rules in the record — illicit removal, living practice, legal title at the moment of acquisition, and conservation capacity — and I suspect they are not independent of one another, and that the urns case in particular is doing double duty. Tell me which rules our verdicts genuinely admit, where they come apart on this claim, which single precedent is the closest analog and why, and what principle we would be committing ourselves to whichever way we decide. Then describe the next claim that would expose which principle we actually hold.

Please write in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
