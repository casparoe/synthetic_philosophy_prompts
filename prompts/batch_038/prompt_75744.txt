I've been landed with something I'm not sure I'm equal to, so I'd like some help. I volunteer two mornings a week at the little museum in Kerswell, a market town of about four thousand people somewhere in the north of England, and on the second Saturday in March the parish council is holding a commemoration for the thing the town has been telling a story about since 1794. The man who normally does the speaking is off sick, so they've asked me to give the address at the unveiling of a new plaque. Eight minutes or so, read aloud, in the church, with the junior school choir doing two hymns, the mayor, the county archivist, and a room of about two hundred — a lot of them direct descendents of the families in the story, plus a coachload from the county local history society who will know far more than I do.

The story is this. In the winter of 1794 a fever came up the valley, and Kerswell shut itself off, put its sick in the mill rather than in their own houses, nursed them there, and lost eleven people. The part everybody is proudest of, and the part that gets read out at every service, is the ending: nobody was sent away and nobody was turned back at the bridge. Now, the county archivist has spent three years in the parish chest and the quarter sessions, and the documents don't say that. The town really did close itself and it really did put the sick in the mill — that part stands up, and it cost people dearly. But the overseers' accounts for that year have two entries in them which I'll give you word for word so you can use them: "Paid to widow Cunditt for nursing in the mill eleven weeks at one shilling, eleven shillings" and "Paid to J. Marlow and son for removing the Hadleys off this parish and for a cart, six and eightpence." There's a settlement examination behind the second one, and the Hadleys were a weaver's family with a claim on the parish, which is exactly why they were moved, and a child of theirs died on the road at the boundary stone. The miller was not a philanthropist either: he sent the parish an itemised bill and then chased it through the courts for four years. And the roll of names going on the plaque — which was compiled in the 1860s by an antiquary who worked from a rate book, so it is a list of people who owned or occupied property, which is why the miller is on it and widow Cunditt is not, and why one man on it was almost certainly with the militia in Flanders that winter. So it isn't a case of a noble lie meeting a sordid truth. The truth is both worse and better than the story, and it's spread across different people than the story says it is. Two things complicate the day further: the plaque has already been cast, so the speech has to survive the moment the cloth comes off it, and a woman who is a Hadley descendant has written to the council saying she'll stand at the back and will leave if the ending is repeated unchanged.

What I want is the speech in full, plus a short note afterwards of maybe three hundred words. Hard constraints: no footnotes, no reading list, no named historians except to thank the archivist, who has asked for that, and no "well, actually the record shows" register, because I've lived here eleven years and half that room has been here eleven generations, and a debunking tone from me would be a kind of vandalism even where it's true. It also can't just repeat the legend, because I'd know I was saying something false in a church, in front of a woman who has every right to be there. It has to work for the choir and the mayor and the people who came because of the Gazette as well as for the local history society, and it has to say something to the room about what a commemoration is even for, since I keep going back and forth between thinking these speeches are making claims about the past and thinking they aren't making claims at all — a library book I've been reading on collective memory suggested those two things aren't the same kind of thing and that neither one is simply wrong, but it didn't tell me what to say on a Saturday in March. My brother thinks we should cancel it and put up a plain information board; my partner thinks I should say nothing and let the plaque be the plaque; I don't think either of them is right but I can't argue it. So: write the speech, and in the note tell me which two or three sentences are carrying the most weight, and go through the places where you had to decide whether the town's memory gives way to the documents or the documents give way to the memory and say what principle settled it each time — including what you do about the names on the plaque, which can't be changed. If you think there's a version of this speech that doesn't have to choose, I'd like to hear why not. British English throughout, please, in the speech as well as the note.
