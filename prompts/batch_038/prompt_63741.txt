I sit on the Therapeutics and Patient Safety Committee at a roughly 500-bed academic medical center, where I hold the clinical informatics seat. My formation is not clinical: I spent a decade reading and posting on rationalist blogs before moving into health IT, and I mention that only because it is why I recognized the shape of the item on our agenda for the November 14 meeting, and why I do not trust myself to referee it. What landed on us is a decision problem about imperfect recall that I first met years ago in a paper about a motorist who cannot tell one intersection from the next, now wearing scrubs. In March we endured an eleven-day downtime after a ransomware event, during which the electronic record was unavailable and our alarm middleware ran in a degraded mode that annunciates normally but retains and displays no event history. One affected protocol has the following structure, which I state as the committee has already agreed to state it, stripped of clinical detail because the clinical detail does not bear on anything: per case there are at most two sequential windows in which the second stage of a loading protocol may be given, each announced by an identical alarm string; giving the stage at the first window completes the protocol, so no second window arises; a second window arises only if the team declined at the first; and if the team declines at both, we fall back on a rescue therapy. Our own decision-analytic model, adopted by the committee as common ground, scores the three reachable outcomes on a 0–100 expected-benefit scale: stage given at the first window, 20; stage given at the second window, 80; stage given at neither, 5. The trouble is that the responder cannot tell which window she is in. The alarm text is identical both times; the middleware shows no history; intervals between windows in our audited cases ranged from about ten minutes to about three hours, so elapsed time is not a usable cue; the float pool rotates every four hours with verbal-only handoff during downtime; and the paper downtime forms were frequently completed retrospectively. Of eleven audited cases, our reviewers could not reconstruct the responder's contemporaneous belief about her position in the sequence in seven, and in two of the four they could, the belief was wrong.

Because the windows are indistinguishable, any deterministic instruction collapses into one of two rules: "give whenever the alarm fires," which scores 20, or "decline and wait," which scores 5. The proposal on the table, from our pharmacy resident — who is considerably better at this than the rest of us — is a randomized rule: at each trigger, decline and wait with probability 0.4 and give with probability 0.6, which she calculates scores 32 in expectation. She adds a second and more controversial claim: that the rule is self-stabilizing, because a responder who forms her credence that this is the first window from the frequencies the policy itself generates — the first window always comes, the second comes only when the team declined — arrives at exactly the credence that leaves her indifferent at the bedside, so the policy carries its own enforcement. Two members call this circular. Our senior intensivist says a clinician's belief about her patient must come from the patient and not from a policy manual, and that flipping a coin over a live human being is not a clinical judgment at any probability. Our risk manager says the edifice comes down as soon as compliance is imperfect, and quotes our own audit, which suggests roughly 80 percent of responders would actually use a coin while the rest would exercise judgment. A retired airline captain on the committee thinks we are overcomplicating a solved problem: in aviation you do not ask crews to reason about their position in a sequence, you install a forcing function that tells them. Engineering has priced one — mirroring the alarm history to local non-volatile storage so that a second trigger annunciates differently — at $240,000 and about seven months, covering the four newest middleware modules, which serve roughly 60 percent of our bays. There is also a free stopgap, a bedside tally card the responder marks at each trigger, but the audit found such cards absent in about a fifth of comparable cases and, when present, wrong in about a sixth, and wrong in both directions. I have read enough arguments about self-locating belief to know that the resident's second claim is the sort of thing that may turn out to be either a discovery or a trap, and I cannot tell which from inside the meeting.

Please draft the memo I will circulate, over my name, seventy-two hours before the meeting. I need a document the committee can act on rather than a survey, so: (1) reach a bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with modifications — and state the exact instruction we should print on the downtime policy page, including the number if the instruction is probabilistic, together with what we should do about the $240,000 fix and the tally card, and by when; (2) show the arithmetic that settles the resident's two claims, and tell me plainly whether she is right, right for the wrong reasons, or wrong; (3) handle the two things that will perturb the rule in practice — unlicensed deviation by the 20 percent who will not use a coin, and licensed deviation by responders who do have some partial cue — and say whether the policy should permit cue-based deviation and whether either perturbation destroys the self-stabilizing argument; (4) give the intensivist's objection its strongest form, and say what would have to be true for it to overturn your recommendation, which I expect is not nothing; and (5) tell us who signs the incident report when the coin comes up wrong, since whatever we print will be read aloud at a root-cause analysis. Everything you need is in this message: please do not go looking for clinical guidelines or for the decision-theory literature, and do not cite anything, because our numbers are ours and I would rather have your reasoning than your references. Aim for 1,000 to 1,300 words, written for a room containing two intensivists, a risk manager, a hospital ethicist, and a pilot — show the arithmetic, but do not fetishize it.
