My friend writes a newsletter that scores the week's political news against the last century of the Roman Republic, and I've been reading her for a year trying to reverse-engineer what rule she's actually running. Her calls so far: a minority using routine procedural obstruction to block a nominee — "an ordinary Senate fight, everyone vetoed everyone"; the majority's answer, stripping that same obstructer of his office and abolishing the seat itself — "that's 133 BC, that's the real Rubicon, the day a veto-holder got unmade"; a leader sending troops into a city over the local government's objection — "Sullan, and only Sullan because there were soldiers in the streets"; two rivals splitting ministries in a backroom deal — "a Triumvirate, and Triumvirates rented the Republic, they didn't kill it"; armed partisans brawling outside a courthouse, a few hurt, nobody dead — "Clodius and Milo, and Rome outlived them by a decade"; suspects rounded up and put to death under emergency authority with no trial — "the senatus consultum ultimum, that's when you stop sleeping."

The story she's got next: a ruling majority, using procedures that are entirely legal and that the courts have already blessed, extends the sitting legislature's term by four years and drops the scheduled election from the calendar. No arrests, no soliders, nobody laid a hand on anybody.

Give me exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two apiece, no intro and no wrap-up: (1) the two or three rules that fit all six of her verdicts equally well, (2) what each of those rules says she'll call the new case, (3) your best guess at her call, (4) why you'd bet on that one over the alternatives, and (5) the one further case that would tell the rules apart.
