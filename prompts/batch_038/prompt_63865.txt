I've answered phones at a regional insurance office for fourteen years, mostly people calling about a denied claim or a bill they can't make sense of. Next month those calls go to an AI assistant instead, and theyve asked me to give the five-minute opening remarks at a public session about the switch. The room will be maybe sixty people: mostly customers over sixty, a dozen of my coworkers who are scared for their jobs, two people from compliance, and somebody from the local paper.

Here's my problem. During the pilot a woman called about a payment plan and agreed to one in about ninety seconds. Two days later she rang back and told me she didn't remember deciding anything, she just found herself agreeing. The people who built it explained that it matches the caller's pace and word choice and knows when to leave a pause. If a salesman did that on purpose I'd call it manipulation. But its also true that most of those calls used to end with the person more confused than when they started, and now they mostly don't. And the vendor keeps saying each version is a lot better at conversation than the one before, so "it's only a chatbot" isn't going to stay true for long.

So: write the remarks for me. What I keep coming back to is that the speech can't do the thing it's warning about — no warm reassurance, no rhythm that makes people feel looked after, no line engineered to get a nod in the moment that they'd resent later. It has to be honest about why the system is good, which is partly that it's extremely good at getting people to yes, without trashing my employer or scaring the room. And I want it to leave them holding something usable — a way to notice in themselves when they've been talked into something, and what exactly to say to get a boring human like me on the line instead. Five minutes, plain words, no lecture.
