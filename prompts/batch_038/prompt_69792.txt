**The question as set (Question 4, 25 marks):**

> Mises's 1920 essay argued that rational economic calculation is impossible in a socialist commonwealth. Sixteen years later Lange set out a model of market socialism in which a central planning board fixes prices by trial and error.
> (a) [10 marks] State Mises's argument as precisely as you can. What exactly is supposed to be impossible, and why is the impossibility supposed to follow from the institutional arrangement Mises has in mind?
> (b) [8 marks] Describe the economy Lange constructs — who decides what, and on what basis — and say what he took it to establish against Mises.
> (c) [7 marks] Is Hayek's later objection the same objection as Mises's original one? Answer in whatever sense of "same" you find most informative, and say what turns on your answer.

**The submitted answer (about 530 words, reproduced exactly as submitted):**

> (a) Mises's starting point is that rational economic action requires comparison, and that comparison requires a common denominator. In a market economy the denominator is money price: because capital goods are privately owned and actually exchanged, every input carries a price, and an entrepreneur can therefore work out whether a given method of production pays for itself or destroys value. Under socialism the means of production are collectively owned and so are never exchanged between owners. Nothing is bought or sold, so nothing has a price, and the planners are left with calculation in kind — so many tonnes of steel, so many labour-hours, so many kilowatt-hours. These quantities cannot be added together or weighed against one another, because they are incommensurable. The consequence is not that socialism would allocate resources badly but that it could not tell good allocation from bad at all: it could enumerate every technologically feasible plan and have no way of ranking them. Mises stresses that this is a point about possibility, not about efficiency and not about the goodwill or competence of officials.
>
> (b) Lange's model accepts the first step of that argument and denies that it is fatal. He constructs a socialist economy in which households are free to choose their occupations and to spend their incomes as they wish, consumer goods are sold for money, and the means of production are publicly owned; the managers of the state enterprises are instructed to combine inputs so as to minimise average cost at the prices given to them, and to produce the output at which marginal cost equals price. Those prices are not arrived at by trading but announced by a Central Planning Board, which raises the price of anything in excess demand and lowers the price of anything in excess supply, repeating the procedure until the announced prices clear every market. What the board produces, however, is not really a price in Mises's sense at all: it is an accounting index, a bookkeeping convenience with no exchange behind it. And that is exactly the point. Once the equilibrium conditions have been written down, the allocation can be computed directly from the physical data, and the trial and error of the market turns out to have been only a slow way of reaching what a system of simultaneous equations states all at once. Lange's conclusion is therefore that Mises mistook a historical feature of capitalism — that prices happen to emerge from exchange — for a logical requirement of calculation. Prices are dispensable; only the information they carry is not.
>
> (c) Hayek's objection, developed through the 1930s and in his 1945 article on knowledge in society, is essentially the objection Mises had already made, restated in epistemic terms: the knowledge the board would need is dispersed among millions of people, much of it tacit and known only to those on the spot, and it cannot be assembled in the form the board would need it in. This was already implicit in the 1920 essay, which is why Hayek's contribution is best seen as a refinement rather than as a new argument. It is also a practical rather than a principled objection. Hayek does not deny that a board could imitate the competitive solution in principle; he denies that it could keep up, since by the time the statistics had been gathered the situation would have changed. In that sense the whole dispute came down to who sets prices rather than to whether prices are needed, both sides being agreed on the latter — which makes the famous impossibility thesis less of a thesis than the label suggests, and turns the debate in the end into a political one about the administration of an economy rather than an economic one about its possibility.
