Here's the question the class was given:

> A puzzle is mailed to 50,000 people. The letter says: if you mail back the enclosed postcard, and exactly one postcard comes back in total, the sender of that one postcard wins $10 million. If none come back, or if more than one comes back, nobody gets anything. There is no cost to mailing the postcard beyond a stamp, and your decision has no way of influencing anyone else's decision. Suppose all 50,000 recipients reason flawlessly about the puzzle and all of them know that they all reason flawlessly about it. Should you mail the postcard? Explain. In your explanation, say what an argument from dominance would recommend, and then say what a superrational agent would do and whether the two verdicts differ.

And here's what the student wrote:

> (1) Look at it from your own seat. The other 49,999 people either send a card or they don't, and nothing you do changes that. Take any way the others might turn out. If at least two of them send a card, the prize is dead no matter what, so mailing costs you only a stamp. If exactly one of them sends a card, then your mailing spoils it — you go from nothing to nothing, but you had no shot anyway. If none of them sends a card, then mailing is the difference between $10 million and $0. So mailing never does worse than sitting on your hands, and sometimes does far better. Dominance says mail.
>
> (2) Superrationality is the idea that when everyone reasons the same flawless way, they all reach the same answer, and a superrational thinker knows this and picks accordingly. It's basically the same insight as the tragedy of the commons — the group ends up worse off because each individual does what's best for them, and the fix is to think of yourself as choosing for everybody at once.
>
> (3) Applying it here: if I mail a card, so do all 50,000, and with 50,000 cards back nobody wins. If I don't, nobody wins either. Since those two outcomes are worth the same to me, superrationality doesn't overturn the dominance argument — I should still mail. And you can confirm it with arithmetic: my chance of being the one and only sender is 1 in 50,000, so the expected payoff from mailing is $10,000,000 / 50,000 = $200, against $0 from not mailing. Mail the card.
