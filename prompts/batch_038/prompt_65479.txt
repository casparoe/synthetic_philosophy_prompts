hi — I write about science and ideas for a weekly, and I've got a piece in the works about the second discoverer. You know the genre: two people get to the same thing within a few years of each other, one of them ends up with the Nobel or the crater, and then there's a hundred years of arguing. My spine is Merton's old point about multiples — that this happens so often it stops looking like coincidence — and the question the piece actually wants to answer is whether that proves results are inevitable or just proves credit is a mess.

There's a sidebar graphic going with it. Tier list of famous near-simultaneous discoveries, ranked by how hard they push toward this-was-coming-anyway. Four tiers:

- it was basically in the mail — delete any one name and someone else walks in within a couple of years
- two roads to the same town — genuinely independent arrivals, and the convergence itself tells you the terrain was forcing it
- coin flip — the tools were lying around but nobody else was pointed that way
- the air was NOT thick with this one — a single wearing a multiple's clothes

My list, and please work off these rather than swapping in your own favourites:

1. Newton and Leibniz, calculus
2. Seki Takakazu and his school in Edo Japan, working out methods for areas, volumes and infinite series with essentially no contact with Europe
3. Darwin and Wallace, natural selection
4. Scheele, Priestley and Lavoisier, oxygen
5. Bell and Gray, the telephone
6. Lobachevsky and Bolyai, hyperbolic geometry, with Gauss sitting on his
7. Whittle and von Ohain, the turbojet
8. Penzias and Wilson versus the Princeton group that was already building a detector for it, cosmic microwave background
9. Higgs versus Brout and Englert (and Guralnik, Hagen, Kibble) on symmetry breaking and mass, with Anderson's condensed matter precursor a couple of years earlier
10. Black, Scholes and Merton on option pricing, with Ed Thorp doing something similar earlier and mostly not publishing
11. Mendeleev and Lothar Meyer, the periodic table

For each one I want three things, and it has to fit on a single page so be brutal about length: a tier; one sentence on why it lands there; and a number — how many years the result would have been late if you deleted everyone on the list except the first person to get there. The counterfactual is that person is never born, everyones biography otherwise unchanged. If your honest answer is it doesn't show up in anything like that form for a long time, if ever, then say so and use a big number instead of a shrug. No ranges. I want numbers I can argue with.

Then two more things, and honestly these are the parts I care about:

(a) flag any case on my list where the popular telling is inflated — where the independence is contaminated because one party had seen the other's work, or where one of them was years behind and it's really a priority fight rather than a multiple. My suspicion is atleast two of these are like that and I'd much rather hear it from you than from a reader with a PhD.

(b) tell me which single case is the worst evidence for my own thesis, and whether I should cut it or lean into it and let the piece be about the exception.

One favour: add a case that isn't on my list that you think belongs in the top tier. Not one of the four everyone reaches for.

And when you're done, tell me in a line whether your tiers and your delay numbers actually agree with each other, or whether you had to bend one of them to fit the other — that's the first question my editor will ask and I'd like to walk in with an answer.
