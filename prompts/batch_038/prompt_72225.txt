I was at a panel Tuesday about all those cooperation-in-the-lab studies and a behavioral economist kept reaching for the same line: "if they're both rational, the centipede game ends on the first move." He used it twice and the two uses didn't feel like the same claim to me. First time it was a gotcha against actual people — the subjects pass, they shouldn't, so they aren't rational. Ten minutes later it was a gotcha against rationality itself — since both of them walk away with way more if the game just keeps going, being rational is self-defeating and the lab subjects were productively dumb. I'm pretty sure you can't have both of those in one paragraph but I can't say why, and my editor defintely will.

Can you pull that sentence apart for me? I want the separate precise theses it could be standing in for — a claim about what the equilibrium predicts, a claim about what a genuinely rational person would do, a claim about what "rational" means here (payoff-maximizing? sane? selfish?), a claim about whether each one knows the other is rational and knows that they know, and whether some of these are even claims rather than stipulations about a technical term. Sort each one: obviously true but doing no work, true and doing the work, false, or not a claim at all. There was also a grad student in the audience who said the whole thing comes apart, because if you're actually sitting at move three you already know the other person isn't a first-move taker, so passing starts looking smart — where does that objection land in the menu, and does it bite the same reading the economist was leaning on?
