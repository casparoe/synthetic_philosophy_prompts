I teach a jurisprudence seminar this fall — 14 students, upper-level, roughly half philosophy majors and half pre-law kids who've read the Hart chapters on primary and secondary rules and basically nothing else in legal theory. I've got one two-hour block that I want to turn into a rule-amending game, basically Nomic: the rulebook includes rules for changing the rulebook. But I don't want the free-for-all version where they just spend two hours faffing around adding silly rules. I want a rigged starting constitution so the room runs head-first into the paradoxes of legal self-reference on purpose.

Here's what I've drafted. Nine rules, all of them in force at the start:

1. Any player may propose a new rule, or the amendment or repeal of an existing one, at any time. Proposals pass on a simple majority of those voting.
2. A proposal that passes takes effect immediately, unless its own text says otherwise.
3. Later rules do not override earlier ones. Where a new rule conflicts with an old one, the old rule governs and the new one is void to the extent of the conflict.
4. Rule 3 may be amended or repealed only by a proposal whose text expressly says it is amending or repealing Rule 3.
5. Rule 9 may never be amended, repealed, or suspended, by any procedure whatsoever.
6. Each player holds one veto for the session. Spending it kills whatever proposal is on the floor. A killed proposal may be reintroduced immediately.
7. Only players may vote, and the chair decides who counts as a player.
8. Every player is bound by every rule in force, including this one.
9. A majority of players may decide any question about the meaning or validity of any rule, and their decision is final.

The thing I'm going for: at some point somebody proposes to use Rule 9 to declare Rule 5 void, and the room has to figure out whether that counts as an amendment of Rule 5, whether Rule 3 already settles it, and whether the majority is allowed to answer that question about its own power. They should end the two hours unable to say whether their legislature can bind its successors, and should realize the answer was never written down anywhere they could look it up. They shouldnt get the vocabulary — continuing versus self-embracing, the rule of recognition, unamendability — untill the debrief, and even then only after they've argued about it in their own words.

So: design the session. How proposing and voting actually runs when fourteen people are all trying to do it at once, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, what props or handouts I need, and what my role is — I want a job for the chair that isnt just refereeing and that doesn't quietly make me the sovereign. Then the debrief, mapping specific moments from the play onto the ideas, plus two or three real-world hooks a room of American undergrads will have actually heard of. The Article V business about no state losing its equal vote in the Senate without its consent is the obvious one; give me two more, each in a sentence or two, and if you're not confident a hook is real, leave it out rather than invent one. No citations, no reading list, nothing for them to look up.

Be blunt about where it breaks. Which of my nine rules is drafted badly and what to replace it with and why, what the three loudest students will exploit in the first fifteen minutes, how I stop it sliding into a pure power grab where nobody cares about validity anymore, and the one moment I have to engineer rather than hope for if the paradox is going to land. Commit to a single design, don't hand me a menu of variants with pros and cons, and give me your reasons. Last thing: tell me straight which feature of the idea a game like this cannot reproduce and that I'll just have to say out loud at the end. American English, please.
