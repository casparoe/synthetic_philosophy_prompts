I have inherited a handout written by a teaching assistant for our introductory game theory seminar, and I am trying to determine whether its closing inference is a slip or a real result that I have simply failed to appreciate. The chain runs as follows.

(1) To say that a player is rational is to say that the strategy she chooses maximizes her expected payoff given some belief of hers about how the others will play; equivalently, a rational player only ever chooses a best response.

(2) In a finite game, a pure strategy is strictly dominated by some mixed strategy of that same player if and only if it fails to be a best response to any belief about the other players' choices. Our text proves this biconditional in an appendix and states it with no restriction on the number of players.

(3) Under common belief in rationality, each player assigns positive probability only to opponents' strategies that themselves survive iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies, and knows that they assign positive probability only to strategies that survive, and so on.

(4) Hence the strategies that survive iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies are exactly the strategies compatible with common belief in rationality, so dominance reasoning is both necessary and sufficient to characterize rational play.

(5) The order in which the deletions are carried out is irrelevant to the set that ultimately survives.

(6) Every pure strategy in the support of a Nash equilibrium survives the procedure, since an equilibrium strategy is a best response to the others' equilibrium strategies and so is never strictly dominated at any stage.

(7) Therefore, in any finite game with a unique Nash equilibrium, iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies must terminate at that equilibrium, and the two solution concepts coincide.

(8) So dominance reasoning is the deeper notion: no equilibrium concept is required to solve a game with a unique equilibrium, and conversely, whenever iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies leaves more than one profile, the game must have more than one Nash equilibrium.

The handout presented (8) as a punchline rather than a conjecture, and (8) is what I cannot accept. It would mean that across a large class of games one could read off the unique equilibrium from nothing but dominance comparisons, and that anyone who found the procedure stopping short of a single profile had thereby established the existence of a second equilibrium. Yet I cannot locate the bad step, because (1) through (6) each read like standard material and (7) is offered as following from (5) and (6) alone. Two classmates have offered competing diagnoses and I distrust both. The first says the trouble is (2), that the biconditional fails with three or more players, and that the handout is therefore unobjectionable once we confine ourselves to two-player games. The second says the trouble is (3), which smuggles in a substantive assumption about what players may believe rather than recording a fact about the game, so that (4) is where the chain first overreaches.
