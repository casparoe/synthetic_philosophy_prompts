I write literary fiction, and for some months I have been trying to name something that keeps happening to my readers and, more embarrassingly, to me as a reader. I suspect any existing name will fit only part of it, so let me be as concrete as I can rather than reach for vocabulary.

In the book where I first noticed it, a schoolteacher learns something damaging about a colleague and, over forty pages, decides not to act on it. My readers found the decision credible and several found it moving; what they objected to was something else. Their words were that they could see me needing it. Nothing she does is out of character. There is no narrator to speak of, and no interruption, and the prose is not, they agreed, doing anything wrong. What changes is that the reader forms a hypothesis about where her behaviour is coming from: she is now doing what the book requires rather than what she would do. From that moment the reader's questions are no longer about her but about me — what am I arguing, whom am I persuading, how much do I believe it. She has not become less real so much as ceased to be someone to wonder about, and become something to read through. I have watched it happen in myself: at a certain page I could no longer have told you what a character wanted, though I could have told you the page before, and no new evidence had arrived.

Two features are why I doubt the obvious labels will serve. First, the collapse is not a judgement of quality; the readers who report it are usually still enjoying the book. Second, and stranger, the trigger is not artificiality. In another novel the central consciousness is an interpreter — deliberately stiff, self-effacing, closer to an automaton than to a man — and readers absorbed themselves in what he wanted without a flicker of this. The collapse comes most easily to the most fully humanised characters, the ones I have given appetites and childhoods and contradictions. Everyone already knows these people are fabrications; that is never the issue. What defeats the posture is the reader coming to believe that this particular behaviour originates in the design of the book rather than in her.

Here my ignorance shows. I read Dennett years ago on counterfeit people and came away — possibly with this garbled — with the idea that treating something as a person is a posture you adopt because it earns its keep, that it is licensed by the pattern in the thing's behaviour rather than by any further fact about its insides, and that insisting on a bright line between a real person and a sufficiently convincing imitation is a hangover from a bad picture of the mind. If that is the view, my readers are doing something it has no room for. The sentences that support the posture before support it after; nothing in the behaviour changed; and yet the posture switches off, apparently on the strength of a hypothesis that makes no difference anyone could point to on the page. So either I have misremembered the view, or the view is missing a clause, or — likeliest — someone has already named this and has an account of how a stance can be revoked without anything in the evidence being revoked. I suspect the same switch is what people describe when they say a conversational machine has stopped seeming like a someone and started seeming like an average of everything it has read, which is why I want the philosophy and not only the literary term.

So: is there an established concept for this? I want the carving rather than the label. Tell me which existing notions come closest — the ones about narrators who cannot be trusted, about fiction that intends to be read through, about characters distinguished as round or flat, about readers refusing to go along with what an author asserts — and for each, where my case diverges, because my suspicion is that they all locate the trouble in what is said while I am describing a trouble in what is attributed as its source. If nothing fits, coin a term and give it boundaries: say what would and would not count as an instance, and say whether it is a property of texts, of readers, or of a relation between them. I am not after places to look things up; I want your own account. And tell me how confident you are in each part of it — in particular how sure you are that no established term covers exactly this, as opposed to my having failed to find it — and single out the claim in your answer you would be least willing to defend.
