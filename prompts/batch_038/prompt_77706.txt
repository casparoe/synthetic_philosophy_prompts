In three weeks I'm giving a forty-minute paper, with discussion, at a workshop on the limits of evidence that my department is co-sponsoring with a statistics group, and I want a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of my own view. My thesis is that the standard underdetermination argument against scientific realism is structurally unsound because it cannot simultaneously fix the scope of the evidence in question and keep its skeptical conclusion global. The argument, briefly: empirical equivalence can be defined relative to all possible evidence, in which case the resulting underdetermination thesis is either trivial or smuggles in an anti-realist account of what could count as evidence; or it can be defined relative to actual and reasonably expected evidence, in which case the skeptic needs a further premise to the effect that present equivalence will persist under evidential extension, and neither the history of science nor the model-construction literature supplies one. My second line is that the standard machines for manufacturing empirically equivalent rivals — reinterpreting a theory's theoretical terms as claims about observable regularities, or trading on the fact that interpolating a rival through the same data is always formally possible — build in a constructive empiricist constraint on admissibility, and that once the realist refuses that constraint the constructed rivals stop looking like theories at all. My positive claim is that underdetermination is best understood as a question about the stability of equivalence classes under the accumulation of evidence, which is a local, case-by-case, partly empirical matter rather than a global thesis about science.

The audience matters here. There will be four or five philosophers of science, most of them sympathetic to constructive empiricism or to some quiet anti-realism; two or three Bayesian epistemologists who work mainly on priors, conditionalization, and confirmation measures; one visiting statistician whose research is on non-identifiability, model selection criteria, and how to hedge predictions across models that fit the data equally well; and a scattering of graduate students, several of them in philosophy of physics and inclined to think that any debate not touching Bell's theorem or the measurement problem is preliminary at best. Nobody in the room works on the realism literature full time, and the statistician has told me in advance that he expects philosophers to misuse the word identifiable.

So please draft the prep sheet. Give me roughly eight objections that this particular room will actually press — not the generic complaints about underdetermination that turn up in every survey, and not the ones a purely historical audience would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if I handled it badly. For each, phrase it in the idiom of the subgroup that will raise it: the way a Bayesian would put it, the way the statistician would put it, the way a constructively inclined philosopher of science would put it, and the way a philosophy-of-physics graduate student would put it. Attach to each a spoken reply of about ninety seconds, a note on where that reply is soft and which subgroup will smell the softness, and an indication of whether the objection is fatal, merely costly, or actually a gift I should welcome. Also tell me what I should concede explicitly during the talk itself in order to defuse two of these before they are ever asked, what I must refuse to concede no matter how much pressure the room applies, and the objection I am hoping nobody raises — with my least bad answer to it. Finally, since discussion may be cut to ten minutes: give me a two-sentence concession to keep in my pocket, and tell me which single question I should try to steer the room toward if I get to choose one.
