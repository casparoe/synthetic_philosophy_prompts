I convene the examination for a taught MPhil paper on interactive epistemology, and I need one essay question on the common prior assumption, worth 25 marks and to be answered in 50 minutes. My candidates have all seen Aumann's agreement theorem and its standard proof, so a question that merely asks them to state and prove it would be worthless. The difficulty must come from applying the theorem to a case in which its premises are not simply supplied but have to be interrogated. Please build the question around the scenario below, which I have drafted. You may tighten my wording, but keep the structure and the numbers as they stand, and do not convert the case into one of the familiar numbered-state-and-partition examples — I want it deliberately free of any spelled-out state space, so that candidates must reason about what a prior is rather than compute one.

The scenario:

Ada and Bo are statisticians at the same medicines regulator. Let E be the claim that a newly licensed drug reduces ninety-day mortality. Each has analysed a different trial: Ada's posterior for E is 0.8, Bo's is 0.35. Both figures appear in a joint report signed by the two of them, so each knows the other's figure, each knows that the other knows it, and so on without limit. Each regards the other as fully rational and as a Bayesian who conditions on her or his own data. Neither budges. Asked whether this is a counterexample to Aumann's theorem, Ada says that it cannot be, since they had different data, and the theorem only rules out disagreement that differences in information fail to explain. Pressed further, both agree that before either had seen any data each would have assigned 0.6 to E, that each knows the other would have, and that this is likewise common knowledge between them. Bo concludes: 'That is our common prior. So every premise of the theorem holds and yet we disagree. The theorem must be false.'

The question should have three parts, marked 8, 9 and 8:

(a) assess Ada's reply and Bo's conclusion, identifying precisely what each gets right and what each gets wrong, and saying which premise or premises of the theorem the case in fact strains;

(b) decide whether the ex ante agreement on 0.6 supplies a common prior in the sense the theorem requires, distinguishing agreement on a single number concerning one proposition from the sharing of a prior over a state space, and addressing what else would have to be true for the counterfactual to do the work Bo asks of it;

(c) examine the status of the assumption itself: if a common prior is read counterfactually, as a belief state no agent ever actually occupied, what would count as evidence that it fails to hold, and what follows for anyone who wants to deploy the theorem as a premise in the argument that persisting disagreement between epistemic peers is irrational.

Please deliver three things: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the scenario and the three parts and their mark allocations; a model answer of about 900 words; and a marking scheme totalling 25 marks which, for each part, names the specific move that most reliably separates a first-class answer from a merely competent upper-second one, and which lists at least four identifiable errors that should cost marks, stating what each costs (for instance, answering by rehearsing the theorem and its proof, or concluding that the theorem is false without saying which premise fails).

The item is closed book and wholly self-contained, so neither the question nor the model answer nor the marking scheme may cite, name or allude to published work, and there is to be no bibliography or reading list: everything must be argued from the scenario and the concepts themselves. Please write throughout in British English.
