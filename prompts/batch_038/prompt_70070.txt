I have published four novels, and the last eighty pages of the fourth narrate a child's death from a fever in the voice of his mother. That is the passage my readers write to me about most often, almost always in the same terms: they wept, and they would not have had the book otherwise. A friend who teaches philosophy has sent me a numbered refutation of precisely this effect. He is a careful man and I do not believe he is playing a game with me, but I cannot accept his conclusion and I cannot locate where I would refuse it. His argument runs as follows.

1. To take pleasure in anything is to want it; and on the account of enjoyment he and I both find natural, to enjoy is simply to want more of the same.
2. My readers take pleasure in the death of the child — their letters say so, and several of them admit to rereading the passage.
3. Therefore they want that death.
4. To want a state of affairs is to be disposed, all else equal, to act so as to bring it about.
5. Whether the state of affairs one wants is actual or invented makes no difference to what is wanted, only to what is believed; and a belief is no part of a desire.
6. Therefore my readers are disposed, all else equal, to act so as to bring about a child's death from a fever.
7. A settled disposition to act so as to bring about suffering is cruelty, whatever the ontological standing of the sufferer.
8. Therefore the pleasure my readers take in my book is not a merit in the book but a vice in them — and, since I produced that pleasure deliberately and by craft, a vice in me.

He adds, not unkindly, that he does not see how the argument is peculiar to fiction: on his premises it condemns the enjoyment of any elegy, any history of a famine, any photograph that stops a person in the street, and even grief itself, since the mourner dwells on a loss with something he would not surrender. That generality is part of why I suspect a flaw somewhere. But each of his steps looks to me like something I already believed before he wrote it down, and I cannot tell you which one I would strike. If I were forced to choose I would keep (2) at any price, because it is the one claim in the list I know from experience to be true; and I would keep (5) as well, since abandoning it seems to mean abandoning the idea that a reader is ever moved by anything other than sentences.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a defense of my book. Name the premise or the inference that has to go, and explain why it looked innocent where it stood — I suspect each candidate looks innocent for a different reason, and I would like those reasons separated. Then price the alternatives: what does the diagnosis cost me if the failing step turns out to be (1), or (5), or (7)? In particular, tell me whether the usual remedies actually block the argument or merely redescribe it — the claim that eloquence converts the pain of the matter into pleasure in the telling, as Hume supposed; the claim, from the make-believe tradition, that what my readers feel is a quasi-grief that was never a desire at all; the claim that what pleases them is their own capacity to be moved; and the older claim that tragedy instructs and purges. Say which of these, if any, has the resources to stop him at an identifiable step, and which of them simply refuses to engage him.

State how confident you are in the diagnosis you settle on, and single out the point in your own answer about which you are least sure. Do not send me to the literature; I will find it myself if I want it. And if you conclude that his argument is sound and that I should accept (8), say so plainly rather than consoling me.
