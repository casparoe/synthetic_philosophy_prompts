I'm the managing editor of a comparative economic history journal — we're basically the venue where the Great Divergence arguments get re-litigated every couple of years. Maybe a third of what lands in my inbox is a contrastive "why did it happen here and not there" paper: why Britain and not the Yangzi delta, why Europe and not China, why movable type took off after Gutenberg and not in Song China. A lot of them have a broken contrast, and right now nobody on staff can say so in a way that survives the author's reply letter. We do desk decisions in about twenty minutes per manuscript, two grad-student screeners split the pile, I make the call, and every reject goes to the editor-in-chief for a second signature before the letter goes out. Only one screener reads Chinese and she's writing her own dissertation, so the procedure has to work from an abstract, an intro, and the tables. Nobody on the team has read anything in the philosophy of explanation and nobody is going to.

So write me a screening procedure we can actually run. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and at which gates a manuscript dies, gets sent back for reframing, or goes forward to review. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of contrastive explanation genuinely says about "why P rather than Q" — the foil, whether the foil is a real alternative to the fact, whether the cause offered would have made any difference between the two sides — but translate it into plain tests a screener can apply without knowing any of the vocabulary. Don't just hand me the theory with the jargon swapped out; tell me what they should look for on the page.

It has to handle, explicitly: a foil and a fact that aren't the same kind of unit (a nation-state against a region, a continent against a river delta, one century against another); a "why not" question where nothing in the paper establishes that the two sides were ever on a shared track such that one could have happened instead of the other; a cited cause that the author himself concedes was also absent, or also present, on the other side, so it doesn't discriminate between the two; a paper where the contrast is fine but the explanation does all its work at a level of aggregation the comparison can't support; and a paper that isn't contrastive at all, which the procedure must not fire on.

Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. And where the question isn't factual at all — whether a given foil is a legitimate thing to be curious about, whether the framing quietly assumes history was supposed to produce something and then asks who got there first — I want the procedure to stop and hand it to a named human with a note about what exactly is being decided, rather than pretending to resolve it with a checklist.

Then run it on three submissions:

1. Forty pages arguing Britain industrialised rather than the Yangzi delta because of coal geography plus access to New World land. The fact-side unit is a country, the foil-side unit is a region, and there's a footnote saying the author would be equally happy to compare Lancashire to the delta. Kill, or fixable in revision, and if fixable, what does she have to do?

2. A paper whose foil turns out on reading to be "Chinese science" in general, and whose proposed cause — something about a lawgiver's conception of natural order — is one the author admits was missing across much of Europe for most of the relevant period too. Desk reject on the grounds that the argument can't be contrastive, or send it out and let the reviewers fight?

3. The one I don't want the answer to. A short, elegant piece by a very eminent author — we ran a symposium on his last book — asking why the Industrial Revolution began in Britain rather than in Song China, nine hundred years earlier, so the comparison is across time as well as space. I think it's brilliant and I want to send it straight to review. Tell me what the procedure says, not what I'd like it to say.

Finally, tell me about false positives: name one kind of genuinely good paper this procedure would wrongly kill if we ran it mechanically, and what a screener should notice that stops that happening.

Two pages max for the whole thing, and the screener version has to fit on one side of one sheet. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I can't check any of it and my screeners won't read it anyway.
