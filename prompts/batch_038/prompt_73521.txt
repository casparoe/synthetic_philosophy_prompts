Two reconstructions of Buddhist ethics have been talking past each other in the secondary literature for decades. The first reads the tradition on the model of virtue ethics: right action is fixed by the quality of the agent's intention and by the settled dispositions of an arhat or bodhisattva; nibbāna is not a state of affairs to be brought about but the extinction of the defilements that distort action; and the doctrine of karma is agent-relative, so that the fruit of my volitional act accrues to me and cannot be traded off against the welfare of others. The second reads the tradition as consequentialist: what makes an act skillful (*kusala*) is its tendency to diminish *dukkha* and to conduce toward liberation; the canonical identification of karma with volition (*cetanā*) is best taken as a claim about how acts produce their fruits, not about what makes them right; and the horizon of rebirth merely widens the range of consequences over which the agent must reckon.

You are to draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign at the close of a serious adversarial collaboration — not a survey of the debate, not a verdict on who is correct, and not a dramatized exchange. Structure it in five sections.

**1. Agreed claims.** A numbered list, each item worded so that both camps could initial it without qualification. Make the items substantive rather than diplomatic. At a minimum, cover: what the tradition takes to be the primary object of moral evaluation; the status of *cetanā*; the role of the four immeasurables (*mettā*, *karuṇā*, *muditā*, *upekkhā*) in fixing right action; and whether the tradition admits of genuine moral dilemmas in which an agent cannot avoid wrongdoing.

**2. Residual disagreements.** A table, one row per disagreement, with four columns: the disagreement stated in a single neutral sentence; the formulation the virtue-oriented camp would accept as its own position; the formulation the consequentialist camp would accept as its own; and what each camp finds objectionable or tendentious in the other's formulation. No row may be a repackaging of something already settled in section 1.

**3. Cruxes.** For each candidate below, judge whether it is a crux in the strict sense — a sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one camp — and say why or why not.

(a) *Merit transfer.* The Pali tradition holds that merit generated by the living can be dedicated to departed kin, and that certain classes of the departed benefit by rejoicing in the dedication. Does this show that karmic benefit is transferable across persons, and hence that karma is not agent-relative in the way the first camp requires? Or does the fact that the benefit consists in the departed's own act of rejoicing show that nothing is transferred at all except an occasion for their own wholesome mental state, leaving agent-relativity intact?

(b) *Skillful means and killing.* The Mahāyāna case of the ship's captain who kills a would-be mass murderer in order to save his fellow passengers and to spare the would-be killer the far worse fruit of the crime he intends, taking the karmic cost upon himself. Is the act presented as permitted, as required, or as merely excused — and is the answer decisive between the two camps, or compatible with both?

(c) Whether the identification of karma with volition is a criterion of rightness or a piece of causal mechanism.

(d) Whether the commentarial practice of analyzing each immeasurable in terms of a near enemy (its counterfeit) and a far enemy supplies a standard of correctness internal to character, or only a set of reliability conditions on dispositions whose point is to produce good outcomes.

(e) Whether the rebirth horizon collapses the rule-versus-outcome distinction, on the ground that the precepts are simply dependable summaries of what yields favorable rebirths.

**4. Tests and predictions.** For every item in section 3 that you judge to be a genuine crux, name the case, argument, or pattern of textual treatment that both camps would accept as bearing on it, and then record in advance what each camp predicts about it. Where the two predictions turn out to be identical, say so explicitly and explain why the crux consequently fails to discriminate.

**5. Verbal dispute.** Identify any portion of the disagreement that is a dispute about the word "consequentialism" rather than about Buddhist ethics, mark its boundaries precisely, and state what would remain of the disagreement if both camps agreed to reserve the term for theories that locate the criterion of rightness in the value of outcomes.

Constraints: no bibliography, no citations, and no invented quotations. Refer to canonical and commentarial material by paraphrase only, and never place a purported passage in quotation marks. Write for a philosopher who reads neither Pali nor Sanskrit, glossing technical terms at first use. Take the sharpest available version of each camp; where one camp genuinely has no answer to a point, say that it has none rather than manufacturing one.
