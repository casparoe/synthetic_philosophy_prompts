THE STUDENT'S SCRIPT:

The word "collapse" does two jobs at once, and the dispute comes from running them together. It describes a process — the rapid loss of sociopolitical structure — and it evaluates that process, as loss or failure. Once the two are separated most of the argument dissolves, though not all of it.

Take the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean. Around 1200 BCE the palace systems went down: Mycenaean centres were destroyed or abandoned, Hattusa was deserted, Ugarit burned, and the Linear B archives simply stop. Egypt, under Ramses III, fought off the Sea Peoples and carried on, weakened. This used to be told as an invasion story, and the Sea Peoples are still cast as the villains, but the invasions read better as symptom than cause: a tightly coupled system of palatial redistribution economies, dependent on long-distance tin and copper, can fail all at once without any single failure being decisive. That is the systems-collapse picture. And the aftermath is not a dark age in every respect — iron metallurgy spreads, the alphabet eventually replaces the syllabic scripts, new political forms appear. Measured by technology and culture, not much collapsed at all.

The Terminal Classic Maya are the cleanest case for the reorganisation reading. The tenth-century abandonment of the southern lowlands — Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán — is archaeologically visible as the cessation of dated monuments and of royal inscription, and isotope records from cave speleothems and lake cores point to a run of severe droughts. But the northern Yucatán did not fall; it flourished, and Chichén Itzá went on to build something close to an empire. Independent Maya polities survived in places until the late seventeenth century, and there are millions of Maya speakers today. What "collapsed" was a particular political formation — divine kingship and its monument-building apparatus — in one region, while the people, the languages and much of the culture continued elsewhere.

Rapa Nui is where the reorganisation reading gets harder, and where the popular version is most often wrong. Diamond's account in *Collapse* is the main source of the rat hypothesis: on this view the loss of the palm forest was substantially the work of the Polynesian rat, which the settlers brought with them and which consumed the palm nuts that would otherwise have regenerated the forest. So the islanders were not the self-destructive ecocidal fools of the older story, and the real demographic catastrophe — slave raiding, smallpox, missionisation — belongs after European contact rather than before it. That makes Rapa Nui a case of resilience interrupted from outside, not of self-inflicted collapse.

Tainter supplies the general frame. Societies add complexity — bureaucracies, specialised roles, infrastructure — in order to solve problems, and complexity costs energy. Marginal returns on that investment eventually decline, until a society is paying more for each further increment of problem-solving than the increment is worth. At that point shedding complexity is not a failure but an option, and often a rational one: for Rome, Tainter's point is that many individuals were better off afterwards and that some local populations welcomed the incoming groups as liberators. If that is right, the objection that "collapse" smuggles in a verdict is answered not by refusing the word but by observing that the verdict is usually false. Collapse is not a catastrophe for the people who live through it, since it is precisely the point at which the costs of complexity stop exceeding its benefits.

Two conditions follow for "collapse" to be legitimate description rather than value judgement. First, the loss has to be at the level of institutions, not merely of personnel: it must be possible to say what structures stopped existing and roughly when. Second, the loss must not be offset by a comparable gain elsewhere in the same system, otherwise we are describing a relocation of complexity rather than a loss of it. Both conditions are met in the Late Bronze Age, at least for the Aegean and Anatolia. The second is not met for the Maya, where the north took up what the south dropped, and arguably not for Rapa Nui either. So the term survives, but only as a claim about political structures, and only where the reorganisation is not a transfer.

Where the reorganisation reading goes too far is in treating every case as reorganisation. Egypt's survival shows that being embedded in a wider system can make collapse unavailable as an option — Tainter's own point that modern societies may be unable to collapse because there is always a neighbour to absorb the pieces. In 1200 BCE the eastern Mediterranean had no such outside, which is why the system went together rather than piece by piece.
