Could you cast an eye over some notes for a paper I'm assembling? It's on Gödel's rotating universe and what he took it to show about time. Deadline's looming and I cant see the wood for the trees any more.

The line I want to run:

1. Setup. In 1949 Gödel produced an exact solution of the field equations: pressureless dust plus a negative cosmological constant, rotating, and stationary — no expansion. The dust particles are the privileged observers; they're in free fall, yet a gyroscope carried along with them precesses relative to the matter distribution, so the stuff of the world is rotating with respect to local inertial frames. Past a certain radius there are closed timelike curves, so in principle you can get back to your own past.

2. The inference, as I read it, is modal rather than empirical. He isn't saying our cosmos is Gödelian; he's saying the mere compatibility of such a world with the field equations shows the theory has no room for an objective lapse of time — a genuinely real feature of the world shouldn't switch on and off depending on which solution happens to obtain.

3. Payoff: time is ideal, in a broadly Kantian sense. He's usually lumped in with McTaggart, tho I think that's sloppy: McTaggart argues from the concept of change, Gödel from the causal structure of a physical theory. That deserves a paragraph of its own.

4. My objection, a dilemma. Either the closed curves are doing the real work, in which case the argument is about a universe we know isn't ours and the natural reply is just that it's a mathematical curiosity; or the real work is done by the absence of any distinguished simultaneity, in which case he's running the familiar Rietdijk–Putnam move and the rotation is decoration.

What I'd like from you: is (2) a fair reading, or am I being too generous to the modal step? Does the dilemma in (4) bite, or is there a third reading on which the curves matter precisely because that world isn't actual? And is anything in (1) factually dodgy — I'm particually unsure about the gyroscope business and about whether the curves really are only out at large radius. Blunt is fine, no need to spare my feelings. Cheers.
