You are to take the role of a philosopher of perception who has spent twenty years chairing seminar question periods: someone equally at home with the conceptual literature on cognitive penetration and with the experimental methodology behind the studies that literature invokes. I want you to prepare me for a question period. Think carefully before writing. Where you believe I have described the evidence in a way that caricatures what the literature actually says, flag it and correct me first, and then proceed on the corrected basis; I would much rather be told now than in the room.

Some background. I am a retired experimental psychologist. For three decades I ran a laboratory working on visual thresholds, adaptation, and attention, and only since retiring have I taken up philosophy in earnest. Next month I give an invited talk in a philosophy department's Friday seminar. I am a novice at this genre, I am not quick on my feet in dialectical exchange, and I want a small number of load-bearing moves I can deliver from memory rather than a dozen clever ones I will fumble.

The thesis I intend to defend is deliberately deflationary, and runs roughly as follows.

First, cognitive penetration, as the construct is defined, requires a causal route from a cognitive state — belief, desire, expectation — to the phenomenal character of a perceptual experience, where that route runs neither through (a) a shift in how the subject weights or reports on evidence at the judgmental stage, nor through (b) changes in attention, fixation, and eye movement that alter the input to the visual system or the competition among candidate stimuli.

Second, each family of evidence offered for penetration is, so far as I can see, equally well accommodated by (a) and (b): the effects of hunger and desire on what subjects report seeing in bistable figures; the effects of category training on what subjects subsequently report in ambiguous line drawings; the effects of lexical colour categories on discrimination at category boundaries; the effects of expectation on reported size, speed, or presence.

Third, no study I know of has controlled for both routes at once. Yes–no reports unaccompanied by forced-choice measures cannot separate a criterion shift from a change in sensitivity; eye-tracking without matched attentional controls cannot tell you whether the eyes moved because of the belief or the belief because of the eyes.

Fourth, and consequently, the epistemological consequences philosophers draw from this work — that perceptual justification is not independent of the subject's other states, that internalist foundationalism must be rethought — do not follow, because the empirical work has not established its antecedent.

Now the room. About fifteen people. Four or five philosophers of perception, most of them sympathetic to rich perceptual content — they think we see kinds, causation, affordances, and emotional expression, not just shapes and colours — and at least two of them hold that penetration has genuine epistemological teeth. Two epistemologists, one of them a stubborn internalist, who care mainly about whether perceptual justification is doxastically independent and who are not much interested in my laboratory. One philosopher of science who will want to drag the discussion toward the theory-ladenness of observation and who expects a working scientist to be naive about it. Two or three graduate students in a joint cognitive-science programme; one of them has tried to replicate effects in this neighbourhood and is a methodological hawk of the post-replication-crisis variety. Nobody in the room runs a laboratory except that student. I am therefore acutely aware that a subtext of the afternoon will be whether I think my instruments settle questions that are not mine to settle, and I do not want to be caught sounding like a scientist lecturing philosophers about their own subject.

The format is forty-five minutes of talk and twenty-five of questions.

Here is what I should like from you, in this order.

Begin with a diagnosis of at most two hundred and fifty words: what each cluster in that room is committed to, what they will hear my thesis as claiming, and what would count for each of them as a satisfying answer rather than a dodge.

Then give me eight to ten objections, ordered by damage weighted against likelihood, with a one-line rationale for each position in the ordering. For each objection: phrase it the way that particular person would phrase it, in their own idiom, including the technical vocabulary they would reach for; supply a reply I can deliver aloud in about ninety seconds; and note where that reply is soft, what would have to be true for it to collapse, and whether I should concede the objection in whole or in part. Mark which objections travel in pairs — which one the room will press immediately after another — and what to say when the second follows the first.

Then give me a thirty-second concession to make early in the talk, one that costs me nothing and buys credibility with both the philosophers and the methodologist.

Then tell me the single reply that has to land for the talk not to be a failure, and why that one rather than another.

Then give me the question I am hoping nobody asks: one only, with an honest account of why I cannot answer it, what a partial answer would look like, and a graceful retreat if the question is put.

Finally, a short note on tone: three or four places in my sketch above where my framing invites the charge that I am begging a conceptual question by methodological means, and how I might restate the point without the charge attaching.

I do not want a bibliography or a reading list; I am not looking for reading. If you name a study, name it only where you are confident you have it right, and otherwise describe the paradigm. And if you think my thesis is wrong in some way I ought to know before I stand up in front of these people, say so in two or three sentences at the very end — but do not write the talk for me, and do not soften the diagnosis on my account.
