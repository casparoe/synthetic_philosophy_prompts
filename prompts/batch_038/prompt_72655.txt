I draft legislation for a living — twelve years of it, mostly unglamorous stuff: licensing regimes, fee schedules, the odd amendment nobody reads until someone sues. And I've noticed something about the textualism-versus-purposivism fight that I can't find anyone saying, so I want to know whether I'm onto something or just being too clever.

Both camps argue as though the things they're fighting about are inaccessible in principle. The textualist says all that survives enactment is the text's ordinary public meaning, fixed at the time it was passed, and that purpose is a fiction because a multimember body has no mind to read. The purposivist says the text runs out in the hard case and you have to reconstruct the point of the provision, because a rule read against its reason is not the rule that was made. Fine. But at my desk, both of those are... records. I know roughly what the ordinary reader hears, because we put clauses in front of non-lawyers before they ship and we change the words when they misread them. And I know what a provision is for, because there's an instruction from the committee, a memo, a redline where we struck one formulation and put another in for a reason that got written down. Neither object is metaphysically spooky. They're evidence.

Concrete case from my own file. A licensing provision requires "three years of supervised practice." Someone applies with three years spent running their own one-person practice in a jurisdiction that never required supervision. Text points one way, the obvious point of the requirement — protecting clients from people who've never had their work looked at by anyone — points the other. Except the drafting file shows we deleted "or equivalent experience" from that sentence deliberately, because the committee wanted a bright line and said so. So the evidence resolves it, and the interesting question becomes whether anyone is allowed to look.

So here's my hypothesis, and I want it pressure-tested rather than flattered: the debate has stalled because both sides are treating as a conceptual question something that is really an evidential one, and neither can afford to say that out loud. Once you admit the underlying facts are in principle discoverable, the fight over method collapses into a fight about admissibility, about who is competent to look, and about how to allocate the risk when the record is thin, sloppy, or lost — which is to say a fight about institutional authority and error, not about what meaning is.

Is that diagnosis right, or is there a residue it doesn't touch — something genuinely normative about what a court may rely on even when the drafter's file is sitting on the table? Tell me which of the usual categories this dispute actually falls into: verbal, substantive, two different questions sharing a word, or an ill-posed one that just looks hard. And tell me what each camp would say back to my reframe, in their own terms, not as strawmen. Then: what would count as progress on my version of it, and does the answer change when there is no record — an old statute, a fire, a legislature that kept nothing?

No citations or case names, I'm not looking for a bibliography. I want the diagnosis and the reasoning.
