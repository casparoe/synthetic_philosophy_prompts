I run a monthly Friday-evening series for adults at a regional science center — free, no prerequisites, usually twenty to thirty people, and largely the same faces every month, which means whatever they leave believing will be sitting in the room again next time. Next month's session is mine, and the advertised topic is the four kinds of creatures in Dennett's scheme: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. I have seventy-five minutes, a flip chart, no projector, and the room has to be cleared by 8:15. This is the same audience that came to our previous two sessions, one on corvid cognition and one on large language models, and those two evenings have left the group quietly split: several people now think the difference between a crow and a person is a question of how much processing power is on hand, while three or four others — software engineers who work on machine learning — arrived already persuaded that current language models are minds of the same kind as ours and want the session to say so. My hope is that this taxonomy hands them a shared set of distinctions to argue with rather than a shared set of adjectives. I read the underlying material years ago and remember the four names and the shape of the argument far better than I remember which particular animals were used to illustrate them, so please do not try to reconstruct the original examples for me; plan the session as though those details are unavailable.

What I want is the plan, not the explanation. First, the order in which the four categories must be introduced and what each step depends on. My own suspicion is that the Darwinian/Skinnerian boundary will cost me almost nothing with this audience, that the Skinnerian/Popperian boundary is where the real work happens, and that the Popperian/Gregorian boundary is where the whole thing either earns its keep or collapses into a restatement of the animals-versus-us platitude. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and if it is not, what the load-bearing joints actually are. Second, the carrying case. I am drawn to the octopus because it is strange enough to hold attention, but I suspect that is exactly the problem: a distributed, alien intelligence is poor scaffolding for a scheme meant to track cumulative additions of learning machinery. Say what an example needs to have and what it needs to lack, and whether one case can be run through all four stages or whether each transition requires its own. Third — and this is the request I most need taken seriously — give me the behavioral criterion that separates a Popperian creature from a merely Skinnerian one, phrased as something a person in that room could actually look for in an animal, together with an account of what a Popperian creature can do that no amount of reinforcement history could produce. If I cannot answer that crisply, the four labels are decoration.

Then the misconception map. I already expect: that the scheme is a ranking of worth, a ladder to climb with us at the top; that each kind replaces the one below, so that we are not also Darwinian creatures; that Skinnerian means performing trained tricks, which makes a circus seal the paradigm and a lab rat something else; that Popperian means doing science, or at least having theories in some grand sense; that Gregorian means having culture or using tools, which would place chimpanzees in the top tier; and that the engineers will immediately map Skinnerian onto reinforcement learning and Popperian onto model-based reinforcement learning and treat the classification of current AI systems as thereby settled. Tell me which of these I have mischaracterized, which are more damaging than I think, at what point in the sequence each should be headed off — before it forms or after it has been voiced — and which one I should let the group discover for itself and then dismantle. I also want a single closing question that would reveal whether the distinctions landed or only the names did: propose the case to be classified, ideally something nobody in that room holds a settled opinion about, and describe what a good answer and a revealingly bad answer would each look like. Finally, tell me what to leave out and to announce that I am leaving out — I assume that includes the intentional stance, and any discussion of whether the four kinds are empirically established rather than a useful fiction — and give me your view on whether flagging that second caveat at this level is honesty or self-sabotage. And since I know the room will not let me escape it: say whether the question of a large language model's tier can be answered by this taxonomy at all, what has to be in place before it can even be asked, and whether it belongs at minute fifteen as motivation or minute sixty as application.
