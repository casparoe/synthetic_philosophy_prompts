Please compose an original work of literary science fiction of approximately 2,200 words, to be delivered complete in your reply. The story must be self-contained and must not draw on any existing franchise, and it must dramatize the structure of the population-ethics debate over quantity versus quality of life without ever presenting that debate as a classroom lecture.

The setting and its institutions are specified below. Treat them as fixed and use the given terminology consistently throughout.

**Setting.** A sealed generation vessel, the *Canticle*, four hundred years into a voyage with no arrival date. The ship's population is governed by a Charter containing two provisions, both of which must be quoted or paraphrased in the text at least once:

1. **The Continuity Clause.** No population proposal may be assessed against any census earlier than the one immediately preceding it. Comparisons to the Founding Census are procedurally inadmissible, on the stated ground that the founders' circumstances are not comparable to anyone now alive.
2. **The Floor.** A legislated welfare score below which a life is not entered into the census as a positive quantity; such a life is recorded as a null entry, contributing nothing to the ship's aggregate tally, but also counting as no loss when it ends.

The ship keeps a running **Tally**: aggregate welfare, computed from scored lives. Each generation's cohort proposal is approved or refused solely by whether it raises the Tally relative to the previous census.

**Required content.**

- Present at least four censuses, separated by decades, each with concrete figures (population, mean welfare score, Tally), arranged so that every step is individually approved under the Continuity Clause, and so that a reader doing the arithmetic can see the Founding Census was better on both per-capita and, by the final step, arguably on every measure the ship still bothers to record.
- Include one scene in which a clerk discovers the perverse operation of the Floor: that in a failing season, admitting a large cohort of marginally scored lives raises the official Tally more than admitting a smaller, flourishing cohort, and that a catastrophe which removes lives scored below the Floor leaves the Tally untouched or improved. The clerk must recognize this as a defect and be unable to propose a revision that survives the Clause.
- Include two interlocutors with genuinely opposed and defensible principles: an auditor who holds that no one can be wronged by being brought into a life worth living and that only actual, identifiable people can be better or worse off; and a colleague who holds that what matters is how much good there is in the world, whoever happens to contain it. Neither may be a strawman, and neither may win by argument.
- Include one passage in which the auditor is asked to rank the Founding Census against the present population and must concede that their own principle delivers no ranking at all, because no one appears in both.
- Include one moment of vagueness: a proposed sharp cutoff for what counts as a life worth living, which its proposer cannot defend against the demand to say what makes the line fall exactly there rather than one increment either side.

**Prohibitions.** No character may explain the underlying paradox to a novice, a child, or a visitor; the reasoning must surface only through procedure, paperwork, and dispute over specific numbers. No dream sequences, no AI interlocutor that resolves the question, no reveal that the voyage ended long ago. No author's note, postscript, glossary, or any meta-commentary on the philosophy. The story may not end in either despair or uplift, and may not end with the discovery of the correct theory. It must end with the auditor signing or refusing to sign a specific proposal, at a cost that is stated, and the final line of the story must be a line of census arithmetic.
