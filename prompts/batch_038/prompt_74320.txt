For two years I have been moderating a monthly reading group on historical drama and narrative nonfiction — nine of us, mostly retired teachers, one county archivist, a woman who writes opera libretti — and we arrive at the same quarrel every single month, from whichever direction we approach it. So I have started writing down what I actually say about particular cases, to see whether there is anything underneath my verdicts at all. A prestige film shows a well-documented statesman weeping alone in a corridor the night before a negotiation; no one recorded it and nothing in the record suggests it happened. I call that acceptable. The same film, in an equally unrecorded private moment, shows him signing a deportation order with a small joke about the family's surname, when the surviving correspondence says he resisted for a week and afterwards described it as the worst thing he had ever done. I call that unacceptable, and I notice that neither of my two verdicts is doing any work with invented, or with private, since both scenes are invented and both are private. A novel takes a real woman who appears twice in the archive, once as a landlady and once as a witness at an inquest, and gives her an interior life, a child who died, a habit of humming while she works. Fine, I say. A different novel takes a different real man, documented just as thinly, a clerk who handled the same file, and makes him the informant who denounces the protagonist. Not fine, I say — and then my archivist observes that the clerk died in 1791 with no traceable descendants and no reputation to damage, while the statesman has a foundation, a prize named after him, and four living biographers who will object in print within a season. Pressed, I do not retreat: I think the clerk is the worse case of the two, precisely because there is nothing about him for the invention to be checked against and nobody left to answer for him.

Here is the rest of the list. When a film invents something false but flattering — the statesman opposing a policy six years before he actually opposed it, and looking prophetic for it — I think it wrongs him even though it only raises him, and I think it wrongs the audience more. When a film asserts nothing false whatever but selects and orders documented facts so that viewers leave certain the man was a coward, I am genuinely torn: I want to call that the worst item in the whole set, and I also want to say it is the one the filmmaker has the strongest right to make. When a novel closes with two pages listing exactly what was invented, and is then read by a million people who will retain the story and not the note, I do not think the note discharges anything; when a film marketed frankly as fiction performs the same invention with no note at all, I think the film comes out less culpable, which I recognize is backwards on any account built out of how much false belief gets into the world. When a diary is published against its author's instruction that it be burned and a dramatist then builds a scrupulously faithful scene out of it, I want to say the dramatist inherits a wrong rather than committing a fresh one, and I cannot say what inheriting amounts to. A screenwriter friend of mine thinks my list is missing a term, and offers her own: what matters is whether an invention is reversible — whether someone who later learns the truth can simply subtract it, or whether it has already become the only image anyone has of that person, so that no correction can reach it. She is confident reversibility, not harm, separates my permissible cases from my impermissible ones, and she thinks it explains the clerk and the statesman without any appeal to who is left alive to complain. My own tentative principle, which the group has spent a year trying to break, is narrower: a dramatization wrongs a real person only if it would have wronged that person had they been alive to watch it. I hold it partly because it seems to sort the weeping from the joke at the desk, and partly because I am reluctant to let obligations run toward people who cannot now be harmed by anything.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of them, precise enough that I could apply it to a case none of us has met, clause by clause, with the item from my list that each clause exists to handle. Test my principle and my friend's against the list, repair or replace them as needed, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts each surviving account forces me to surrender; if a considerably simpler principle gets everything except the clerk or the flattering falsehood, argue for the simpler one and say what it costs to give the other up. Then tell me what your best account rules about three situations I have not listed: a film that puts a real soldier's accurately recorded last words into the mouth of a character who is otherwise wholly invented; a documentary about a man who died eight years ago that reconstructs a humiliation from the recollection of a single classmate who may well be misremembering, and whose widow has asked for it to be left out; and a video game in which the player, as a real named monarch, orders an atrocity that never occurred, for an audience that will never read a history book. And finally, the case I keep going back and forth on, which is my own: I am writing a short play about a nurse attested in three letters, and in my version she stays at her post after the others have left. The letters neither say she stayed nor that she went; the one person who might know is her ninety-year-old daughter, who has already told my sister she does not want the play written at all. I want to know whether my account permits the play, forbids it, or permits it with a change I have not thought of.
