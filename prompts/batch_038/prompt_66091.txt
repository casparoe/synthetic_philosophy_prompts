You're a philosopher of mathematical practice here — think carefully, and do a short bullet brainstorm before you write anything up.

Take this arc: Zermelo's 1904 well-ordering proof, the exchange of letters between Baire, Borel, Hadamard and Lebesgue in 1905 where they basically insist that an existence you can't specify and a choice you can't carry out isn't mathematics; then Hausdorff's paradoxical decomposition of the sphere in 1914 and Banach–Tarski in 1924 — cut up one ball, reassemble two of the same size. And yet by the thirties AC is uncontroversial working mathematics, Zorn's lemma in the curriculum, and Solovay's 1970 model later shows you can have dependent choice with every set of reals measurable, so the non-measurable sets really were the price of full choice.

Analyze that whole episode through Gödel's split between intrinsic and extrinsic justification of axioms. Map it properly: what did the critics take thier defeater to be, was Banach–Tarski the defeater they wanted or a different one, and why did the extrinsic route — fruitfulness, Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff — steamroll the intrinsic complaint? Where does the lens go quiet or misdescribe the case, e.g. does it say anything at all about the fact that no decomposition can ever be exhibited? Then run the live parallel: determinacy versus choice in descriptive set theory — same shape of dispute or something genuinely different? Be willing to conclude the critics were right about AC and lost anyway, and say whether that's an indictment of the whole justification framework or just of intrinsic justification.
