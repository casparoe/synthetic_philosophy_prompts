I'm a philosophy PhD student and I'm the student member on my institute's Replacement Technologies Panel — we review grants that swap animal experiments for non-animal methods under the 3Rs. Thursday we vote on one that's split us down the middle and I got volunteered to write the memo that goes in front of the commitee. The case:

The PI wants about $2M over four years to build a neuromorphic chip model of rodent nociceptive processing, validated by reproducing ~40 published withdrawal and avoidance assays, with the stated goal of retiring the lab's chronic-pain mouse colony. Two clauses in our charter pull against each other: a replacement has to be scientifically valid, AND adopting it must not itself create morally considerable suffering. One half of the panel says if the chip really is functionally equivalent to the mouse system, then we may just be building a pain machine that's harder to detect than the colony — which is worse, not better. The other half says behavioral equivalence at the input/output level tells you nothing about whether the internal states line up (they keep gesturing at the China-brarn worry), and that if you think substrate matters at all, no chip could be equivalent in the first place — which would make the science pointless too. The PI's answer is that nobody can assay "what it's like" in a mouse either, so his evidential standard is no worse than the incumbent method's.

Draft the memo, ~800 words: fund, reject, or fund with conditions — and if it's conditions, make them concrete enough that we could actually monitor compliance, with a milestone review point. Give the losing side its strongest version rather than a strawman. And please mark where you're standing on settled ground vs. where people who work on this genuinely disagree; I don't want the memo asserting functionalism as the consensus view if it isn't one.
