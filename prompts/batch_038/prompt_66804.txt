Write a poem of exactly twenty lines about a heap — you may choose what accumulates (sand, stones, coins, sleepless nights, unreturned letters) and the vague predicate that the accumulation is meant to satisfy — under one strict formal rule: the first line contains a single word, and every line thereafter contains exactly one word more than the line before it, so that the twentieth line contains twenty. The growing lines should make a reader ask, at some genuine point in the reading, whether the thing has yet become what the poem keeps implying it is becoming.

Beyond that, the poem must sustain two incompatible readings of itself, and this is the real task.

It must be readable the way an epistemicist reads such a series: there is one line at which the predicate determinately comes to hold, fixed by the poem's own words and by the facts, and yet no amount of attention could let a reader say which line it is. Present the failure to know not as mystery but as the ordinary consequence of a margin for error — one grain further on, everything looks the same, so a reader who guessed correctly could never be in a position to know that they had.

And the same lines must be readable the way a supervaluationist reads them: no line is the one; a run of them lies in a stretch where the predicate and its negation are each neither true nor false; and what makes any line true is that it holds on every way of sharpening the language that the poem leaves admissible. Let the ties between that stretch and the clear cases survive — no admissible sharpening may pull apart two lines the poem has left indistinguishable.

Neither reading may win. The poem may not use the vocabulary of the debate: no "borderline," "vague," "penumbra," "supertrue," "precisification," "epistemic." The final line should be the single place where the two readings meet — a disjunction about the pile that both must grant, for entirely different reasons — and the pile at that point should be one a reader could honestly call either way, so that the agreement is not a cheap one. Someone who knows the argument should be able to recover those two reasons from the poem alone; someone who has never heard of it should simply find a good poem about something piling up.
