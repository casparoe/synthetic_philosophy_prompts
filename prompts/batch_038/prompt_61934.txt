So in my AP Gov class we did the Electoral College unit and my teacher brought up the faithless elector thing and I cant stop thinking about it. The setup, as far as I understand it: in a lot of states (I think it's more than 30 now) there's a law saying the people who get chosen as presidential electors have to vote for whoever won the popular vote in that state, and if they don't, the state can fine them, cancel their ballot, and swap in somebody else. In 2016 a bunch of electors actually tried to go off script, including a group that called itself the Hamilton Electors who wanted to block Trump, and a few of their votes got thrown out and replaced. Then in 2020 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that states are allowed to enforce those pledges, and the reasoning was basically that the elector is acting for the state's voters and has no personal stake in the choice. My friend said "obviously that's right, the electors are just a counting machine, if you dont like the result then vote differently in November." And separately a bunch of states have signed onto that compact where they'd hand their electors to whoever wins the national popular vote even if that person loses locally, which hasnt hit the threshold yet but shows you can just re-point the machine at a different target.

Here's what bugs me though. The exact same people who say binding electors is obviously correct will also say it's admirable when a senator breaks with their party on a war vote, or when a jury refuses to convict under a law they think is unjust, or when a legislature gives its members a free vote on something like abortion. In those cases we treat going against the expected line as integrity and call the person principled. For electors we call it faithlessness and fine them a thousand bucks. And also: I as a regular voter am legally allowed to write in my neighbor, or leave the presidential race blank, or spoil my ballot on purpose as a protest, and plenty of people argue thats a legitimate expressive act. So the ordinary voter gets both freedom and expression, and the elector who was picked because of who they are gets neither.

What theory of voting does the binding practice actually presuppose? Like what does it have to assume a vote IS? Is the elector's ballot just one causal link in a transmission chain running from the state's voters to the final tally, with zero room for judgment or for saying anything by casting it? Does it require that "the will of the voters" is already a fully determinate thing before anybody deliberates, so an elector thinking it over can only subtract and never add? Does it assume that the only legitimate function of voting is choosing between the options already on the ballot, so that voting for the lesser of two evils isn't a compromise anyone makes but just the mechanical output, and a vote for a third person is noise or sabotage? Also I'd want you to say where the practice is underdetermined, because I suspect several different theories could license it and they'd disagree at the edges: a straight majoritarian one, versus an agency/trust one where the voters have hired the elector the way you'd hire a lawyer who has to do what the client says, versus some kind of anti-corruption one where the point is just to stop backroom dealing over votes. Do those actually justify the same rules, or would they diverge on things like whether the fine is punishment or just voiding the vote, or whether an elector could ever be released from the pledge? And how do you square all this with the fact that the institution was supposedly invented so that a small group of people with real judgment could stop a demagogue (I'm pretty sure there's a Federalist paper that argues something like that)? If that was the design, what had to change in how people think about voting for the binding laws to now look like the obvious common-sense position?

Last thing: tell me what would have to change if we flipped it. If a state decided to treat its electors as free agents who could vote their conscience, what would we have to add or give up to keep that from being chaos — a public reason requirement, some standard for when going faithless is defensible, a totally different way of picking who becomes an elector in the first place? I want to know which specific features of how elections currently work only make sense under the binding view, and which ones would survive the switch.
