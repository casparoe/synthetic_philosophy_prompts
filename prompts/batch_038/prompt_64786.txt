I'm in a philosophy-of-math reading group and I put this argument together half as a joke, but now I can't find the exit and two people in the group think it's just straight-up sound. Here's the thing.

(1) By Gödel's second theorem, any consistent theory with enough arithmetic in it can't prove its own consistency. (2) So a proof that PA is consistent has to come from somewhere else — some other theory S, like ZF or whatever Gentzen was working in. (3) Any S that can prove Con(PA) is at least as strong as PA, so S is exactly as doubtful as PA is, and by (1) S can't settle its own consistency either. (4) Which means the only remaining support for S is either a proof in some yet stronger theory (same problem, regress) or the inductive fact that nobody has managed to derive a contradiction in over a hundred years of very heavy use. (5) But that inductive support is worthless. If PA were inconsistent, there's no bound on how long the shortest derivation of a contradiction could be — it might be longer then anything a human or a computer could ever write down. So the fact that nobody has found one doesn't tell you anything at all; PA looks exactly the same whether it's consistent or inconsistent-with-an-enormous-proof. (6) Therefore we have no more reason to think PA is consistent than to think it isn't, and every theorem of PA rests on a premise nobody can defend. (7) Therefore most of mathematics isn't known, and the difference between a mathematician and a gambler is just that the gambler has seen the odds.

Someone suggested Gentzen gets us out — he proved PA consistent using induction up to ε₀. But that looks like it just moves the furniture: now you have to trust ε₀-induction, and by (3) trusting that is no better than trusting PA in the first place.

So which step do I reject? Obviously I'd rather give up (6) and (7) than (1), but I want to know what I'm actually paying. Three things I'm stuck on. First, is (5) right? It feels like there's a gap between "there's no upper bound on how long a consistent-looking theory can keep looking consistent" and "the evidence we actually have has literally zero weight," but I can't tell if that gap is a real logical error or just me not wanting the conclusion. Second, I want someone to pressure-test the strength comparison in (3) — I assumed that if S proves Con(PA) then S is somehow bigger than PA and so at least as shaky, and that inference feels obvious to me, but I'm not sure "bigger" and "shakier" are even the same kind of thing. Third, does the whole argument prove too much? Steps (1) through (7) are themselves mathematical reasoning, so if it works shouldn't it also undercut my confidence in the argument itself — and is that a fatal self-refutation, or just a bite-the-bullet move a skeptic can live with?
