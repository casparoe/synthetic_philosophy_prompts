So I started three weeks ago at a tiny startup doing spiking-neural-network chips, and our founder is a hardcore von Neumann fanboy — The Computer and the Brain is on basically every slide deck he's ever made. Yesterday he forwarded me a chunk of some pop-sci retrospective about the book and asked me to fold it into a blog post I'm suppossed to write. The bit he liked goes roughly like this:

"Von Neumann did what nobody else had bothered to do and looked at the actual numbers. A neuron is a hopelessly sloppy component: it can fire only a few hundred times a second, where the vacuum tubes of his day were switching a million times, and it can't be trusted to transmit anything precisely. So he reasoned that the brain must cope the way any good engineer would — redundancy and error correction — and that underneath all the biological mess it's really a digital machine, since an axon spike either happens or it doesn't. His back-of-the-envelope calculation put the brain's storage capacity far above anything built in his lifetime, which he took as showing that machines could never compete on memory. His conclusion: brains and computers differ in speed and scale, not in principle."

Mostly I want an accuracy audit before I put my name next to this. Which of those claims is actually his, which is a defensible compression, and which is just not what he argued? A few specific things I'm unsure about: did he think he'd settled the digital-versus-analog question for the nervous system, or did he think it stayed open? Is the redundancy-and-error-correction line something he endorsed as the mechanism, or something he floated as a possibility? And does that last sentence — speed and scale, not principle — survive once you fix whatever's wrong above it? My gut says it doesnt, but I can't say why, and I'd really rather not have an actual historian of computing write in to correct us after it goes live.
