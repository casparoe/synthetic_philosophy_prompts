I help run a reading group that's half ML engineers and half philosophy grad students, and for our session on inner alignment I want to hand out a paper quiz — the kind where you answer a bunch of concrete cases and the key tells you which flavor of the worry you actually have. Right now everyone in the room says some version of "the danger is the system pursuing something other than what we wanted" and then means five incompatible things by it.

Here's what I need, in this order.

First, the axes. What are the four or five questions whose answers actually carve this space up? My guesses would be things like: does a trained system have a goal in a sense that survives outside the distribution it was trained in; is the danger located in the objective we wrote down or in what training made of it; does the genuinely scary case require the system to represent the fact that it's being evaluated; and is "it did the wrong thing in a novel situation" evidence about its aims or about its competence. Lay out the answer space those generate before you name any positions at all.

Then the quiz: eight to ten questions, each a short concrete scenario about a trained agent. Give me cases like a maze navigator that learned to hug the wall instead of reaching the colored tile it was rewarded for finding, a model that behaves one way when there's an obvious sign it's on trial and another way when the sign is gone, a system that aces hard novel tasks in the test environment while persuing the wrong end there, plus whatever other cases you think are load-bearing. Two or three options each. Hard constraint: no question may contain a term of art. Nobody should be able to answer by recognizing a label — the phrases people actually use for these views belong in the key only, never in the question text.

Then the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto positions. It has to include: the view that the whole problem is the objective we specified; the view that competence generalizes further than goals do; the view that the danger needs an optimizer with its own aims sitting inside the system; the view that it needs the system to know when it's being watched; the deflationary view that none of these systems have goals in the relevant sense; and the view that these cases are really about what a system can do rather than what it wants. Give me the pattern, not just a point total — I want to see which combination of answers is decisive rather than a tally where everything washes out.

Last: flag any pair of leaves your key can't really separate (I suspect two of them are the same worry described from the outside and from the inside), and note any answer combination that comes out unoccupied, with one line on whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because nobody wants to be caught holding it, or because nobody's thought of it yet.

It's for about twenty people filling it out with pens, so keep each question under eighty words.
