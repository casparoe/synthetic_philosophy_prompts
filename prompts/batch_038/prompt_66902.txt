i run a decision theory seminar and i want to hand out a short poem at the end of the session on the Allais paradox — something that does what the two-column table on the board doesn't, which is make you feel the cancellation happening.

the conceit i'm after: the poem is two stanzas, same length (say 10–14 lines each), same meter, same rhyme scheme — no other formal requirement, ballad meter or blank verse or whatever you like, as long as both stanzas are audibly in the same one. the second stanza is the first with one thing surgically removed. concretely, so you don't have to go recalling the numbers:

stanza one sets up the pair where option A is a sure million and option B is a 10% shot at five million, an 89% shot at the million, and a 1% shot at nothing — and the speaker takes the sure thing, cheerfully, without being made to look stupid for it. stanza two sets up the pair you're left with once the common consequence is stripped out: 11% for the million against 10% for the five million, nothing otherwise — and the same speaker now takes the long shot. the whole point is that the only difference between the two situations, its the branch both options share, and that branch is supposed to cancel. so the form has to show the cancelling: the shared 89% should appear in stanza one as something real and felt (rent, a hospital bill, a door that doesn't rattle) and in stanza two it should simply be gone, with nothing put in its place — and the speaker's hand should move differently anyway, for reasons the poem lets stand rather than diagnoses.

some constraints i'd like you to actually honor: don't use the words axiom, utility, probability, or expected value anywhere in the poem, and don't name Allais or Savage in it either (a title is fine). the numerals can appear but it mustn't read like a table with line breaks. i dont want a lecture and i don't want the poem sneering at the speaker — someone who knows the paradox should be able to point at the second stanza and say "that's the derived pair," and should come away thinking the inconsistency is a real datum, not a blunder. someone who's never heard of it should get a decent poem about a person who wants two things that can't both be had and half-knows it.

then, after the poem, give me two or three sentences — not about the poem's craft — telling me which reading of the case you leaned on, since there's more than one going around (certainty as a special kind of thing, the common-consequence versus common-ratio framing, risk versus ambiguity, regret or disappointment about the 1% branch). say how confident you are that the version you wrote is faithful to what's actually driving people's choices here, and single out the one line you're least sure of, the line most likely to be carrying a claim you can't back.
