I'm in a seminar on societal collapse where one participant keeps pressing this analogy: a society no more collapses than a caterpillar collapses when it becomes a butterfly. The larva dissolves inside the chrysalis, the adult is built from what survives, and it is one continuous life throughout. So, he says, the Classic Maya did not fail — they reorganized: the people, the languages, and the ritual calendar persisted, and the lowland cities were abandoned rather than destroyed. His conclusion is that "collapse" is an engineering metaphor doing covert moral work, that societies only ever change, and that the concept should be dropped from serious use.

Audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table — caterpillar, chrysalis, the dissolved larval tissue, the imaginal discs, the genetic program, the imago — saying which social feature each is meant to pick out and whether anything in the historical record actually occupies that slot. Then separate the disanalogies that genuinely defeat his conclusion from those that are only unflattering surface differences. In particular: what would have to persist for a society to count as the same society, and is there anything in these cases that answers to tissue broken down rather than carried over?

Tell me whether the conclusion survives, whether there is a non-evaluative description of what is lost that the analogy has no room for, and whether some other analogy would carry his point better or worse. Finally, say what would change your verdict — which evidence or argument would push you the other way — and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
