Okay so I've been in the same ethics reading group for three years and there's one argument we have now had maybe four times without anybody moving a millimeter: whether there are external reasons. You know the shape of it. On one side, Bernard Williams's line that a reason has to hook up with something in the agent's subjective motivational set — via rational deliberation if need be, not just by straightforward instrumental calculation. On the other, the response that some things are reason-giving whether or not the agent could ever be moved by them, full information or no. Both sides agree on all the non-normative facts about every case we bring up. Both sides think the other is confused rather than merely wrong. Nobody has budged since 2022.

My hypothesis, which I want you to attack at least as hard as you defend it: the disagreement isn't really about reasons at all, its about two different jobs we ask reason-claims to do. The internalist is answering "what could legitimately figure in this person's own deliberation, and what could make their action intelligible from the inside?" The externalist is answering "what makes it legitimate for the rest of us to blame, advise, or demand things of them?" If that's right, then Williams's young-man-from-a-military-family example and the usual counterexamples are just the two camps reporting intuitions generated by different questions that happen to share a word, and the whole thing is one big equivocation. There's a weaker second version too: internalism is a kind of relativism about normative claims — structurally like relativism about what's tasty — and the stalemate is the standard one you get when a relativist and an objectivist argue about a predicate without noticing that they disagree about what correctness consists in rather than about any particular case.

Two cases I want the diagnosis to actually chew on, because I think they pull apart informatively. First: my aunt, who knows everything there is to know about her estranged brother, has thought about it for years with a therapist's help, and reports — sincerely, I'm convinced — that the idea of visiting him while he's dying does not and would not move her in the slightest. Her family says she obviously has a reason to go. She says she doesn't. Neither side has an argument the other accepts, and the phrase everyone reaches for is some variant of "she's not really taking it in." Second: someone with no motive whatsoever to learn the cello who acquires one through reflection alone. The internalist cheerfully counts that as a reason coming into existence, which makes me suspicious that the deliberation clause is so permissive it can never actually be violated — in which case internalism isn't a thesis with content, it's a placeholder with a normative theory smuggled into the word "deliberation."

So: diagnose it. Verbal, substantive, or two different questions sharing a name — and be specific about which parts land in which bucket, because I'd bet the answer is mixed. Where exactly does the deliberation clause do the work, and is there any specification of it that both camps could sign without surrendering their view? Does either side come out right by accident, i.e. is there a reading on which internalism and externalism are both true and compatible, and if so why hasn't that reading ended the argument already? And then tell me what would count as progress from here — a distinction, a reformulation of the thesis itself, a new species of case, or an honest argument that this one is permanently stuck and should be abandoned. I don't want a survey of the positions and I don't want a reading list, I want the diagnosis and what follows from it. If you think my hypothesis is wrong, say what the real fault line is instead.
