For about six years I have co-hosted a reading group that grew out of a LessWrong meetup — roughly ten of us, half software engineers, one ex-physics PhD, two actual philosophers who tolerate us — and we work through the population ethics and distributive justice literature in small chunks, keeping a shared document of what we take the live cruxes to be. That document has been accumulating entries we cannot close, and the worst by far concerns the levelling down objection. As we understand the dialectic: Parfit's point is that if equality is intrinsically valuable, then in a case where we merely take away the advantages of the better off, leaving some people worse off and nobody better off, the resulting world must be better in some respect — it is more equal — even though nothing good has happened to anyone; and a theory committed to that has lost the plot, since value ought to be good for somebody. Prioritarianism, which says only that a benefit matters more the worse off the person who receives it, is supposed to escape the problem entirely. The egalitarian replies we have collected run three ways: that the objection attacks a claim no egalitarian makes, because the theory posits a pro tanto reason and never asserts that levelling down is better all things considered; that if the objection is pressed at the level of pro tanto reasons it condemns every pluralist who admits any non-welfare value — desert, autonomy, aesthetic value — and therefore cannot be a special problem for equality; and that prioritarianism yields unpalatable verdicts of its own once it is pushed inside a single life or across changing population sizes. In our group, and in the EA Forum threads where the same two paragraphs get pasted back and forth, the objection and the reply both reliably attract upvotes, and nobody has updated in six years.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a conclusion, is that this is a disguised query: the two sides have never agreed about what kind of claim the assertion that equality is valuable is supposed to be, so the objection slides between levels. It looks decisive if being better in some respect means something capable of being weighed into an overall verdict; merely interesting if it means only that one value term in a pluralist axiology has gone up; and arguably empty if it is really a claim about which attitudes would be fitting toward a pattern. On that reading the objection functions as a motte and bailey, and the stall reflects a failure to fix the level of the claim before arguing about it. I have a second suspicion, which may be naive: that the disagreement is extensionally empty. We ran a small exercise in the group, representing telic egalitarianism as ranking outcomes by a total-welfare term plus an equality term, and prioritarianism as ranking them by the sum of a strictly concave transform of each person's welfare, and on every fixed-population pair we tried the two orderings agreed; they seemed to come apart only when we varied who exists, or when we asked how goods are distributed across the stages of a single life. If that generalizes, I worry the quarrel is about how to write down theories that issue the same recommendations, which would explain why no case intuition has ever settled anything.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on whether equality is valuable. Specifically: (a) tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, and if so name the term doing the work and say whether anything substantive survives once the ambiguity is resolved; two camps answering different questions under a single heading; a hidden disagreement about a background principle, which I suspect is the principle that value must be good for someone, that neither side has argued for; or a substantive disagreement that is merely hard; (b) work through the three levels I listed and say, at each, whether the objection is trivially true, trivially false, or genuinely biting, and identify the level at which each of the three standard replies is actually pitched; (c) assess my extensional worry — is it right that the two theories coincide in their verdicts over fixed populations, and if they diverge only under variable populations or intrapersonally, does that divergence supply a crux capable of forcing an update on either side; and (d) tell me what would count as progress here, whether that is a distinction that dissolves the problem, a formal result isolating exactly where the two theories part company, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Close with your honest view on how a non-specialist should treat the levelling down objection in practice: as a decisive refutation, as a legitimate constraint on how value claims should be phrased, or as a rhetorical device that has done more work in comment threads than in arguments. Please give me no references, no bibliography and no suggested further reading — I cannot check any of it, and if you are unsure which author advanced which reply, describe the reply without naming anyone.
