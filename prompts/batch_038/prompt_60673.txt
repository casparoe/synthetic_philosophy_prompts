I'm auditing a first-year graduate seminar in classical Chinese philosophy, and the line the instructor returns to most often is the one from the white horse dialogue: "a white horse is not a horse." What frustrates me is that it seems to be doing very different jobs on different weeks, and I can no longer tell whether the disagreements in the room are substantive or merely verbal. In the first session it was offered as evidence that the School of Names anticipated a distinction between intension and extension, and that Gongsun Long was making a point about concepts rather than about animals. A few weeks later, the same sentence was invoked to support the far stronger claim that the dialogue shows the Chinese notion of "is" was never a relation of class membership to begin with, so that translating the formula with the copula already imports a foreign logic. Most recently a classmate used it in a dispute with a visitor from the linguistics department: the visitor had argued that the paradox rests on a simple equivocation, and my classmate replied that the whole point of the dialogue is that "white horse" and "horse" are non-substitutable in exactly the contexts the argument turns on, so there is no equivocation to find. I suspect all three uses cannot be right at once, and I suspect at least one of them is smuggling in a much stronger thesis than the sentence itself carries.

What I would like is a disambiguation rather than a defense of any single interpretation. Please lay out, as a menu I can bring to the next session, the distinct precise claims that this one sentence could be making, ordered roughly from the least to the most revisionary. For each reading, state the thesis in a form that can actually be assessed — what it says about names, about what a compound name picks out, about the relation between a thing and its properties, or about the semantic behavior of the copula across the two languages; say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or flatly false; and say what else would have to be true for it to hold. Two of the readings I'd especially like treated explicitly are one on which the argument is about satisfaction or selection — roughly, that what is asked for under one name is not what is asked for under another, so a demand for a horse and a demand for a white horse are distinct demands even though every white horse is a horse — and one on which it is a claim about the components of a compound name, that combining a color term with a shape term yields a name whose content is not just the content of the shape term plus something incidental. I would also like to know where the dialogue's own supporting moves, such as the observation that if you ask for a horse a yellow or black one will do but if you ask for a white horse it will not, actually support which of these readings and not others.

Finally, please adjudicate the classroom uses rather than leaving the menu freestanding. Which reading, if any, licenses the inference that the paradox anticipates a distinction between intension and extension? Which reading would make the visitor from linguistics right that an equivocation is being exploited, and which would vindicate my classmate's appeal to non-substitutability without committing anyone to a general theory of opaque contexts? And is the claim about the copula a thesis about the classical Chinese language itself, or a methodological warning about translation that has been restated as a metaphysical discovery — if it is the latter, say what would count as evidence for it. Please write in American English, avoid presenting the readings as a survey of the secondary literature, and feel free to note where a reading depends on textual or philological assumptions I have not stated.
