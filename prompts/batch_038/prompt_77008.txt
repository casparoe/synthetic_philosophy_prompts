My sister lent me a book by a law professor making the case for restorative justice, and one chapter leans really hard on Strawson. Here's roughly how it puts his argument -- I've cut it down but kept the shape:

'Strawson showed that being responsible just is being an appropriate target of reactive attitudes, resentment and indignation chief among them. These are feelings, not beliefs, so they have no truth conditions and cant be contradicted by any fact, including the fact of determinism. We couldn't give them up if we tried anyway; they're constitutive of being a social animal. So the free will debate was a pseudo-probelm: the pessimist who thinks determinism destroys responsibility is simply confused, and holding people responsible turns out to be a matter of expressing that we demanded decent regard from them, not of inflicting anything on them. Strawson also treats the objective attitude as a kind of failure of respect, a way of demoting a person to a thing to be managed. It follows that a Strawsonian community has no use for retributive punishment at all.'

She then uses all that to argue criminal prosecution should mostly be replaced by structured dialogue between offender and victim.

Is this a fair reading? Go through it claim by claim -- what's accurate, what's a legitimate simplification, what would a careful Strawsonian push back on. Two spots bother me already. One is the feelings-with-no-truth-conditions line, because as far as I remember his whole discussion of excuses and exemptions is about whether an attitude was called for in the case, which sounds like something that could be gotten right or wrong. The other is the objective attitude -- I thought that was more like the stance you take toward a small child or someone whose capacities are damaged, not automatically a moral failing in the person adopting it. And once those are sorted, does the no-punishment conclusion come out of Strawson, or is the book loading it onto him?
