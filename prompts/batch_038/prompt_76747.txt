I am convening an interdisciplinary reading group on the science and philosophy of consciousness this term, and I am putting together a handout for our opening session because a single sentence keeps derailing us. The sentence is "consciousness is an illusion". I have now heard it do three quite different pieces of work in three different rooms. At a departmental seminar on machine sentience, a speaker used it to suggest that asking whether a large language model is conscious is an empty question, since nobody — including us — is conscious in the sense the question presupposes. At a public debate, a panellist used it to dissolve what is usually called the hard problem: there is no explanatory gap to cross, because there is nothing on the far side of it. And a friend of mine who works in experimental psychology read it as a licence to discard first-person reports altogether, on the grounds that if experience is an illusion then introspection is a broken instrument and nothing it registers counts as evidence. I am fairly confident that these three moves are not licensed by the same claim, and that at least one of them is not licensed by any claim that is both defensible and interesting. Someone also told me, and I could not follow the argument, that there is an important difference between saying that our introspective reports are inaccurate and saying that what those reports are reports of is inaccurate, and that only one of the two can be true. I would like the handout to settle this.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu rather than as a continuous essay, the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be expressing, ordered from the most anodyne to the most radical. For each entry give me: a one-sentence formulation in which it is clear exactly what is being denied and exactly what is being conceded; a verdict on whether the thesis is trivially true, substantive and live, false, or self-undermining; and a note on whether it is compatible with treating introspective self-reports as data in cognitive science, since that is the point my psychologist friend pressed. I would also like each reading paired with the familiar illusion it is implicitly modelled on — the stick that looks bent in water, the sun that seems to travel across the sky, the phantom limb, the change-blindness demonstration, the retrospective sense of having willed an action one did not in fact initiate — together with a statement of whether the model actually transfers. The question I care about most is this: in each of those cases the illusion leaves a genuine appearance in place, something that it is like to be fooled, whereas the radical readings seem to eliminate the appearance along with the thing it purported to reveal. Tell me which readings are vulnerable to that objection, whether the vulnerability is fatal or merely rhetorical, and whether any reading can be an illusion claim at all without presupposing the phenomenal character it denies.

Two further requests. First, flag explicitly any pair of readings on your list that you cannot really distinguish from one another, and note any combination that seems to be left unoccupied — saying in each case whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has thought of it. Second, tell me which reading each of my three speakers was most probably invoking, and whether their conclusion survives once the reading is fixed; if one of the three uses rests on a straight conflation, say which and where the conflation happens. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words in polished prose I could hand to students without editing. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or verbatim quotations from the literature — I can track sources down myself, and what I need from you is the analysis, not the apparatus. If you think the slogan is best abandoned rather than disambiguated, say so at the end and explain what would be lost by dropping it.
