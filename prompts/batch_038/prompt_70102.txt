The question as it appeared on the paper (30 marks, 50 minutes, closed book):

> In the notes published posthumously as On Certainty, Wittgenstein distinguishes a class of propositions that are held fast by everything around them from ordinary empirical propositions, and compares them to hinges on which our questions and doubts turn. Consider four claims:
> (i) There are two armchairs in this room.
> (ii) The earth existed for a long time before my birth.
> (iii) The mass spectrometer down the hall is reading correctly right now.
> (iv) Things go on existing when nobody is looking at them.
>
> (a) [8] Explain how a hinge proposition is supposed to differ from an ordinary empirical proposition that happens to be extremely well confirmed. Why isn't it simply the top of the same scale?
> (b) [8] For each of (i)–(iv), say whether it's a good candidate for being a hinge, and explain what makes the verdict vary from case to case.
> (c) [8] What is the notion supposed to do for the problem of radical skepticism? Does it show the skeptic to be mistaken, and if so, how? Bring in Moore's hands somewhere in your answer.
> (d) [6] State one serious objection to the view, and say how a defender might reply.

The script:

(a) The point of the hinge category isn't to pick out beliefs we happen to be more confident about than others. An ordinary empirical proposition, however well confirmed — say, this wire conducts — is still the sort of thing evidence speaks for and against, and there's always some further check you could run. A hinge isn't at the top of that list, it isn't on the list at all. It isn't supported by evidence, and no evidence defeats it either; it's held fast by the whole system of what we do and say around it, and it's what makes asking questions, running checks and doubting possible in the first place. Doubt only makes sense where there's some procedure for settling it, so if you tried to doubt everything at once you wouldn't end up with an enormous doubt, you'd end up with no doubt, because there'd be nothing left standing for the doubt to be a doubt about. Hinges are therefore a different kind of item entirely: for them error isn't even a live possibility, which is a stronger standing than knowledge itself, since knowledge can be lost or overturned and a hinge cannot be given up.

(b) (i) is not a hinge. It's an ordinary empirical claim, it could be false — somebody might have carried one of them out — and it's settled by looking. (ii) is a hinge, and it's the paradigm case. Nothing in my experience supports it the way experience supports (i); my memories and the historical record presuppose it rather than testifying to it, and there's no procedure by which I could check it, since any check would have to assume it already. (iii) is the interesting one. As a bare sentence it looks perfectly checkable — run a control, compare it to a second instrument — but in the middle of an actual experiment it functions as a hinge, because every reading the scientist takes presupposes it, and the only way to check it is with other apparatus she trusts in exactly the same way. (iv) is a hinge as well, though it's dressed in a philosopher's vocabulary: what it articulates is the practice of leaving the room, coming back, and finding the chair, a practice nobody could so much as describe without it. The verdicts come out differently because being a hinge isn't a property of a sentence considered on its own; it's a property of the role that sentence plays in a particular practice, so the same sentence can be a hinge in one language-game and an ordinary empirical claim in another.

(c) The skeptic says you can't know you have hands, since you can't rule out being a handless brain fed appropriate stimulations. The hinge answer is that the claim about my hands was never the conclusion of an argument, so demanding an argument for it demands something that was never on offer. And the doubt doesn't get going, because it has to be voiced in a language whose words get their meaning from the very practices the doubt is meant to call into question: strip those away and you're left with noises, not a question. So yes, the skeptic is wrong — but not because we've come up with better evidence than she has. She's wrong because her question isn't a real question. This is also where Moore goes wrong, though in a different way. He isn't wrong about the facts, there's a hand there obviously, but he presents a hinge as a piece of knowledge he is in a position to establish, and by doing that he's already playing the skeptic's game on the skeptic's terms: he uses "know" in just the place where it has no job to do, since you'd only say you knew something where there was a genuine question about it.

(d) Objection: the account threatens to make hinge-hood a matter of psychology. A test that suggests itself is that whatever you would refuse to give up no matter what evidence arrived is a hinge. But then stubbornness becomes certainty. Scientists routinely refuse to abandon a well-confirmed principle in the face of anomalous data — conservation of energy is the classic case, where an anomaly gets treated as the sign of some unaccounted third factor rather than a refutation — and on that test those principles count as hinges, so every scientific revolution is just a hinge swap and there's no difference in kind between Einstein dropping Newtonian mechanics and a man refusing to accept that his wife is dead. A defender can reply that the test is too crude: hinges are fixed by their role in a shared practice, not by anyone's disposition to hold on, and the physicist who keeps conservation of energy is working inside a practice that still has a settled procedure for adjudicating anomalies, whereas there is no procedure at all for adjudicating (ii). That's a real difference, but it doesn't rescue the test, and it points at something a sharper version of the objection should press. A scientific principle, however entrenched, is revisable in a way a hinge is not: it can be replaced by a better one and we can date the replacement. Nobody could say when we stopped holding fast to the past, or what evidence made us stop, because there wasn't any — which is exactly why a hinge isn't the sort of thing that could ever be revised.
