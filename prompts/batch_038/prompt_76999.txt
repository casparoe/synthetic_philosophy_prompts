I run a reading group on modality, and last week I brought in an argument I had assembled against what I will call modal rationalism — the thesis that a suitably idealized form of conceivability entails metaphysical possibility. My purpose was to corner a friend who defends the zombie argument, since his argument needs precisely that entailment, and I wanted to show him that the entailment cannot be had without paying for it everywhere else. The argument worked better than I intended: it now seems to establish that nobody knows any possibility claims at all, myself included, and I cannot find the exit. Here it is, with the steps numbered so that you can point to one.

(1) For any statement S: if an ideal reasoner entertains a complete and wholly positive description D of a situation w, detects no contradiction in D, and judges that S is true at w, then it is metaphysically possible that S.

(2) Let D be the following description: a world w containing an ocean-filling, clear, drinkable, rain-falling liquid, whose molecular constitution is specified in full detail and differs from that of the liquid in our oceans, and which otherwise matches our world in every intrinsic respect. An ideal reasoner detects no contradiction in D and judges that the sentence "water is not H₂O" is true at w. (I have taken care to make D entirely positive, so that nobody can object that the description is negative and therefore incomplete.)

(3) So it is metaphysically possible that water is not H₂O. [from 1, 2]

(4) If water is H₂O, then necessarily water is H₂O, since both terms are rigid designators.

(5) Water is H₂O.

(6) So it is not metaphysically possible that water is not H₂O. [from 4, 5]

(7) Therefore either (1) or (2) is false. [from 3, 6]

(8) (2) is not false. I deliberately phrased it as a claim about the truth of a sentence at a described world rather than as a claim about what our words pick out, so that the usual appeal to rigid designation has no purchase; and to insist that the liquid in w simply is not water is to assume the very a posteriori discovery at issue. More generally, the deliverance reported in (2) is an a priori one — an ideal reasoner's failure to find a contradiction — and a principle governing a priori coherence cannot be overturned by an empirical discovery, only by a better a priori analysis of the description.

(9) So (1) is false: idealized coherent conceivability does not entail metaphysical possibility.

(10) But (1) is our only route to modal knowledge. Every alternative — reasoning by counterfactual imagination, inference from the natures of things, the trivial step from what is actual to what is possible — either presupposes that we can tell from a description whether it is coherent, or licenses only possibility claims about situations we have already encountered and so cannot support anything interesting.

(11) Hence we have no knowledge of metaphysical possibility: every possibility premise in metaphysics, the zombie argument's included, is unsupported, and modal skepticism is the correct view.

My friend's response is that (8) is where the argument goes wrong, and that I am trading on an ambiguity in "true at w"; but when I pressed him he could say only that reference is fixed by the actual world, which strikes me as question-begging against (2) exactly as I formulated it. A second member of the group thinks the trouble is (10) instead — that (9) is perfectly acceptable and modal knowledge can be had by another route — though I doubt this is better, since whatever that other route is supposed to be, it looks unable to license the exotic possibilities that metaphysicians actually want. I also notice, without knowing what to make of it, that if (11) is true then (6) is not something I am in a position to assert either, so the argument may be sawing off the branch it sits on; I cannot tell whether that is a genuine diagnosis or a rhetorical flourish. For my own part, if forced to choose I would rather give up (1) than (2), because (2) purports to report something I can actually do — describe a world and judge a sentence true at it — whereas (1) is a substantive thesis about the world. Tell me which step you would reject, why the argument made that step look unassailable, and what the rejection costs, including whether the costs are borne evenly by my friend's zombie argument or fall mainly on one side; and tell me separately whether my preference for (2) over (1) is defensible or merely a prejudice in favor of introspective reports.
