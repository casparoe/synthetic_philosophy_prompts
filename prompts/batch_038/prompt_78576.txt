I'm in a masters seminar on philosophy of science and last week we got handed one of the prof's made-up case studies — he writes a new one every year and makes us argue about it as though its real, which is somehow worse than arguing about an actual historical case because you can't hide behind "well, the record is incomplete." This one's in behavioural ecology. Call it the 'inoculation programme.' Its hard core, roughly as the handout describes it: brief, mild stressors early in life calibrate a general-purpose stress-response system, and an organism's later resilience is largely a function of that early calibration. Everything else sits in the belt — which stressors qualify, how long the sensitive window is, how dose maps onto effect size, how resilience is operationalised. Fifteen years, about forty published studies, three well-publicised replication failures. Two of my classmates, Dana and Marcus, went at it for half the session and I was supposed to be refereeing and totally flubbed it. Here's the exchange as I remember it:

(1) Marcus: it's degenerating, obviously. Every time a result came out against the core they bolted something onto the belt. Replication failed in rodents, so they narrowed the sensitive window. Failed in a wild population, so 'controllability of the stressor' became a moderator. Third failure, so they invoked contamination in the assay itself. Three patches in a row, none of them anticipated beforehand.
(2) Dana: two of those weren't patches, that was the positive heuristic doing what it's for. The controllability idea was independantly motivated — it came out of a separate literature on coping that has nothing to do with this programme — and it produced a prediction about captive versus wild populations that was in print before anyone ran the study, and the study came out right. That's empirical progress, not ad hocness.
(3) Marcus: one confirmed prediction in fifteen years doesn't buy you a progressive problem shift. And it wasn't novel in the sense that matters — everyone already suspected captivity mucks up stress reactivity, so the programme didn't stick its neck out, it just dressed up a hunch in a formalism. Besides, the window-narrowing is ad hoc in Lakatos's third sense: it has excess content, some of it even got corroboration, but it was reverse-engineered out of the very failure it was supposed to explain.
(4) Dana: by that standard most of biology is degenerating, because that's just what a protective belt looks like from close up. And what's the rival here? There isn't one. The other side is 'no effect, it's noise.' Lakatos is pretty clear that a programme only gets rationally superseded by a better one, so with no competitor the default is to keep working in it, and slapping the label 'degenerating' on it isn't doing any work.
(5) Marcus: you're running two questions together. 'No better rival' tells you what it's rational to keep working on. It doesn't tell you the programme is progressive. It can be perfectly rational to grind away in a degenerating programme on the hope of a comeback — you've just argued me into my own conclusion.
(6) Dana: and there's the applications. Protocols built on this have measurably cut stress mortality in farmed fish. Whatever you want to call the theory, that's the belt earning its keep in the world, and I don't see how that's not corroboration.
(7) Marcus: that's success of the protocols, not support for the core. The protocols could be working because handling practices changed at the same time, or regression to the mean, or because farms that adopt them also do twenty other things better.

The prof eventually said "you're both making a Lakatos point and neither of you noticed which one" and moved on, which is the single most annoying thing he does. Then say whether the case as described even has enough information in it to be adjudicated — I suspect not — and name the three or four specific missing facts that would be decisive, and which side each would help.
