I'm a staff engineer on the checkout team at a company that sells point-of-sale software to independent cafés and quick-service restaurants in roughly thirty countries, and one of the surfaces I own is the tip prompt that appears after a card payment is authorized. Our current build shows three suggested amounts — 18%, 20%, and 25% of the pre-tax subtotal — with the middle one preselected when the screen renders, a custom-amount button, and a no-tip control that is styled as a text link rather than as a button. Two implementation details matter for what I'm asking about. First, in the configuration tier most of our merchants enable, selecting the no-tip link does not finish the transaction; it advances to a second screen asking the customer to confirm, and only then prints the receipt. Second, the screen renders from a single global code path, so a customer in a market where tipping is not customary, paying for a coffee they walked over and collected from a counter themselves, sees the identical prompt with the percentages merely converted into local currency and a translated label. Merchants choose the percentages within a range we allow, and we ship them a dashboard that reports tip rate and average tip per transaction by store and by shift, which our sales team also references during renewals. Our revenue on these accounts is a small fraction of total payment volume, and total payment volume is calculated after the tip is added, so we are not neutral with respect to the number that screen produces.

The reason I'm writing is an inconsistency I can't resolve at our design reviews. Our own product design guidelines, which I helped draft, prohibit defaults that steer a customer toward an outcome that costs them money; that is why our loyalty-program and marketing-email checkboxes ship unchecked, and why we deleted a preselected add-a-pastry upsell last year after a bad press cycle. The tip screen was granted an exception, and the rationale recorded in the doc is that the money is not going to us but to the worker. When I raised the tension again last month, a PM answered that this is just a nudge of the ordinary kind, no different in principle from making organ donation the default on a driver's license form, and that we are merely reducing friction for people who intend to tip anyway and would be embarrassed to fumble the amounts. That answer seemed to satisfy everyone in the room, including me at the time, but it leaves several things unexplained: why the confirmation step attaches only to the refusal, why the suggested figure is derived from the price of the goods rather than from anything about the service rendered, why the same screen is correct in every country, and why a store with a low tip rate shows up in our reporting as an underperformer rather than as a place with different customs. I don't have a philosophy background, so I'm not asking for the right vocabulary or for a list of things to read — I want the reasoning itself, spelled out in plain terms.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory this screen is built on. Does a preselected default presuppose that the customer already owes something and the interface is only reminding them of the amount, or that the interface is generating the expectation whose compliance rate it then reports, and can both readings sit with our internal description of the amounts as suggestions? What account of wages and of who is responsible for paying them does the screen assume — that the posted menu price understates the cost of producing the item and the customer is being recruited to finish compensating the person who made it, or that the payment is a reward for service quality, or a gift? If it is a reward for service, how should we understand a computation keyed to the price of the beans and the rent, presented on an order with no service attached? What does the asymmetric exit path presuppose about who owes an explanation to whom? And on the exception in our guidelines: does it presuppose that the wrongness of a manipulative default lies in who profits from it rather than in whether the resulting choice was the customer's own, and is that a principle we could state without it also licensing the upsell we deleted? As you answer, please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit comfortably with — I suspect several incompatible moral views could license this exact screen — and identify where the design is genuinely underdetermined rather than committed. Finally, tell me what would have to change, in the screen itself, in the merchant dashboard, and in the guidelines, if we replaced the presupposed view with a service-inclusive pricing model in which the labor cost is folded into the menu price and no tip prompt exists at all: what would that alternative assume instead, and what would it be unable to express that the current screen does express?
