Question 3. Dana and Ravi are both experienced clinical ethicists with comparable training and comparable track records on cases of this kind, and each knows the other to be careful, informed and honest. They disagree about whether it is permissible to withdraw life support from a patient in a minimally conscious state. Dana's initial judgment is that it is permissible. She then learns of Ravi's contrary judgment.

(a) [9] Explain what it takes for Ravi to be Dana's epistemic peer with respect to this question, and why the conciliationist thinks the resulting revision of Dana's confidence is required of her rather than merely permitted.

(b) [9] The steadfast view holds that Dana should retain her original judgment. Give the strongest argument for that view, and then explain what the steadfast view has to say about a case where the disagreement concerns something simple and checkable — say, two people who have looked at the same paddock in good light and disagree about whether there are twelve or thirteen horses in it. Does the view give a plausible answer there, or does it owe us an account of the difference between the two cases?

(c) [7] It has been objected that conciliationism undermines itself: since very good philosophers disagree about how to respond to disagreement, the conciliationist must, on her own principles, give up conciliationism. And here's the script, from a student who has been solid all term:

Answer: (a) Being an epistemic peer isn't a matter of having the same CV. Dana and Ravi have the same training and the same record, but what matters, as Elga puts it, is whether they are equally likely to be right about the particular question in front of them. Credentials are necessary but nowhere near sufficient: a retired professor of bioethics and a bright fourth-year are not peers on this question even if the fourth-year is being perfectly rational, and two people with identical credentials may still fail to be peers if one of them has a blind spot about, say, patients from a religious background. The conciliationist's argument for thinking Dana's revision is required rather than permitted is that she has no way of showing she is the one who got it right without begging the question. Any reason she could offer for preferring her own assessment — that she saw the case more clearly, that Ravi has overlooked what minimal consciousness actually involves — is precisely what is at issue between them. So from inside her own point of view the two judgments have to be given equal weight, and equal weight means she can't keep her original judgment as the settled answer. She has to drop to roughly even odds that withdrawing support is permissible, which is to say she should suspend judgment and treat the question as open. This also shows why peerhood is a fragile thing. The moment the disagreement is on the table one of them is wrong, and you cannot be equally likely to be right as somebody who is wrong. So strictly speaking Dana and Ravi are peers only up to the point at which the disagreement is revealed, and everything the conciliationist says has to be about that moment, because after it there's no peerhood left to reason from.

(b) The strongest argument for steadfastness is that disagreement bears on a question only indirectly. What settles whether withdrawing support is permissible is the first-order evidence: the patient's condition, what she would have wanted, what minimal consciousness amounts to. Ravi's contrary judgment is a sign that Dana may have misread that evidence, but a sign is only as good as the reason there is to think the sign misleading, and Dana's only reason for thinking Ravi misread the evidence is her own reading of the evidence, which is where she started. So the disagreement gives her no new reason to move; it just presents the old reason again from the other side. This is basically the total evidence view — Kelly's version of the position is really the steadfast view with an extra step, since on it the fact of disagreement contributes nothing that wasn't already in Dana's possession.

The awkward case for the steadfast view is the paddock. Two people count the same horses in decent light, one says twelve and one says thirteen, and neither has any special reason to distrust their own counting. Intuitively they can't both rationally stick to their guns, and steadfastness looks like it licenses exactly that. The view has something to say here, though: the evidence about the horses is decisive, or nearly so, in a way that the evidence about permissibility is not, and where the evidence is decisive there is no genuine peer disagreement left to respond to, for the same reason as before — once they've compared notes one of them has got it wrong and neither can tell which, so each is entitled to go on trusting her own count. Whether that's satisfying depends on whether you think the horse case differs in kind from the ethics case or is only easier.

(c) The self-undermining objection is that conciliationism can't be applied to itself without disappearing. There are very good philosophers on both sides of the question about how to respond to disagreement — Kelly on one side, Elga and Christensen on the other — and each camp thinks the others are mistaken about the meta-question as well. If conciliationism is true, then when Elga learns that Kelly rejects conciliationism he has to stop believing it, since Kelly is his peer on that question too. But then he no longer holds the principle that told him to stop, and the same goes for anyone who ever accepted it. So the view defeats itself: it's true only if nobody should believe it. I think that's decisive and I don't see how the conciliationist is supposed to answer it.
