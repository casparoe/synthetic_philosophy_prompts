I am working through an introductory linguistics textbook whose pragmatics chapter opens with a two-page exposition of Grice, and I would like to know how much of it is safe to carry forward into my own notes. The exposition runs as follows:

'Grice's central claim is that meaning is a matter of intention: to mean something by an utterance is to intend to bring about some effect in an audience by means of that audience's recognition of the intention. What a speaker says is fixed by the words and the grammar; what a speaker means frequently outruns what they say, and the gap is filled by implicature. Implicatures are the inferences a hearer is licensed to draw by assuming that the speaker is observing the Cooperative Principle and its maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and manner. The defining feature of an implicature is its calculability: whatever follows from that assumption together with the literal content is implicated, whether or not the speaker had it in mind. This is what allows metaphor, irony, and hyperbole to be treated uniformly — in each case the speaker conspicuously fails to fulfil a maxim, and the hearer, rather than concluding that cooperation has broken down, supposes the failure to be deliberate and looks for what the speaker is getting at. The same feature explains why implicatures can be cancelled where entailments cannot: since the implicature was never part of what was asserted, a speaker is always free to add that no further suggestion was intended. On this account semantics is left holding the code and pragmatics takes over everything else, so the boundary between them is a matter of degree rather than of kind, and the theory of meaning becomes a branch of the psychology of interpretation.'

Two things about this passage leave me uneasy, and they pull in opposite directions. A philosophy of language course I once took insisted that for Grice implicature remains a species of what the speaker means, not of what a hearer may reasonably reconstruct on their own initiative — which seems to contradict the sentence on calculability. But the same course also told me that Grice wanted to explain meaning in terms of speaker intentions, which seems to lend support to the closing claim that meaning becomes a psychological matter. I cannot tell which of these recollections is the misleading one.

Could you go through the passage claim by claim and sort its contents into three categories: what Grice would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification for an introductory audience, and what he would reject as a misrepresentation? In particular, I would like the calculability claim examined closely — can it really be detached from the speaker's intentions within Grice's framework, and what role, if any, does the requirement that the speaker be taken to have meant the implicated content play? And I would like the claim about cancellation examined against the different kinds of implicature Grice distinguished, rather than against conversational cases alone. Also tell me whether the account of what is said as fixed by words and grammar is something Grice could sign up to without qualification.

Finally: does the closing sentence follow from anything accurate in the passage, or is accurate material being recruited for a conclusion it does not support? And does any of this make a practical difference to how one should analyse the standard scalar case — someone who says that some of the applicants were qualified, and is taken to convey that not all were — or is that example handled the same way before and after the corrections?
