Been going in circles on the hole argument in a phil-of-physics reading group and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not another survey. I've read the surveys, assume I know the history.

Where things sit as far as I can tell: Earman and Norton press diffeomorphism invariance to show that manifold substantivalism makes GR radically indeterministic, on the grounds that a hole diffeomorphism drags the fields around inside the hole while leaving everything outside it alone, so if manifold points are real individuals whose identity is independent of the fields sitting on them, the dragged model is a distinct physically possible future for the same initial data. The responses are basically: give up substantivalism; or keep it and shrug that the difference is haecceitistic and makes no observational difference; or accept Leibniz equivalence, i.e. isometric models represent one and the same physical situation so there was never any indeterminism; or the more Maudlin-flavored line that the whole thing turns on an assumption about what makes two models represent the same situation that nobody ever argued for independently, and that dropping it costs you nothing about the reality of the manifold. Forty-odd years on everyone in my group is standing exactly where they started, and the physics half of it says "it's a gauge symmetry, move on."

Here's a toy case I made up that I think makes the stalemate legible, and nobody in the group will engage with it. Models of a theory T are pairs (S, φ): S is a set of four bare slots, φ maps each slot to 0 or 1, and the sole law of T is "exactly two slots get 1." Every permutation of S maps models to models, and every fact about T is common ground between my two camps. Yet one half says T is trivially deterministic once you quotient by its symmetries and the other says it is as indeterministic as a theory can possibly be, since six distinct situations satisfy the same law. I cannot find a single further question about T that would decide between them.

My hypothesis, offered strictly as a hypothesis: the real dispute isn't about spacetime, isn't about whether manifold points exist, and isn't a verbal fight about the word 'determinism' either. It's a fight about where the representational content of a formalism is allowed to come from — whether the equations plus their symmetries fix the identity conditions on physical situations, or whether that has to be supplied by an interpretive layer that can't be read off the math, and so can't be settled by better math. If that's right, nobody can win from inside GR and the impasse is permanent.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden substantive question doing the work that could in principle break it — something about observables and whether diffeos really are gauge in the relevant sense, or about whether haecceitism is even coherent? And is my toy case faithful to the actual argument, or has it smuggled something important out (I suspect it has, but I cant say what)?
