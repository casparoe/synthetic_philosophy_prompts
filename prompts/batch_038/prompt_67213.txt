**Question 4 (25 marks; suggested time 45 minutes).** Consider the following gloss: *"The Humean holds that a law of nature is nothing over and above a pattern in the total distribution of particular matters of fact, whereas the anti-Humean holds that laws are something additional to that distribution, and that this addition is what makes the pattern hold."*

(a) State Lewis's best-systems account of lawhood with enough precision to show how it differs from naive regularity theory. [8 marks]
(b) State the view on which laws are relations of necessitation between universals, and explain what it claims to gain over the Humean's account. [6 marks]
(c) Does the anti-Humean's addition buy anything the Humean cannot also have? [7 marks]
(d) Identify at least one respect in which the gloss misdescribes one of the two positions it purports to contrast. [4 marks]

The instructor's bands for this paper are: 70%+ distinction, 60–69% merit, 50–59% pass, 40–49% marginal fail, below 40% clear fail.

Below is the candidate's answer, unedited apart from the paragraph numbers I have added for reference.

> **1.** The gloss frames the dispute as a dispute about what there is. On the Humean side, the world consists of local particular matters of fact — Lewis's mosaic — and a law is a pattern in that arrangement, true in virtue of it and in virtue of nothing further. On the anti-Humean side, the world contains something more, and that something more is what accounts for the pattern's holding. I will argue that the gloss is broadly accurate but misleading in two places.
>
> **2.** Lewis's account is not the naive regularity view. On the naive view a law is simply a true universal generalization, and that view cannot separate laws from accidents — it makes "all gold spheres are less than a mile in diameter" a law alongside "all uranium spheres are less than a mile in diameter" — and it cannot explain why some generalizations support counterfactuals and confirmation while others do not. Lewis's improvement is to consider all the true deductive systems whose axioms state particular matters of fact, and to rank them by two competing virtues: simplicity, meaning few and short axioms, and strength, meaning how much of the mosaic the system entails. The laws are the theorems of the system, or systems, that achieve the best combination. Since simplicity is relative to the language a system is written in, Lewis restricts the competition to systems formulated in a vocabulary of perfectly natural properties, which supplies an objective standard of eligibility and keeps the account from being a report on our habits of systematizing. It is worth stressing that Lewis's laws are not summaries of the data: the best system systematizes the entire mosaic, including regions no one has observed, so lawhood can outrun the evidence.
>
> **3.** The deeper point is that Humeanism is a thesis about truthmakers rather than about surface grammar. Law-statements look identical on both views and both sides take them to be true or false; what differs is what makes them true. On the Humean view, what makes them true is the whole pattern of particular fact across spacetime, including the particular facts about the causal powers and dispositions that each object has at each point. The anti-Humean denies that this pattern is the whole story.
>
> **4.** Armstrong, following Dretske and Tooley, holds that a law is a relation of nomic necessitation, N, holding between universals: for the law that Fs are G, N(F, G). Because the relation holds between properties rather than being a quantification over instances, it is supposed to underwrite counterfactuals and inductive projection in a way that a bare regularity cannot. Armstrong also insists that the universals be instantiated, so his view allows no uninstantiated laws, a cost he accepts. Here the anti-Humean camp is more unified than it is usually presented as being. Dispositional essentialists such as Bird and Mumford make the same commitment from the other direction, locating the modality in the essential dispositions of the properties themselves, and Armstrong's necessitation is a relation that, once it holds between F and G, holds wherever those universals do. The "contingency" Armstrong emphasizes is only that which necessitations obtain cannot be read off from the natures of the properties in advance; it is not that the necessitation could have failed while the properties remained what they are. So both anti-Humean families hold that genuine laws are necessary, and this, rather than any additional furniture, is what the gloss's "something additional" should be taken to mean.
>
> **5.** Turning to (c): much of what the anti-Humean claims as an advantage, the Humean can match. Counterfactual support, prediction beyond the observed cases, and objectivity about lawhood are all available to the best-systems account, and the Humean can even agree that lawhood depends on the whole course of history, future events included, so that a candidate law's status is not fixed by any finite segment of the mosaic. That dependence is a genuine cost, but it is not an inconsistency, and it is not obviously worse than positing an unanalyzable necessitation relation. There is also a live objection that the best-systems account leaves lawhood partly hostage to our epistemic situation, because simplicity is judged by creatures like us; I do not think Lewis's appeal to natural properties fully removes the worry, and I would want credit for saying so rather than for dismissing it.
>
> **6.** But there is one advantage the anti-Humean gains that the Humean cannot match, and it is the one the gloss puts at the centre. If a law is nothing over and above the pattern it systematizes, then it cannot explain that pattern, since an explanans cannot be constituted by its own explanandum. Both sides accept this much: Lewis concedes that on his view law-statements do no explanatory work beyond compactly restating what happens, so that explanation by law is really economical redescription of the facts explained. The Humean's laws are therefore at best useful summaries, and the anti-Humean's addition purchases precisely the explanatory priority the Humean has agreed to forgo.
>
> **7.** The gloss misdescribes both positions, though not badly. "Nothing over and above a pattern" suggests the Humean denies that laws are real or objective, whereas the Humean holds that laws are perfectly objective features of the world, fixed by the whole mosaic, and disputes only what fixes them. "Something additional to that distribution" suggests the anti-Humean posits extra items scattered among the events, whereas Armstrong's addition is a second-order relation between universals — not another event, and not anything one could point to inside the mosaic. A related problem is that "what makes the pattern hold" already assumes a direction of explanation that belongs to the anti-Humean's own thesis, so the gloss is not a neutral description of the two views.
>
> **8.** In conclusion, the anti-Humean's addition does buy something: necessity, and explanatory priority. The Humean can have counterfactuals, prediction, and objectivity, but not laws that explain the facts that make them true. Which side to prefer therefore turns on whether explanation must terminate in something that governs, or whether best-systematization suffices. I take this to be a substantive disagreement about the world rather than a verbal one.
