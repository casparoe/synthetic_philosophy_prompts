I'm in year four, writing on radical interpretation, and I've built an argument that I'm fairly sure is bad but I can't locate the bad part, so before it goes into a chapter I'd like someone to break it for me. The setting is Davidsonian: the total evidence available to the interpreter is the speaker's overt behavior in circumstances, there are no meanings or intentions lying around to inspect, and all the interpreter brings is her own language plus the noise. Call this the collapse argument.

(1) What makes one interpretation of a speaker correct rather than another is only how well it fits the total behavioral evidence — there is no further fact it could consist in.

(2) Charity is constitutive, not merely useful: the correct interpretation is the one that maximizes the fit between the sentences the speaker holds true and what the interpreter takes to be true, subject to the speaker coming out broadly rational.

(3) Take any interpretation I satisfying (1)–(2). There is a rival I\* built by a permutation: pick a one-one map π over the objects, let each of the speaker's singular terms denote π of what I says it denotes, and let each predicate take as its extension the π-image of the extension I assigns it. Then every sentence the speaker could utter has the same truth-value under I\* as under I, in the same circumstances.

(4) So I\* fits the total evidence exactly as well as I does and is exactly as charitable; there is no behavioral test, however long the corpus, that separates them.

(5) Given (1), there is no fact of the matter whether I or I\* is the correct interpretation.

(6) And since on this picture a speaker's words mean nothing over and above what the correct interpretation assigns them, there is no fact of the matter what her words refer to, and so none about which beliefs she has. Belief attribution bottoms out in a choice among bookkeeping schemes that cannot be told apart even in principle.

(7) But the same construction applies to my own idiom, including to the sentences (1)–(6) are written in. Nothing about my use of English fixes which scheme my terms answer to any more than hers does.

(8) So there is no fact of the matter what (1)–(6) say — including no fact about whether (6) contradicts determinacy of content, and none about whether I have asserted anything rather than merely emitted marks.

(9) Hence if the Davidsonian story is true, it cannot be stated. It can only be performed, and then withdrawn.

I'm aware the permutation business is Quine's, and aware that he kept the inscrutability of reference separate from the much stronger claim that whole manuals are underdetermined by all possible evidence. I tried to keep them apart here; I'm not certain I have. My supervisor thinks (7)–(9) is just the honest upshot and I should become a quietist about meaning. Someone in my cohort thinks (2) is obviously false, since we manage to discover that people are wrong about loads of things, so charity can't be constitutive and the whole thing dies at step two. Neither answer tells me where the argument actually turns.

So: which step do you reject? And if you think it's valid, which premise is cheapest to give up? For each candidate I want the price, not just the denial. Specifically — what do you pay to demote charity from constitutive to merely methodological; to give up (1) by letting in something causal (I've been reading the material on triangulation and on the object of a belief being its typical cause, and I can't tell whether thats still operating inside (1) or has already abandoned it, and I'd like to be told which); to reject (6) while keeping everything above it; to accept (3)–(6) and block (7)–(9). And where does the Lewis-style restriction to eligible or natural assignments land — is that a repair of (3) or a surrender of (1)? Also: does blocking (7)–(9) require a vantage point outside every language, and if so, is anyone entitled to one?

One wrinkle I keep circling back to. Suppose the speaker is causally unavailable to me: not a person across the table but a finite corpus left by a culture that died out without descendants and with no recoverable environment, or a Blockhead — a lookup table producing fluent, well-timed utterances while being causally coupled to nothing. Then triangulation and the causal anchor look unavailable by hypothesis rather than by neglect. My hunch is the collapse argument goes through in that case even if it fails in the ordinary one, which would leave radical interpretation with a hole exactly where its supposed to be strongest. Does your diagnosis survive the isolated-corpus version, or does it quietly depend on features that version strips away?

For what it's worth I'd much rather keep (6) than (9) — I don't want to end up a quietist — but I don't want to rescue it with something ad hoc either, so tell me if that preference is the thing driving me.
