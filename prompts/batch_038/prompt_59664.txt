I chair a departmental series in legal and political philosophy whose closing session will take the form of a public interview rather than a lecture, and I would like assistance composing my questions. Our guest is a legal philosopher who has developed a distinctive account of constitutional entrenchment. I will set out that account as I understand it from her manuscript, since the questions need to engage this description rather than the generic precommitment literature.

She defends what she calls the revocation-cost account. On her view, a constitutional provision counts as a genuine Ulysses-style self-binding of the polity only if, at every later moment at which the polity has an incentive to violate it, the expected cost of formally revoking the provision exceeds the expected gain from yielding to that incentive — and exceeds it by a margin wide enough to survive the fact that both quantities must be estimated under uncertainty, a margin she labels the credibility margin. Supermajority amendment thresholds, entrenched clauses, and judicial review are, on this account, instruments for calibrating that margin: their function is to make revocation expensive enough that defection is not rationally available at the moment of temptation. She further holds that a provision whose amendment rule makes revocation cheap is not a precommitment device at all but merely advisory; and she answers the dead-hand objection by claiming that the constitution records the polity's second-order volitions while a later majority's impulse to revoke is a first-order, temptation-driven preference, so that revocation is not an exercise of the polity's own will but a capitulation to its weaker self. She insists the account is conceptual and structural, not a recipe for computing particular thresholds.

Against that background, please draft ten to twelve interview questions. For each entry, give (i) the question itself, (ii) a sentence or two naming the commitment of her account that the question is designed to expose, and (iii) a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give; for at least three of the questions, supply a second follow-up in case the first is parried. The questions must turn on the internal logic of the position as I have described it, and the set should include at least one question on each of the following:

- whether a polity persisting through overlapping generations can be the subject of self-binding at all, particularly if one is a reductionist about identity over time, since a polity is a weaker candidate for continuing agency even than a person;
- the Jeffersonian proposal that constitutions and laws should lapse of their own accord after nineteen years, the span he took to be a generation, and what her credibility margin has to say about a deliberately expiring constitution;
- the regress latent in the second-order-volition move: who, on her account, is entitled to determine that a later majority's preference is merely first-order, and whether a fully informed, reflectively endorsed later preference to revoke is simply excluded by stipulation;
- the calibration problem: the margin is fixed ex ante, while the magnitude and even the identity of future temptations are unknown ex post, and whether any non-arbitrary procedure could set it;
- suspension, derogation, and emergency clauses, on the thought that if the meta-rule governing when the constraint may be set aside is itself revocable at ordinary cost, the whole problem relocates one level up rather than being solved;
- the implication for unamendable eternity clauses: whether her account makes them the paradigm of legitimate self-binding, and if she rejects that, what principle bounds the margin;
- a diagnostic that would distinguish her account from the rival view on which entrenched provisions bind chiefly as focal points coordinating expectations: what pattern of cases would come out differently under the two accounts;
- the asymmetry that in the Homeric episode the ropes were held by others at the agent's own instruction, and that a polity's members cannot exit as a sailor can: whether a constraint imposed on non-consenters and enforced by officials is self-binding at all, or a theory of enforcer incentives in disguise;
- the toxin-style problem: if the polity knows that revocation will in fact prove cheap when the moment comes, what work the earlier commitment is doing, and whether her account requires the polity to be systematically mistaken about its own future;
- and, finally, what observation or historical pattern would lead her to abandon the account.

Please avoid questions that invite her to restate her view or to recount her intellectual trajectory; the session should be adversarial but scrupulously fair. At the end, indicate which two or three questions you expect her to handle worst, and describe what a successful dodge would look like in each of those cases, so that I can be ready to decline it.
