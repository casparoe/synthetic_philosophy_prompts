I am preparing a take-home exercise for a graduate seminar on intention and practical rationality, and I would like your help constructing the case on which it turns. For two years running I have simply asked the seminar whether Kavka's agent can form the rewarded intention — the one offered a million dollars provided that, at midnight, he intends to drink a toxin the following afternoon, a draught that will make him wretchedly ill for a day and that, by the time it falls due, he will have no reason at all to take, the money having reached his account hours earlier. The discussion collapses, with depressing regularity, into two camps: those who hold that the intention is impossible because one cannot intend what one knows one will not do, and those who follow Gauthier in holding that an intention once sincerely formed constrains what it is rational to do the next day, so that the toxin duly gets drunk. What the camps exchange is intuitions rather than arguments, and neither is ever forced to say precisely which feature of the case generates the impossibility. So this year I want to hand them a pair of matched cases, and I need you to build them.

The design is as follows. Construct two subjects, whom I shall call A and B, each offered the same arrangement by the same institution: a substantial payment, delivered in advance of the act, conditional on a prediction — made now, by a fallible instrument rather than by an omniscient benefactor — that the subject will at a specified later hour still hold an intention to perform a specified act, an act that costs the subject something real but nothing irreversible, and one that, when the hour arrives, she will have no independent reason to perform. The payoffs, the act, the predictor, the timing, and everything either subject knows about the arrangement must be identical in the two cases. Exactly one thing differs: A can rationally form and retain the rewarded intention, B cannot, and the difference must lie in what each takes herself to be like as a deliberator — in her self-knowledge or self-conception concerning her own future deliberation — rather than in any payoff, incentive, or piece of information about the scheme. State that differentiating feature in a single sentence. Some constraints, which I would ask you to observe strictly. The setting must be an ordinary institutional one — a laboratory protocol, an employer's incentive scheme, a clinical programme — with no telepathy and no science fiction. The predictor must be fallible, and you must state its accuracy; the case may not quietly depend on infallibility. No precommitment technology of any kind: no contracts, no hired enforcers, no bribes to one's future self, no drugs, hypnotic suggestion, or other self-administered manipulation. Both intentions, where they are formed at all, must arise from ordinary deliberation; and it may not simply be asserted that B cannot bring herself to intend, since the impossibility has to be generated by the structure of the case together with the constraints on intention-formation, not stipulated. The scenario itself should run to no more than three paragraphs.

After the scenario, please supply: (i) a payoff table for intend-and-perform, intend-and-not-perform, and not-intend, with the predictor's stated accuracy plugged into the expected values; (ii) a diagnosis for each subject, articulated in terms of the conditions usually taken to govern intention-formation — in particular the relation between intending and believing that one will act, and the requirement that intention track reasons — explaining why A can and B cannot intend; (iii) an identification of the single feature doing the work, together with what becomes of the case if that feature is deleted, or if it is bestowed on both subjects; (iv) the strongest objection a defender of Kavka's diagnosis would press against A — presumably that her resolution is either empty or else smuggles in an independent reason to perform the act, in which case the scenario has ceased to be a toxin case at all — and an assessment of whether that objection, if sound, generalises into a demonstration that Gauthier-style replies to the puzzle are incoherent; and (v) a candid verdict on whether your case genuinely elicits the two verdicts from the theories or merely announces them. Begin your reply with the scenario itself, with no preamble, no restatement of my specifications, and no commentary on your design choices until the analysis; let the first sentence of that analysis state your bottom line on whether the construction succeeds.
