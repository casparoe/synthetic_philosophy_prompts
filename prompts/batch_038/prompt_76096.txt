In my AP psych class our teacher ran a demo and then two kids in the front row argued about it for like three days and I still cant tell who won.

The demo: we all saw the same washed-out photo of a small patch sitting on a colored background, and had to pick which paint chip from a tray matched the patch's color. Half of us were told beforehand that the patch was a lime under red light, the other half that it was a gray rock under red light. The lime group picked greener chips on average.

(1) Maya said that proves what you believe changes what you actually see, and she pointed to that dress photo from a few years back where which colors you see depends on what you assume about the lighting.

(2) Jonah said it proves nothing. Picking a paint chip is a judgement, not a visual experience. The real evidence goes the other way: you can know the Muller-Lyer lines are the same length and they still look different, and you can be told two squares in that checkerboard illusion are the identical shade and they still look different. If belief got into seeing, telling yourself would be enough to un-see it, and it isnt.

(3) Maya said those are the dumbest, most hardwired bits of vision, so they don't generalize. Color under weird lighting is exactly where the input is ambiguous and something has to fill the gap. And in the demo we weren't using words at all, we were matching, so it can't be a language or reporting thing.

(4) Jonah said matching is still a decision, and nobody in the room was blind to the story they'd been given, so half of us were probably guessing what the teacher wanted. He added that if the effect only shows up when the image is degraded, that might just be the visual system's normal guess about illumination, which happens whether or not you believe anything.

(5) Maya said he's drawn "seeing" so narrow that nothing could ever count as penetration, which makes his position impossible to disprove. Jonah said it is disprovable: the checkerboard is a case where you believe the truth and the appearance doesnt budge, which is what his view predicts and hers doesn't.

Our teacher mumbled something about priors and precision weighting that neither of them picked up on. Can you go through moves 1 to 5 and say who's right at each one, not just overall, and then tell me if there's a third possibility both of them skipped?
