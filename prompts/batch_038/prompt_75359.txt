I am four chapters into a dissertation on intertemporal agency, and next term I am co-convening a workshop that will put Davidsonians, Aristotelians, and a group of behavioural economists who model intertemporal choice in the same room for two days. Before I read another account of weakness of will, I want the specification rather than the survey: the list of things any adequate theory of akrasia must deliver in order to count as a theory of the phenomenon all three camps think they are arguing about. Since adequacy is relative to purpose, here is mine. I want an account that can do three things: ground the asymmetry in our reactive attitudes between an agent who acts against her own better judgment and one whose deliberation is bypassed or overwhelmed; be stated precisely enough that an experimentalist could say whether her elicitation procedure actually measures it; and survive the discounting literature rather than being re-described as a by-product of a non-exponential discount function. Write the desiderata as a numbered list, and for each item give a short name, the case or consideration behind it, and a tag saying whether it is non-negotiable or is in fact the proprietary requirement of one camp. Cover the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must not collapse — akrasia against weakness of will as the too-ready revision of one's resolutions, against compulsion and addiction, against ordinary preference change, against simply never having formed the better judgment — and the results it must be consistent with: reversals between smaller-sooner and larger-later rewards, the instability of elicited discount rates across domains and elicitation methods, and the fact that some subjects correctly anticipate their own future reversals and still fail to bind themselves optimally. Then tell me which subset, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied. My suspicion is that the formal demands and the phenomenological ones pull in opposite directions here, and I would much rather be told that plainly than handed a longer list; that tension, if it is real, is the main thing I want out of this exercise. Also identify the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating candidates.

Some candidate requirements I have been working with, which you should feel free to reject, reformulate, or tell me are question-begging. First, what I have been calling the retention clause: the agent's better judgment remains intact through the action, so any account on which the judgment simply flips at the moment of acting does not explain akrasia but explains it away — it owes me an account of a failure that occurs while the relevant cognitive state persists. Second, the inverted Odysseus case: an agent binds herself on Monday to refuse dessert for a month, and on Wednesday acquires decisive evidence that the binding rested on a false belief about her own health, whereupon she breaks the binding and does what is, all things considered, better. Any account that defines the phenomenon as a reversal against the preferences of an earlier self must classify this as akrasia; my intuition says it is not, and I cannot tell whether that intuition is tracking something real or is a prejudice of the judgment-first tradition I was trained in. Third, the divorce clause: the account must be statable without presupposing any particular discount function — exponential, hyperbolic, quasi-hyperbolic, or none — because we want it to apply to agents whose choices are not representable by a single ordering at all; I want to know whether this is compatible with the most natural formal accounts, which seem to define the phenomenon as dynamic inconsistency and therefore as a property of a curve. Fourth, the precommitment recursion: if binding is the rational remedy for anticipated akrasia, why does not the decision to bind inherit the very failure it is meant to correct, given that it too is made at some particular moment by some particular self? And fifth, a constraint I keep having to argue for with the economists: an account must say why the Socratic argument that no one does wrong willingly fails, rather than insisting by stipulation that it does, and must explain the characteristic affective signature of the akratic agent — shame and agent-regret rather than the surprise or alienation appropriate to someone who was acted upon — since a theory that predicts the wrong emotional sequelae has probably identified the wrong mechanism. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements, which are redundant, and which are covert statements of a substantive view dressed up as neutral criteria.

Finish with no more than a short paragraph each on four contenders scored against the list: the Davidson-style relativization of practical judgment, the Aristotelian diagnosis in terms of the practical syllogism, the resolution-based account, and a purely economic account that identifies akrasia with dynamic inconsistency — but keep the weight on the criteria, since I can do the scoring myself once I have them. Please be committed throughout: take a definite position on which requirements are real and which are camp-local, state flatly whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable and which camp has to give way if so, name the requirement you would drop under pressure and the one you would defend to the end, and mark the one or two places where you are genuinely least confident rather than distributing the burden back to me.
