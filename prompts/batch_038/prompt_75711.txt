Before you build anything, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming out loud about whether care ethics can be proceduralized at all without destroying the thing it's about — it's famously suspicious of rules and impartial checklists — and if your honest answer is no, say so and then build the least-bad version anyway.

Here's my situation. I'm one of two care coordinators at a home-care agency, about 60 clients, mostly older people with dementia or frailty plus a handful of younger adults with acquired brain injuries. Our main payer just switched to task-based 15-minute visit slots, and we've also rolled out a scheduling system that rotates carers every few weeks on purpose — it spreads the unpleasant and antisocial shifts evenly and it's meant to stop favourites forming. Alongside that, management wants a KPI they're calling a relationship continuity score, basically cumulative hours a client has logged with the same carer, and there's talk of a hard rule that no carer can be assigned to the same client more than four visits in a row. I think both of those ideas are confused but I can't articulate why beyond a vibe.

What I need is a decision procedure my team can run on the escalations that land on my desk, roughly 40 a month: a family furious that the rota moved a carer their mother has had for years, a client who wants something done that isn't on the task list, a carer who wants her unpaid extra twenty minutes with a lonely client recognised somehow, a client who insists on walking to the shops alone after two falls. Constraints: it has to be runnable in about twenty minutes by someone with zero ethics training, it has to end in a written justification our director of care signs off on, and that justification has to survive an audit by the payer, so it can't just say we did it because it was caring. Tell me whether you should have a no-answer at some step, where the procedure should hand the decision up to a human because it's a question of values rather than facts, and — this matters — flag every step where a judgment call is wearing a procedure costume.

So, concretely:

1. Give me the ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what doesn't, and mark the exit points (stop-and-refuse, stop-and-escalate, proceed-with-conditions).
2. Say which distinctions from care ethics you deliberately kept and which you had to drop to make it fit in twenty minutes, and what breaks because of the ones you dropped.

Run it on three cases: the daughter demanding we put Maria back with her mother with advanced dementia, when Maria is the only carer trained on the hoist and was moved to cover three other clients; a client with moderate dementia asking his carer to drive him out to his old workshop, which his son says is unsafe and distressing; and a carer asking us to let her keep cooking proper meals for a client instead of the pre-packed ones the task list funds. One of these should come out against what I'd want it to, and I'd rather you not soften it.
4. Then tell me what the procedure genuinely can't do — where it'll misfire in a way I should expect and can't design around, especially anything about the carer's own dependency needs and about clients who can't signal whether the care landed.
