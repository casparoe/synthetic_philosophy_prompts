I spent thirty-five years in the physiology of homeostatic regulation, and since retiring I have turned to philosophy. I have drafted a short piece on Strawson's Freedom and Resentment and would be grateful for critical comments. I should say at once that I cannot chase secondary literature, so please address my argument directly rather than pointing me toward further reading.

**Draft: Resentment as negative feedback**

Strawson's strategy, as I read it, is to shift the question of responsibility away from metaphysics. Whether an agent could have done otherwise does not decide whether it makes sense to resent him; what decides that is whether he is a proper object of the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, guilt — which are not conclusions we argue our way into but the framework within which questions of desert get asked at all. Determinism is therefore beside the point, since no truth about causation could by itself license abandoning these attitudes. The one escape route is the objective attitude, in which we treat a person as an object to be managed, treated, or trained rather than as a participant in interpersonal exchange. Strawson grants that this attitude is available but thinks its wholesale adoption is not a live option for beings like us, because our commitment to ordinary interpersonal relationships runs too deep.

My proposal is that the reactive attitudes are error signals in a homeostatic loop. Resentment fires when a partner's contribution deviates from the value the relationship's norms set as expected; its effect is to impose a cost, which pushes behavior back toward the set point. Indignation is the same loop run in the third person. Guilt is its inward-facing branch. On this description the authority of the attitudes is conditional on the loop's operating range, exactly as a sensor's reading is a fact about the variable it monitors only while the sensor is properly calibrated, unsaturated, and aimed at the right thing. And we can specify, without invoking any thesis about causation, when those conditions fail: the agent's regulatory machinery is broken, saturated, or directed at outputs we can no longer expect of him. Those look to me like precisely the circumstances Strawson reserves for the objective attitude.

So his argument, I suggest, does not insulate responsibility from natural science; it relocates the question from metaphysics to the specification of a mechanism's proper working range. Once that range can be stated functionally, the objective attitude becomes available case by case, which is all that anyone worried about responsibility-talk really needs.

**What I want from you.** First, is my summary accurate — in particular, is the objective attitude what I have made it, and is Strawson's reason for dismissing its wholesale adoption a claim about our psychology or a claim about what we have reason to do? Second, a colleague tells me my central inference confuses the origin of an attitude with its justification, and that this is why the whole piece fails; I do not feel the force of that objection and would like it spelled out, together with your view of whether he is right. Third, does the control-loop analogy carry argumentative weight, or is it merely picturesque? Finally, please mark any place where I have overstated a claim, and tell me what the strongest version of my position would look like if it survives at all.
