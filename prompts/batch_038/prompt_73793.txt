I have been defending a fairly strong version of Good's theorem on the value of information: the result that a Bayesian decision maker who chooses optimally relative to her posterior should never decline a costless observation bearing on her decision. My interest is not purely academic. I work on the information-gathering layer of a planning system that has to decide which sensors to query, which tests to commission, and which reports to read before committing resources, and the theorem functions there as a default rule — acquire anything that is genuinely free and decision-relevant, then optimize — so I have a professional stake in knowing how much weight it will bear. A colleague thinks the result is a disguised tautology that purchases its safety by assuming everything interesting away, and my own response has been that the standard textbook defense is subtler than it looks: the reason free information is said to be harmless is that the agent can always act as though she had never received it, but that escape is available only because the theorem already assumes her post-observation choice is optimal for her posterior, so an agent who cannot bind herself to ignore what she learns loses the defense rather than gaining a comfort. I am not certain that reply is correct, and I want the position stress-tested rather than reassured.

So: what would count against the thesis that a rational agent is never worse off for costless, decision-relevant information, and never has a reason to refuse it? I would like the defeaters sorted by how damaging they actually are — fatal, decisive, merely awkward, or purely dialectical — and, for each one, an identification of which hypothesis it targets: that the evidence is a proposition the agent could already represent before receiving it, that she updates by conditionalization, that her preferences over outcomes are unaffected by the learning itself, that her action set is unchanged, that her later self chooses optimally given the posterior, that her beliefs do not enter her utility directly, or that the act of acquiring the information is unobservable to others who might respond to it. Please tell me which of these failures are live in practice for real agents, markets, and deployed systems, as opposed to being logically possible but never instantiated. I am especially interested in several families I cannot yet evaluate properly: preferences that violate expected utility in ways that make dynamic choice inconsistent, so that an agent who cannot commit is genuinely harmed by free information; ambiguity and non-Bayesian revision, where additional evidence can leave a decision maker worse off; models in which optimistic or pessimistic beliefs are themselves sources of utility, so that resolution destroys a valued anticipation; present-biased or myopic agents whose future selves predictably misuse what they learn; cases where learning supplies concepts the agent did not previously possess, so that the posterior is not her prior conditional on any event she could have entertained; and strategic settings in which becoming informed is visible and thereby changes what others do, so that the information was never free in the relevant sense. Where you think a purported counterexample merely smuggles out a hypothesis and redescribes the assumptions as a refutation, say so plainly rather than granting it.

Two further things. First, tell me which of these a committed defender of the theorem would herself concede as counting against the position, and which she would dismiss as changing the subject, and explain the difference in standards she is applying — in particular, whether the theorem is a substantive claim about rational agents or an analytic consequence of a framework, and whether the normative use I make of it in system design is justified, undermined, or simply untouched by that distinction. Second, assess the insulation charge directly: if every counterexample turns out to violate some assumption, does the view risk anything at all, and is its safety a virtue or a defect? Along the way, please separate the descriptive reading, which I take to be false as an empirical generalization about human subjects, from the normative reading; I recently ran a small elicitation in which roughly a third of participants paid a modest amount to prevent the resolution of a lottery they had already chosen, and I want to know what that result can and cannot establish against each reading, including whether a rival account that treats ignorance as sometimes intrinsically preferable predicts the data without positing an extra utility term over beliefs. Finally, if you conclude that the position is well insulated, tell me what a genuinely damaging counterexample would have to look like — an agent satisfying every standard hypothesis and still made worse off by a costless observation — and whether anyone has constructed one.
