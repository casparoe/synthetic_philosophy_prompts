I am writing the final examination for a graduate seminar on Hobbes and the mathematical sciences, and I need help drafting one of the four essay questions. The exam is closed-book and runs three hours; candidates answer two questions, so this one should be scoped for roughly 75 minutes and carry 25 marks. Do not ask candidates for citations, page references, or chapter numbers — the seminar's assigned apparatus was the first two parts of *De Corpore*, the first two parts of *Leviathan*, and selections from the Savilian professor of geometry at Oxford's published attacks on Hobbes together with Hobbes's replies — and I want the question to test reasoning, not recall. Please also state any premises the question depends on in a stipulated box given to the candidate, in paraphrase rather than quotation, so that nobody is marked on their memory of wording.

Here is the crux I want the question to turn on, because my students consistently flatten it. Hobbes gives what I take to be two distinguishable grounds for treating geometry as the paradigm of demonstrable science, and the secondary literature my students read runs them together into a single doctrine called constructivism:

- A generative ground: scientific knowledge, on Hobbes's account, is knowledge acquired by reasoning from the manner in which a thing is generated to its properties, or from its properties back to some possible way of generating it. Geometry alone seems to satisfy this condition among the sciences of natural things, because figures are objects whose generation by motion — a point carried into a line, a line swept into a surface — we can ourselves set before the mind.
- A semantic ground: geometric names have definitions that are determinate and depend on our own imposition, so only in geometry is the chain of reasoning immune to the equivocation and insignificance Hobbes blames for the failures of scholastic natural philosophy.

The two grounds do not stand or fall together, and that is the point I want examined. The semantic ground secures the necessity of geometric consequence but, if pressed, seems to reduce geometry to correct bookkeeping about our own stipulations — which is very close to the charge Hobbes's Oxford antagonist leveled when he treated Hobbesian geometric method as arbitrary symbol-mongering rather than as the analysis of quantity. The generative ground, by contrast, is the one that promises knowledge of causes, but it is exposed in a different way: the geometer does not actually generate his figures, he only considers them as generated, so what the condition delivers may be merely a possible generation rather than an actual cause. And if that is right, the one place where Hobbes's making condition is met in earnest is civil philosophy, where the commonwealth is genuinely made by those who institute it — which raises the uncomfortable question of whether Hobbes should rank political science above geometry rather than beside it, or whether actual making imports something (contingency, passion, the resistance of matter) that considered generation excludes.

Please write the following.

1. The question itself, in two parts. Part (a) should require candidates to separate the two grounds above and to explain why the Oxford objection bites on one of them and not, or not in the same way, on the other. Part (b) should require them to take a position on whether the merely-considered character of geometric generation undermines the claim that geometry yields knowledge of causes, and to say what follows for the comparison with civil philosophy. Candidates should be told that a survey of Hobbes's mathematical quarrels or a restatement of the thesis that we only know what we make will not earn credit.

2. A model answer of about 900 words that argues a position rather than balancing considerations. I expect it to concede something real to the Oxford objection.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, broken into five or six required moves with the marks attached to each, and — this is the part I most need — an explicit statement of what separates a B-range answer from an A-range answer, named as a specific move rather than as a general quality judgment.

4. A list of six characteristic errors, each with the deduction it should attract and one sentence on why the error is tempting. Please include at least these: treating the objection as being about the failure of Hobbes's quadrature of the circle rather than about his conception of method; reading Hobbes's account of truth as a property of well-ordered names as if it entailed that mathematical truths are true by convention; assuming that generation in geometry means actual construction with straightedge and compass; and supposing that the generative and semantic grounds are two names for one thesis.
