I'm a second-year graduate student, and I drafted the following argument as a handout for a seminar on modal epistemology. My supervisor called it confused but would not say where, and I've come to suspect that every step is defensible while the conclusion is not something I can accept. The conclusion I don't want is that Kripke's necessary a posteriori is either unsupported or self-undermining.

(1) A term is rigid if, given how it is actually used, it designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists, and never anything else.

(2) Proper names and natural kind terms are rigid: 'water' designates the stuff that fills the lakes around here whatever its inner constitution turns out to be, and 'H₂O' designates that compound in every world containing it.

(3) If a and b are rigid and a = b is true, then it is necessarily true. Hence water is necessarily H₂O, and heat is necessarily the motion of molecules.

(4) These truths were discovered empirically. Before the chemistry it was open, for all anyone could tell, that the clear drinkable liquid in the lakes was something else. So there are necessary truths knowable only a posteriori.

(5) What made it look possible that water is not H₂O is that we could coherently imagine a situation: a transparent, drinkable, lake-filling liquid with a different constitution. Our capacity to imagine such situations is, in practice, the only route we have to claims about what is possible.

(6) But (4) shows that capacity misfires. The situation imagined is one in which a watery liquid is not H₂O, not one in which water is not H₂O. The situation was possible; what we took it to demonstrate was not.

(7) So the fact that a situation can be coherently imagined tells us nothing by itself about whether a proposition is possible. Imagining yields epistemic possibility, which coincides with metaphysical possibility only where we can independently rule out hidden a posteriori identities — and ruling those out in advance is exactly what we cannot do.

(8) The claims in (1) through (3) are modal claims of the same kind. They say how our terms behave with respect to other situations and how the objects themselves do: that a person could not have originated from a different gamete pair, that a table could not have been built from a different hunk of wood.

(9) The support offered for those claims is a sequence of imagined situations together with verdicts about how to describe them — whether the person in the described situation who lost the election is Nixon, whether the table built from another hunk is the same table. That is an exercise of the very faculty discredited in (7).

(10) Therefore either (3) and (4) are unsupported, or their support is self-undermining: the case for the necessary a posteriori requires trusting a faculty whose unreliability is that case's own discovery.

I am most attached to (3) and (4), and least willing to abandon the thought in (5) that conceiving gives us some purchase on possibility, because if it does not I cannot see how I am entitled to assert (3) either. But I cannot tell whether the failure is at (5), (7), or (9), and the three seem to demand quite different repairs.

Tell me which single step is illicit. Commit to one diagnosis and argue for it, rather than mapping the places a Kripkean might dig in and leaving me to choose among them. Then price the repair. If (9) is the weak link, say what is left to establish rigidity and the necessity of origin that is not itself a verdict about how an imagined situation strikes us. If (7) is the weak link, give me a criterion that actually separates the water case from cases where conceivability does track possibility — something I could apply to a new case, not a label. If (5) is the weak link, name the faculty that replaces imagination and explain why it does not inherit the same problem. And if you think the argument is sound after all, say so plainly and tell me what I ought to give up in order to live with (10).
