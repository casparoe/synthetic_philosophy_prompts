I'm deep in revisions on a novel about a museum conservator and my two advisors have gotten into a fight I can't referee. Neither of them is a character, they're real people I asked for help with the research, and I'm basically the idiot taking notes in the corner.

The plot device: a terracotta maquette, a kneeling figure about a foot tall, drops off a trolley in the storage annex and shatters into forty-odd pieces. The conservator — I'll call her M — grinds the shards down to grog, works them into a fresh batch of clay with a binder, rebuilds the figure from photographs, and refires it. Then, and this is the bit I invented to make the last act hurt, the estate turns up a second visually identical version the sculptor made decades earlier out of a different batch of clay, which has been sitting in a vault in another country the whole time. So the final chapter has two kneeling men on the same plinth and a room full of people who don't know which one to call the original.

Advisor A, who's a philosopher, says the maquette died on the floor of the annex. The stuff survived; the work didn't. Those are two different objects, she says, and you don't get the second one back by rearranging the first — what M made is a new piece built out of the corpse. And the vault version proves it: that one is the surviving original and M's is the copy, which is exactly backwards from what everyone in the book assumes.

Advisor B, thirty years in conservation, says A is committed to something unhinged — on her view every object in every museum that has ever been re-adhered, filled, re-fired or re-cast is a copy, meaning his whole profession has been manufacturing fakes since the nineteenth century. What makes the work the work is the continuity of the making, he says, and M's refiring continues that history just as much as an in-fill in a fresco does. He also says A owes him a line she can't draw: a chip on the knee, a replaced forearm, half the torso, all of it — where does repair stop being repair?

Then A came back with something I don't follow but that B laughed at, and I can't work out who should have laughed. It's about the kiln log. M keeps a ledger of every firing an object gets, and the rebuilt figure picks up a second entry under the same accession number. A says that's the museum telling on itself: one number, two firings, so on its own bookkeeping the thing has a gap in its existance, and gaps are precisely what identity doesn't survive. B says the shared accession number proves the opposite — it's one object with a history that happens to include being broken.

So: who's right about which move? Please take them one at a time — the shattering, the grind-and-refire, the where's-your-line challenge, the vault duplicate, and the ledger — and say for each whether A, B, or neither has it, plus where the two of them are just talking past each other. What I most need to know is whether the kiln log is doing real argumentative work or whether I've written a prop and dressed it up as a premise.
