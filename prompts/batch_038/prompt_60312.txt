I serve as methods editor of an interdisciplinary journal in historical sociology, and each month I handle six to ten submissions whose central explanatory claim takes the form "a set of values caused an outcome": an ascetic religious ethic producing a regional savings rate, a code of honor producing resistance to wage labor, a civic ethos producing low administrative corruption, a theology of vocation producing a demographic transition. My two standing reviewers are historians — excellent with sources, impatient with methodology — and they cannot agree whether such claims are explanations at all or descriptions wearing a causal costume. I need a refereeing procedure we can actually run, in about forty minutes per manuscript, with no training in philosophy and no statistics beyond what a reviewer picks up by reading.

Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers kill the paper outright, which earn a major revision, and — explicitly marked — the points at which the question is no longer about evidence but about what this journal wants an explanation to be, so that it must come to me rather than be settled by the reviewer.

Ground it in what is genuinely available rather than in slogans. Draw on Weber's own methodological scruples about the Protestant ethic argument: the relationship he posits is one of elective affinity rather than deduction, the ideal type is a constructed benchmark against which the actual case is measured rather than an average of cases, and he closes by refusing to substitute a one-sided spiritualistic causal account of culture for a one-sided materialist one, treating either as useful only as a beginning of an inquiry and never as its conclusion. Then work through the general difficulty of explaining with meanings — that values enter explanations in at least three distinguishable ways, as a reason an actor has for doing something, as a constraint or selector operating on a set of otherwise available options, and as a constitutive part of the very thing being explained — and that only some of these uses are causal in the counterfactual sense, while others buy intelligibility instead.

The procedure must compel the reviewer to name, for every manuscript, at least the following: the outcome and its contrast class, since explaining why A rather than B is not the same as explaining A; the value as it is operationalized, and whether the indicator would still register if the belief were absent; the mechanism at the level of individual action, with the step where many such actors become a macro pattern identified rather than assumed; the candidate causes that co-vary with the value in the same places and decades — literacy, urbanization, inheritance law, political jurisdiction, market access; the direction-of-causation question, including the possibility that the value was selected by the outcome or that both issued from a common cause; and finally whether the paper is offering a causal claim or a claim about what a practice meant to its participants, and whether the latter satisfies our standard on its own.

Build in detection for two failure modes we see constantly. The first is functionalism: an ethic is credited with an outcome because the outcome would have needed such an ethic. The second is constitutive circularity: the explanans is a redescription of the explanandum, as when a discipline of labor is explained by an ethic whose only evidence is that same labor. A reviewer should be able to spot both without having read the philosophy of social science.

Then run the procedure on three submissions and report the verdict on each, with the step that decides it:

1. A quantitative paper arguing that a Reformed ethic of calling and methodical life explains a divergence in capital formation between two adjacent river valleys from 1650 to 1750, drawing on church membership rolls, probate inventories, and sermon literature, with the author candidly conceding in a footnote that the pious valley also had better navigable water and a toll exemption.

2. An ethnographic paper arguing that a Mediterranean code of family honor explains the persistence of smallholder plots against consolidation by larger estates over two centuries, drawing on land registry data and oral histories, in which the honor code is described chiefly through the terms people use to refuse offers to sell.

3. A beautifully written essay that I find persuasive and would hate to reject, arguing that a monastic ideal of ordered, measured hours helped produce mechanical clockmaking, where the evidence is entirely textual and the vocabulary describing the value and the vocabulary describing the outcome are nearly the same.

Be candid about where the procedure will misfire — the papers it wrongly passes, the papers it wrongly fails — and about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; where you rely on Weber's text, paraphrase it rather than quoting, and do not put words in his mouth that you cannot reproduce exactly.
