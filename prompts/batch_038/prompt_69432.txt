I belong to a Tuesday-evening reading group that meets in the back room of our public library — nine of us, mostly retired schoolteachers, two graduate students, and a hospice nurse. We spent the autumn working through Plutarch's *Parallel Lives* and in January moved on to late-antique saints' lives: Athanasius's *Life of Antony*, Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Macrina*, and a stack of excerpts from the *Golden Legend*, which as one of us pointed out is organized by the liturgical calendar rather than by any person. Because the Plutarch was still fresh, most of the sessions turned into comparisons, and the group collectively underlined the remark in the preface to the *Life of Alexander* that, in the Clough translation we were using, "sometimes a matter of less moment, an expression or a jest, informs us better of their characters and inclinations, than the most famous sieges, the greatest armaments, or the bloodiest battles whatsoever." Two members — Wendell, a retired secondary-school classics teacher, and Aisha, a doctoral student in the history of late antiquity — got into a sustained argument over whether hagiography can be a vehicle for moral learning from a life at all, or whether it is a different kind of thing that only looks like Plutarch. Neither budged. Our convener asked me to write the exchange up, and since I was sitting between them taking notes and have no horse in the race, I want an outside adjudication of the argument as it actually went.

Here is the sequence, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Wendell opened by granting that Plutarch is tendentious — he shapes material to suit the pairings and says so himself — but argued that the shaping is disciplined by two features hagiography drops: the subject's faults stay on the record, and the closing comparison chapter invites the reader to rank two lives against one another. He noted that not all of the surviving pairs actually have that chapter and said this is revealing: without the comparison, a *Life* reads less like an argument than like a monument. His principle: moral learning from a life requires that the reader be licensed to judge the subject, and hagiography withdraws that license in advance.

(2) Aisha replied that he had imported a modern, private, deliberative model of reading. These texts were composed to be read aloud to a community on the appropriate day, so the question is not whether an individual reader is invited to assess the subject but what a community does with the recitation over generations. And the text offers itself as a pattern, not a puzzle: Antony's biographer evidently wants monks to copy a way of living down to the manual labour and the diet, which on her account is a sturdier form of moral learning than adjudicating between two dead statesmen and preferring one.

(3) Wendell said that was precisely his worry, and that it made things worse rather than better: an exemplar presented as unassailable licenses admiration without emulation. The reader enjoys the glow of appreciating a saint and experiences the demand as discharged — he called it a moral anesthetic. On his telling, hagiography is more dangerous than Plutarch not because it is less accurate but because it is more soothing.

(4) Aisha said he had the miracles exactly backwards. The miracle material is not padding meant to overwhelm; it functions as a marker of the limit of imitation, the way a stage direction tells you what is not part of the action. Where Antony does what no reader could do, the text says so in the only idiom it has. Remove the miracles and you get something worse: a wholly human paragon whom the reader is expected to match and cannot, which yields either despair or self-deception. The miracles are what keep the exemplar honest about the gap.

(5) Wendell answered that this defense concedes his conclusion. If the miraculous marks everything the saint does that lies beyond the reader's reach, what is left to imitate? He pressed a distinction: events can be separated from dispositions only if the dispositions are independently available, but patience is learned from patience-in-this-story, and if the story is invented, what the reader acquires is fictional patience, untethered from anything that occurred. He said he could not see how a disposition survives the loss of the events that constitute it in a narrative.

(6) Aisha said that argument proved far too much for him to want it: we acquire patience from Anna Karenina, and from Plutarch's own reshaped anecdotes, and nobody thinks the invention of a scene disables moral learning from fiction. She added that the flatness people object to is designed — a richly interior character invites you to second-guess him, whereas an outline invites you to fill it in with your own life. A template has to be flat to be usable.

(7) Wendell said he was glad she had gone there, because now he could put the dilemma. Hagiography either makes a truth-claim or it does not. If it does, it is history, and as history it fails by any standard the group would accept: unverifiable, suppressive of contrary material, often composed long after the events by an interested party. If it does not, it is fiction, and should be judged as fiction — and as fiction it is incompetent: no interiority, no reversal, no development, stock scenes recycled from text to text. Either way the genre has nowhere to stand, and calling it liturgy merely names the place it is standing rather than showing that the place can bear weight.

(8) Aisha's last move was that the dilemma is false because it assumes the point of such a text is to be believed or to be admired, when on her account the point is to be inhabited — as a rule of life or an anthem is inhabited, neither of which is assessed for accuracy or craft. She also said Wendell had missed the theological structure of the texts: the saint's virtue is repeatedly presented as not his own but conferred, so imitation was never about copying achievements; it is about positioning yourself so that the same thing can happen to you. That, she said, is why flatness and miracles sit comfortably together in one document.

One thing I noticed while taking notes and could not place: every example Wendell offered of Plutarch's candour was a case where a detail could embarrass the subject, whereas every example Aisha offered of hagiographical concreteness was a case where the detail confirmed something the text had already told us. I do not know whether that marks a real fault line between the genres or is only an artefact of how each of them was arguing on the night.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than delivering an overall impression. For each of the eight moves, assign a rating — clearly mistaken, partly mistaken but containing a real point, essentially neutral, partly right, clearly right — and justify it briefly, including the strongest available counter to any move you rate favourably. Then tell me specifically whether the dilemma in (7) is sound as Wendell stated it, and whether (8) escapes it; if it does escape, say what Aisha has to give up in order to escape, since I suspect the escape is not free. Finally, say what each of them is failing to see: whether the difference between Plutarch and the saints' lives is one of kind or of degree, whether Aisha's appeal to communal liturgical use actually answers Wendell or merely changes the subject, whether Wendell's move in (5) about dispositions and their constituting events can be sustained by someone who also wants to learn patience from novels, and what my own observation about embarrassing versus confirming detail is worth. I would like a verdict on who is more right overall, and, if a formulation of Aisha's position survives the dilemma, a sentence or two stating it in a way our group could test against an actual text at the next meeting.
