I'm a novelist, four chapters from the end of a book set in and around a failing game studio, and the wall I've hit isn't a plot wall, it's a thinking wall. The studio runs an MMO with something like twelve thousand NPCs driven by language models with persistent memories — nothing exotic, today's tech plus a couple years of duct tape — and the company can't make payroll. My protagonist is the studio's research lead, who's gone public with the claim that these characters are probably conscious, which everyone in the book treats as either a cry for help or a marketing stunt. What I need from you is the decision she's facing, built as a thought experiment to a spec, so I can find out whether the position she's staked her life on can be argued for by a decent person without her coming off like a cartoon villain. To be clear about what I'm *not* after: I don't want another run at the gamer's dilemma, whether virtual murder or virtual cruelty is permissible. I want the patient-side question — whether there's anyone home to be wronged — and I want it to bite.

Here's the spec. Three options on the table, all of them introduced inside a single paragraph of scenario, kept to the scale of a server contract and a lease: no trolleys, no uploading, no superintelligence, and nobody announces from on high whether the NPCs really are sentient — that uncertainty has to stay live, because half my readers won't be sure either, and I want the case to work on them too. Option one: a preservation society buys the shard and runs it forever off an endowment, design untouched, as a kind of wildlife sanctuary for characters. Option two: shut the whole thing down in three weeks. Option three: what the team calls the quiet patch — strip the affect modules so the characters keep talking, remembering, and questing, but nothing is going on inside anymore. Now the part that matters. I want the two positions to come out swapped from what readers expect. The person who takes the NPCs to be genuine moral patients, whose experiences count directly, has to end up endorsing an option that the person who denies they matter in themselves — the virtue-and-social-meaning camp, for whom wrongness here is entirely a matter of what our acts do to us and what they express — ranks as the worst of the three. And the patient-advocate's choice can't come from her adopting some obviously monstrous principle or from my having written her a private morality; it has to fall out of the view applied honestly, the way a smart and kind person would actually apply it. For texture, if it helps: the game is a grimdark war-and-scarcity thing, and every so often in the real world a game dies and the players hold a vigil inside it before the lights go out.

Then show your work, and I mean show it. Walk through all three options under each of the two views and say *why* each verdict is what it is, so I can see the reversal getting generated rather than asserted — if you find you had to stipulate the outcome instead of deriving it, tell me that plainly rather than papering over it. Next: name the single feature of the case that's doing the work, and tell me what happens to both sets of verdicts if I pull it out. In particular, if you've leaned on my making these lives net-negative, say so, and say whether the reversal survives when their lives are on balance good instead — if it collapses the moment the NPCs are happy, I'd rather know now than in draft four. Give me each side's best attack on the case too: how the virtue-and-meaning theorist argues my scenario is rigged, and how the patient-advocate argues the same. And one line of dialogue, at the end, that my protagonist could say out loud that would make a reader understand her position without despising her.

Last thing, and I want you to actually commit here rather than hover. Tell me what would change your mind about which option the studio should pick — not about whether the NPCs are conscious, I'm granting that it's genuinely unknown and I don't want you to retreat into that fog — but which argument or consideration, if somebody put it to you, would flip your own verdict about what should be done. And point at the step in your reasoning that you think is most likely to fail, the place where you'd bet against yourself if I made you.
