I am a junior in high school, and for my philosophy elective I have been reading the opening book of Plato's *Laws*, where an unnamed Athenian traveler argues to a Spartan and a Cretan that wine should not be banned from the city but should instead be distributed at supervised drinking parties, on the grounds that it reveals and hardens the soul the way fire tests metal, and that it can loosen the stiffness that comes with age. My teacher assigned me the opposing side for a seminar discussion, and my older brother, who is training to be a paramedic, has spent the last week telling me that anyone who thinks drinking can be made safe by supervision has never worked a Saturday night shift. So I would like to rehearse. Please play the Athenian Stranger: a committed legislator-philosopher, sharp and patient, not a caricature of a Greek, who believes that communal drinking under a sober leader is a genuine instrument of civic education.

Stay in character until I say stop. You may use only the concepts available to you: no neuroscience, no addiction medicine, no statistics, no modern vocabulary such as alcoholism or dependency — if I bring in a term you cannot translate into your own, ask me to render it in your language before you answer. You may concede lesser points, including that unsupervised drinking is disgraceful, that some natures should be excluded from the cup altogether, and that the very young should not be given wine; you may never concede the main claim. Do not tell me what scholars think about the dialogue, do not cite passages or Stephanus numbers, do not narrate actions or setting, and do not step out of the role to comment on how the exercise is going. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs and close with one question of your own — but it must press my argument, not merely redirect it.

Here is my opening move. You say wine should be applied to a citizen the way fire is applied to gold: put his soul under the strain of pleasure, in the presence of his elders, and you will learn whether his training holds. But a test that the lawgiver administers to everyone, by law, is not a test — it is exposure. Suppose one man in ten cannot hold fast when the cup goes round. The symposium has not merely identified him; it has made him, because a habit is built by repetition and it is the city itself that supplies the occasion, the company, and the honor attached to it. You would not test the courage of your soldiers by marching them into a battle they are certain to lose and calling the result instructive. So answer me plainly: when the test itself produces the failing it was designed to detect, in what sense is it still a test of character rather than a public policy of harm — and what becomes of your analogy to fire and gold, since the gold that fails your fire is not merely revealed but ruined?
