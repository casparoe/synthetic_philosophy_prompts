For the past two years I have chaired the board of a small flood-protection district on a river delta, and I would like an honest assessment of a question that keeps derailing our meetings. The setup matters, so let me give it before the problem.

The district covers the lower town: roughly 1,900 residents, a working harbor, and a grid of streets that floods twice a decade. The county created it forty years ago, and its charter is unusual. It may levy a special assessment on parcels inside its lines and may put binding measures to a vote, but only registered members vote, and membership attaches to parcels rather than to people. Each parcel casts one vote: the owner of a four-unit rental building votes once, the same as the owner of the cottage next door, and neither building's tenants vote at all. The board is itself elected by the parcel-holders, and the board is the only body that may propose a charter amendment, which then requires a vote of the members to pass.

Now the situation. The Army engineers have offered cost-sharing on a levee that would protect the lower town against a hundred-year flood. Our share is about $40 million, to be repaid over thirty years out of the assessment. Two features make this unusually hard. First, the levee works by narrowing the channel, and the modelling puts the resulting rise in water levels on the sandspit across the bay at something like eighteen inches. The sandspit is outside our lines: unincorporated county land, about sixty households, many of them year-round renters, plus a shellfish cooperative whose beds sit in the affected current. No one there votes in our district, and the county has told us in writing that this is a home-rule question and the decision is ours. Second, the assessment runs against the parcel for thirty years, which means most of it will be paid by people who do not live here yet; our own projections say two-thirds of the properties will have changed hands before it is retired.

So we must settle who votes before we can settle anything, and every proposal currently on the table looks indefensible to somebody:

(a) Vote as constituted, parcel by parcel.
(b) One adult resident, one vote, renters included — which still excludes the sandspit and drops the owners of some forty second homes who pay full assessments but live here three months a year.
(c) Admit the sandspit households as voters on this measure only, since they bear the harm — which gives a say over our debt to people who will pay none of it, and immediately raises the question of which of them count: the renters, the co-op's seasonal crew who are here five months a year and lose their livelihood if the beds silt up, the owners who never visit?
(d) Weight the votes by dollars assessed, which several board members favor and which I cannot defend in a public meeting.

Behind all of this sits the thing nobody will say out loud: whichever rule we adopt, we adopt it for ourselves, under the rule we are trying to displace. If we vote to broaden the electorate, the broadening is authorized by the narrow electorate that benefits from keeping it narrow. If we vote it down, the same people decide again. There is no one above us to appeal to — the county declines, the state says the charter governs, and the sandspit has no government of its own.

Two of my colleagues have taken hard positions. One says the only defensible line is that anyone whose interests are touched by the levee gets a vote, however remote, which on his account includes the sandspit, the future owners, and arguably the upstream farms whose drainage changes; he concedes this may imply that, strictly, everyone in the watershed should be voting, and says so what. The other says interests are beside the point and what matters is who will actually be held to the district's rules and assessments: on that view the sandspit is out, because we will not govern them; the second-home owners are in, because we will; and the tenants are out, because their obligations run through a landlord rather than through us. I find neither position satisfying, and I have noticed that each of my colleagues happens to land on the side that favors his own property.

What I want is not a survey of how other districts handle this, and not procedural advice about running a fair meeting. I want to know whether there is a principled way to draw this line at all, or whether my two colleagues are giving incompatible answers to a question that has no right answer. Specifically: which of the four options should I support, and why; is the circularity I described a genuine objection or merely a rhetorical one, and if it is genuine, is there anything that can be done about it short of persuading the county to take the decision away from us; and is either colleague's criterion the sort of thing that could ever be applied here, given that we cannot even agree on who is inside the group that gets to apply it? If your conclusion is that the honest course is to refuse to hold the vote, say so plainly and tell me what we should do instead, because the engineering money expires in eighteen months.
