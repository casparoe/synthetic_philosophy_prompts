I'm drafting a short workshop piece on a piece of diagnostic sloppiness I keep seeing in value theory discussions: people read felt difficulty as evidence that two options are incommensurable, and read the absence of felt difficulty as evidence that one option is determinately better. Neither inference holds, and I want a matched pair of cases to make the point rather than an argument about it. Please construct the pair to the following specification.

Hold fixed throughout: the same agent, the same two concrete options, the same stakes, the same information available to her, and the same covering consideration under which the options are assessed. Exactly one structural feature of the options may differ between the two cases, and you must name it explicitly.

Case A: under that covering consideration the options are genuinely non-comparable — neither better, nor worse, nor equally good — and yet the agent chooses promptly, without dithering, and with no residual regret or sense of loss.

Case B: under that very same covering consideration there is a determinate verdict as to which option is better, and yet the choice is agonizing and leaves her with something like agent-regret.

Further constraints. Ordinary life only: two specific things a person might do next month, not two competing life plans. No deaths or triage decisions, no career-versus-art, no romantic rival, no dignity priced against money, no forced-choice-between-two-people, no science fiction or neurotechnology. One short paragraph per case. Nothing in your description of the agent's inner state may be what generates the verdict — the structural feature has to do the work, and a case that produces the intended phenomenology simply by asserting it fails. For Case A, state which diagnosis you are claiming: Raz-style incommensurability, Chang-style parity, or the deflationary account on which the appearance of non-comparability is really vagueness in the covering consideration or incompleteness in the agent's own commitments. Then show that the other two diagnoses do not apply, and run the small-improvements test explicitly — build the modestly improved version of one option and say whether it settles the ordering and why. For Case B, identify the mechanism by which a determinate comparison comes to feel hard, and demonstrate that it is not incommensurability in disguise.

After the two cases, step outside them and audit your own construction. Tell me which single feature is load-bearing; tell me what happens to each case if I remove it (does A collapse into parity, or into a plain equality, or into something with a determinate better option? does B become an easy choice?); and give me the strongest available attack from someone who maintains that felt difficulty really does track incommensurability. I expect that attack to go after Case A first, on the grounds that a settled commitment just is a stronger desire, which would be a cardinal fact about the options after all — so please steelman it rather than swat it. Finally, if any part of the specification strikes you as unsatisfiable as written, say so and explain where it breaks, instead of quietly relaxing it and handing me a case that looks like it fits.
