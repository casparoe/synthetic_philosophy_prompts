So my reading group is working through a chapter of one of those glossy pop-Buddhism books, the kind that gets quoted everywhere, and the chapter is supposed to explain anatta to people who've never met Buddhist philosophy. It goes roughly like this:

"The Buddha's claim is that there is no self — not a little one, not a big cosmic one hiding behind the little one. When King Milinda asked the monk Nagasena who exactly he was, Nagasena answered with a chariot: is the chariot the axle, the wheels, the yoke, the pole? Go through the parts and you never run into a chariot, so 'chariot' turns out to be a label we stick on an arrangement, and 'Nagasena' is a label stuck on a stream of physical and mental events in exactly the same way. That's also why the Buddha wouldn't answer when asked point blank whether a self exists — he treated the question as unanswerable and kept quiet, which makes him look less like a metaphysician and more like a therapist. On rebirth the view has no problem to solve, because there is nobody who gets reborn: what carries over is just a causal chain, like a flame touching off the next candle, and nobody claims the second flame is the first. European philosophy caught up much later — Hume's bundle, then Parfit's argument that identity isn't what matters, and Parfit even waved at the resemblance himself. The one thing the Buddha had that they didn't was a method: dissolving the illusion is supposed to take the sting out of suffering, not just tidy up your ontology."

Most of the group nodded along, and honestly I nodded too, but on the walk home I remembered someone mentioning that there were actual Buddhist schools — "personalists" or something — who insisted the person was real and got attacked for it by other Buddhists, which doesn't square with a chapter that presents the whole tradition as agreeing. I don't remember enough about them to press the point.

Can you go through that passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a scholar of Buddhist philosophy would sign off on, what's a harmless simplification, and what's actually wrong or misleading? I'm especially unsure about three things: whether the chariot exchange really licenses the flat "there is no chariot" reading the author gives it, whether "the Buddha kept quiet because the question was unanswerable" is the right story about his refusals, and whether "there is nobody who gets reborn" is what the tradition says or a modern way of dodging the hard part. Then tell me how much of the closing comparison survives: is anatta basically Parfitian reductionism, or does the analogy break somewhere important? And finally, where do those personalist Buddhists fit in — what were they actually claiming, why isn't that just sneaking the Hindu self back in, and what was the objection that everyone else thought finished them off? No need to point me at sources, I just want the reasoning spelled out.
