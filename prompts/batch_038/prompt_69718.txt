You are a philosopher who works on population ethics and on the value of the future, and who has thought carefully about Scheffler's claim that the extinction of humanity would be a catastrophe over and above the deaths it involves, together with the "afterlife conjecture" he deploys in support of that claim: the conjecture that if we came to believe humanity would be destroyed shortly after our own deaths, a great many of the things we now take to be worth doing would lose their point for us.

I am building a seminar exercise and I need a case constructed to specification, not a survey of the literature. The case is meant to pry apart three candidate sources of the "extra" badness of extinction:

A. Foregone lives — the welfare of the enormous number of future people who will never exist.
B. The loss of a collective afterlife — the dependence of our present, forward-looking projects on humanity's continuing after we are gone.
C. A residue, if there is one, that would have to be located in humanity's own ongoing story — its achievements and its continuity as a lineage — rather than in anyone's welfare or anyone's projects.

Design brief: build a single scenario in which A and B are driven as close to zero as the case honestly allows, while whatever C amounts to still has room to register. The intended lever is transformation rather than destruction.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Exactly one piece of non-actual machinery: a deliberate, collective, multi-generational program of self-modification — biological, cognitive, or both, your choice — described concretely enough that the reader can see why the resulting lineage would no longer count as human. No other speculative technology: no uploading, no aliens, no plague, no asteroid, no engineered infertility.
2. Nobody dies who would otherwise have lived, and the number of future persons is not reduced; if anything it is increased, and their lives are better. Participation is voluntary at the level of individuals and uncoerced.
3. The successor lineage retains what we would ordinarily recognize as the substance of our projects: it continues the sciences, the arts, the institutions, the memory of us, and the evaluative vocabulary in which our commitments were expressed. This stipulation is doing a great deal of work, and part of your task is to say exactly how much.
4. Keep the scenario itself to roughly 250 words, and include within it the reactions of three ordinary people with ordinary forward-looking projects — say, a researcher mid-career, a parent, and someone whose work is essentially about posterity, such as an archivist or a composer — so that the reader can judge for themselves whether B has really been neutralized.

Then supply the following, in this order:

(a) Verdict by theory. State what the deprivation account (extinction is bad only insofar as it involves deaths and foregone lives), Scheffler's account, and an impersonal "humanity's story" view each say about your case, and for each say whether the case confirms it, embarrasses it, or is simply beside its point.
(b) The load-bearing feature. Name the single stipulation that does the most work, and explain why.
(c) The removal test. Describe what happens to each verdict when that stipulation is withdrawn, and whether the case then collapses into the familiar doomsday scenario or turns into something else.
(d) The best defense. Give the strongest reply available to whichever theory comes out worst — including, if applicable, the reply that your case is no counterexample at all because the stipulated "continuation" already presupposes what is in dispute.
(e) A note on cheating. Tell me plainly where the case risks stipulating its verdicts rather than generating them from the theories, and what you did to limit that risk.

Before writing the scenario, spend a few lines planning: list the constraints and sketch how you intend to satisfy each one. Think the design through instead of settling for the first version that comes to mind.

Two closing requests. First, tell me what would change your mind: which stipulation, revised in which direction, would flip your overall judgment about the case, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. Second, put your bottom line in a fixed form — end with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of these labels: C-SURVIVES (a residual loss beyond A and B is real and the case exposes it), NO-RESIDUE (nothing beyond A and B is lost; the felt loss is bare species-preference), B-NOT-ZERO (the case fails because the collective afterlife was never in fact preserved), INDETERMINATE (the case cannot discriminate between the accounts as specified).
