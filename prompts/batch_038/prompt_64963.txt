Hey — freelance science-and-ideas journalist here. I've got a feature coming out next month about how big food companies keep a product tasting the same for decades: sensory panels, reference lots, the whole apparatus of enforced consistency. The editor wants a piece of short fiction to run alongside it, and I'd really rather it wasn't the usual "AI learns to taste" number.

What I actually want the story to chew on is a philosophical problem I fell into while reporting. It's Dennett's old Chase-and-Sanborn intuition pump, roughly: two coffee tasters have spent years on a panel certifying that the brew hasn't changed. One of them starts saying the coffee doesn't taste to him the way it used to, and he genuinely cannot tell whether what shifted is the experience itself, or his snap judgment about it, or his vocabulary for it, or just his mood about it. The other one, a neuroscientist who knows the trick of fatiguing your visual system, deliberately and slowly inverts his own colour vision over a stretch of time — so gradually that nothing ever looks different to him at any given moment — and afterwards lands in the awkward position of admitting he never noticed. The upshot he's pushing is that if qualia are supposed to be things you have direct, incorrigible acquaintance with, then a situation where you can't tell "it changed" from "I changed my mind about it" looks like a situation where there was no such thing there in the first place.

Here's what I want you to build. Set it at a company that runs something called a drift audit on its own panelists — this is my invention, not real industry practice, so run with it however you like. Once a week, each taster cups a sealed reference lot that cannot possibly have changed, its the same frozen harvest from the same year held in the same freezer, and writes a fresh description of it from scratch without being allowed to look at anything they've written before. Years of descriptions pile up in a database nobody reads. Then somebody feeds the archive to a model that just measures how far the words have wandered, and it turns out one taster's language has drifted enormously and in a consistent direction while he insists nothing at all has changed for him, and another taster's language has stayed nearly identical while she can no longer stand the coffee.

I want the story to make a reader feel why that asymmetry is philosophically interesting rather than merely quirky, and I want it to press on both sides. On one side: maybe there's nothing further in there beyond the dispositions and the reports, and the audit is just making that visible. On the other: maybe the audit is measuring the wrong kind of thing altogether, because a description was never a transcript of an experience to begin with, so drift in the words can't be evidence of drift in anything else. Give me at least one character who's convinced the audit proves something deep and one who thinks it's a category mistake, and let the second one actually land some hits — I don't want a strawman getting knocked over in act three.

Practical notes. Around 1,500 to 2,000 words. Real people, real workplace friction, and no lecture-hall dialogue where characters explain the argument to each other; the philosophy should come out of what happens, plus at most a couple of sharp exchanges. The sealed reference lot should matter structurally, not just as set dressing. And please don't have anyone deliver a verdict about consciousness in the last paragraph — ending on something unresolved but pointed is fine, better actually.

Then separately, give me an author's note of a couple hundred words. This is the part I care most about for fact-checking, so be blunt in it: seperate out what is genuinely settled among people who work on this stuff from what is still live disagreement, and don't let the story smuggle a contested position in as though it were the consensus. Tell me which of my invented details are doing philosophical work the real arguments don't actually license, which side the story ends up favoring whether you meant it to or not, and where you took liberties — with the thought experiments, with how sensory panels really operate, with what a model could plausibly detect in a pile of tasting notes. No bibliography and no reading list, I'll chase sources down myself. I just want the note to be honest about confidence levels.
