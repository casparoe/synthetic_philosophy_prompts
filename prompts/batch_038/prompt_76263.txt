For the past academic year I have co-run an interdisciplinary working group on temporal agency with a behavioral economist, funded by a small center grant that requires us to submit a joint written product rather than two separate papers. The group has met fortnightly, and honestly it has been a year of talking past each other, so the deliverable I need from you is the document we ought to have produced in month three. The two parties are these. Party A is a dynamic-inconsistency theorist in the tradition that runs from Strotz's 1955 treatment of myopia and inconsistency through the planner–doer models of Thaler and Shefrin, Laibson's quasi-hyperbolic discounting, and the naif/sophisticate taxonomy of O'Donoghue and Rabin, and who also takes seriously Parfit's reductionist view of persons and Schelling's account of self-command as strategic self-management; on this view, so-called weakness of will is the predictable output of a preference structure whose discounting is not exponential, the agent is best modeled as a sequence of partially conflicting decision-makers, and the interesting normative questions are about which self gets to bind which, when commitment devices help, and why a sophisticated agent can sometimes be worse off than a naive one. Party B is a philosopher of action in the broadly neo-Aristotelian and Davidsonian line, sympathetic to Holton's account of willpower as the disposition to stick with a resolution without reconsidering it in ways the resolution was meant to foreclose; on this view, akrasia is defined by an agent-level failure — acting against an all-things-considered judgment the agent still endorses at the moment of action — and the discounting literature misdescribes the phenomenon because it treats every choice, however framed and however bracketed, as a revelation of an underlying preference. Party B's sharpest objection is what I will call the bracketing objection: a discount function fitted to choices made across differently framed decision brackets is not evidence about a single agent's ranking over the same option set. Party A's sharpest objection is what I will call the measurement objection: any operationalization of the all-things-considered judgment is itself a choice, and so cannot be held fixed against the choices it is supposed to explain.

To keep the document from floating free of evidence, please organize it around a specific result from our own pilot, which you should treat as given. In a study of 240 participants we ran an incentivized multiple-price-list task over real delays to real payments, plus a diary arm in which half the participants wrote down and re-read a self-directed resolution two weeks before a second, structurally identical choice. At the aggregate level, reversal patterns were fitted well by a quasi-hyperbolic form with a substantial present bias parameter, and the fit was markedly better than any exponential specification; Party A regards this as the core datum. At the individual level, however, the strongest single predictor of not reversing was having written and re-read the resolution — an effect far larger than any manipulation of the size of the immediacy premium — and, in follow-up interviews, a plurality of the participants who did reverse said that what changed was not how they weighed the outcomes but that they had talked themselves into a new description of the option they were taking, offering justifications they had not entertained at the first choice. Party B regards that second cluster as the core datum, and insists that the aggregate fit shows only that choice is describable by a discount function, not that the agent has one. I want both parties to have to reckon with both clusters, and I want a third case used as a shared test bed: call it Priya's manuscript. At time one Priya resolves to write every morning, binds herself by booking a paid 7 a.m. co-working slot that she forfeits if she does not appear, and at 6:55 the next day judges all things considered that she should go and write, goes, and writes. Party A wants to describe the forfeit as having shifted the choice architecture so that what her 6:55 self most prefers is now also writing; Party B wants to say that no revision of judgment occurred at all, and that the commitment device worked precisely by foreclosing a deliberation that would otherwise have been available. The document must say, for this case, what each side is committed to, whether both descriptions can be simultaneously true, and what would have to be the case for one of them to be false.

Format the output as a single joint statement, roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words, in five parts. Part one: a numbered list of at least twelve agreed claims that both parties can sign without reservation — including claims about what the phenomenon of acting against one's own considered judgment looks like from the inside, about what the formalism of time-inconsistent discounting does and does not by itself establish, and about which remedies (commitment, framing, habituation, environment design) are known to have some effect. Part two: a disagreements table with five columns — topic, the formulation Party A would accept as a statement of its own view, the formulation Party B would accept as a statement of its own view, a single neutral formulation both can sign, and a judgment of whether the disagreement is substantive, partly verbal, or wholly verbal. Part three: the cruxes. I suspect at least three of them are these — whether an all-things-considered judgment can be operationalized as a ranking over the very option set available at the moment of action, or whether it is necessarily a ranking over a set that includes options deliberately foreclosed; whether the unit that has preferences is a temporally extended agent or a time-slice, and what would count as evidence either way rather than as stipulation; and whether the sophistication results (that an agent can rationally prefer not to know her own future behavior) show something deep about self-knowledge or simply show that the word preference has been stretched past its useful range — but you may propose better cruxes if you find them, provided you justify the substitution. For each crux, give one concrete case, experimental design, or argument that both parties agree bears on it, and record each party's prediction in advance, in a form specific enough to be embarrassing if it fails. Part four: a short section on verbal dispute, stating explicitly which parts of the year-long argument were about words, and proposing a shared stipulative vocabulary — say what preference, better judgment, self-control, agent, and inconsistency are to denote in the joint document, and note for each stipulation which party gave up more. Part five: defeaters, in which each party states one empirical finding and one argument that would force it to abandon or substantially revise its core claim, including a finding Party A would count as bad news for the discount-function framework (for instance, a pattern of reversal that no monotone discount function, however parameterized, can represent) and an argument Party B would count as bad news for the judgment-based definition of akrasia. Throughout, the quality bar is that no claim be so weak that signing it costs nothing, no formulation be slanted toward its author's side, and neither party be permitted to characterize the other; do not include a bibliography, and refer to named researchers only where an attribution is doing actual work, describing their ideas in your own words rather than quoting them.
