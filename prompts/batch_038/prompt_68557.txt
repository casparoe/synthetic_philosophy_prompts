So I audit a philosophy-of-probability seminar at the state university a couple evenings a month — I do data work by day and this is basically my hobby — and we've now burned three whole meetings on Bertrand's chord problem and gotten absolutely nowhere. You know the setup: equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, a chord is "chosen at random," what's the chance it's longer than a side of the triangle. Three different ways of cashing out "at random" give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, all apparently licensed by the same principle of indifferance, and the seminar splits into three camps that never really engage each other's reasons. Camp one says the problem is just ill-posed, full stop: there's no such thing as the probability of an unspecified experiment, the three answers correspond to three different generating mechanisms (two random endpoints on the circumference, a random point on a radius, a random midpoint in the disk), and the so-called paradox is a cautionary tale you'd tell in an intro class about the difference between a probability and a random variable somebody forgot to specify. Camp two says that's a cop-out and that the problem statement really does constrain the answer: if you insist the answer not depend on the size or the position of the circle, then invariance to scale and translation pins down a unique distribution and one of the three answers falls out of it; on this view indifference isn't a license to guess, it's a defeasible constraint that gets tightened by symmetries. Camp three says both of the others are demanding a precision the evidence doesn't support, that the right state of mind is a whole set of credences or an interval, and that it's simply a mistake to force a single number out of an underdetermined question — which the decision-theory people in the group then attack as unactionable, since you can't place a bet with a set.

My hypothesis, which nobody in the group will engage with and which I might be totally wrong about: these three camps aren't actually disagreeing about chords or about the math, because everyone accepts the math and everyone can reproduce all three answers in ten minutes on the whiteboard. They're disagreeing about what a probability claim is even a claim about — a feature of a specified generating process, a constraint on a rational state of mind derived from the way the question was asked, or something deliberately vague because the question is vague — and they've been arguing under one word for three weeks. If that's right, the whole thing is closer to the "two groups answering different questions" case than to a genuine factual dispute, and the stalemate is a sign of a badly chosen question rather than of anybody being dumb. But there's a worry pointing the other way too: maybe camp two is smuggling in an empirical assumption — the picture of straws being thrown onto the circle — while presenting it as a priori, in which case the disagreement is at least partly substantive and could in principle be settled by facts about the world instead of by stipulation. And maybe camp three's move is a real diagnosis, or maybe it's an evasion dressed up as sophistication, and I can't tell which.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on which camp wins. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, about what "the probability" means; a disagreement over the standards for when a probability question is well-posed; a hidden empirical question wearing an a-priori costume; bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for; or three separate questions being run under a single heading — and support it with what each camp actually says and what each is committed to, rather than by reciting the history of the problem. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: some distinction that would break the logjam, an actual physical experiment with straws or needles that could bear on it, a formal result about invariance or about sets of credences, a reformulation of the question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, make the argument for why nothing should be expected and how we should run the fourth meeting instead. Two other things. Be honest about which camp has the strongest position once you strip out the rhetoric, even if your main answer is that they aren't really in conflict. And write it in American English, as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I also don't want citations, footnotes, or a reading list; I can't chase those down and I'd much rather have your own reasoning about the arguments than a list of who said what, so if you mention a position, just describe it in your own words.
