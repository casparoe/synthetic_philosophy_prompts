I am a junior in high school, and our literary magazine is putting together a spring issue on the theme of unintended consequences. I would like to submit a piece of speculative fiction, but I do not yet trust myself to dramatize a philosophical problem without letting it collapse into a lecture, so I am hoping you will write the story and I will study the choices you make. Please write a short story of roughly two thousand words.

Here is the problem I want the story to be about. Environmental ethics tends to treat the preservation and restoration of wild habitat as an uncomplicated good. If you take animal suffering seriously, though, the picture becomes much harder to read: a very large share of wild animals — particularly insects, fish, and other creatures that produce enormous numbers of offspring — die of starvation, predation, parasitism, disease, or exposure within days or weeks of hatching, and the sheer quantity of those lives may mean that a thriving, intact ecosystem contains more misery than it prevents. That raises a genuinely disturbing question: could destroying habitat sometimes reduce the total amount of wild suffering in the world, and could someone who cares deeply about animals therefore be pushed toward preferring pavement to marsh? I want the story to take that question seriously enough that a reader who has never entertained it feels a little vertigo.

For the concrete situation, please use something like the following. A small coastal town is deciding whether to spend a great deal of money breaching a dike built in the 1960s for mosquito control, in order to restore tidal flow to a salt marsh that has been a stagnant brackish impoundment and shrub swamp for three generations. If the town declines, the parcel goes to a developer who intends to fill and grade it for a distribution center. Your protagonist is not a local. She is an ecologist working for a consultancy that has been hired to produce a "welfare assessment" — a ledger estimating net hedonic value per acre per year for each of the three futures: restored marsh, impoundment left alone, or filled and paved.

Please make that ledger a real object in the story rather than a metaphor for one. I want to see columns, assumptions, footnotes, sensitivity analyses, a contested coefficient for the probability that a fiddler crab or a mayfly nymph is the kind of thing that can suffer at all, and an argument about whether to count the several years a mayfly spends as a nymph on the bottom of a tidal creek against the few hours it spends as an adult with mouthparts too vestigial to feed. I want the reader to feel how much of the total is being carried by terms nobody can measure.

The story should resist both easy answers. Do not write the marsh as a kingdom in which every animal lives a full and flourishing life, and do not write it as a torture chamber where the merciful act is to pour concrete over it. Give at least one character a serious, non-sentimental reason to doubt that the assessment can be performed at all: someone who argues that the ranking of the three futures flips depending on assumptions you cannot test, and that acting on a bad estimate in one direction could produce suffering on a scale that has never existed anywhere before. Also give the town's conservationists their best case, which I think is not about counting at all.

Some further requirements:

- No character should serve as a mouthpiece for the author's view, and no scene should consist of two experts explaining the argument to each other for the reader's benefit.
- Include at least one scene in which a person with no philosophical training — a selectman, a crabber, a parent at a public comment period — says something plainly practical that cuts deeper than anything in the ledger.
- Let the technical material appear the way it actually appears in working life: in spreadsheets, in forwarded email threads, in a contractor clearing her throat before she reads a number aloud into a microphone, in the moment a slide does not render.
- Your protagonist should reach a decision, and the ending should make the reader feel the price of that decision rather than its correctness. Please avoid the stock endings I expect: no last-minute revelation that the data were falsified, no dream sequence, no animal making eye contact with the protagonist in the final line.
- Close third person or first person, present or past tense, your choice. Salt, mud, diesel, cordgrass, the sound of water moving through a culvert at low tide. Concrete detail over abstraction.

Please title the story and give it in full, without an author's note explaining what it means.
