For eighteen years I have worked at a national metrology institute. I write calibration certificates and uncertainty budgets, I take part in interlaboratory comparisons, and I think about claims to know a quantity in a very particular way: every result has to name a measurand, identify the measuring system, state which reference standard it is traceable to, separate the components of uncertainty that come from statistical analysis of repeated observations from those that rest on certificates, specifications, and professional judgment, declare a resolution and a decision rule, and publish the whole budget so that someone else can audit it. A colleague of mine who has just finished a philosophy degree keeps telling me that physicists' thought experiments are a live epistemological problem, and I would like the dispute put into terms I can actually inspect. I cannot check any references you might give me, so please keep it self-contained; names for the positions are optional and the substance is what I want audited.

The dispute, as she describes it, has two main camps. One holds that a thought experiment is an argument in disguise: whatever it delivers was already latent in its premises and in ordinary experience, the vivid scenario is a presentational device, and any real content can be reconstructed as an explicit inference. The other holds that some thought experiments let one apprehend a law of nature as necessary, that this apprehension is not a deduction from accumulated observations, and that the reconstruction therefore always loses something. She also mentions a third and vaguer position, on which a thought experiment is a kind of simulation run on tacit empirical know-how.

Please do the following.

1. Translate the disagreement into my vocabulary. Assign, so far as you honestly can, the roles of measurand, measuring system, reference standard, calibration hierarchy, Type A and Type B uncertainty components, systematic versus random error, resolution, decision rule, and interlaboratory comparison. Where a slot has no occupant, say that the slot is empty rather than filling it with something approximate, and place the third position somewhere in this scheme.

2. Write three mock certificates for three canonical cases, each of which I understand only roughly: the argument that ties two stones of different weight together so that the rule "heavier bodies fall faster" predicts the pair falls both faster and slower than the heavy stone alone; the observer in a sealed chest who cannot distinguish uniform acceleration in empty space from standing at rest in a gravitational field; and the spinning bucket of water, whose surface becomes concave even once the water is at rest relative to the bucket's walls. For each: what is being measured, where every input comes from, how you would evaluate its uncertainty, and whether the conclusion claims a generality across merely possible situations that no apparatus could license.

3. Flag your own smuggling. I suspect that treating an intuition as an instrument automatically settles the dispute in favor of the first camp, since the calibration chain of any instrument must bottom out in prior observations. Identify the exact step in your translation where that outcome is produced, and tell me whether a faithful translation could have avoided it or whether the second camp simply cannot be stated in my terms.

4. Give the residue report in three tiers: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion you should describe, and what cannot be expressed at all. For each untranslatable item, take a position on whether the failure is mine — my framework has no way to express necessity, or the difference between what an instrument reports and what a theory licenses — or evidence that the philosophers' disagreement is thinner than it sounds.

5. Answer two questions directly. Do I have a legitimate objection that the second camp is positing an instrument with no traceability chain, or does that objection beg the question by assuming everything knowable must be measured? And if intuitions are instruments, can their systematic error be bounded rather than merely suspected, given that competent people report different results for the same case — in my world, agreement between laboratories is weak evidence when the laboratories share a common cause, and I want to know whether the analogue of that worry applies here and what a key comparison for intuitions would even consist in.
