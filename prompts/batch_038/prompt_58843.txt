Background: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, but I wrote something up for a little "ideas lunch" we run at work and I want to sanity check it before I present. It's about the extended mind thing — Clark and Chalmers, 1998, Otto with the notebook. Here's the draft, please rip into it.

**1. The setup as I understand it.** They argue that if a process outside the skull does the same job a process inside the skull would, we have no non-arbitrary reason to deny it counts as cognitive. Their case is Otto, who has Alzheimer's and carries a notebook he consults constantly; what's in the notebook does for him what memory does for Inga. They add conditions: the notebook has to be around when he needs it, he has to reach for it without deliberating, he takes what he reads more or less at face value, and it has to have served him reliably in the past. The standard pushback is Adams and Aizawa's — causing cognition isn't being cognition, or else my laptop, the power grid, and the oxygen in the room are all part of my mind.

**2. My worry: the notebook is append-only from the world's side.** Nothing writes into Otto's notebook except Otto, and that's exactly what makes the trust condition checkable. Dev tooling isn't like that. My editor's autocomplete, the model behind our internal code assistant, a vendored dependency, the shared library everybody imports — I lean on those the same way Otto leans on the notebook, but they also get written to by processes I never see. The model behind an endpoint gets swapped on a Thursday. A transitive dep changes behavior overnight. So the thing I treat as trustworthy isn't a fixed resource, it's a channel whose contents can change without me ever re-checking them.

**3. Two horns.** Either extended cognition flickers — my mind is extended on Wednesday and not on Thursday, which seems to wreck the idea that I have beliefs partly located outside my head, since beliefs that vanish overnight aren't beliefs — or the trust condition gets restated as trust in a channel rather than in a snapshot of content. I like the second horn better, but then the boundary of the mind looks more like a permission scope in an IAM policy than a place. Which might be fine, maybe that's the right revision, but its no longer the argument they made.

**4. A sharper point, I think.** They allow that Otto does a quick coherence check on what he reads. But a check is only a check if you have a second source. When the external resource is the only copy — my head genuinely doesn't retain the information — the coherence check compares the notebook against itself. In engineering terms that's a test with no oracle. So either trust has to be inherited from some other, redundantly held resource, which puts a hard cap on how much you can offload, or Otto's endorsement is unearned and the conditions aren't actually met. This feels like it does real work and I can't tell whether someone already said it twenty years ago.

- Does the Adams and Aizawa reply in section 1 also kill sections 2 through 4, or does the write-back thing give me something they don't have an answer to?
- Where am I overclaiming? "Flickers" feels like rhetoric doing the job of an argument.
