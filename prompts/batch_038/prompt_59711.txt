So I've been going down a rabbit hole on how Wikipedia and Wikidata handle the question of when two names are names for the same thing, and it's starting to look to me like the whole operation has a buried theory of names and identity that nobody ever wrote down. Some things that are bugging me:

- The rule for picking an article title is basically "use the name that most reliable English-language sources use". So where someone has a birth name and a later name, or where the same historical figure is known by two names in different languages or eras, which one gets to *be* the title is settled by counting usage. Nothing about the names themselves decides it.
- Every other name for that same topic gets a redirect, so both work, both land you on the same page, and once the page's first line has announced the alternates, the prose then uses them interchangeably, as though nothing at all turns on which one you pick.
- But Wikidata, the structured database underneath, will not merge two items when it isn't sure. There's a dedicated property for "said to be the same as" (P460) that you put on both items to record that they might be one real-world thing, and you just leave them both sitting there unmerged. And there's a companion property, "different from" (P1889), for when two items look like they ought to be one but aren't.

What does all that presuppose about what a name contributes, and about identity claims? If a name contributes nothing but its bearer, then the interchangeable-usage practice is exactly right — but what on earth is "said to be the same as" doing? That looks like a category error to me: either they are the same thing or they aren't, and there's no such state as provisionally-same. Unless the practice is quietly assuming that you never get at the thing except through some particular way of picking it out, in which case two pickings-out really can fail to settle whether they land on one object or two, and you'd need a flag like that. Which is it?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and be strict about separating what the practice actually *requires* from what it merely happens to sit comfortably with. My suspicion is that the titling rule and the redirect/merger practice pull in different directions and I can't tell whether that's a genuine inconsistency or just me not seeing the move that reconciles them — so tell me either way. Also say where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where more than one incompatible story about names would licence exactly the same editing behaviour, because that would explain why nobody notices.

Then: what would have to change if you rebuilt the whole thing on the assumption that names are contentless tags? Imagine an entity-resolution system that merges any two records above a similarity threshold, irreversibly, with no way to record doubt and no separate layer of alternates. What specifically breaks, and what does the fact of it breaking tell you about what the current practice is committed to? And is there anything in the existing setup that would survive the rebuild untouched — because that bit is presumably the part that isn't really about names at all.
