I teach a 75-minute session to second-year public-policy undergraduates who have finished one term of intermediate microeconomics. They arrive having been told that utility is purely ordinal, and most of them infer that comparing one person's welfare with another's is therefore meaningless. My topic is interpersonal utility comparisons: what claim is actually at issue, and what survives that objection.

Don't explain the subject to me; plan how I should explain it. Give me the order in which the pieces must go in, noting what each depends on, and name the single concrete case that should carry the session — what it must have (a transfer that leaves both parties' budgets intact? a good that cannot be traded away?) and what it must conspicuously lack so that the question is not settled by an appeal to obvious need. Then map the misconceptions they will bring or form, in order of how much damage each does if left standing, including the collapse of three distinct claims: cardinal measurability within a person, comparability across persons, and whether a comparison could be true even if no one could ever verify it. Say exactly where the assumption of equal capacity for satisfaction has to be exposed, since that is what quietly licenses any averaging-behind-a-veil argument, and where in the sequence to head off each misconception. Finally, tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level and say so, and give me one closing question whose answers would show me that they understood the problem rather than merely accepted my verdict. Please write in American English.
