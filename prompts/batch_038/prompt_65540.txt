For the past year I have been sitting in on a reading group in historical sociology working through the cyclical philosophies of history — Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, Toynbee — alongside their critics. Our discussions have stayed almost entirely at the level of exegesis, and last week one member made an objection I have not been able to shake: these schemes are never tested against anything but the material their authors already had in hand. Polybius built his cycle around Rome and the Greek cities, Ibn Khaldun around the Berber and Arab dynasties of the Maghreb, Spengler and Toynbee around a comparative survey of civilizations assembled for the purpose. The fit is therefore guaranteed by construction, and the theories are made unfalsifiable not by argument but by choice of evidence. To test the objection I want to run one modern episode through all four of them and then ask what the episode does to the theories, not merely what the theories say about the episode. The episode is the end of the Weimar Republic, roughly 1930 to 1933, with its aftermath in view. The facts I take to be common knowledge and that you should not need to look up: after the Depression destroyed the last coalition with a workable parliamentary majority, Germany was governed by cabinets appointed by an aging president and ruling largely through emergency decrees under Article 48 of the constitution; parties at both extremes maintained paramilitary formations and fought in the streets while still contesting elections that returned no majority; the National Socialists became the largest party in the Reichstag without ever winning a majority of the vote in a free national election; the transfer of the chancellorship to Hitler in January 1933 was not a coup or a revolution from below but a legal appointment engineered by a small circle of conservative notables who believed they had secured enough cabinet posts and institutional leverage to contain him and were wrong within weeks; and the Enabling Act followed in March, passed by a legislature that had just consented to its own dispossession. The regime that resulted was terminated from outside, by total military defeat twelve years later, not by any internal rotation back to a previous constitutional form; the democracy that succeeded it was imposed by occupying powers and, in the eastern half of the country, supplanted by a different imposed order.

First, the mapping. For each theorist state precisely what the theory identifies as the relevant actors, the mechanism of change, and the stage of the cycle that Germany in 1930 to 1933 occupies. Polybius's sequence runs from monarchy through kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy and mob rule and back again, and he credits Rome's stability to a mixed constitution rather than to any single stage, so say which element of that apparatus is doing work here and whether a polity in which a senile field marshal and his court dispose of the chancellorship while mass parties with street armies contest elections is best described as mob rule, as oligarchy, or as something his vocabulary simply lacks. For Ibn Khaldun, take asabiyyah and the generational lifespan he assigns to dynasties and be blunt about whether the concept retains any content in a modern bureaucratic state or can be made to fit only by stretching group solidarity to mean something as thin as party loyalty; note also that his cycle is a rotation of ruling groups within a persisting urban civilization rather than the death of the civilization, and ask which of those two things happened in Germany. For Toynbee, work through the vocabulary of breakdown — creative minorities degenerating into dominant minorities that rule by force because they can no longer attract followers, the schism in the body social between dominant minority, internal proletariat and external proletariat, the universal state, the pattern of rout and rally — identifying which of these the Papen and Schleicher cabinets, the Nazi movement and the Third Reich correspond to and which do not fit at all; attend to the apparent inversion in which the movement his schema would place in the position of an internal proletariat captured the apparatus of the universal state instead of generating a church alongside it. Treat Spengler separately and more carefully, since he is the one case of a theorist who forecast a coming Caesarism in the West, in his own culture and his own century, in advance; the question is what evidential weight such a forecast carries when it was widely read in the country where it was borne out, and whether a prediction that may have helped produce its own fulfillment counts as confirmation, as a refutation of the theorist's claim to detachment, or as neither. Second, the assessment, which should run in both directions. Separate what each theory genuinely explains about the collapse — features of 1930 to 1933 that an event-driven narrative leaves unaccounted for — from what it misdescribes and above all from what it is silent about; do not force it to speak where it has nothing to say, and say so plainly when that is the situation. Then give a verdict for each: confirmation, anomaly absorbable by amendment, or a case of a kind the theory never contemplated and has no category for. Keep two versions of the cyclical claim distinct throughout, because I suspect my group has been conflating them: a strong version on which polities pass through a fixed sequence of stages under an internal mechanism and must return to the starting point, and a weak version on which certain dynamics recur — the erosion of the solidarity that brought a ruling group to power, the conversion of a persuasive elite into a coercive one, the generational loss of the dispositions that built the institutions — with no claim of fixed order or of return. Weimar may well refute the strong version for all four authors while supporting the weak version for two or three, and if that is your conclusion I want to see which parts of which theories belong to which version.

Third, the meta-question the objection was really about. Each of these schemes seems to have a ready answer to the awkward fact that the cycle never closed: the sequence was interrupted from outside by military defeat and so never had the chance to run. Take up whether that reply is legitimate — an interrupted mechanism is a real possibility in the natural sciences too — or whether it is an immunizing strategy revealing the theories to be untestable in principle, and say what a cyclical theorist would have had to commit to before 1933 for the outcome to count as evidence either way. In particular, specify what these theories owe by way of a reset condition, an account of what returns a polity to the beginning of the cycle, and whether an externally imposed democracy can ever count as such a reset or whether its imposition shows that the cycle had ceased to be a cycle. Close by saying whether the episode tells in favor of the linear, anti-historicist position associated with Popper's critique of historical prophecy, or whether it exposes that position to a mirror-image difficulty, since a view on which history has no recurrent structure whatsoever would also have had nothing to say about 1930 to 1933 and cannot claim to have explained what happened either. A few practical notes: around two thousand words, organized so that I could hand a different section to a different member of the group; no bibliography, no reading list, no page citations, since I have the texts and what I want is an argument rather than a survey. Where you are unsure of a historical detail or of what an author actually says, flag the uncertainty in the sentence rather than papering over it with a confident paraphrase. And resist making the conclusion tidy — my suspicion going in is that these four theories fail in four different ways for four different reasons, and if that is right I would rather have the differences sharpened than averaged into a single verdict about cyclical history in general.
