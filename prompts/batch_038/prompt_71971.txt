I help administer a small arbitration panel that settles two-party splits — a dissolved partnership, two towns sharing one water supply, that sort of thing. Every arbitrator here runs the same rule: we take each side's walkaway (what it gets if there's no deal) and its ideal (the most it could possibly get, given the other side won't accept less then its walkaway), then we find the biggest t such that giving each side walkaway + t × (ideal − walkaway) is still feasible, and we award exactly that. So both sides end up the same fraction of the way from their floor to their dream. We also hold ourselves to two side conditions: the answer can't depend on what units people happen to quote in, and if the pie grows in one side's favor so that its ideal rises while the other side's stays put, that side must not come out worse off than it would have. In the rare disputes where the two situations are mirror images, we know the mainstream alternative — the one that maximizes the product of the two gains over walkaway — lands on the same number as us and nobody notices; the fights all start in the lopsided cases.

So what theory of fairness is this panel actually running, and what does it presuppose that the product-maximizing rule doesn't? Which of the standard axioms for this kind of rule are we keeping and which have we traded away, and what's philosophically loaded about the trade — does our version require aspiration levels to be the sort of thing that can carry moral weight at all, or is that just a heuristic that happens to work? Are our two side conditions even consistent with each other and with the main rule, or is something giving? And what are we assuming in treating walkaways and ideals as real, discoverable facts, when in practice we just ask each side to state them privately — tell me what we'd have to redesign if that assumption is false.
