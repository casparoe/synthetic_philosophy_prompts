I am in the first year of a physics PhD in a group that works on quantum gravity, and by the end of the term I have to commit to a project. Over the holidays I got into a long argument with my brother, who writes software but reads a fair amount of philosophy of science, and I would like to settle it before I make my choice, because the two questions have turned out to be the same question. He told me that what my field does is not science, on the grounds that nothing we propose will be testable within my working lifetime. I answered him with something I had picked up from a book on the scientific method of string theory: that a theory can accrue what is called non-empirical confirmation, and in particular that there is a no-alternatives argument, according to which the fact that decades of work by very able people have failed to produce any rival framework that does the same job is itself evidence that, within the relevant domain, there is no such rival to be found. I added that this struck me as the same move as the pessimistic induction from unconceived alternatives — the argument that scientists in every past era failed to conceive of the theories that ended up replacing theirs — since both turn on what researchers have not thought of; and I said the two support each other, because the history of science shows that unthought-of alternatives are a genuine phenomenon, while in our case none have ever surfaced.

My brother was unmoved. He said that if a theory can be confirmed without anything happening in the world, then confirmation has stopped doing any work at all, and that physicists who go on talking about experiments and observations are simply hedging: quietly conceding that the non-empirical support is insufficient while pretending in public that it is not. He also said I had got the history-of-science argument backwards, though he could not articulate how, and that irritated me more than the rest of the exchange. So my question has two parts, and I would rather have the first answered honestly than have my side of the argument vindicated. If string theory has in fact already been non-empirically confirmed in the way I described, what is left for an experiment to add, and why would anyone in the field keep hunting for empirical tests? And more personally: is it rational for me to spend five years on a research programme whose confirmation is, on this account, already in hand — or does the existence of such an account mean I should read it as a warning rather than as a licence?

Please tell me plainly where my description is wrong, including the part my brother could not put into words, and please correct the confusions rather than simply working within them. I would also ask you to keep a clear line between what is settled here and what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions: I do not want a disputed position presented to me as the consensus of philosophers of physics or of practising physicists, in either direction, and if the well-informed are split, I would rather be told how they are split and what the split turns on. Finally, if it helps to have a verdict rather than a survey, say how confident you are that a philosopher of science who has actually read the relevant literature would accept the correction you give me, and flag any place where your own reading of the debate is idiosyncratic.
