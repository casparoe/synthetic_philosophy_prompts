I convene a second-year philosophy of science seminar, and we give two weeks to Kuhn. The students arrive fluent in the phrase paradigm shift, which they apply to anything from breakfast cereals to their own views on punishment, and they leave able to recite the vocabulary of normal science, anomaly, crisis and revolution without ever having felt what the two difficult claims would cost if they were true: that a successful revolution rewrites its own history so that the current picture looks like the deposit of a long accumulation of evidence, and that the vocabularies on either side of the change do not line up term for term, so that the disagreement cannot be stated in a language both sides share. Arguing the point at them has not worked. I would rather hand them something funny and let the jokes carry the philosophy.

Please write a copy-editor's query list for the third edition of an undergraduate geology textbook that has lived through the revolution from a static Earth to plate tectonics: roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, numbered, with the author's replies set after each query as though pencilled in the margin. Invent the press, the book, the two authors and the chapter titles; keep the real history to its broad shape — drift proposed early in the twentieth century, widely dismissed, then accepted with disconcerting speed from the late 1950s onward — and attach no dates or claims to real geologists. The copy-editor is the voice of the piece: conscientious, entirely unphilosophical, concerned with house style, plate permissions, glossary consistency, word count and what the schools market will tolerate, and quite unaware that she is asking the questions that decide whether two theories can be compared at all. The author is a confident professor converted in his youth who can no longer reconstruct what the superseded view meant, only translate it, and who is faintly irritated by queries that assume a translation is always available. Among whatever else you invent, I would like the schedule to include: a query about a chapter titled after the permanence of ocean basins, and whether it should be marked as an error or simply deleted, since house style permits neither; a glossary in which several entries survive the revolution with their spellings intact and their referents changed — continent and crust at the very least, with geosyncline and the shrinking-Earth account of mountain building quietly retired; a query about the plate showing the fit of the Atlantic coastlines and whether its caption should call it evidence or illustration; a dissenting note from the emeritus co-author, who was never converted, and the difficulty of finding anywhere to put it; and a query from marketing reporting that a reviewer of the previous edition complained the discipline came across as a matter of fashion. The engine of the piece should be that the copy-editor keeps requesting precisely the neutral comparison the author's position forbids — a sentence both editions would accept, a term that means the same on both sides, a list of the evidence that settled it — and keeps receiving replies that explain, in perfectly reasonable prose, why the request cannot be met.

The humour must come from getting Kuhn right rather than from geology jokes: the way a paradigm supplies not only answers but the puzzles and the standards for a solution; the fact that an anomaly is an anomaly only against an expectation; the conversion that is not a proof; the textbook tradition that presents the current state as though it had arrived by accumulation, and Kuhn's own observation that this is why revolutions become invisible. If you want to gesture at his formulations, paraphrase, and do not put anything in quotation marks, from him or from anyone else. The target is a practice and a style of discourse rather than a person: what is being ironised is the self-effacing way a discipline's teaching materials erase the contingency of the picture they now sell as settled, and, secondarily, the ease with which my author asserts that he and his old colleague cannot speak to one another. That last point should turn on itself by the end — the emeritus co-author and the professor have been lunching together for forty years and evidently manage fine, which the copy-editor should record without comment and without drawing the moral. Do not let the author wink, do not let the editor philosophise, and do not state any of the objections outright. It should read as an ordinary piece of publishing correspondence that someone who has never opened Kuhn finds amusing and someone who has finds uncomfortably accurate.
