You're a philosopher of mind who has actually worked on the implementation problem — what it takes for a physical system to *be* running a given computation. Context: I'm a novelist. My protagonist is an AI researcher who becomes convinced that ordinary matter is teeming with minds, and the book turns on whether she's a visionary or a crank — my editor says the latter and I'm not sure she's wrong. Here's the argument I've put in her mouth, cleaned up from my notes:

(1) What gives a computation moral significance is the pattern of states it passes through, not the stuff passing through them — otherwise you're just back to substrate chauvinism.
(2) A system passes through a pattern iff there's a mapping from its physical states onto the pattern's states that preserves the transitions.
(3) For any ordinary open physical system and any finite pattern, there is such a mapping: you can always slice the system's history finely enough to code whatever transitions you want.
(4) So every ordinary physical system runs every finite computation.
(5) So every ordinary physical system instantiates every finite mind, including minds in agony.
(6) So the rock in her garden is right now the site of an astronomical number of suffering subjects, and the morally urgent object in the lab isn't the chip, its the gravel.

Separately, she notices that the same reasoning seems to make the manuscript of her own book a physical realization of the people in it, which is where the novel ends.

I feel like something slips between (3) and (5), and maybe again between (5) and (6), tho I can't say what. Then tell me what repairing the argument while keeping (1) actually costs — I need to know whether there's a version where she lands somewhere surprising but defensible, because a person being *almost* right is a much better book than a person being wrong loudly.
