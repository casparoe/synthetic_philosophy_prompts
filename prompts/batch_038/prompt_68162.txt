I coordinate a community youth track club. Last week a parent bought a mail-order DNA test advertised as showing whether a child is built for sprinting or for distance, and has asked our board whether the club should use results like hers to place nine-year-olds in events. Three other families are considering the same test before spring registration closes, so the board has given me seventy-five minutes at the next parents' meeting: about twenty-eight adults, no statistics background, folding chairs and a whiteboard, and no budget beyond paper, pens, and two decks of index cards I can prepare myself.

Design the activity I should run. I want them to leave having felt, rather than been told, at least four things: that a heritability figure is a ratio of variances in one population under one range of environments, not a statement about any particular child; that the same trait can be almost entirely heritable in one environment and only modestly heritable in another, with nothing about the genes changing; that the genetic share of the variation they observe grows once players are allowed to choose their own training in response to what they already know about themselves; and that a high figure computed within a group licenses no inference whatsoever about a gap between two groups.

Please give me the card mechanics and scoring rules; how the rounds are ordered so that each of those four points emerges from play rather than from my narration, with the term itself withheld until the debrief; a payoff the parents genuinely care about (placement in a mock championship lineup at the end of the evening would work), since the choice round only bites if choosing well matters; a method for the group to derive a heritability estimate from their own scores using arithmetic twenty-eight non-mathematicians can complete in the room, with no software and preferably no squared deviations; a timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments onto each point and onto what the mail-order test can and cannot support; the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect small-sample noise to produce impossible numbers at some stage — and how to turn each failure into the lesson; and an honest statement of which feature of real heritability estimation the game cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply verbally.
