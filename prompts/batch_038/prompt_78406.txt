I'm a mathematics graduate student auditing my department's philosophy-of-mathematics reading group, and last term the group spent two sessions on Benacerraf's dilemma: the identification problem from "What Numbers Could Not Be" (1965) and the epistemological problem from "Mathematical Truth" (1973), together with Hartry Field's recasting of the second as a challenge about reliability rather than causal contact. To test the challenge, the group invented its own case, which everyone kept returning to: the Vel, a species of pure mathematicians with no perceptual access to anything and no applied mathematics whatsoever, who arrive by contemplation alone at exactly our arithmetic. Two members, Nadia (a logician with platonist sympathies) and Owen (a nominalist of Fieldian persuasion), argued about the Vel for the better part of an hour, and although I followed every technical step I could not tell you at any point who was ahead. They have asked me to circulate a reconstruction of the exchange to a referee, and since I'm the one who wrote it down, I've been volunteered. Here is the argument as it actually went.

(1) Owen opened by conceding that the 1973 version of the dilemma is dead on its own terms, since it presupposes a causal theory of knowledge that essentially no epistemologist holds today, but insisted that the challenge survives the swap: the question is not how we causally interact with numbers but why our belief-forming practice lines up as well as it does with the facts it purports to describe. On platonism numbers are causally inert, so nothing about them shaped the practice, and the match is a standing coincidence; on nominalism the sentences are useful fictions whose fit with our practice is explained by the fact that we authored both sides of the fit. (2) Nadia replied that the demand for an explanation is illegitimate in this domain: arithmetical truths are necessary, so in every world in which our mathematicians believe there are infinitely many primes, there are infinitely many primes, and a correlation that holds in every world is not a coincidence owing a causal story any more than the bachelor case is. (3) Owen said this proves far too much — if there were necessary truths governing the conduct of undetectable angels, any belief we formed about them would be "safe" in exactly her sense, and modally guaranteed correlation is not reliability. Reliability, he argued, is a property of a method, and a method can issue necessarily true beliefs while still being unreliable in the sense that matters: a slightly different rule would have issued different, equally safe-looking beliefs. What needs explaining is why the rule we in fact use is the one whose outputs are true. (4) Nadia answered that Owen had stopped arguing against platonism and started arguing against realism about necessity as such: the same challenge applies to logic (why do our beliefs about validity track the facts about validity?) and to the modal claims his own fictionalism requires, so if it generalizes it is not a targeted objection to abstract objects but a plea for quietism about necessity — companions in guilt. (5) Owen gave two replies: the generalization is not a reductio, because the nominalist can answer the reliability question about his own modal beliefs naturalistically, in terms of inferential dispositions and their usefulness, without contact with abstracta; and the challenge has a version that does not generalize, namely the identification problem, since even granting reliable beliefs about structure, platonism cannot say which objects our numerals denote, Zermelo's and von Neumann's reductions being equally adequate, so the unexplained coincidence is between our terms and their referents rather than between our beliefs and the truths. (6) Nadia said the identification problem is not an objection to platonism but its moral: Benacerraf's own 1965 conclusion was that numbers are positions in a structure rather than objects, and structuralism preserves mind-independence, objectivity and necessity while dissolving the reference question, since nothing in arithmetic turns on which objects fill the positions and any two structures of the relevant kind are isomorphic, leaving nothing to choose between. (7) Owen's last substantive move was that structuralism must say what its claims are about, and both options are bad: if the claim is that arithmetic holds of the unique structure of that kind, understood as a necessity about what structures could be instantiated, then Nadia has traded arithmetic for modal commitments and now owes precisely the epistemology of modality she was trying to dissolve, since modal facts are no more perceptible than numbers; if instead she reifies the structure as an abstract object, she is back at platonism with the original challenge intact. Her parting shot, which Owen did not get to answer before the session ended, was that the closing is not symmetric: her residual problem is how we know modal facts, whereas his is how he knows arithmetic is fiction rather than truth, and his answer must appeal to the authority of the very inferential practices that are in question.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step? For each of the seven moves, give a rating on a simple scale — clearly right, largely right, partly right, largely mistaken, clearly mistaken — with a short justification that identifies the specific claim in the move that earns the rating, since several moves contain both a good point and a bad one bundled together. Where a move is partly right, say which part survives. Then say who is more right overall, and whether the disagreement would resolve differently if one of them adopted a view neither defended here (I have in mind, for example, a platonist who simply denies that semantics must answer to epistemology at all). Separately, tell me whether the Vel are doing any philosophical work in this argument or whether they are decoration both sides are content to keep: state what the case is supposed to show and whether it in fact shows it. Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are missing — a live option the exchange never reaches, or a shared assumption that does most of the damage. I want a committed answer rather than a survey: take a side on each move, explain why, and if you think the whole framing is confused, say so and say what should replace it. Please do not hedge by listing considerations on both sides and leaving the judgment to me; I already have the two sides, which is why I'm asking.
