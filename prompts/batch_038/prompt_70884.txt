I am a second-year philosophy graduate student, and my qualifying paper is on the quarrel between Hobbes and the Oxford mathematicians in the 1650s and 1660s: Hobbes's De Corpore and its mathematical sections, Wallis's Elenchus geometriae Hobbianae, Hobbes's Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematics, and Wallis's reply. My supervisor has warned me twice against writing Whig history in which Wallis wins because calculus subsequently worked, and a historian in my seminar has accused me of anachronistically reading constructive scruples back into a dispute that was really about Hobbes's vanity and his terror of anything smacking of scholastic infinity. Partly to answer both of them, and partly because I could not stop noticing it, I have spent the last month keeping a notebook of my own verdicts about which demonstrations I actually accept, in the hope of extracting from them a standard I can state without any historical apparatus. The results are embarrassing. Euclid's very first proposition, the construction of the equilateral triangle, I accept without qualification, and I think the construction is doing the justificatory work rather than merely exhibiting the result. The familiar nonconstructive argument that there are irrational numbers a and b with a to the power b rational, via considering the root-two-to-the-root-two case, I accept as a proof, though I register a residue of dissatisfaction: I have been told that such a pair exists without learning which pair it is. Wallis's characteristic procedure in the Arithmetica Infinitorum, tabulating the ratio of the sum of the first n natural numbers to n squared for n equal to one, two, three, and onward, watching the values crowd toward one half, and then pronouncing the value for the infinite case to be exactly one half, I do not accept as a proof at all; I would call it a conjecture wearing the clothes of a demonstration. Yet the epsilon-and-delta definition of a limit, and everything proved from it, I accept completely, despite its quantifying over infinitely many positive quantities; and I accept Cantor's diagonal argument with no unease whatsoever, even though it concerns an uncountable totality that on Hobbes's view of the infinite ought to be unintelligible to me.

Here is where it gets uncomfortable. The computer-assisted proof of the four-color theorem I accept as establishing the theorem, but if you ask whether I have followed a proof I say no, and I describe my position as reasonable trust in testimony rather than knowledge by demonstration, which makes me feel like a fraud who is grading his own exam leniently. Nonstandard analysis, with its infinitesimals and its transfer principle, I accept as rigorous, although I reject the seventeenth-century infinitesimal reasoning that looks to me structurally similar; I cannot say what the difference consists in beyond a vague appeal to the fact that one of them comes with precise rules for when the reasoning is licensed. The picture of two stacked triangles that establishes the closed form for the sum of the first n natural numbers I accept as carrying the proof rather than merely suggesting it. But the picture of two curves that must cross, offered as a proof of the intermediate value theorem, I reject, because I think the eye is being asked to supply a completeness principle about the continuum that the drawing does not contain. And a conjecture in number theory confirmed by machine out to some astronomical bound I treat as barely supported at all; indeed large-scale verification makes me slightly less confident, since I keep thinking of the Mertens conjecture, checked to enormous ranges and eventually refuted.

My tentative principle is this: a demonstration gives me knowledge only where I could in principle retrace it myself, step by step, and only where those steps terminate in objects I have constructed or can survey. That principle is already in trouble, and I want you to tell me how badly. It seems to mispredict the computer-assisted case, which is a finite enumeration I could in principle retrace and in practice cannot; it seems to mispredict the epsilon-delta and diagonal cases, where I survey an infinite domain by grasping a single rule rather than by any stepwise retracing; and it seems to leave the Wallis case unexplained, since his table is a retraceable finite procedure with exactly one bad step at the end, and I would like the principle to locate the badness in that step rather than in the procedure. So please do the following. First, give me the principle, or if a single one cannot be had, a small set of candidate principles stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, and for each clause tell me which of the cases above forced it in. Second, tell me what your principle says about cases I have not listed: a consistency proof for arithmetic that proceeds by transfinite induction up to the first epsilon number; a theorem of ordinary analysis whose only known proof essentially invokes large cardinal axioms; an argument establishing that some property holds with probability one; and a proof whose validity depends on the consistency of the very system in which it is carried out. Third, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and what it would cost to save all of them, since I suspect that accommodating everything requires clauses so baroque that the resulting standard is no standard at all. Fourth, and this is what the paper turns on, tell me whether the principle that survives is Hobbes's, Wallis's, or neither: was their fight genuinely about what counts as a demonstration, or about something else, such as whether mathematics is knowledge of causes and constructions or a disciplined technique for manipulating marks we have ourselves appointed, and was Hobbes's conviction that infinite quantities were the thin end of a scholastic wedge a philosophical insight or a reflex of his politics?
