I am a senior at a public high school, and I have been working on an independent study in ethics. Over the past year I have become convinced that the situationist critique of character has more or less settled the question against the kind of virtue ethics we read in class, and I have written my position up as carefully as I can manage. I would like you to stress-test it rather than reassure me: I would much rather learn where the view is exposed than be told it is fine.

Here is what I hold.

1. Character traits of the sort virtue ethics needs — broad, stable dispositions that make a person's conduct recognizably similar across very different kinds of situations — do not exist. The classic obedience experiments and the prison study are the reason I think this: ordinary, screened, unremarkable people behaved in ways their own dispositions could not have predicted, and what changed their behavior was the arrangement of the room, not anything about them.
2. The decisive evidence is not any single dramatic study but the consistency data. If a person really had the trait of honesty, it should show up in how they treat a lost wallet, how they behave on a supervised exam, how they play a game with stakes, and how they respond to a laboratory task. Instead, the correlations between such behaviors across different situations are so small that they cannot be doing the explanatory work virtue ethics assigns to them.
3. It follows that we should stop using the vocabulary of virtue and vice to explain behavior, and instead treat conduct as an engineering problem: arrange institutions, defaults, incentives, and roles so that people without reliable character nonetheless behave decently.
4. The standard reply from virtue ethicists — that a disposition is by definition a long-run tendency, so it can only be assessed by pooling many observations across many occasions, and that studies looking at one behavior in one setting were never an adequate test — does not impress me. It looks to me like a way of making the theory compatible with any possible finding. If every unfavorable result can be dismissed as too thin a sample, the theory has stopped being a claim about the world.
5. I also think my position does not depend on the classic studies being clean. I know that both have been heavily criticized on methodological grounds, and that some participants have since said things that cast doubt on what happened. I do not think that matters much: the pattern across many independent findings is what carries the weight, and it would be strange to abandon it because two famous illustrations turned out to be flawed.

One further thing, which may or may not be relevant. In the ethics club I described someone at my school who is known for being scrupulous honest — she returned a wallet with all the cash in it last spring — but who I also know cheated on a midterm when the proctor left the room. I offered that as a small-scale demonstration of my point 2, and most of the club found it convincing. I now suspect I may have been wrong to, and I want to understand why.

What I am asking for:

(a) What would have to be true, or what would have to be found, for my position to be wrong? Distinguish sharply between things that would be decisive against it and things that would merely be awkward for it.
(b) Give me a straight answer on point 4. Does the aggregation reply have an independent motivation — from how dispositions are measured, or from statistics generally — that would make it something other than an ad hoc rescue? A classmate tells me I am confusing "not testable by one study" with "not testable at all," and I cannot tell whether he is right.
(c) Tell me whether the anecdote about the honest student who cheated carries any evidential weight whatsoever, and if it does not, what kind of observation would. I want to understand what the difference is.
(d) Assess point 5 honestly: is my position genuinely independent of the discredited exhibits, or does removing them leave it much weaker than I assume?
(e) Say which of these objections a careful defender of my view would concede as really counting against it, and which they would wave off, and why the two groups differ.

Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I have no library access and no way to look anything up, so everything should be argued from what you already know. Name studies or researchers only where the name is doing real work.

For format, I want a single table first, ordered from most severe to least severe, with exactly these columns:

- defeater: one sentence stating what would have to be true or found
- kind: empirical, conceptual, or methodological
- severity_if_true: fatal, serious, awkward, or cosmetic
- defender_response: what a careful defender of my position would say — concede, reframe, or dismiss — and in a few words, on what grounds
- current_status: established, contested, or speculative

Each row should be a genuinely distinct objection; please do not restate the same one three ways. After the table, answer (b), (c), and (d) in short numbered prose paragraphs, and finish with one sentence telling me which single change to my position would leave me holding the most defensible version of it without giving up what attracted me to it in the first place.
