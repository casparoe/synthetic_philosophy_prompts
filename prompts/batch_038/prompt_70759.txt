I write about science for a general-interest magazine and have become more interested in the Nobel rules than in the laureates. Two of them matter here: no prize may be shared by more than three individuals, and prizes are not awarded posthumously, unless the laureate dies between the announcement and the ceremony. Rosalind Franklin is the case I keep returning to — she died in 1958, and when the 1962 medicine prize for the structure of DNA went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins, both rules bit at once, the list of names already being full.

What conception of a discovery, and of the life a discovery belongs to, do those two rules jointly presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not labeled. Is it Plutarch's — that a career becomes intelligible once compressed into one life set beside others, which is why the Lives announce that they are concerned with lives rather than histories and dwell on remarks, jests, and small acts rather than campaigns — or is it merely administrative, a device for producing a short list and a photographable moment? Which rival accounts of scientific credit does the three-name cap actually exclude, and which is it only awkward alongside: could a genuinely collective theory of authorship be served by three names, or does the cap commit the committee to something stronger? And are the two rules even consistent with each other, when the posthumous bar makes a contribution's recognizability depend on how long its author happened to survive it? Finally, tell me what the institution would have to become if it adopted an openly hagiographic rationale — prizes as moral exemplars, in the Plutarchan sense — or an openly anti-hagiographic one, prizes attaching to bodies of work rather than to persons.
