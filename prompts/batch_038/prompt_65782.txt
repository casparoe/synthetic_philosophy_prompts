I work as a data architect for a unitary authority in the north of England, and for the past eighteen months I have been rebuilding the part of our land and property system that records parcels: the polygons, their identifiers, and the interests registered against them. What I did not expect is that the hardest questions are not computational but metaphysical, and that the literature on how objects persist — endurantism, perdurantism, and the stage-theoretic variant on which ordinary talk of a continuing thing is really talk of momentary stages linked by counterpart relations — bears directly on the semantics of a public register. Before I read any more of it, I want the specification laid out: what any theory of persistence would have to deliver in order to serve as the account of identity-over-time implicit in such a register.

Here is the material the theory has to cope with. Parcel 47 was a terrace of Victorian houses, compulsorily purchased in 1972, demolished, kept as open car park for some thirty years, and then rebuilt to roughly the original footprint under the original title number; our system currently asserts that the parcel existed continuously throughout, and a claimant's solicitor has written to say that cannot be right, since there was a stretch in which nothing of the sort existed at all. A tidal parcel on the foreshore gains and loses ground by accretion and erosion, so that no two surveys agree on its extent. A single freehold is subdivided into eleven leasehold flats, each with its own register entry, while the block itself continues to appear in our holdings data as one object; much the same goes for the airspace above and the subsoil below, which we track separately for minerals and for telecommunications wayleaves. Two adjoining parcels are merged, and later re-separated along a slightly different line. Boundary walls are rebuilt, survey monuments replaced, hedges moved, so that almost no physical marker present at the original conveyance survives today. And at every moment the authority must be able to state how many parcels there are in the borough, and what is held in each, without contradiction or double counting.

Against that background, give me the desiderata as a numbered list. For each: a one-sentence statement of the requirement; the case above, or another you construct, that motivates it; and a tag in square brackets saying whether it is non-negotiable, negotiable, or in truth a prejudice of one camp rather than a genuine constraint on any theory whatever. Draw the line explicitly between requirements that come from the metaphysics of persistence and requirements that come from the institution of a public register, since the second sort I can buy my way out of by changing the design and the first sort I cannot. Respect, and do not collapse, at least these distinctions: numerical versus qualitative identity; the object versus the region of space it occupies; the parcel versus the estate or interest held in it; conditions of persistence versus conditions of identity versus conditions for counting how many there are; and existence at a time versus existence across times. Say which results the theory must be consistent with, including whether identity can be vague or indeterminate, whether two distinct objects can coincide in the same place at the same time, and whether something can go out of existence and come back into it. Then tell me plainly whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — if one is, that is the finding I most want — and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Close with three or four lines at most on how endurantism, perdurantism and stage theory fare against the list, and flag any requirement that only one of them could ever meet. Name positions by their standard labels; I do not want references or a reading list. Begin with one sentence naming the requirement you take to be most exclusionary, and keep the whole answer under 900 words.
