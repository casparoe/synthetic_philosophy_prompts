I'm on the board of a small community foundation in a river town (pop. ~30k) and I've been handed the job of writing the recommendation on a decision that's eaten our last four meetings. We have about $4M, most of it from a single bequest, and there's a proposal to spend ~$3.5M buying out and demolishing roughly 120 houses in the low part of town that floods every decade or so. The people living there now are mostly in their 60s and 70s. The honest case for the buyout isn't really about them: it's that it protects houses that will be owned by people who aren't born yet, and it takes the whole emergency-services-and-relief cost off the town's books for the next fifty years.

Our finance guy ran it and says the buyout loses, don't build, invest instead. At his 6% the $3.5M is about $65M in 2075, which could fund a hundred buyouts, and a dollar of benefit in 2075 is worth about five cents today, so the project doesn't come close. One of the other board members called this monstrous — it means we've decided a kid in 2075 counts for a nickel. A third said the 6% only earns its keep if you assume the future is richer than the present, and that assuming that in a town whose main employer left in 2009 is begging exactly the question at issue. A fourth, and he's the one I can't shake, said nobody actually believes 6% holds in year 70: the finance guy's own number for a 30-year bond is 4%, and he proposed 3% for the first twenty years and 1% after, which brings the buyout to roughly break-even with investing.

Then there's the part that makes me think we're all having one argument while answering four different questions. This same board refuses to spend down principal, ever, "because we owe it to the generations after us," and last year we borrowed at 5% to redo the community center roof, which nobody called irresponsible. So we behave as if future people matter enormously when the question is whether to touch the endowment, and as if they matter about a twentieth of what we do when the question is whether to build something for them.

My own suspicion, which I can't argue for cleanly: the 6% is an answer to a question about what money can earn, and the "nickel" complaint is an answer to a question about whose wellbeing counts, and those come apart in a way we keep sliding over. But I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just me wanting the argument to stop. Also worth saying out loud — I'm 62, I won't see the payoff either way, and I probably have a stake in which of these turns out to be true.

What I want: tell me which of those four arguments is actually doing work and which are confused, whether my suspicion survives inspection or collapses, and — the part I need most — whether there's any principled way for us to pick the number at all, or whether the number is doing something other then what everyone on the board thinks it's doing. Then tell me what you'd put in the recommendation.

Don't give me an intro to discounting or a reading list; I've been reading around this stuff on and off for fifteen years and I'm offline in a hotel room regardless. Give me your actual confidence rather than a both-sides fog — if you think one of these people is just wrong, say so and say how wrong. And if you think I've stacked the setup, that there's no interesting problem here but a budgeting question, or that I've described the disagreement in a way that guarantees the answer I already want, say that plainly instead of playing along with my framing.
