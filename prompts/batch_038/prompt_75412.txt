I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether industrial animal agriculture counts as cruelty, and the argument went nowhere because we turned out to be using the word in incompatible ways. I was treating cruelty as a fact about the animal — how much suffering it undergoes, and whether that suffering was avoidable — while my friend kept insisting that cruelty is essentially a fact about the agent: it has to involve malice, or gratuitousness, or some departure from what the situation calls for, so that inflicting enormous pain as a routine, economically necessary, socially sanctioned part of one's work is not cruelty at all but simply husbandry. By the end I realized that our disagreement about the concept is not really settleable by argument between the two of us, but that there is an institution that has already taken a side, more or less explicitly, and I would like to understand which side it took and whether that side is coherent.

The institution is the criminal law of animal cruelty, and specifically a feature of it that I only noticed recently. In most American jurisdictions the general anti-cruelty statute — the one that makes it a crime to torture, maim, or kill an animal needlessly — is drafted so that it does not reach ordinary farming. The carve-out is not a list of permitted acts but a reference to practice: conduct that is customary, normal, or commonly accepted in agriculture, animal husbandry, or slaughter is placed outside the offense altogether. The consequence is that whether an act is cruel depends on who performs it and on what people in that line of work generally do. A trespasser who beats a dairy cow with a length of pipe commits a crime; a producer who inflicts the same injury as part of a standard handling or husbandry routine does not, and the reason is not that the injury is smaller or the purpose better, but that the injury is customary. Meanwhile the very same body of law protects dogs and cats with no comparable exemption, and it also outlawed organized dogfighting and cockfighting — practices that were long-established, deeply traditional rural customs with their own codes of sportsmanship — without pausing to ask whether they were customary in the communities that practiced them.

So my questions are about what this arrangement presupposes, and I would like them answered in that order.

First: reconstruct the implicit theory. What conception of cruelty must the customary-practice carve-out be assuming in order to make sense? Is it assuming that cruelty is a status conferred by deviation from the internal norms of a practice rather than a matter of how the animal fares — so that the standard is endogenous, set by the conduct of the people whose conduct it is meant to evaluate? Or is it assuming a two-tier view of moral standing, on which farm animals count for something but less than companion animals, with custom operating merely as the instrument for fixing the discount? Or is it assuming nothing normative about animals at all, and instead reflecting ordinary legal values — fair notice, the void-for-vagueness constraints on criminal statutes, reluctance to have prosecutors second-guess an entire industry's methods, deference to legislatures rather than courts on matters of production — values that would justify the carve-out even if one believed farm animals counted exactly as much as dogs?

Second: test a specific analogy I find both attractive and disturbing. Medical malpractice law defines the standard of care by reference to what the profession customarily does, and commercial law often fixes the content of a duty by reference to the custom of the trade. On that model, the farming exemption is just an application of a thoroughly ordinary legal technique, and the objection to it is the familiar objection that any group which gets to define its own standard of reasonable conduct has an obvious conflict of interest. Tell me whether that analogy genuinely holds — whether the two practices presuppose the same thing about the concepts they operationalize — or whether it breaks down in a philosophically important way, given that the malpractice and trade-custom standards are understood as evidence about what is in fact competent or reasonable, whereas the farming exemption seems to make custom constitutive rather than evidential, and given that the beneficiary of a duty of care is presumed to be a person whose interests the custom is supposed to track.

Third: separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. I suspect that this piece of drafting is philosophically underdetermined — that a welfarist who thinks only suffering matters, a contractarian who thinks animals are outside the moral community but that wanton cruelty corrupts the agent, and a pragmatist who simply distrusts criminal law as a tool for regulating production could all endorse the same statutory text for reasons that are mutually inconsistent. If so, say plainly which parts of the practice are licensed by several theories and which parts are licensed by only one, and identify anything in the arrangement that no plausible theory can accommodate — my candidate is the asymmetry with animal fighting, where custom was treated as irrelevant.

Fourth: tell me what would have to change, in the drafting and in the enforcement, if the presupposed view were replaced. Sketch concretely what the statute would look like under a purely suffering-based account on which the moral question is only how much and how avoidable, and then under an account on which animals have claims that no amount of social consensus can override — and say what each replacement would do to the role of custom, to the mens rea requirement, and to the question of whether the act of killing an animal for food can itself be within the statute's reach.

Finally, a request about how you should answer. I do not want a survey of positions with a balanced concluding paragraph noting that the issue is contested; I have had enough of that, from my friend and from everything else I have read on the subject. Commit. Tell me which reconstruction of the carve-out you think is correct, whether the endogeneity objection is fatal or whether the practice is defensible on purely legal grounds, whether the malpractice analogy survives your scrutiny, and which of the two positions in my argument — cruelty as a property of suffering, or cruelty as a property of the agent's relation to a practice — the law as it actually stands vindicates, if either. Give your reasons, and where you think a step in your own reasoning is shaky or rests on a contested reading of the law, flag it rather than smoothing it over.
