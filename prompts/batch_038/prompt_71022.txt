I am a fifth-year doctoral candidate in the history of science and, this academic year, the graduate representative on my department's undergraduate curriculum committee. At our meeting on Thursday the 14th we must dispose of the most contentious item on the agenda, and the chair has asked me to circulate a recommendation memo in advance so that we are not debating from scratch in the room. I would like you to draft that memo.

The item is a four-course required survey sequence in the history of science whose skeleton has been essentially unchanged for some thirty years: an ancient course, a medieval course, "The Renaissance," and "The Scientific Revolution, 1500–1750." The sequence satisfies a college-wide historical breadth requirement, and the four titles appear, in that form, in the catalog and on the advising sheet handed to first-years in September. The external reviewers in last spring's quinquennial report gave the sequence two full pages. Their complaint was not about the content — they singled out several weeks for praise — but about the scaffolding: that the course titles assert as settled a set of boundaries the department's own faculty have spent two decades contesting, and that a student who completes the sequence as designed will leave believing that something happened in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries which conspicuously did not happen in the twelfth, and that the difference is legible from the labels alone. The chair has told us that "no change" is no longer an available motion, because the dean's office will read the review back to us at the next budget cycle.

Four proposals are on the table. I will give them as their sponsors would.

Proposal A (an early-modernist): retitle only the fourth course, as "Early Modern Science, 1450–1750"; leave its content and the other three courses alone. Her argument is that the old label has hardened into a term of art with a determinate referent, that students and employers recognize it, and that a single department cannot repair the discipline's vocabulary from inside one catalog line.

Proposal B (two mid-career faculty): merge the third and fourth courses into one, "Print, Instruments, and the Making of the Early Modern World," and use the freed slot for a new course on the circulation of technique and natural knowledge across imperial and commercial networks.

Proposal C (the course's current instructor): keep the title precisely because it is the label students arrive with, and make the first fortnight of the course an interrogation of it — begin with the medieval, scholastic, and translation-movement antecedents the label suppresses, teach the argument that the period had no single origin of the kind the title implies, and set as the final assignment a historiographical essay either defending or prosecuting the term. His slogan is that the best place for a student to discover that a category is constructed is from inside the category, where she can also feel how convenient it is.

Proposal D (the medievalist, who is also my sharpest interlocutor): abandon the period scaffold entirely and replace the four surveys with three thematic courses — matter and measurement, bodies and populations, instruments and authority — each ranging across all periods, with period coverage guaranteed by cross-listing and team teaching.

Constraints and entanglements the memo must acknowledge rather than wave away:

- The registrar's code freeze for the coming catalog falls on 7 November. Anything adopted now takes effect next September at the earliest, and a retitle forces renumbering, which breaks the advising sheet for two cohorts mid-degree unless we legislate substitutions.
- Three faculty hold teaching commitments tied to the existing course codes; one of them holds an endowed chair whose description references the fourth course.
- A 1998 alumna gift endows an annual public lecture series whose founding instrument names it after that fourth course. Retitling the course cannot amend the endowment, but the flyer has always advertised the lecture as accompanying the course, and the development office has asked what we intend to do.
- Both graduate instructors who taught sections of the fourth course last year are covered by the local agreement's clause on notice before an assigned course is altered or discontinued; one of them sits on the bargaining unit's negotiating committee.
- A student newspaper column last month defended the old title on the ground that students need a map of the past, however crude, before anyone tells them the map is a fiction. It circulated widely, and the chair expects someone to raise it.

What I need from the memo:

1. A recommendation in the first paragraph: one of the four proposals, or a specific hybrid that you define precisely enough for a motion. Not a weighing of merits.
2. Reasons drawn from the substance of the dispute rather than its slogans. In particular, the memo should separate at least three claims that get run together in this argument — that a period label is retrospective and was coined by people living after the fact; that a label so coined therefore picks out nothing real; and that a label is politically load-bearing in ways that disqualify it from a required course title — and should say which of these the external reviewers' objection actually needs. It should likewise say which diagnosis of the fourth course's title you take the review to be pressing: a problem of continuity (what the label excludes at its leading edge), of scope (what it excludes at its margins), or of teleology (that the label is the opening move of an origin story about something called modernity, which the course then owes a definition of). These three diagnoses license different repairs, and the memo should say why the one you choose fits the remedy you recommend.
3. An honest statement of the strongest case against your recommendation. If you recommend B or D, this must engage the pedagogical point in the student column as a point about teaching rather than dismiss it as naive. If you recommend A or C, it must engage Proposal D's sponsor's consistency objection directly: that a department which deconstructs only the last label in a sequence implicitly certifies the first three, and that a partial retitling is a way of looking radical that costs nothing while entrenching the scaffold the review objected to.
4. An implementation plan with dates — what goes to the registrar and by when, how the two affected cohorts are handled, what we tell the development office about the endowed lecture, and what notice the graduate instructors receive.
5. A short paragraph sketching the first two weeks of the resulting course, week by week, because the committee asks this every year and will ask it again.

Register and length: 1,000–1,300 words, memo format with headings, readable in ten minutes by seven faculty members and one dean's observer. Formal but not stiff, and no throat-clearing about how difficult periodization is. Do not include a bibliography or works-cited list, and do not put anything in quotation marks except when quoting me or the proposals and constraints above; I want your account of the arguments, not your recollection of other people's wording. And do not assume anyone in the room accepts "periodization is a construct" as an argument: two colleagues regard it as a truism dressed up as a discovery, and the memo loses them the moment it leans on it.
