I produce a small podcast that sits between ancient philosophy and clinical psychology; our listeners are a mix of classics graduate students, practicing therapists, and generalists with long attention spans. Next month's guest is a philosopher of psychiatry — please leave her unnamed and don't invent biographical details — who has built a career on a deliberately unfashionable claim: that the discussion of wine and drinking parties in Book I of Plato's Laws is neither period color nor a deferential nod to Cretan and Spartan custom, but a general and still-defensible theory of character formation. Her reconstruction runs roughly as follows. The Athenian Stranger first imagines a drug that would induce fear and argues that any legislator would prize it as a safe, cheap test of courage: you could expose citizens to graded doses of frightening things and, by exhortation, reward, and the threat of public disgrace, habituate them to hold their ground. He then concedes that no such drug exists, but observes that wine does the reverse — the more a man drinks, the more cheerful and confident he becomes, until he is puffed up and licentious in speech and action. From this she draws her central principle, which she calls counter-pharmacological habituation: a substance whose characteristic effect is to weaken a capacity can, when administered in graded amounts under supervision, strengthen that capacity — here by placing people in the condition that most pushes them toward shamelessness and training them to feel the appropriate sort of fear inside it. She also insists the institution cannot function without the sober, wise, and imperturbable commander the Athenian demands of any drinking party, and she argues that this is where Plato anticipated the trained-sitter requirement in contemporary psychedelic-assisted therapy.

Write nine to eleven interview questions for her, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give; where two answers are equally likely, write the follow-up as an explicit fork with a branch for each. The sequence should escalate rather than merely enumerate, moving from exegetical pressure to falsifiability. I want at least one question on each of the following, and I want each to press where the position is genuinely vulnerable rather than where it is merely unusual.

1. Direction of effect. In the standard exposure or stress-inoculation model, the trainee is habituated by repeated contact with the feared stimulus; here the pharmacological agent produces the opposite of the target affect. So what is the drug actually doing — supplying an affect to be mastered, or only amplifying the drinker's existing dispositions so that they surface and can be corrected in public? These are different theories with different predictions, and she should have to choose.
2. The destroyed-faculty objection. Immediately after the puppet image — in which the golden leading-string of calculation is the cord we are supposed to pull along with, against the harder cords of pleasure and anger — the Athenian gets Cleinias to agree that strong drink intensifies pleasures and passions while opinion, memory, and thought quit the drinker entirely, leaving him in something like a young child's condition with little command of himself. But on Plato's own educational theory a young child is precisely the sort of being who cannot yet be trained in moderation, because calculation is not there to be engaged. How is training possible in a state defined by the absence of the faculty being trained? Anticipate her likely reply — that the point is to shape the pre-rational cords directly — and follow up by asking what then distinguishes the resulting disposition from the merely habituated boldness that both Plato and Aristotle refuse to honor with the name of courage.
3. Diagnosis versus formation. Much of Book I reads as a claim about discovery rather than transformation: the Athenian's strongest argument is that drinking with a man is a cheaper and safer way to learn his temper than entrusting him with money or with one's wife and children, and he concludes that knowledge of the natures of souls belongs to the political art. If the real payoff of the institution is information, her formative thesis is doing most of its work in the passages that least support it. Make her say which she thinks it is, and give a branch for each answer.
4. The supervisor paradox. The commander must already be sober, wise, and unperturbed. Does the institution produce the trait it supervises, or presuppose it? And who qualifies the qualifier? Follow up with the worry that a theory whose every application is shielded by a competent-supervision clause may be unfalsifiable, and ask her to restate the principle in a form that could be refuted.
5. State dependence. Capacities acquired in a chemically altered state often fail to transfer to the sober state. Ask her to explain, at the level of mechanism rather than citation, why a trained response should survive the drug's absence, and what evidence would show that it had.
6. The two wines. Book I treats wine as a training device for adults in their prime; Book II forbids wine to the young altogether until eighteen, allows it only in moderation until thirty, permits intoxication after forty at the feasts of Dionysus, and recasts wine as Dionysus's remedy for the bitterness of old age, loosening the elderly into song and participation. Is this one mechanism or two stapled together? If two, does counter-pharmacological habituation survive the loss of the second?
7. Modern boundary cases. Put a concrete list to her — a beta-blocker taken before a recital, propranolol administered during memory reconsolidation in PTSD treatment, a benzodiazepine taken before a first flight, microdosing for resilience, a psychedelic session with a sitter — and ask which are instances of her principle and which are not, and for the criterion that sorts them. Then press the authenticity objection: if a disposition requires the drug's presence, or was formed in the drug's shadow, is it the person's courage or the regimen's?
8. The civic load-bearing question. The Athenian's training operates through public exhortation, reward, and disgrace among companions, and the whole discussion is framed by common meals as preparation for war. Can the pharmacology be detached from the polis, or is the drug doing nothing that audience and shame could not do alone? If it is dispensable, what remains of Plato's contribution beyond the observation that low-stakes settings reveal character cheaply?
9. The irony reading. Some readers take the wine digression as playful, or as the Athenian testing his own interlocutors, noting that he introduces a drug that does not exist and then slides to one that does. What textual consideration would settle this, and how much of her position survives if the passage is ironic?
10. Close with falsifiability: not what would refute her reading of Plato, but what would refute the theory — the study she would run, the outcome she would measure given that the target is a dispositional trait rather than a symptom score, and the result that would make her abandon it.

Format the questions as a numbered list, each followed by a clearly labeled follow-up. Every question should be self-contained enough that a listener who has never opened the Laws can follow it, so identify the Athenian Stranger and note in passing that his interlocutors are a Cretan and a Spartan, and gloss any technical term on first use. Assume she has fielded the state-dependence and authenticity objections many times, and write those questions so that her standard reply does not dispose of them. No questions about what drew her to the subject, her teachers, or her writing process, and none that simply invite her to summarize the dialogue. Keep the empirical questions at the level of design and mechanism — do not ask her to name specific trials, report specific effect sizes, or supply references she would have to look up. Paraphrase Plato throughout; do not put any words of the dialogue in quotation marks.
