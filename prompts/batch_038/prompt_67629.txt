Question: State Jeffrey's rule of probability kinematics. Explain what problem the rule is meant to solve and how it differs from ordinary Bayesian conditionalization, and say whether ordinary conditionalization falls out as a special case of it. Finally, say what — if anything — the rule leaves unsettled.

Answer: Bayesian conditionalization says that when you become certain of some proposition E, you should set your new credence in any hypothesis H equal to your old conditional credence in H given E. Jeffrey's rule drops the requirement that E become certain. Suppose experience shifts your credences across a partition E1, ..., En without settling any of them. Then your new credence in H should be the weighted sum P_new(H) = the sum over i of P_old(H | Ei) times P_new(Ei). What changes is only the probability of the partition elements; every conditional P_old(H | Ei) stays exactly where it was. Jeffrey calls this the rigidity condition, and it is what makes the update determinate: once experience hands you the new probabilities for the Ei, the rule pins down the new credence in every other proposition you care about.

This is the right account of perceptual learning, because perception almost never delivers certainty. Looking at a sock under bad light I might move from .4/.6 to .7/.3 on its being red rather than green, and I certainly don't become certain of any proposition about my sensory state. Plain conditionalization has nothing to say about that case; probability kinematics handles it directly. And conditionalization falls out as a limiting case, because if experience takes P(E1) to 1 the sum collapses onto P_old(H | E1), which is just what conditionalization prescribes. So the two rules are not rivals.

The rule also underwrites the dynamic coherence arguments. Lewis showed that an agent who plans not to conditionalize can be sold a package of bets that guarantees her a loss, and the same argument goes through verbatim for Jeffrey updating: any departure from probability kinematics makes you Dutch-bookable, whatever the source of the change in your credences. So the rule is not a mere technical relaxation of the old one, it is forced on you by coherence alone.

Two further payoffs. First, since no level of the hierarchy ever needs certainty, the rule strips the last vestige of foundationalism out of Bayesianism — the inputs are just more probabilities, all the way down, and that is a feature rather than a bug. Second, it dissolves the problem of old evidence. Glymour's worry is that a theory cannot be confirmed by evidence you already had, since conditionalization only fires on things that become certain; but probability kinematics lets your credence in a proposition you were already sure of move again, so re-examining a consequence you already knew can now raise P(H) after all.

The main thing the rule leaves open is which partition to use, since different partitions over the same experience can produce different posteriors. Jeffrey's answer is that you should use the partition experience itself imposes, and that seems right to me, so the openness is more apparent than real.
