I work on tooling for an internal model-monitoring harness, and I don't have the philosophy vocabulary for what I need, so bear with me — this ended up being a longer ask than I expected.

Setup: we have a thing that watches a deployed model for "the mechanism that used to solve this task has changed in a way that looks deliberate." Concretely, we keep a reference library of features/circuits extracted from the base checkpoint (crosscoder-style joint dictionary trained over base + current weights), and at every new checkpoint we try to match the current computation on a fixed probe set against that library. If the match fails or drops under a cosine threshold we raise a flag for a human. The problem is nobody on the team can agree on what "match" is even supposed to mean, and our flags are either twitchy (a re-seed trips them) or useless. Cases that break us: (1) continued pretraining shuffles which neurons implement the circuit but the counterfactual profile on the probes is identical — geometry says different, behavior says same; (2) the same feature direction keeps firing but its selectivity has broadened, so it now also lights up on inputs it used to ignore; (3) the circuit still computes the same thing internally but its output gets routed to a different head that uses it differently downstream; (4) the model has started solving the probe partly in its chain-of-thought text, so the "same mechanism" is now smeared across weights and tokens and our detector only sees the weights; (5) it gets the right answer through what looks like a memorized lookup path instead of the general algorithm, and falls over on out-of-distribution probes; (6) it's 97% similar and indistinguishable on our eval set but diverges on held-out inputs. Me and one of the researchers spent an hour arguing about (6) and realized we were using "same mechanism" to mean two totally different things and neither of us could say which one the spec wanted.

Deliverable: a taxonomy / decision tree of the positions on "when is it the same mechanism," sized for a ~2 page handout to a mixed audience (two interpretability people, four engineers, nobody with formal philosophy training). I want 6–8 branch questions, each one a concrete scenario in the spirit of the cases above with two or three answer options, phrased so an engineer can answer it without knowing where each answer leads — please don't let any question just be "do you think mechanisms are defined by their function or by their implementation?" Then a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions. The leaves I know I want: strict weight/neuron identity; causal-role identity in the multiple-realizability sense; interventionist identity (same mechanism iff ablating it breaks the same behaviors); informational/geometric identity (same feature direction in a shared basis); historical or lineage identity (same mechanism iff it descends continuously from the same trained circuit, regardless of what it does now); interest-relative identity (no fact of the matter independent of which property you're monitoring); and the deflationary leaf where mechanism just isn't a natural kind and only behavioral equivalence claims are real. Add any leaf you think I'm missing, including one for "identity is graded, not all-or-nothing" if you think that's actually a position and not a dodge.

The angle that matters to me: build the tree out of distinctions that could change what our detector actually flags on cases (1)–(6), not out of general metaphysics. So for each leaf, tell me what the corresponding detector criterion would look like in practice and which of those six cases it fires on or stays silent about. Also mark the empty cells of the logical space — e.g. is there a coherent view holding that weight identity is necessary but functional equivalence isn't sufficient? — and for each empty cell say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really separate; I suspect causal-role and interventionist identity might only come apart in weird setups, and if so I'd rather know that than ship a fake distinction in the handout.
