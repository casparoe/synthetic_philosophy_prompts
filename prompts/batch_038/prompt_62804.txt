"Multiple realisability shows that mental states cannot be identical with brain states." Explain the argument behind this claim, and discuss whether it also undermines functionalism.

Here is the script in full:

Putnam's argument starts from a simple observation about identity. If pain is identical with some neural state, say the firing of C-fibres, then that identity has to hold wherever there is pain, because identity is a one-to-one relation and nothing can be identical with two different things. But the range of creatures that plausibly feel pain is enormous: mammals, birds, reptiles, and even molluscs such as the octopus, whose nervous system is organised on completely different lines from ours. There is no reason to expect a single physical-chemical kind to run through all of these, so the identity theory asks for a common neural correlate that biology gives us no grounds to posit. Putnam then notes that the identity theorists of his time treated the identity as contingent but nomologically necessary, which means any physically possible pain-bearer would have to have that state as well; and since we can perfectly well describe a silicon-based creature, or a Martian with green slime pulsating where its brain would be, whose behaviour and inner causal organisation match ours, the theory is not merely unsupported but almost certainly false.

Functionalism is normally presented as the way out, and correctly so. Instead of identifying pain with a physical kind, it identifies pain with a causal role: the state typically caused by damage and producing wincing, distraction and a desire for it to stop, where the realiser of that role is left open. Pain on this view is a second-order property, the property of having some first-order state or other that plays the role, and that is precisely why multiple realisability is an advantage for functionalism rather than a threat to it. The very feature that defeated the identity theory is built into the functionalist definition.

Two further consequences follow. First, the argument does not stop at type identity. If my pain at this moment could equally well have been a state in a silicon circuit, then it is not identical with the particular firing in my brain either, since nothing can be identical with something it might have failed to be identical with. Putnam's reasoning therefore refutes token identity too, and that is the more damaging result, because token identity was supposed to be the safe fallback for the physicalist. Second, functionalism does not in fact escape unharmed. Block's Chinese Nation asks us to imagine the population of China organised so that each person plays the part of a single neuron, communicating by radio and collectively instantiating the functional organisation of a mind hooked up to a body. Functionalism is obliged to say that this system has pains and beliefs, which shows that the theory is chauvinist: it credits mentality to something that plainly has none. The objection generalises, since any theory that defines mental states purely by their causal profile will be committed to minds in places where there are none.
