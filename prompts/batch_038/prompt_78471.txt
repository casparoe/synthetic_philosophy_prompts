I am an applied machine learning engineer — six years building and shipping production models, mostly tabular forecasting and risk scoring — with no philosophy training beyond what I have picked up on my own. I have been reading Milton Friedman's 1953 methodological essay on positive economics and the controversy around it, and I want a serious translation of that controversy into the vocabulary I actually work in, followed by an accounting of what the translation loses.

Here is the position, stated as I understand it, so you do not have to guess at my source material. Friedman argues that the assumptions of an economic theory should not be tested for descriptive realism; the only relevant question is whether the theory yields sufficiently accurate predictions about the phenomena it was designed to handle, and a criticism that its assumptions are unrealistic is therefore empty unless it is cashed out as a demonstrated predictive failure. He goes further, in the step that gave the whole argument its derisive name from Paul Samuelson: the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be, because a genuinely important theory isolates a few crucial elements and deliberately omits a mass of complicating detail, so that it is a wildly inaccurate description of the world in exchange for being a good prediction engine. Related to this is the "as if" doctrine: firms behave as though they had solved the relevant marginal calculations, and what managers report about their own decision procedures is beside the point. The standard criticism, which Samuelson pressed, is that this arrangement is too cheap: if assumptions are insulated from evidence and the theorist is free to say which phenomena the theory was aimed at after seeing the results, then predictive success can always be had by selecting the target.

The vocabulary I want this rendered into is the one I use daily: training, validation, and held-out test splits; evaluation metrics chosen in advance; regularization, model capacity, and the bias–variance tradeoff; ablation studies; toy environments and synthetic data; distribution shift and out-of-distribution generalization; spurious correlations, shortcut learning, and the phenomenon of a model that performs well for reasons unrelated to what we think it has learned; calibration; benchmark saturation; feature-attribution methods and mechanistic interpretability as attempts to say what a model has actually latched onto.

Do the translation claim by claim — the anti-realism-of-assumptions thesis, the stronger twist, the "as if" move, and Samuelson's cherry-picking objection — and for each say what maps cleanly, what maps only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. Be concrete about the distortions rather than gesturing at them. In particular I want your honest answer to three questions. First: does "predictive accuracy" in Friedman's criterion have a counterpart in my framework, given that in machine learning accuracy is meaningless until a data-generating distribution and a metric have been fixed, and Friedman never specifies an evaluation set — and is that silence a gap in his view or a gap in mine? Second: is "realism of assumptions" best understood as interpretability, as correspondence between a model's internal structure and real causal structure, or as robustness under distribution shift, and does having a proxy for a concept count as having the concept? Third: is shortcut learning the empirical vindication of Samuelson's objection, or does it belong to a different argument entirely?

Then give me the residue report and a verdict. Where the F-twist refuses to translate, is that because my vocabulary is genuinely missing a concept, or because the economists are disputing something less substantive than it appears — a matter of rhetorical posture about what counts as a good theory?

Do not smuggle in assumptions from my framework and then credit them to Friedman as translation: if a step of the argument only works because I have silently imported the idea of a fixed test distribution, or the idea that a model's success is measured against a pre-registered metric, say so explicitly and mark it as your addition rather than his claim.
