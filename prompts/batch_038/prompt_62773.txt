I work in infrastructure engineering, and outside of work I've been reading about forecasting — the Tetlock-flavored material on base rates and the outside view, along with the recurring argument that a hegemon should be expected to follow the lifespan distribution of past great powers. The immediate occasion for my question is an argument with a friend who listens to a lot of history podcasts: he cited a figure for the median lifespan of great powers, and when I asked which great powers the median was taken over, and what counted as one, he couldn't say. That is when I realized my objection was not about the number but about the whole move, and that I could not state the objection rigorously. So I would like you to build the case for me, as strongly and as specifically as you can.

The position I want you to argue for is this: applying reference-class forecasting to civilizational-scale targets is not merely difficult or error-prone but structurally illegitimate — the warrant that makes the outside view trustworthy in ordinary use runs out at precisely the scale where people are most tempted to invoke it. My intuition is that the argument has at least three load-bearing components, and I want each developed, with an explicit statement of which one does the most work. (1) Sampling: a base rate is informative only if the target and the class members are draws from the same process — call it exchangeability, or whatever the right term is — but the class of past empires is assembled after the fact out of whatever left durable records, so the criterion for class membership is partly a function of the outcome being predicted. (2) Label sensitivity: at fine granularity there are too few members to support a distribution, and at coarse granularity the class is defined so loosely that the base rate becomes a free parameter you can move by relabeling — and a rule whose verdict you can flip by changing the labels is not tracking anything. (3) Reflexivity: unlike a bridge, a hard-drive fleet, or a server rack, the target can read the forecast and adjust.

Please argue the position on its merits rather than surveying who holds it. I know there is a literature on the reference-class problem and on survivorship bias, and you are welcome to use those concepts, but I do not want a literature review, a list of names, or recommendations of what to read — I want the argument itself, with the premises stated so that I can see which ones are doing the work and which could be denied. Include the best reply a sophisticated defender of the outside view would make, and give me your assessment of whether that reply succeeds. Do not sandbag the thesis to make it more comfortable: I would rather see the ambitious version together with a clear account of where it fails than a hedged version I already agree with.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the conclusion you have just argued for. Which piece of evidence, argument, or reframing would lead you to withdraw it or to narrow it substantially, and where do you think the reasoning above is most likely to break? I am not looking for a general caveat about complexity — point at the specific premise you would bet least on, and say why.
