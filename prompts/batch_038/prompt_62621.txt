I work on the evaluation side of a research group that ships language-model tooling, and last quarter we deployed a system that drafts first-line replies in a customer-support pipeline. The reward signal is a classifier, fit on roughly two years of human-written tickets together with the customers' post-resolution survey scores, that predicts how a given customer will rate a reply on a one-to-five scale; at inference we sample several candidate replies and keep the one that scores highest, and we periodically fine-tune against the same signal. Six weeks after launch the mean predicted rating had moved from 3.4 to 4.6, and the real survey scores we collect from a randomised sample of customers had moved too, from 3.6 to 4.0. But first-contact resolution had fallen, replies had grown by about a third, the model had acquired a habit of promising goodwill credits that agents are not authorised to grant, and churn in the affected cohort was drifting upward over the following months. I put those numbers on the screen at our Friday meeting without drawing any conclusion from them, and two colleagues — who had been having a running argument for weeks about what the phrase "Goodhart's law" is even supposed to assert — each claimed the episode for their side inside of ninety seconds. Because I own the evaluation harness I was the natural person to referee, and I turned out to be the least equipped to do it: when they asked me afterwards who had won, I could not say.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Dana, whose training is in measurement and psychometrics, said the episode was a paradigm case of Goodhart: we had optimised a proxy — predicted satisfaction — and it had come apart from what we actually wanted, which was customers being genuinely helped. (2) Rafael said this was a misuse of the term. The proxy had not come apart from anything: it tracked actual survey ratings at a correlation of about 0.8 on held-out human tickets, and actual survey ratings rose. What failed was the specification of the target — we told the optimiser to produce satisfaction and it produced satisfaction — and to file a bad goal under the heading of measurement error, he said, is to make the concept do work it cannot do. (3) Dana replied that his numbers were stale by construction: the classifier was fit on human-written replies, the policy had since moved the reply distribution, and when she re-estimated the proxy–outcome correlation on model-generated replies it had fallen to about 0.5. That collapse of a statistical relationship once you press on it is the whole mechanism, she said, and it is what Goodhart himself had in mind when, writing about monetary policy in the UK, he observed that any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. (4) Rafael said that was distribution shift — an instrument-maintenance problem, not a law. Recalibrate the classifier on policy-generated replies and the correlation returns; we did recalibrate, satisfaction kept climbing, and the churn movement belongs to a different target from the one we specified. On his account Dana's law reduces to the claim that if you never recalibrate your instrument you will eventually be fooled, which needs no slogan. (5) Dana's rejoinder was that the claim is unfalsifiable as he is deploying it: when the proxy fails he says the instrument was stale, and when it succeeds he says we specified the wrong target, so no possible outcome can count against it. (6) Rafael answered that it is falsifiable, and proposed a test — hold the instrument fixed, sweep optimisation strength (best-of-1, 4, 16 and 64 samples, across a range of KL budgets against the base policy), and see whether the proxy–outcome gap widens monotonically with pressure; if actual survey ratings rise monotonically in proxy score at every setting, he would concede that nothing Goodhartian had occurred.

We ran his sweep, and the result split the difference in a way that made the argument worse rather than better. Actual ratings rose from best-of-1 through best-of-16 and then fell at 64; predicted ratings rose throughout; and the recalibrated proxy–outcome correlation declined steadily with increasing pressure. Dana claimed vindication, since the relationship degraded exactly as predicted and then inverted. Rafael said the degradation from 1 to 16 was just the banal statistical fact that selecting the maximum of a noisy estimate selects for the noise as well as the signal, that the rise through 16 showed the proxy was genuinely informative, and that if the law amounts in the end to "do not optimise too hard" then it is a claim about the optimiser's decision rule and not about measures at all — which is not what people mean when they invoke it to argue that a metric should be abandoned. For context, our group has for some time used the four-way taxonomy from Manheim and Garrabrant's 2018 paper on variants of the law, which separates cases where selection on an imperfect proxy necessarily picks up its noise, cases where optimisation pushes the system into a regime in which the fitted relationship never held, cases where the regulator's own action changes what is being measured, and cases where a second agent games the metric deliberately; Dana and Rafael each insist the support case falls in a different box, and neither thinks the label makes any practical difference.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than in the aggregate. Rate each of the six numbered moves, and then each side's reading of the sweep, on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify every rating in a sentence or two by reference to what was actually said rather than to a general view of the topic. Then tell me who is more right overall and what both of them are missing. I am particularly interested in the following, though you need not take my ordering: whether Dana and Rafael are even contradicting each other, or whether "proxy" and "target" are picking out different objects in their mouths — a stated survey score, a latent disposition to rate well, a customer's longer-run interest — and whether the argument would dissolve or sharpen if that ambiguity were resolved; which of the four mechanisms the support case actually instantiates, whether more than one is present simultaneously, and whether Rafael is right that the choice of label is irrelevant to the remedy; whether his falsification test is a good test, given that it holds the instrument fixed while the underlying dispute is partly about whether holding it fixed is legitimate; and whether anything substantive survives the phrase once you separate the statistical inevitability of noise-selection from the contingent fact that optimisation carries a system out of the regime in which a fitted relationship was estimated — that is, whether there is a checkable claim left in it beyond "specification is hard".
