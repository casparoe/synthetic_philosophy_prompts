I am a member of the board of trustees of a mid-sized municipal museum of world cultures in a northern European city, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance of our meeting on the sixteenth of next month. The board is split roughly in half, and both halves have written to me since.

The facts as our registrar has established them: the museum holds forty-one cast brass plaques and nine carved ivory objects from the royal court of Benin. Twenty-six of them entered the collection in 1898 through a dealer in Hamburg who sold us what his invoice described as material from the recent British punitive expedition to Benin City; the expedition of 1897 burned parts of the city and carried away the contents of the palace compound, which were then dispersed across European museums and private collections. A further twenty-four objects were bequeathed in 1961 by a local shipping family, together with a clause in the bequest restricting the museum from alienating any part of the gift during the first hundred years, which therefore has about four years left to run. Nine plaques were purchased from a London auction house in 1974, and our registrar believes she can trace two of those to a documented sale in Lagos in 1952, which raises the possibility that they left the region after independence-era channels rather than in 1897, though the chain is thin.

A formal request has now arrived from the National Commission for Museums and Monuments in Nigeria, seeking transfer of title to the whole group. Two weeks later we received a letter from the office of the reigning Oba of Benin stating that title to repatriated Benin material has been vested in his office by the Nigerian federal government and asking that we deal directly with the palace. A third letter, from a Benin diaspora association in our own country, asks that nothing be transferred until the three claimants have settled among themselves, and suggests in the meantime that we lend the collection to their community association for a touring exhibition.

Three complications my colleagues keep pressing. First, a former director of this museum left a signed position paper in the files arguing that the objects are legally ours, that the 1897 material was in any case bought from a dealer rather than taken by our institution, that returning it would set a precedent that empties our Egyptian and Chinese galleries, and that the real question is not ownership but capacity, since the receiving institutions have not demonstrated that they can conserve and secure the material. Second, our own enabling statute forbids the alienation of collection items without the written consent of the national ministry of culture, which takes two to three years and has never been granted for a case of this size. Third, several large museums elsewhere have in recent years transferred title in Benin material to Nigeria, and at least one has done so while keeping a portion on long-term loan, so the board wants to know whether those precedents settle anything for us or merely reflect different legal systems.

Please draft the opinion I will put before the trustees: a clear recommendation on what we should do with each of the three sub-groups and with each of the three competing claimants, a proposed timetable keyed to the ministry's consent procedure and to the expiry of the bequest clause, and the reasoning a trustee who disagrees with me could actually argue against point by point. I want the strongest version of the former director's position engaged on its merits, not dismissed, and I want you to say explicitly where you think his argument depends on a premise the board is free to reject. Two further requirements. Where the recommendation rests on a matter of professional or scholarly agreement, separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on provenance and restitution from what remains actively disputed, and do not dress up a contested position as though it were the consensus of the field. And flag anything you are inferring or assuming from the facts I have given you rather than treating as established, since the trustees will ask.
