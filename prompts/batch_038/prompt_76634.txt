I write about science and ideas for a monthly magazine, and I am currently reporting a long piece on turnout — specifically on why the claim that voting is irrational has survived being announced as settled more or less every decade since Condorcet noticed that a single voter's influence shrinks as the electorate grows. The political scientist I interviewed last week waved the whole problem away in about ninety seconds, and my editor has now asked me for something better than another paragraph of abstraction about probabilities and benefit differentials: one clean, concrete case that can go in the article and then be put, unchanged, to the economists and philosophers I still have to interview. So I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specification, and then demonstrate that it actually meets the specification.

The case should be set in a real, contemporary, small-scale British election — a parish council by-election or a town referendum will do — with an electorate of a few hundred and a protagonist who is an ordinary person facing ordinary costs: a bus fare, half a day off work, a queue in a drafty village hall. No perfect predictors, no superintelligent campaign managers, no exotic physics; her beliefs about how close the race is should come from ordinary sources, such as a canvass return printed in the local paper and her own count of the doorsteps along her street. The engine of the case is that three accounts of electoral rationality must give three *different recommendations*, not merely three different reasons for the same recommendation. First, the self-regarding Downsian calculus: given what she personally stands to gain or lose, staying home is the rational choice. Second, a social-regarding revision of that same calculus, on which the benefit term counts what the better outcome is worth to everyone in the electorate rather than only to her: here voting is rational, and rational for the candidate who would actually produce the better outcome for the town. Third, the expressive account associated with Geoffrey Brennan and Loren Lomasky, on which a vote is consumed rather than invested, more like cheering for a team than buying a share: here voting is rational too, but for the other candidate, the one whose support expresses something she values. Two further constraints matter to me. The expressive candidate must be the worse one for the town, but not cartoonishly so — no monsters, nothing that makes the choice trivial by inspection; the gap should be one a reasonable person could describe as a genuine loss. And she must have no freestanding sense of civic duty, so that the duty term in the Riker and Ordeshook version of the calculus is unavailable as an escape hatch; tell me briefly, in the scenario, what it is about her that closes that door. Finally, and most importantly, she must know that the contest is expected to finish within a vote or two, so that her subjective probability of being pivotal is not negligible — give me an approximate figure and keep it plausible, and give approximate figures too for her private benefit differential, the town-wide benefit differential, the cost of voting and the expressive differential, so that each verdict can be checked against the arithmetic rather than against the way the story is told.

What I need back, in British English throughout, is the scenario itself in no more than about two hundred words, followed by roughly seven hundred words of walkthrough. In the walkthrough, take the three accounts in turn and state for each what it recommends — vote or abstain, and for whom — showing the reasoning and the numbers rather than asserting the conclusion. Then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work; my guess is that it is the combination of a non-negligible pivotality probability with her *knowledge* of it, which ought to break the analogy between cheering and voting, but I would rather you said so if the real load-bearing feature is something else, such as the way the expressive candidate is described. Then remove that feature — scale the electorate up to a national general election, holding everything else as fixed as you can — and show whether the three accounts converge again, and on what. Then give me the best available attack on the case from whichever account comes out worst, stated as strongly as its defender could state it: that expressive utility is not in fact contaminated by knowing one's vote will decide the result; that expression can be bought elsewhere — a badge, a canvass, a donation — so the ballot remains the cheapest outlet; that a voter who sincerely believes herself pivotal has thereby revised her estimate of the tie; or whatever you think is the sharpest objection. Close with two or three sentences on what, if anything, the case suggests about whether the so-called paradox of voting is a paradox about rationality at all, or a paradox about what we are prepared to count as a benefit. Please do not send me references, a bibliography or a reading list — I want the positions and their logic, not page numbers, and naming a school of thought is quite enough.
