I am a novelist working on a third collection of stories — provisionally titled after a piece of office furniture — in which the recurring subject is institutions that run on inference: cadastral bureaus, exam boards, tribunals, standards committees. One story keeps failing me, and I suspect it fails because I have been trying to be clever rather than exact. The story is meant to dramatize the problem in Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" — the discovery that a party who accepts the premises and even accepts the conditional linking them to the conclusion can still refuse to be moved, and that writing further conditionals into the rulebook only lengthens the list. I have no interest in a retelling, in reptiles, in footraces, or in a philosopher appearing as a character. What I want is an original story, set in a world you invent, in which the regress is a matter of procedure and paperwork and therefore has human consequences. Here is my seed, which you may reshape but should not abandon: a small archipelago jurisdiction whose civil code requires that any decision transferring land be accompanied by a chain of license, each link of which cites the standing clause that authorizes the move from the previous link. A junior clerk, Ilse Vahn, twenty-six, is assigned a petition that has lain undisturbed for forty-one years. An old man named Odras Tull declines to accept the conclusion of a chain that conveyed his family's shore land away, on the ground that the final link was entered by a clerk who wrote the connective and cited nothing. Tull is not being difficult for its own sake — he was himself a clerk in that office, he keeps a notebook in which he has copied out by hand every clause ever added to the code in response to him, and the notebook is nearly full. He is asking a question about who is answerable for a move that was never authorized.

The conceptual machinery must be right, because a philosophically literate reader will be reading over my shoulder, and the machinery must be carried entirely by scene, procedure, and dialogue about procedure. I want at least two rounds in which the office drafts a new clause specifically to close Tull's gap and the drafting itself opens a further gap, so that the reader feels the shape of the ascent rather than being told about it; I want the code to contain, visibly, a clause of the form "if these premises hold, that conclusion follows," sitting inert among the others, and I want the story to make the reader notice that possessing such a clause is not the same thing as anyone moving anywhere. No character may say the word regress, and no narration may explain the logic in essayistic terms. Ilse's own competence should outrun her ability to cite it: she can produce a hundred warranted chains from memory and cannot tell you which clause first licensed her hand to write the connective, because she was taught to write it before she was taught any clause at all. That fact about her training should do real work in the plot, not merely decorate it.

Length should be between eighteen hundred and twenty-six hundred words, in close third person on Ilse, with a formal, slightly bureaucratic register that permits itself one or two images of real beauty — the shore land itself, the notebook, the smell of the archive. The ending must choose an exit and pay for it on the page. The available exits, as I understand them, are roughly these: the chain terminates because a rule inside a system is not itself a premise anyone must accept; or because inference is something people do rather than something any formula represents; or because the office is entitled, by default, to trust the first step without showing it, and that entitlement is a grant rather than a discovery; or because Tull is simply right and the chain was never completed and the jurisdiction has been resting for four decades on an unlicensed move that everybody has agreed not to look at. Whichever you take, I do not want the shrug version — I do not want "logic is a social convention" delivered as a shrug. If the story lands on entitlement or practice, it must show what that costs the one person who was never granted the entitlement. Finally, after the story, give me a note of no more than two hundred words, written for me and not for a journal, saying which exit you took, what you had to suppress or soften to take it, and which single scene is carrying the conceptual weight — so that I can tell whether you chose it or whether it merely happened. No citations, no reading list, no names of philosophers.
