Six months ago I was in the field complaining about interpretive overreach in other people's papers. Now I'm the one writing the wall text. I took a job at a small regional museum doing collections and interpretation, and my first task is rewriting the labels in the prehistory gallery, which went up in the nineties and are, uh, confident. The old label on the case of bronze bits tells visitors that the people of this valley worshipped a warlike sun god. I want to delete that sentence. But going through it object by object, deciding what I'm actually willing to say, my gut turns out not to be consistent, and I'd like to know if there's a real principle hiding in there.

Here's my verdicts.

1. A clay pot with a hole punched clean through the base, in a child's grave, otherwise undamaged. I'm happy to write intentionally broken, probably as part of the burial. I'll say ritual.
2. A hoard of bronze ornaments and two swords, one sword bent, buried in a pit with no grave. I want to write deliberately deposited, possibly after being taken out of use, and I will not write offering.
3. Stone weights from six sites across maybe 200 years that come in a repeating series of ratios. I'll write that this implies someone was holding standards over a wide area for a long time — a real institution, with authority behind it.
4. One incised motif, a zigzag inside a double circle, on pottery from four different valleys over the same period. I won't write shared religion, or the same god anywhere. At most: a style that travelled.
5. Wall painting where one figure is twice the height of the rest. I'll write that the bigger figure matters more and that this society ranked people. Second scene, same building, where that same figure is drawn normal size next to a bigger one — now I won't say anything about rank at all, just that size varies with the scene.
6. Charred grain in a stone-lined pit beside a building, with the remains of maybe sixty animals and no everyday rubbish anywhere near it. I'll call that a feast and say it was probably a communal occasion. Identical charred grain in a midden behind houses: garbage.
7. A coin in the mouth of a skull. I'll write a rite, and I'll say it points to a practice known from later written sources in the same region. But I refuse to name the practice or the god.
8. A row of standing stones that roughly lines up with solstice sunrise. I'll write aligned to the solstice. I won't write calendar and I definately won't write observatory.
9. A mass grave, bodies piled, several with fractures around the time of death and points still in the bone. I'll write violence, probably a massacre. No hedging.

The obvious story is that I allow the inference when the object is weird, when ordinary life doesn't explain it, and block it when there's a plausible boring explanation. But 3 and 4 break that. Both are regularities spread across space and time; I let one of them carry an institution (someone enforcing weights) and I won't let the other carry a belief system (people sharing a god), and I cant tell you why those two feel different. Same problem with 1 and 2: broken things put in the ground, one gets ritual and one gets possibly. And 2 versus 7: both single objects, both with a good comparative analogue, but I'll name a rite in one and not the other.

My rough working principle, which I know isn't right: I attribute belief or intent when at least two independent lines license the same conclusion, and I'll attribute function or institution off a single line. That gets 6 (grain plus animals plus absence of trash), gets 9 (fractures plus points plus how the bodies lie), maybe gets 3. It doesn't get 7 or 1, which are one object each. And it makes 4 come out too strong, since the motif is on dozens of pots from four valleys and I still won't say a word about religion.

So — is there a principle, or two or three candidate principles, that gets most of these? I want them stated precisely enough that I could apply one without consulting my stomach: tell me what each clause is for and which of my cases it exists to handle, and be straight with me about which verdicts they can't accommodate and would force me to give up. I'd rather lose 5 than lose 9, for what it's worth, but tell me if that's backwards.

Then run them on four things I haven't had to decide yet, because I do have to write labels for all of them: a building three times the footprint of its neighbours on a low hill, no hearth, no storage, no floor trash; forty identical unfired clay figurines with no faces, all in one pit; a skull with a trepanation that healed; and a bronze bowl out of a bog with a clumsy ancient repair done before it went into the water. Right now I have four different instincts about those and no idea whether any of them is principled.
