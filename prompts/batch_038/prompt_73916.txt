I'm helping run an interdisciplinary workshop where about half the room are philosophers and half are people who do causal inference for a living — DAGs, the do-operator, propensity scores, that lot — and basically nobody in the second group has ever read a word about counterfactual accounts of causation. I want to hand everyone the same decision tree at the start and have it sort them onto a position about what causation actually is: from Lewis-style counterfactual dependence, through Woodward-style interventionism, to process/production views, contrastivist views, the two-sorts-of-causation pluralists, and the deflationists who think there's nothing over and above the Humean mosaic.

Here's what I need:

Eight or nine branch nodes, each one a concrete case rather than a slogan. People answer the case; the label they land on only shows up at the leaf. The words counterfactual, intervention, and process should not appear anywhere in the question text — only in the labels at the end — otherwise half the room just answers by recognizing a term.

The cases have to include, at minimum: two assassins who both shoot and either shot would have been fatal on its own; the plant that dies because nobody watered it (is the not-watering a cause, or a difference-maker of some second-class kind?); the earlier spell that beats out the later spell even though the later one would have produced exactly the same result; the pilot whose bombing run succeeds because he first shot down the fighter that would have shot him down; Suzy's rock arriving a moment before Billy's otherwise identical one; and the moon and the tides, where there's clearly a dependence but nobody can wiggle the moon.

I want the moon-and-tides node flagged. My own guess is that it does not actually seperate Lewis from Woodward, and if it doesn't, say so in the writeup instead of quietly designing around it. If you think it does do real work, tell me exactly how — but give me a verdict either way.

Order matters. The nodes should be sequenced so nothing depends on an answer you haven't gotten yet — don't ask whether causation is contrastive before you know whether they let absences be causes at all. Give me your reasoning for the order you chose, and if there was a runner-up ordering that would have worked just as well, say what it was and why you dropped it.

After the tree, lay out the logical space behind it: which combinations of answers are genuinely available and which cells are empty. For each empty cell, tell me whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's merely unpopular, or because nobody has bothered to stake it out. I have a suspicion there's a live cell for "absences really are causes, but dependence isn't what causation consists in, and there's no connecting process either" — I want to know flatly whether I'm wrong about that.

Then list the leaves your tree can't tell apart, and say for each whether that's a defect in the tree or evidence that the two "positions" arent really distinct. I suspect some of what gets presented as a fight between interventionists and contrastivists is just disagreement about how much of the metaphysics you say out loud, and I'd rather you settle that than tiptoe around it.

And commit. No "one might reasonably hold either" tour — tell me where the branches go, which cells are empty, which of my suspicions above is mistaken. If you think one of the leaves I listed is a verbal variant of another and doesnt deserve to be its own position, cut it and defend the cut.
