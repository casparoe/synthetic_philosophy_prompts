Our reading group has been arguing about the common prior assumption for going on two years and we've finally agreed to do an adversarial collaboration and produce one document we can all sign. Help me draft it.

The two camps, as fairly as I can state them:

Camp A (call it the Harsanyi–Aumann side). Rationality plus total evidence pins down a unique prior — there's no extra slack for "starting points" that evidence doesn't already fix. So the common prior assumption is a substantive idealization, usually approximately right, and its failure is a diagnosis of something gone wrong (missed evidence, motivated reasoning, non-Bayesian updating) rather than a license. When two well-informed people disagree and both know it, someone is making a mistake, and Aumann's result is what makes that claim precise rather than merely hectoring.

Camp B (the permissivist / anti-CPA side). The prior isn't a deliverance of evidence; it encodes things evidence can't settle — weights on simplicity, tolerance of imprecision, innate disposition to treat certain hypotheses as live. Two people with literally identical total evidence can both be rational and still differ in priors. So rational agreeing-to-disagree is possible in principle, and Aumann's theorem is best read as a theorem about the informational structure of disagreement (what common knowledge of posteriors forces) rather than about what rational agents are obliged to believe.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. Include the bare mechanics both accept: what the theorem assumes, what a type space is supposed to represent, what work the word "prior" is doing as a piece of bookkeeping, whether the assumption is even in principle testable from inside an agent's own reasoning, and which relaxations of the theorem both camps regard as unobjectionable — e.g. that if priors only need to be close rather than identical then common knowledge of posteriors forces the posteriors to be close too, and that weakening common knowledge to common p-belief gives a bound on how far apart the posteriors can be that tightens as p goes to 1.

2. A disagreements table. Each row: the disagreement stated precisely, with a column where Camp A's position is worded the way Camp A would accept and a column where Camp B's is worded the way Camp B would accept — not strawmen, not a single "neutral" gloss that quietly favors one side. Last column: is this row a real disagreement or a verbal one, and if verbal, exactly what the two sides are using the same words to mean.

3. The cruxes. Sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. For each: the case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps accept as bearing on it, and each camp's prediction written down in advance, before anyone looks.

At least one crux has to be worked, not just described. Here's the toy case I want the collaboration to grind through together: states 1, 2, 3, 4. Alice's partition is {{1,2},{3,4}}, Bo's is {{1,3},{2,4}}. Alice's conditional beliefs: given {1,2} she puts 3/4 on state 1; given {3,4} she puts 3/4 on state 3. Bo's: given {1,3} he puts 3/4 on state 1; given {2,4} he puts 1/4 on state 2. Do the algebra and say whether any probability distribution over the four states reproduces all four of those conditionals. Then, whichever way it comes out, answer the two follow-ups both camps have to face:

- Work out what is and isn't common knowledge in this space. Specifically, what's the finest common coarsening of the two partitions, and given that, can Alice and Bo ever have common knowledge of their posteriors for a non-trivial event here? Does the example's verdict about common priors and the example's verdict about common knowledge line up, or does it show that a space can fail the common prior condition and still never trigger the agreeing-to-disagree scenario?

- There's a stronger betting-flavored condition that's supposed to be necessary and sufficient for a common prior to exist: a set of transfer random variables, one per agent, summing to zero, such that each agent regards his own as favorable given his information. If the arithmetic above says no common prior exists, try to actually write down such a bet for this space. If you can't, say which step blocks you — that gap is itself informative and both camps should record what they made of it.

Two other things I want sorted out explicitly, because they're where the group talks past itself most:

Someone keeps insisting the common prior assumption is vacuous, since anything you'd call a difference in priors can be redescribed as a difference in information — the prior just absorbs everything upstream of the partition. Someone else insists it's restrictive, since you can build type spaces with no common prior at all, which is a real mathematical fact about belief structures and not a relabeling. Figure out whether these two claims can both be true, whether the fight between them is about words, and if so which words.

And handle the escape hatch where priors are allowed to be improper or only finitely additive: does opening that up rescue Camp A, sink Camp B, or just move the disagreement somewhere else? Say which.

Finally, one empirical crux, with predictions lodged in advance: well-calibrated forecasters and prediction-market participants who share the same public evidence and know each other's numbers nonetheless hold steady different probabilities for months. Both camps have to say beforehand what they'd conclude if that turned out to be robust — is it evidence against the common prior assumption, or only evidence that common knowledge of posteriors never obtains, or neither, and what would distinguish those readings?

No verdicts, no "and so Camp B is right after all." It has to read like a document both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Also, no bibliography and don't cite anything — I don't care who said what, I care that the formulations are right. Typo in my partion above notwithstanding, the numbers are what I mean.
