**Mixed domination and the correlated-belief smuggle**

*1.* Everyone who teaches game theory hits the same spot in week three: iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies only behaves properly if you let mixed strategies do the dominating. The reason given is always the same — a player who is permitted to randomize can have a 50/50 mixture that beats some pure strategy at every profile even though neither component does on its own. Texts state this, note that the surviving set shrinks, and move on, treating it as a technicality about the strategy space. My claim is that this is the wrong description of what is going on. In games with three or more players, licensing mixed domination is not a technicality about randomization at all. It is a substantive assumption about the *correlation structure of players' beliefs*, and that assumption is doing the justificatory work that the epistemic story is usually asked to do.

*2.* The relevant duality is standard: a pure strategy is strictly dominated, possibly by a mixed strategy, if and only if it fails to be a best response to some probability distribution over the opponents' pure strategy profiles — that is, iff it is a best response to some *correlated* conjecture. In two-player games the phrase "correlated conjecture" is empty, because there is only one opponent and a distribution over a single player's strategies is just a mixed strategy. Nothing controversial lurks there, which is why the two-player case feels clean. With three or more players, a distribution over profiles need not factorize into a product of marginals, and suddenly the duality is saying something: deleting a strategy because a mixture beats it is equivalent to declaring that the player is entitled to non-factorizing beliefs about her opponents.

*3.* Here is the smallest case I could build. Player 1 chooses U, M, or D; players 2 and 3 each choose L or R. Player 1's payoffs, indexed by the profile of the other two:

```
              (L,L)   (L,R)   (R,L)   (R,R)
      U         1       4       0       1
      M         1       0       4       1
      D         0       3       3       0
```

D is never a best response to any pure profile: U and M tie and beat it at (L,L) and (R,R), and each strictly beats it at one of the two disagreement profiles. Now suppose player 1's conjecture factorizes — let p be the chance she assigns to player 2 playing L and q the chance she assigns to player 3 playing L. Writing a = p(1−q) for (L,R), b = (1−p)q for (R,L), c = pq and d = (1−p)(1−q), her expected payoffs are EU = c + 4a + d, EM = c + 4b + d, and ED = 3a + 3b. Since c + d = 1 − (a + b), the condition for D to be strictly best becomes b > 1/4 and a > 1/4 simultaneously. But ab = p(1−p)·q(1−q) ≤ 1/16, so both cannot exceed 1/4. D is never a best response to any independent conjecture.

Under the correlated conjecture that puts half its mass on (L,R) and half on (R,L), by contrast, EU = EM = 2 and ED = 3, and D is a strict best response. So by the duality D is not strictly dominated by anything — mixed or pure — yet an independence-respecting account of rationalizability deletes it in the first round. Pearce's version, which permits correlated conjectures, keeps it, and Pearce's version is exactly what you get from iterated deletion under mixed domination.

*4.* This is where I want to say something normative rather than bookkeeping-ish. The standard epistemic gloss on deletion is: everyone is rational, everyone knows it, everyone knows everyone knows it, so each player rules out what the others wouldn't play, and so on down. But in a three-player game that story is silent on whether a player's uncertainty about the *other two* factorizes. Silence is not neutrality. Independence is a real commitment — it says the player treats her opponents' choices as probabilistically unrelated even when she knows they are both reasoning about one and the same situation, and even when she may know that they have been in communication, or share a common signal, or are simply both responding to a salient feature of the game. A common prior over profiles, or any of those, delivers correlation for free. So my suggestion is that the ordering in the textbooks is reversed: it is mixed-domination deletion that is licensed by the bare common-knowledge-of-rationality story plus a common-prior-ish picture of beliefs, and it is the independence restriction that needs an extra argument. Nobody ever gives one; it's just assumed because two-player examples dominate the pedagogy and the question never arises there.

*5.* Consequences, if that's right. (i) Bernheim's and Pearce's notions coincide in two-player games, so the near-universal use of two-player illustrations isn't a simplifying convention — it structurally conceals the only case where the assumption has content. (ii) "Rationalizability" is not a single notion but a family indexed by a belief-structure assumption that is almost always left implicit, and members of the family are not interchangeable in arguments. (iii) Any argument of the form "rationality plus common knowledge of rationality yields the deletion set" is enthymematic for n ≥ 3, and the suppressed premise is about correlation, not about randomization. (iv) More speculatively: once you have conceded correlated conjectures in the epistemic analysis, the distance between rationalizability and correlated equilibrium looks much shorter than the standard presentation implies, and the choice between solution concepts starts to look like a choice about how much of Aumann's apparatus you are prepared to build into an account of what merely rational players would do.

*6.* Two things I'm not happy with. First, there may be an independent motivation for factorizing beliefs that I'm underestimating: the players choose separately and simultaneously, so nothing in the physical setup correlates them, and one might think a rational agent's beliefs should mirror that. I think this confuses causal independence with probabilistic independence under incomplete information, but I haven't argued it. Second, (iv) may be too quick — a correlated equilibrium is driven by an actual public signal, whereas a correlated conjecture is just the shape of a subjective belief, and I'm not sure the first reduces to the second without extra premises. I expect a referee to go straight for both.
