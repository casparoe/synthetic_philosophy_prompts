A colleague has turned the no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning into what he calls a proof that Hume wins outright, and I cannot locate the break.

His setup: fix a finite input space and a finite label set; a target function is any assignment of labels to inputs; a learning algorithm maps a labeled sample to predictions about the rest. The theorem says that if you average off-training-set error uniformly over all target functions (and over the samples those functions generate), then every algorithm has exactly the same expected error as every other, at every sample size — including an algorithm that ignores its sample and predicts at random. His illustration: eight inputs, binary labels, hence 256 target functions; you observe the labels on three of the inputs, leaving 32 consistent functions, and under a uniform weight on those 32 every algorithm errs on exactly 2.5 of the five unseen inputs in expectation. Conditioning on the data, he says, therefore rescues nothing either.

The argument:

(1) Under the uniform average over all target functions, no algorithm's expected generalization error differs from any other's, at any sample size.

(2) The class of all target functions is just the class of epistemic possibilities about how the unobserved labels go, so averaging over it is not adopting an assumption but declining to adopt one.

(3) Therefore whether one method of forming beliefs about the unobserved is better than another is determinate only relative to a prior over target functions.

(4) Any such prior is a principle of the uniformity of nature in disguise.

(5) Support for that principle would be either a priori, which assumes what it must establish, or inductive, which infers uniformity from a record of past success that already presupposes it.

(6) Hence no prior is non-circularly available, and so no inductive method is better supported than guessing.

(7) The theorem itself is a mathematical result, and accepting it means trusting one method of belief-formation over its rivals, which by (6) is unsupported. So the reasoning either refutes its own premises or leaves all methods on a par — and since the theorem is proved, all methods are on a par.

I want to keep (1), and I would rather keep (7) than surrender the self-undermining force. Tell me which step or steps fail, what exactly is misdescribed at that point, and what keeping (1) and (7) costs once the failing steps are removed. In particular: does the repair concede anything to my colleague, or does it leave Hume's circularity worry standing untouched, now unsupported by the theorem he invoked?
