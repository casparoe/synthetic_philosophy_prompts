I run a 32-unit supported housing scheme — adults with long-term mental health conditions, each with their own assured tenancy and their own seperate front door, but sharing a corridor, a lounge/kitchen, a laundry, a courtyard and a garden. Every week we get four or five disputes that all start the same way: someone is doing something entirely inside their own home, and somebody else says it's making their life unbearable. Right now it gets handled by whichever worker is on shift and their gut, it's wildly inconsistent, and we've had two complaints to the ombudsman in a year.

What I want is a written decision procedure a support worker can run on a dispute in about 15 minues, with no economics background and no legal advice to hand. Please ground it in the actual tension Sen identified, which I think is exactly our situation: you can't in general both give each person a protected private sphere where their own say-so settles the matter, and also always go with what everyone unanimously prefers. Sometimes the thing inside the protected sphere is precisely what everybody else wants changed, and sometimes what a resident says they "prefer" isn't about their own life at all but about somebody else's private conduct. So something has to give somewhere, and I'd rather the procedure admit that and tell me what it's sacrificing than paper over it.

Tell me:

- the questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each
- where the procedure just stops and the person keeps doing what they're doing, no further discussion
- where the issue moves to the house meeting instead, and what to do about the fact that only 11 of 32 residents ever turn up to it
- where it needs manager sign-off, and where it isn't a factual question at all but a values question that belongs in a conversation with the resident rather than a ruling
- which distinctions absolutely have to survive being simplified for frontline staff, and which I can safely drop
- which steps are judgement calls wearing a procedure costume
- what the procedure flatly cannot settle, so I stop expecting it to

Then run it end to end on four cases, which are close to real ones:

1. A resident cooks strongly spiced food in her own kitchen most evenings and the smell travels the corridor; four neighbours say they can't use the lounge at that time and one says she's stopped wanting to come home. The cook says what she eats in her own flat is nobody's business.
2. A resident leaves his blinds open and his lights on late and moves around in ways three others find distressing to watch from across the courtyard; those three want a house rule about blinds after 10pm. Nobody's complaint is about their own conduct — it's entirely about his.
3. The tenancy gives one night-shift resident sole say over whether the courtyard gate is left unlocked. He'd honestly rather it were locked, and so would everybody else including him, but he thinks the rest of the house expects him to stand on his right and he doesn't want to be the one who caved.
4. He now offers to give up the gate right if another resident gives up the reserved parking bay; a third resident objects to the swap happening at all, on the grounds that it sets a price on things that shouldn't have one.

Also handle the fact that some residents' capacity fluctuates week to week, so the same person may hold the same preference strongly in March and not care in April — the procedure should say what to do when the right-holder's own view is the unstable thing.

Be straight with me if one of these comes out somewhere I'll hate. I expect case 2 will, and I'd rather know now than find out in front of the regulator, who inspects us on tenant autonomy and on communal living simultaneously and does not seem to notice that those two pull apart.
