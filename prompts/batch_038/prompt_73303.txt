DRAFT (~750 words)

**Reversibility, not autonomy**

The debate about whether we should want AI as a tool or as an agent usually gets drawn along one axis: autonomy. The tool camp says keep the model as an instrument — a person decides what to do, the system executes, and responsibility stays legible. The agent camp says the value is in delegation — give the system an objective and let it plan, act over hours or days, and report back. What separates them is supposedly the question of who decides.

My complaint is that autonomy is a bad axis, mostly because it's continuous and empirical. Every system decides something and everything is decided-for to some degree; a spreadsheet formula chooses a value, a thermostat chooses to fire the furnace. So the axis doesn't do any normative work. Two systems with exactly the same amount of "autonomy" can differ enormously in how much trouble they cause, and two systems that differ wildly in autonomy can be equally harmless. If your distinction can't rank the cases you care about, it isn't the distinction you care about.

Here's what I think is underneath it. Every system has an action space — the set of states it can move the world into — and a reversal space: the set of moves that get you back to roughly where you were, at a cost you'd actually pay. What makes something feel like a mere tool isn't that it lacks goals. It's that its action space is small and its reversal space is nearly as big. A hammer can dent a wall, and you can patch the wall. What makes something feel like an agent is that its mistakes are cheap to make and expensive to notice, and once noticed, expensive or impossible to undo.

That gives two variables worth measuring separately. The first is reversal cost: how much does it take to get back. The second, which I think is the neglected one, is the notice gap — the time and effort between when a agent acts and when a supervisor could recognize that the action was wrong. Notice gap is not the same as autonomy. An email agent that drafts but doesn't send has a notice gap of zero regardless of how clever it is; an agent that sends has a notice gap measured in social damage, since you find out about the bad email when the recipient replies.

If that's right, the design question isn't "should we let AI have goals?" but "how do we shrink the notice gap and enlarge the reversal space?" Staging environments, dry-run modes, mandatory previews, undo windows, rate limits, actions that are provisional until a human ratifies them — these are all reversal engineering, and they matter more than how agentic the underlying system is. A wildly ambitious system operating inside a huge reversal space can be safer than a dumb one-click script with no undo. Nobody in the tool camp is arguing about that script, but they should be.

Two objections I can see coming. First: some harms are constitutively irreversible — a public statement, a fired employee, a released model, a market move, a death. For those, no amount of reversal engineering helps, and the tool camp is simply right that you can't delegate what you can't take back. My answer is that this rescues the tool camp's conclusion while destroying its premise: they're right about irreversibility, not about autonomy. The domain where human-in-the-loop is mandatory is defined by the action, not by the system.

Second, and I think this one is harder: the notice gap assumes there is a supervisor and that they're paying attention. Agents run in parallel, at scale, overnight. Detection isn't just delayed, it's combinatorially overwhelmed — you can't read ten thousand traces, so the effective notice gap goes to infinity even if each individual action is reviewable in principle. I don't have a good response here except to say this is a reason to cap concurrency rather than to distrust agency as such, and I suspect that's a dodge.

What I want to end on: the tool/agent framing borrows a control metaphor — hands on the wheel, eyes on the road — that assumes a single channel through which a human governs a machine. Agents don't have a single channel. So arguing about how much control to keep is arguing with the wrong picture. Argue about what can be taken back.

END DRAFT
