Third-year PhD student in philosophy here — nominally working on personal identity, currently moonlighting as the house ethicist for a clinical team at a teaching hospital, which is my supervisor's idea of applied philosophy. I’m writing up a section on advance decisions and I keep getting stuck, so I want to hand you the whole apparatus and have you tell me what it assumes. Please answer in British English (‑ise spellings; ‘whilst’ at your discretion).

The practice, as I understand it for England and Wales under the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (ss. 24–26). Shout if I’ve garbled any of it.

1. The statute only recognises advance decisions to *refuse*, and that’s built into the definition rather than tacked on: an advance decision is a decision made by a capacitous adult that if they later lack capacity, a specified treatment is not to be carried out or continued. As far as I can tell there is no equivalent instrument with binding force for *asking* for treatment in advance — advance statements exist in some corners and get taken into account, but nobody I’ve spoken to thinks they bind. And no practicioner I’ve met treats a piece of paper as a way to demand a particular drug or procedure.

2. A capacitous person can withdraw or alter it at any time, orally, with no paperwork.

3. It’s invalid if they’ve later done anything ‘clearly inconsistent’ with it remaining their fixed decision.

4. It doesn’t apply if there are reasonable grounds for believing that circumstances exist now which they did not anticipate and which ‘would have affected his decision had he anticipated them’.

5. Refusals of life-sustaining treatment need extra formality: writing, signature, witness, plus an express statement that it stands even if life is at risk. Every other refusal needs none of that — a scribbled note, or a reliably reported conversation, will do.

6. If valid and applicable, it has effect ‘as if he had made it, and had had capacity to make it’ at the moment the treatment is proposed. So legally it is not the earlier self reaching forward; it is the present patient deemed to be refusing now, competently.

7. While anyone goes to court for a declaration about whether it exists, is valid, or is applicable, life-sustaining treatment can be given anyway.

8. On the ward, the person under a directive refusing antibiotics or artificial nutrition still gets mouth care, pressure-area care, warmth and analgesia. Directives are never read as covering that, and I can’t find anyone who thinks that’s a judgement call.

The case my team keeps reaching for: a refusal written twenty-odd years ago by someone now in advanced dementia who is, by all reports, content — enjoys music, holds her daughter’s hand, no sign of distress. The directive says don’t treat the pneumonia. Everyone agrees she would not have written it had she known she’d end up like this, and nobody thinks she’s suffering now.

So:

(a) What picture of the person does this bundle presuppose — one continuing subject with standing to bind its own future stages, a sequence of selves with something like a contract between them, or something else? Clause (6) especially: does the ‘as if now’ fiction concede that diachronic authority isn’t really what’s operating, and if it isn’t, what is?

(b) What does the refusal-only asymmetry presuppose? Negative versus positive liberty, autonomy as veto rather than claim, something about who bears the burden of acting, or just clinicians’ professional judgement and budgets wearing no philosophical clothing at all? Separate these rather than blending them. Relatedly — a self-binding or Ulysses-style directive, where someone with a bipolar diagnosis pre-consents to admission and medication during a future manic episode, doesn’t seem to bind anyone here. Deep commitment, or administrative inertia?

(c) Clause (4) looks to me like it hands authority to a *hypothetical* earlier self — the better-informed one who would have decided differently. Is that consistent with (3) and (6), and with the entire point of binding your future? Or does it smuggle present-sensitivity in through the back door while claiming to be about the past?

(d) What does the two-tier formality in (5) presuppose — an evidential worry about forgery and drift, or a substantive presumption in favour of life that shows up disguised as paperwork? Same question for (7), and for the comfort-care carve-out in (8), which strikes me as doing a lot of unexplained work.

(e) Which features strictly presupose a single view, and which are merely compatible with several — identity-based, sovereignty-of-the-earlier-will, best-interests, narrative? Give me a clean ‘this bit needs X, this bit is happy with X or Y or Z’ map, and flag the bits where the scheme is genuinely underdetermined and nothing in the practice decides between rivals.

(f) Is the bundle internally coherent, all things considered? And what would actually have to change — statute, form design, ward routine, who gets to decide — if we swapped the presupposed view for (i) one on which wellbeing is entirely a matter of present experience, (ii) one on which the demented patient and the directive-writer are relevantly different people, or (iii) the disability-rights insistence that capacity is always supported rather than simply present or absent, so substitute decision-making should be rare to vanishing?
