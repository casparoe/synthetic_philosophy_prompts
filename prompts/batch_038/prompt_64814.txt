Next month I deliver a forty-five-minute paper at a workshop on consciousness and causation, defending the claim that the paradox of phenomenal judgment can be defused without giving up epiphenomenalism. My argument accepts causal closure of the physical and the non-identity of phenomenal from physical properties, and therefore accepts that experience has no physical effects; the defusing move is to reject the premise that a belief's being accurate requires a causal link to what makes it true. On my view phenomenal concepts get their reference by acquaintance rather than by causal role, acquaintance confers non-inferential warrant without doing any causal work, and so my zombie duplicate's tokens of "it seems red to me" are either unwarranted or fail to express what mine express. The room will be mostly physicalist philosophers of mind, including two defenders of higher-order and global-workspace accounts and one illusionist about phenomenology; three experimental psychologists who work on introspection, confidence ratings, and confabulation; and a metaphysician who works on causal exclusion and mental causation. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the thesis: the eight to ten objections this audience will actually raise, ordered roughly by when they will come, each phrased the way that group would phrase it — the psychologists in their own vocabulary, not mine — with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a candid note on where the reply is softest. At least one objection should target the claim that acquaintance can warrant without causing, and at least one the claim that zombie tokens differ in content from mine; flag any pair of objections that turn out to be the same worry in two vocabularies. Close with what I should concede in the opening five minutes to buy credibility, which line of attack the exclusion metaphysician can press hardest, and the single question I am hoping nobody asks.
