I lead a small internal AI policy function at an insurance group, and once a quarter I run a fifty-minute lunch session for our "emerging tech" working group — about twenty people, mostly underwriters, claims folks, two compliance lawyers and an internal auditor, plus two data engineers who'll know what an MDP is but have never read anything on AI safety. Everyone in the room now uses the word agent constantly, in vendor decks and in our own procurement language, and almost nobody could say what separates an agent from a tool that happens to be very good. Next month the session is exactly that: tool AI versus agent AI, and I want a plan for how to teach it, not the teaching itself — I've tried twice in hallway conversations and both times I could feel myself losing them.

So give me the sequence and say what depends on what. There is a standard move where you point out that the intro-AI textbook defines things so loosely that a household thermostat comes out as an agent, and then the real distinction you actually care about isn't the degree of smartness but something about how the system is built and what it's structured to pursue across situations it wasn't designed for. I think that move belongs somewhere early but I can't work out whether it goes before or after they've got a felt sense of the difference, and I don't know whether it lands with a room of underwriters or just reads as pedantry — tell me where to put it and why. I want one running example carried all the way through, not five toy cases, and I want you to be explicit about what that example must contain and what it must pointedly lack in order to do the job; whatever you pick should be something an insurance operations person would actually recognise from their own workflows rather than a robot in a gridworld.

Then map the misconceptions. The ones I'm braced for: agent means autonomous or unsupervised, so anything with a human approving each step is safe by construction; it's a binary property of the system rather than a set of design choices someone made and could have made differently; agency and capability are one dial you turn up, so "more agentic" automatically means "more useful and more dangerous" at the same time; a system only becomes risky once it's frontier-scale; and the idea that a tool can't hurt you, which I think is flatly false and I want them to leave not believing it. I'd also like you to flag the misconception I'm missing that a room of auditors will specifically bring. For each one, tell me the point in the sequence where it has to be headed off — I suspect at least two of them can't be fixed with a correction later and have to be designed out of the order from the start.

Finally, be decisive about what I should refuse to cover in fifty minutes and say out loud that I'm skipping it (I assume the formal machinery, and probably the whole question of whether the words consciousness or intentions apply), and end with one question I can put to the room that would show me the distinction actually landed rather than them having picked up new vocabulary to hide behind — ideally a question whose right answer depends on what you'd want the distinction for, since half of them are going to use it to write procurement requirements and the other half to review incident reports.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per block of the fifty minutes, each one to three sentences covering what gets introduced in that block and why it can't come earlier, which misconception that block heads off, and the one-sentence bridge into the next block. No intro, no wrap-up, no headings beyond the numbers, no readings. The closing question goes in the fifth item.
