I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I should warn you that the thing I'm asking about has already eaten a term of my life. Every year I set the same seminar question — is misleading worse than, better than, or just different from lying — and every year we end up in exactly the same place, which is nowhere, with both sides perfectly convinced and nobody having said anything new since about week three.

Here's the shape of it as I've come to see it. One camp says lying is a matter of assertion: you lie if and only if you assert what you believe to be false, and so the minister who says something literally true while knowing full well it will be taken the wrong way has not lied at all, and the interesting question becomes whether misleading is a lesser, a greater, or a seperate kind of wrong. The other camp says that's fetishising the surface. What matters is what the speaker makes herself answerable for; if you deliberately let a false belief take root that you could cheaply have prevented, or you lean on an accurate detail in order to steer somebody wrong, you've done the same damage and exploited the same trust as the outright liar. Both camps have cases galore. Every time the assertionists produce one where the misleader looks clearly less blameworthy — the doorstep question about whether anyone is hiding in your house, say — the other side replies that the case has been built to smuggle in a mitigating feature (no relationship of trust, no expectation of candour, no chance to check) that is doing all the work on its own. And every case designed to show the misleader is worse gets the mirror-image reply that it's really a lying case in disguise.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and quite possibly wrong: they aren't disagreeing about lying at all. One side wants a category that can do institutional work — perjury, contract, the published correction — where you need a bright line round which utterances count, and the other wants a category for moral assessment, where a bright line is precisely the thing the clever speaker is gaming. On that reading it's partly two questions under one name. But I don't think that dissolves it, because underneath there does seem to be a real issue about where the norm attaches: to what is said, or to what the speaker warrants, implicates and stands ready to defend.

There's a wrinkle I'd like you to take seriously rather than set aside as an aside. Call it exculpatory precision: the speaker who inserts a scrupulously accurate detail into an answer not because anyone asked about that detail, but so that she'll have something true to point at afterwards. My students' reaction to that is uniform — it's worse than a plain lie, not better — and its not just squeamishness about hypocrisy, I don't think; there's something about manufacturing your own future alibi whilst the deception is still running. If that reaction is right and reasonably general it seems to press on both camps at once, and I can't work out how.

So: what sort of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, or about the function of the category, or substantive about what makes deceptive communication wrongful in the first place, or just a stalemate manufactured by the fact that each side gets to design the cases that are supposed to decide it? Argue for your diagnosis from what the two positions say and do, not from a survey of who has held which view. Then tell me plainly what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal account of what a speaker warrants, a change of question, an agreed protocol for building cases — or make the case that nothing will and say why. Please write it to me as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I'll read it on the train.
