I run a recorded foundations series at my university in which a visiting speaker is interviewed for forty minutes in front of an audience drawn from both the philosophy and the mathematics departments. Next week's speaker works on the foundations of analysis and defends constructive mathematics in a particular form: on her view the law of excluded middle is not false but unlicensed — a principle we have no right to apply once a statement ranges over a completed infinite totality — so that classical analysis survives as a reliably useful discourse whose results can be read back into the constructive framework whenever they bear on anything we can actually compute or exhibit. Her signature move, and the one that generates most of the discussion, is to treat intuitionistic validity as indexed to stages of an unfinished mathematics: a statement's being unwarranted now is not merely a report on our ignorance, and she wants "not yet" to carry genuine logical weight. Treat the position exactly as I have described it; do not try to identify her with any actual author or to match her to someone's published formulations, and please do not put words in her mouth beyond what follows from the view as stated.

I need ten questions to work from. Each one must have four parts: the question as I would read it aloud, in no more than three sentences, with the specific machinery at issue named inside the question rather than gestured at; the answer she is most likely to give, in the register she would use; exactly one follow-up that forecloses that answer — not a re-ask, but a step she cannot take without giving up something she has already conceded earlier in the hour; and a single clause stating what a genuinely satisfying answer would have to contain.

Coverage. At least one question each on:

1. The double-negation translation, and specifically on the fact that Peano arithmetic is Π₂-conservative over Heyting arithmetic — if the two frameworks agree on the arithmetical statements anyone outside this room cares about, what substantive disagreement is left?
2. Kripke-style semantics for intuitionistic logic, whose soundness and completeness are established by classical reasoning in the metalanguage: embarrassment, pragmatic concession, or category mistake?
3. Markov's principle — accepted in the Russian constructive tradition, refused by Brouwer, set aside in Bishop's program — and what her placement on it reveals about who her real opponent is.
4. Brouwer's weak counterexamples to excluded middle, the familiar one turning on an unresolved question about the digits of a transcendental constant, and the objection that these display our ignorance rather than a defect in the principle.
5. Countable choice: why the constructivist is permissive about a principle classical set theory treats as substantial while restrictive about excluded middle, and whether that permissiveness is doing unearned work in the versions of analysis she recovers.
6. The casualties — bounded sequences with no located supremum, Bolzano–Weierstrass in full generality, the intermediate value theorem except under extra hypotheses such as local non-constancy, bases for arbitrary vector spaces, Hahn–Banach — and what she says to the working analyst who asks what the program has cost them.
7. The gains — above all the intuitionistic result that every real function on the closed unit interval is uniformly continuous, which classical analysis denies — and whether she wants her framework described as a restriction of classical mathematics or as a rival theory with different theorems.
8. The Curry–Howard correspondence and proof assistants, given that Lean's library builds in choice as an axiom and derives excluded middle from it, while Coq's underlying calculus is constructive but ships excluded middle as an importable axiom.
9. Undecidable equality on the reals and the role of apartness: how she answers the complaint that her framework cannot so much as pose the ordinary question whether two given real numbers are equal.
10. The Bishop–Brouwer split: Bishop deliberately avoided mental constructions and choice sequences, and her stage-indexed semantics reintroduces precisely that apparatus — is she an intuitionist or a constructive mathematician, and does the label have content?

Order them so that the questions run from the achievements she can point to, through the collapse-and-conservativity objections, to the ones about her stage-indexed semantics; I want her to accumulate equity before I spend it. Nothing about her intellectual formation, her influences, her teachers, or advice to students.
