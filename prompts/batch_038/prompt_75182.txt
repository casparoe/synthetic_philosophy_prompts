"On Certainty takes up G. E. Moore's claim to know such propositions as that here is one hand and that the earth has existed for a very long time. Here is the script. Paragraph numbers are mine, for ease of reference.

[1] On Certainty is not a book Wittgenstein published. It is made up of notes written during the last eighteen months of his life and issued posthumously in 1969, and they circle repeatedly around two of Moore's papers, Proof of an External World and A Defence of Common Sense, in which Moore lists propositions he claims to know with complete certainty — that here is one hand, that the earth has existed for very many years past — and offers them against scepticism about the external world. Wittgenstein's interest is not in whether these propositions are true. It is in what Moore is doing when he says he knows them, and whether that amounts to anything.

[2] The objection, as I read it, is that 'I know' has work to do in ordinary language and Moore has taken it out of that work. To say one knows is to imply that one has grounds, that one could produce them, that one might be mistaken, and that the matter could be settled by looking into it. Moore's certainty is supposed to be exempt from all of that: it is offered as a report about the world while being withdrawn from every procedure by which such a report could be checked, and so the expression is left idle. Wittgenstein's contrasting description is that certainty of this kind is shown rather than asserted. Someone who held up a hand and said that here is one hand would be doing nothing; someone who grasps, points, and does not check overnight whether his hands have ceased to exist is manifesting it. This certainty is nearer to a way of behaving than to a cognitive state, and he speaks of it as something animal, a trust that sits on the near side of the distinction between justified and unjustified.

[3] Hinges differ from ordinary empirical beliefs in the part they play. An ordinary belief — that it rained last night, that the kettle is on — is answerable to evidence: it can be tested, borne out, overturned, and its truth is a matter of how things stand. A hinge proposition is not what gets tested but what testing presupposes. The image Wittgenstein reaches for is of a river: the water flows, and the bed in which it flows shifts, but slowly, so that some stretches are hard rock and others are sand the current moves about. Inquiry is the water; hinges are the bed. That is why, on his account, the testing of any hypothesis, together with its confirmation or disconfirmation, already takes place within a system which is not itself arrived at by testing. To doubt a hinge is not to raise a difficult question but to lose the frame in which questions have sense, since a doubt must have grounds, and those grounds are hinge propositions in turn.

[4] It does not follow that hinges are unjustified, and I do not think Wittgenstein means that they are. Justification comes to an end, but it does not end in a bare given; it ends in a ground of a different kind. A hinge is held fast by what surrounds it, and what surrounds it is the practice it makes possible. So a hinge is best understood as a hypothesis confirmed not by any particular test but by the success of everything it has licensed: the reason we hold fast to the earth's having existed for a long time is that our historical, geological and everyday dealings with the world all depend on it and all work. My answer to (c) is therefore that hinges are justified pragmatically rather than evidentially. This is also what lets Wittgenstein accept Moore's propositions as true while denying that Moore knows them: Moore is right about the truth and wrong about the grammar of his own claim.

[5] Wittgenstein distinguishes subjective certainty, which is complete conviction and the absence of doubt in a person, from objective certainty, where there is no longer room for mistake. Hinges have the second. Since mistake is excluded in their case, they are not merely believed: they are true, and true in a stronger way than any conclusion of inquiry can be.

[6] Because hinges are not answerable to evidence, they need not be the same for everyone. What functions as a hinge in one community may be an ordinary empirical belief in another, and the riverbed can be rearranged, so that a proposition once immune becomes testable and a testable one hardens. But the deepest hinges belong to every form of life, since no community could act at all without them — that there are physical objects, that the earth has existed a long time, that people are not generally mistaken about their own pain. The variability is real, but shallow.

[7] Scepticism, then, is not refuted but diagnosed. The sceptic's doubt is not a doubt, because a doubt that doubts everything doubts nothing: it lacks the grounds that give a doubt its content. The reply is available to us, though not to him. And since argument always presupposes shared hinges, someone whose framework differed at that depth could not be reasoned with at all, but only persuaded or converted, where persuasion cannot mean the production of evidence. To this extent hinge epistemology is a relativism: the truth of a hinge is relative to the form of life in which it does its work. It is also, I think, the ancestor of the contextualist reply to scepticism, on which the standards governing knowledge claims shift with the conversational context, since both hold that the sceptic cannot be met by further evidence.

[8] So my answer to (c) is yes, in the pragmatic sense; and to the final part, that Wittgenstein replies to the sceptic only by declining to treat him as a participant in inquiry.
