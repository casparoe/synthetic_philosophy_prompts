I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized outlet, and last week I was stuck in a green room before a public talk about hospital labor and overheard two of the other panelists go at it for maybe twenty minutes. I want to write some of it up but I honestly couldn't follow all of the moves, and I don't want to make either of them look dumber than they were.

The setup: a contract fight at a big teaching hospital. The sterile processing techs didn't strike. They ran a strict work-to-rule instead — every mandated safety check logged to the letter, no covering for absent coworkers, nothing done that wasn't literally in the job description, instrument trays run through the protocol one at a time instead of batched. Elective surgeries slipped for about six weeks. Nobody was harmed as far as anyone could establish, but the OR schedule fell apart.

Call them N (a labor lawyer) and R (works in hospital operations, bioethics background).

(1) N opened by saying the work-to-rule isn't just legitimate, it's *more* legitimate than a walkout: nobody abandoned patients, the techs stayed on the clock and did precisely what the employer's own manuals demand. If following your rules exactly breaks your system, that's information about your rules, not a wrong the workers did.

(2) R said it's actually worse than a strike. A strike is declared, dated, visible — everyone knows the service has stopped. Work-to-rule keeps the appearance of normal operation while removing the substance, so patients and administrators can't tell what state the system is in. The concealment is the harm.

(3) N: there's no concealment when you announce it. The union put out a release and the local paper covered it on day two. And where does the duty to do more than the contract come from? The tacit extras — covering shifts, batching, staying ten minutes past the end of a case — were never paid for and never promised. That's goodwill, and goodwill is by definition withdrawable.

(4) R: in a hospital that goodwill margin *is* the safety buffer. It's the slack that lets the place absorb a bad day. Withdrawing it on purpose isn't the same as withdrawing your labor; you're pulling out redundancy other people are depending on, while still being counted as present and collecting full pay, which means nobody can price the risk you've introduced.

(5) N: so your complaint is that the hospital was under-resourced and the techs had been eating the shortfall for free. That's an argument for staffing ratios, not for an obligation to keep subsidizing the shortfall during a dispute. And notice the asymmetry — when management runs a "productivity improvement" that deletes slack, single-sources vendors, cuts weekend shifts, tracks badge-in times to the minute, nobody calls it deceptive or says they're withdrawing a buffer they owe anyone.

(6) R said that's a false equivalence: management owns the system and can be sued or voted out, whereas the techs are exercising power *inside* a system they don't own, using rules written for one purpose and putting them to another.

(7) N: written by whom? Their own union negotiated those job descriptions and checklists. If complying with them exactly is illegitimate, then the rules covertly encode a duty to work beyond the rules — which is precisely what a union exists to contest. Your reading makes the contract mean "the contract, plus whatever the system happens to need," and that isn't a contract.

(8) R conceded that much, but said it's a reason to go back and renegotiate the descriptions, not a reason to pretend to comply with them while emptying them out.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each step — rate each of the eight from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each — and then say which of them comes out ahead overall? I'm especially interested in whether (7) actually kills R's position or whether there's a version of it that survives, and in what both of them seem to be skating past: is there a real difference between withdrawing labor and withdrawing discretion, does deception need a false statement or just a misleading appearance, and does the fact that the leverage is being applied to patients who never agreed to anything change how the strike comparison in (2) and (5) is supposed to work.
