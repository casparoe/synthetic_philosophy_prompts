I'm on the board of a county historical society museum and we're opening a new permanent gallery in the spring about the closure of the Kestrel Works — a stamping and plating plant that employed about 4,000 people in a town of 30,000 until 1978, plus the twenty years of decline after. Everyone still living in this county has an opinion about why it closed and no two of them agree. The decision in front of us next month is narrower than that: the gateway panel at the entrance of the gallery has a hard limit of 120 words, it has to say something about why the plant shut, and there are three drafts on the table.

Draft A (our curator): one thread — the parent company's decision in March 1978 to consolidate stamping at a newer plant in another state, with everything else demoted to "background." Her argument is that the board minutes exist, that a person signed a memo, and that a museum is not a seminar.

Draft B (our head of education): a web graphic with four equal nodes and text saying the closure was overdetermined — corporate consolidation, the fourteen-week strike in 1977, the state effluent rule that made the plating line uneconomical to retrofit, and the industry-wide shift away from stamped steel. Her argument is that any single-cause story picks a villain, and that picking a villain is a political act whether you intend it or not.

Draft C (someone on the education committee): keep all four but rank them by contribution with a percentage bar chart, so visitors can see which mattered most.

Facts you should have. The plant's internal planning files from 1975–77 say in effect that any one of the four would have forced closure within a few years; those files were written by the plant manager to argue *for* more investment, so he had every motive to say the opposite of what they say, and that's part of why the staff trust them. In 1976 the town won a state tax abatement that made the plant competitive again on paper, and it closed two years later anyway. And each factor sits downstream of another one: the strike was a response to a speedup proposal, the effluent rule passed with the support of our own state's senator, and the consolidation decision was made against the backdrop of the materials shift.

What I need is the memo I'll circulate to the board, not a survey of the issues. Please:

1. Recommend one of the three, or construct a fourth, and say what the gateway panel may and may not claim within its 120 words. I don't need you to write the panel copy, I need the memo to settle the claim.

2. Tell us whether "overdetermined" is even the right word here, and if it isn't, what the gallery is actually asserting. There's a distinction I can feel but can't state: four factors that would each have been enough on their own is not the same thing as four factors that all mattered, and it's also not the same thing as four factors that each shaped how and when the outcome arrived. Draft B seems to slide between them. Sort that out in language a board member who hasn't taken philosophy since college can follow.

3. Say whether Draft C's percentages are coherent or nonsense. If they're coherent, say what would have to be true for a number like that to come out right. If they're nonsense, give me one paragraph on why, because that committee is going to keep pushing it.

4. Give the exhibit team a rule they can apply to the other eleven panels — something more useful than "acknowledge complexity." I have a half-formed working vocabulary in mind (trigger, background condition, enabling condition, response-to-something-else) but I don't know how to make it stick, or whether its the right cut.

5. Deal with the two fights, concretely. The retirees' union chapter has circulated a petition saying that naming the strike as a cause blames the workers for their own layoffs, and one of the signers sits on our board. Our largest living donor is the granddaughter of the executive who signed the consolidation memo, and she has hinted she'll pull a pledged gift if her grandfather is presented as the man who closed the plant. I do not want the memo to say we should listen to all stakeholders and seek common ground. Tell me what we owe each of them and what we don't.

6. Be honest about the strongest case for whatever you're rejecting. If you go against Draft A, take seriously that structures don't decide anything, that a person signed something, and that the version with a person in it may be truer to the documents we actually hold than the version with a web in it.

One more thing I keep circling, and I want the memo to address it head on because it might change the answer. If the four factors really were each sufficient, then not one of them made a difference to whether the plant closed at all. So maybe "why did it close?" is the wrong question for this gallery, and the question we can actually answer is "why 1978 and not 1985, and why this way?" — which is also the question the tax-abatement episode speaks to directly. Is that a better target for the gateway panel? Does it dissolve the union's objection, or the donor's, or neither? And can it be held in 120 words without turning into a shrug?

Aim for roughly 1,000–1,400 words, recommendation at the top, so the board members who read nothing else still know what they're being asked to approve. No bibliography needed — I'll handle anything that requires sourcing.
