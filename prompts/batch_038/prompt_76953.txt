ok so I've been reading about benefit systems and there's a thing I keep failing to put my finger on. In a lot of countries the same misfortune, losing your job say, gets handled by two completely separate machines.

Track one: if you've paid payroll or National Insurance type contributions over the last couple of years, you get an earnings-related insurance benefit. It's a fraction of your old wage. Nobody asks what your partner earns. Nobody asks what's in your savings account. Politicians across the spectrum defend it, and claimants themselves say things like "I paid into this, its mine, it's not charity." It runs out after roughly six months.

Track two: after that, or if your work history never qualified you, you go onto means-tested assistance instead. Now the whole household's finances get examined, there's a claimant agreement specifying hours of job search per week, money can be stopped for missing an appointment, there are time limits, and there's a sizeable administrative and journalistic industry devoted to people claiming fraudulently. The public rhetoric about this track is entirely about strivers and scroungers.

Same person, same income, same rent. Nothing about him changes between month six and month seven except that a contribution record runs dry, and yet he goes from "entitled" to "suspect."

What does this two-track design have to assume, philosophically? Not the political history, I mean what theory of entitlement would have to be true for the difference in treatment to make sense rather than look arbitrary. It seems to treat the insurance benefit as something like a property right earned by past payments, and the assistance benefit as a discretionary gift from the collective. But that can't be literally right, because the insurance system is pay-as-you-go: my contributions went to last year's claimants and pensioners, not into an account with my name on it. So what is "I paid for it" actually doing there? Is it tracking desert, reciprocity, contract, insurance in the actuarial sense, or something else entirely?

And be honest about where the practice fails to pin the theory down. I suspect the identical design could be defended without buying any of the moral story at all: incentive effects, stigma management, the fact that contributory schemes are politically harder to cut, whatever. So separate what the two-track system strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably. Also tell me whether the parts presuppose consistent things. The insurance track treats the claimant as an individual and ignores his family; the assistance track treats him as a node inside a household. The insurance track has work-search conditions and sanctions too, so it isn't simply the no-strings one. Does that break the theory, or is it historical residue the theory doesn't need?

Finally, if you dropped the contribution premise and built the whole thing on present need alone, what would have to change, and what would we lose that isn't just money?
