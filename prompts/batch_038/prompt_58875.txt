Third year running an optional evening session on Skolem's paradox for our joint honours maths-and-philosophy students, and the first two times it went off the rails. 75 minutes, blackboard, fourteen or so students. They've had one term of first-order logic: they know what a model is, they've seen the completeness theorem stated though not proved, and they all know Cantor's diagonal argument informally from a pop-maths book or first-year analysis. Nobody has seen Löwenheim–Skolem proved and I dont intend to prove it. Both previous years the failure mode was identical — about twenty minutes in somebody says "so there aren't really uncountably many reals, Cantor was just wrong," and the rest of the session turns into an argument about whether mathematics is made up. I can't do that a third time.

So plan the session for me rather than explaining the paradox to me. I want the dependency order: what has to be nailed down before Löwenheim–Skolem can even be stated without being misread — do they need the idea that "countable" might not be absolute *before* the theorem, or is producing that idea exactly what the theorem is for, and can I get away with asserting the theorem as a black box? Also where ZFC comes in: does it go first as the target, or last as the punchline? Then the example. I keep reaching for a countable model of ZFC itself, but I suspect that's too much structure to hold in your head at once, and I've been toying with warming them up on the real algebraic numbers as a countable model of the first-order theory of the reals, to get across that the elements the model actually has aren't the elements they were picturing. Tell me whether that earns its ten minutes or plants a wrong idea I'll then have to dig out, and tell me what the one carrying example has to have and what it has to lack.

Then the misconception map, in the order they'll actually show up, with the precise point in the sequence where each gets headed off. I can predict "so truth is relative" and "just use second-order logic then," and one of the reading responses this year said the paradox is about what humans can never know, which feels like a category error I wouldn't know how to fix in front of a room. Build in the confusion about whether the witnessing bijection exists-but-is-out-of-reach versus doesn't exist in the model at all, because I think that's precisely where "Cantor was wrong" is coming from. Give me one closing question whose answers would sort the room into got-it / half-got-it / confidently-wrong — not a definition recall. And be explicit about what I should refuse to touch, and say out loud that I'm refusing: I'm assuming the Henkin-style construction, forcing, and Putnam's version of the argument all go, but tell me if any of those is a case where mentioning it half-way does more damage than leaving it out entirely.
