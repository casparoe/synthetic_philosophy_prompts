I'm running a philosophy-of-math seminar and I handed out a chapter from a trade book as a gentle intro to Benacerraf. Here's the paragraph that's meant to state the dilemma:

"Benacerraf's dilemma is a choice between two ways of thinking about mathematical truth. Either mathematical statements are about abstract objects — numbers, sets — that sit outside space and time, in which case you get a nice uniform story about what makes '2 + 2 = 4' true, but no story at all about how flesh-and-blood creatures could ever know it, since knowing something requires being causally hooked up to it; or you give up the abstract objects, in which case you can explain mathematical knowledge as a matter of our conventions and practices, but you lose the idea that mathematical claims are true in the same robust way empirical ones are. Benacerraf pressed this hardest against the platonist: becuase there are endlessly many ways to identify the numbers with sets and nothing picks out one of them as the intended one, we can never be causally connected to 'the' numbers in particular, so platonism simply cannot explain mathematical knowledge. His conclusion — and the one most philosophers of mathematics have accepted ever since — is that realism about math has to go: arithmetic should be treated as a useful fiction, true inside the story we tell, and then we can get on with science."

It reads fine to me on first pass, but I don't want the students walking out with a tidy takeaway that isn't actually in Benacerraf, and I suspect more than one of his arguments is being run together somewhere in there. Go through it and tell me what holds up, what's a defensible simplification, and what a working philosopher of mathematics would push back on — including whether that last sentence about the field's verdict is anything like right, and which live responses to the dilemma get wiped out by framing this as a choice with an obvious winner.
