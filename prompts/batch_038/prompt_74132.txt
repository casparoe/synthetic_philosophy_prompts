For thirty-one years my aunt ran the forecasting unit at a state transportation department. She died in March, and I'm giving the eulogy. Her professional reputation rests almost entirely on a single call: in the late 1990s she testified that a bridge retrofit would overrun its budget by roughly a factor of three, and it did. Half the room will be planners, engineers, and economists who knew precisely how she worked — reference classes assembled from hundreds of comparable projects, base rates held against every proposal, and a standing refusal to be moved by the particulars of any one case, which she called the inside view's charm offensive. The other half is family, who knew someone warm, funny, and genuinely dangerous behind the wheel.

The difficulty I want the eulogy to solve is this: by her own lights, one spectacular hit proves nothing. She would have insisted that of a hundred confident forecasters a few will look like geniuses on luck alone, and that she had no way of knowing she wasn't one of them. I want to honor her by taking that seriously rather than waving it away — and yet a eulogy that concludes she was a coin that landed heads would betray the people in the room who planned their careers around her numbers. There's also a live dispute among her colleagues about whether she was really selecting reference classes at all or simply had an unteachable feel for which class a project belonged to; she never wrote her method down, and the speech may acknowledge that dispute without resolving it.

So write the eulogy, about five minutes spoken, under two constraints. First, no number may appear in it as evidence that she was good: she can be shown reasoning from figures, but the speech may not cite one to establish her excellence. Second, she must be rendered as particular rather than representative — whatever about her generalizes belongs to the base rate, and the listener should feel the gap between those two registers without ever being told what they are. It should be affectionate, it should land for the family, and it should not turn into a lecture on forecasting that the experts recognize as a lecture.
