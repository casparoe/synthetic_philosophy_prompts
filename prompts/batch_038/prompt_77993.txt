In our philosophy-of-history seminar we have spent three weeks on contingency claims — Cleopatra's nose, the argument that chaotic sensitivity makes every specific historical outcome a matter of luck, and the reply that agriculture, writing, and the state arose independently often enough to look convergent. Before I commit to a thesis, I want the specification rather than the contenders.

Write the desiderata for an account of historical contingency: the phenomena it must explain; the cases it must get right (including one in which an outcome really did turn on a trivial event, and one in which the same is asserted but nothing in the evidence distinguishes it); the distinctions it may not collapse — nomological determinism versus unpredictability, sensitivity to perturbation versus historical importance, contingency in the type of outcome versus contingency in its timing and route; and the results about sensitive dependence it cannot contradict. I am particularly interested in whether such an account can underwrite anything like a measure of contingency. My working idea is that contingency is a three-place relation among a cause, an outcome, and a scale of description, and that an adequate account should keep any measure built on it from coming out maximal everywhere; treat that as a candidate requirement and say whether it survives scrutiny.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp, give the argument if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and identify the single requirement that excludes the most accounts. Close with a brief paragraph on how the usual positions fare against the list.
