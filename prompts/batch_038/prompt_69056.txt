so last thursday I went to a public lecture at our natural history museum about the origins of farming, and afterwards two people I don't know — I'll call them Marcus and Ruth, they were at the next table in the cafe and got going — had an argument that ran for like forty minutes and I couldn't stop listening. Neither seemed to be a professional archaeologist but both had clearly read around the subject. Roughly in order:

1. Marcus: the agricultural revolution was a mistake, full stop. Skeletons from farming villages are shorter, have more tooth decay, more enamel defects from periods of stress, more infection-related lesions than the foragers before them, and that's before you count the work hours and the fact that crowding plus domestic animals handed us the epidemic diseases. Also they died young anyway, life expectancy around thirty, so most of them never got old enough for the wear-and-tear stuff to register. We traded a good life for more of a worse one.

2. Ruth: you can't call it a mistake, because nobody made it. It accumulated over something like two hundred generations, and every step was reasonable — you're already sedentary because the local food is good, you start tending the stands you like, population ticks up, now you have to feed the extra mouths. There was no moment where anyone stood at a fork in the road. "Mistake" needs a chooser.

3. Marcus: that's a ratchet, and ratchets are exactly what we call mistakes in groups all the time — overfishing, leaded petrol, irrigation salinizing a whole valley. No single chooser, still an avoidable bad outcome. And your no-fork-in-the-road bit is wrong: groups picked up cultivation and then dropped it, farmed only part of the year, or sat next to farming villages for centuries and declined the package. People evaluated it and said no. The door swung both ways.

4. Ruth: maybe. But then your comparison group is the problem. The foragers anthropologists actually measured live on land nobody else wanted and have been trading with farmers for a very long time, so the famous fifteen-to-twenty hour work week and all that leisure came from people in a situation that hadn't existed for millennia. And the numbers got worse when other people recalculated them, partly because the first counts left out food processing and cooking entierly. So your healthy foragers are half a mirage, and you're comparing bones to ethnography.

5. Marcus: fine on the leisure numbers, but you don't get affluence back just because that one cracked. And here's what you're dodging — if farming made individual lives worse but supported many more of them, and more lives worth living is a good, then it was a gain, not a mistake. You can't have it both ways. Either average wellbeing is the metric and I win, or total wellbeing is the metric and you have to admit a crowded, sicker, harder-working world with ten times the people in it was an improvement. Pick one.

6. Ruth: neither, because your average already has a body-count problem, and because I don't think the damage was mainly to bodies. What storage did was make surplus accumulable and defendable — granaries need guards, and guards turn into something else — so the real loss was the exit option disappearing. A forager who doesn't like how he's being treated walks away. A farmer with a field and a seed stock can't. That's the mistake: not the calories, the lock-in.

7. Marcus: except there were hierarchical foragers who never farmed — sedentary ones with big stored salmon runs and lavish burials — and there were large farming towns that stayed stubbornly flat for a thousand years. So your granary-to-chiefs chain is as loose as my skeleton chain, and at that point we're both just narrating. He then said the identical argument is running right now about whether governments should push people to have more children, Ruth called that a cheap move, and they both laughed and bought another coffee.

They noticed me scribbling and, embarrassingly, asked me to referee. So: can you go through it move by move? For each of the seven, say whether it's clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and why — including anywhere one of them states something as settled fact that isn't, because I'm pretty sure that happened and neither caught it. Then tell me which of the two did better overall, and settle the thing they never did: is "was the agricultural revolution a mistake?" even a well-formed question? If it has to be repaired before it can be answered, say exactly what needs pinning down first — which measure, which counterfactual, which time window, whose wellbeing — and what the answer looks like once you've pinned it down. Last, what did both of them leave out that a third person at that table should have raised? You don't need to give me sources or a reading list, I want your judgement on the arguments as they actually made them.
