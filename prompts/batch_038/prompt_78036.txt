I am writing an essay for a final-year social ontology module and have reached an impasse on a case study of my own choosing. What follows is a request for analysis rather than for a survey of the literature; I have read the standard positions and do not need them rehearsed except insofar as the episode puts pressure on them.

The episode. From 1 May 1970 the clearing banks in the Republic of Ireland — Bank of Ireland, Allied Irish Banks, Northern Bank and Ulster Bank — were shut by an industrial dispute for approximately six and a half months, the largest of the Irish bank closures between 1966 and 1976. Cheque clearing simply stopped. Rather than the payments system seizing up, uncashed cheques continued to circulate: they were tendered and accepted in ordinary transactions, sometimes passing through several hands before anyone thought to present one. Local publicans and shopkeepers, who knew their customers' means and habits, appear to have functioned as informal assessors of creditworthiness, so that a cheque's acceptability turned on standing within a face-to-face network rather than on bank verification. When the banks reopened the backlog was presented and settled, apparently with remarkably few items dishonoured. The Central Bank of Ireland did not erect an alternative settlement mechanism and was afterwards criticised for its passivity. The best-known economic treatment of all this is Antoin Murphy's 1978 study of money in an economy without banks. I have set out the facts I take to be established so that you need not worry about them; please do not spend words contesting them, but do tell me if any of them is being made to carry more weight than it can bear.

The theoretical lens. I want the episode run against the social-ontological account of money: money as a status function imposed by collective intentionality on some physical or inscriptive vehicle — Searle's scheme in which an X counts as a Y within a context C, and the Y carries deontic powers that exist only in virtue of being collectively recognised. As its rival, take the state theory of money in the tradition of Knapp, on which money is a creature of law, the unit of account being fixed by the sovereign's designation of what will discharge a debt.

What I want from you. First, a disciplined mapping. Identify X, Y and C for the cheques that circulated during the closure; say who the collective is on this story — the Irish public at large, the regulars of a particular pub, the customers of a particular shop — and whether the relevant deontic powers were conferred by anyone or merely presupposed by everyone. Second, assess the two theories against the mapped case: which features each explains, which each misdescribes, and, just as importantly, where each falls silent. I have in mind such silences as who actually bore the risk of non-payment, why the arrangement might work only in a small, dense, reputation-saturated economy, what became of people without local standing or of trades that could not be conducted on personal trust, and whether anything in either theory predicts the eventual settlement rather than merely accommodating it afterwards.

Third, and this is where I am stuck, consider a deflationary reading I have begun to suspect. It may be that this was never a case of money existing without institutions at all. What circulated were unsettled claims on the very banks that were shut, denominated in a unit of account — the Irish pound, then linked to sterling — that nobody ceased to accept, and sustained by the universal expectation that the items would eventually be cleared at par. On that reading the medium of exchange merely detached from the settlement machinery while the institutional kind itself remained wholly intact in everyone's expectations, and so the episode tests nothing whatever about whether a social kind could persist without the institutions that constitute it. If that is right, the case may in fact favour a third account — money as a hierarchy of claims anchored by a unit of account, or money as a credit relation — over both Searle and Knapp. I would like you to say whether the deflationary reading succeeds, and if it does not, exactly what it costs to resist it.

Please do not finish in equipoise. I want a verdict on what this episode shows about the social construction of money, argued for rather than announced. Then, separately, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or which further fact about the case would reverse your verdict, and where in your own reasoning you judge yourself most likely to have failed — including whether you have construed collective acceptance so thinly that no conceivable episode could falsify it.
