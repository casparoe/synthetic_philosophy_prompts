Our reading group — seven of us: two philosophy grad students, a couple of psychologists, one lawyer, and me (I do data work for a living and come at this from the side) — meets in the back room of a pub every second Thursday, and we've burned the last month going around in circles about famous thought experiments. The lawyer's line is that every objection to a thought experiment just amounts to "that's a leading question," which doesn't feel quite right to me. What I keep wanting to point at is a structural feature, so I've been drafting a metric and I want to see whether it survives being applied. I'm calling it stipulation load: how much of the intuitive verdict a case produces depends on things the author has to *tell* you to hold fixed, rather than things the story itself shows you. A case scores high if the reader's natural instinct to flesh it out with ordinary background — a plausible mechanism, a timescale, who else knows about it, what happens next Tuesday — would move or break the verdict, so the author has to keep saying "imagine none of that changes anything." A case scores low if you let the reader add every gloss they want and the verdict still survives. This is meant to be seperate from two things I don't want mixed into it: how sci-fi or outlandish the setup is, and how contested the conclusion is. I expect some wildly unrealistic cases to score low and some quite homely ones to score high, and if your scores come out showing that's impossible, that's a finding I want to hear, not a bug to smooth over.

Here's the list: Thomson's violinist, Searle's Chinese Room, Nozick's experience machine, Parfit's teletransporter, Jackson's Mary, Putnam's Twin Earth, Block's China-implements-a-brain, and Foot's original trolley case with the bystander at the switch. Rank them highest stipulation load to lowest and give each a number out of 10 — I want actual scores, not a vague ordering, and break ties in favour of whichever case has its load-bearing stipulation arriving earliest in the description, since that's the one the reader has to swallow before they've built up any investment in the story. For each, a sentence or two naming the single stipulation that does the most work and saying what a reader who refuses it ends up saying instead. I'm turning the table into a poster for the wall of the room, so keep the per-case notes tight enough to fit.

Then the part I actually care about. Tell me whether the metric is real. Does stipulation load genuinely come apart from "weirdness" and from "controversial conclusion," or is it a rebrand — another name for the charge that a thought experiment is underdescribed, or just a count of how many knobs the reader is being asked to hold still while the author turns the rest? If you think its redundant, say so plainly and say what it collapses into. If you think there's a real fourth thing here, give me the cleanest pair of cases from the list that separates it from the rivals, and I'll take that to the pub next Thursday.
