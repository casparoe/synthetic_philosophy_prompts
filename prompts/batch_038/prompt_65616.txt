I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine and I'm working on a piece about a hospital group that quietly started using a language model to draft the reasoning sections of its ethics-committee decisions — the committee still votes, but the "why" gets generated and then lightly edited. No one will admit to it on the record, which is a story in itself. The philosophy I need to get right is moral testimony, and there are two positions I keep seeing described in loose terms that I want compared properly. Position one: forming a moral belief on someone else's say-so is defective even when the belief is true and the source is reliable — not because you'll get it wrong, but because you don't grasp why it's right, you can't extend it to cases your informant never covered, and, the part that always gets emphasized, acting on a moral belief you picked up second-hand isn't the sort of thing that reflects well on you as a moral agent. Position two: there's nothing special about the moral case. Almost everything we believe morally we inherited from other people, exactly the way we believe almost everything we think about quarks or about the causes of the Irish famine, so demanding that each of us re-derive our moral views from first-hand reasoning is a hyper-individualist fantasy, and the squeamishness about moral deference is a disguised taste for a certain self-image rather than an epistemic insight.

Where I'm stuck is that a friend who does litigation has convinced me there's a third framing that might actually cut between the two: the hearsay rule. Courts exclude out-of-court statements offered for the truth of what they assert not because witnesses usually lie but because the factfinder can't see the declarant or test them — cross-examination is structurally unavailable, so the belief can't be interrogated by the very people who have to act on it. Then the law grows a huge pile of exceptions (statements against interest, business records, spontaneous exclamations) where surrounding circumstances substitute for cross-examination. Her claim is that the pessimists about moral testimony are basically running a hearsay objection, and that the "no special problem" camp is basically arguing that all moral testimony falls inside one of the exceptions. So please compare those two positions through that lens. Does the hearsay framing actually capture what the pessimist is worried about, or does it change the subject — the complaint about praise and moral worth, say, seems to have nothing at all to do with testability. And where does the analogy run out: could anything count as the moral equivalent of a well-founded hearsay exception, i.e. a marker that a moral claim is one you're entitled to swallow whole without being an abdicating passenger in your own judgement?

Last thing, and it matters because I'll have to say something in print: is the pessimist view the mainstream one or a minority heresy? Please seperate out what's actually settled or close to universally agreed among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus. If the honest answer is that both camps are fully live and it's a real stalemate, just say that plainly — my editor will accept a shrug, he won't accept a fake one.
