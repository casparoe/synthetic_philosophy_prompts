So I'm the faculty member on our library's archival review panel — six of us, two archivists, two faculty, somebody from the law school, and a community rep who gets appointed through the city's historical commission. Next Tuesday we're suppose to hand down a decision on one collection and I've been volunteered to circulate a written opinion beforehand. I could use a draft to react to.

The collection: in 1974 the university cleared a Black working-class neighborhood — call it the Bottoms — to build the medical center's east campus. Around the same time a sociology grad student, working on his own time with borrowed equipment, recorded something like 90 interviews with people who lived there: the streets, the churches, the eviction notices, the going rate for a house nobody would buy. He donated the reels to us in 1988, we digitized them in 2019 and put them online behind a finding aid. Almost nothing else survives. The city's records mention these families only in demolition permits and tax rolls, and the university's own files treat the neighborhood purely as a real estate problem. So the tapes are, functionally, the only voice recordings of the place that exist anywhere.

Now a group calling itself the Bottoms Descendants Assembly has asked us to take the whole collection offline. Their reasons, roughly: (1) several narrators were minors in 1974, and the consent forms are one-pagers saying the material may be used "for research purposes," which nobody living can tell you what that was understood to cover; (2) the interviews were given to an outsider while the neighborhood was being actively taken apart around them, which they say isn't consent in any sense worth the name; (3) most pointedly, the interviewer's widow told one of our archivists last spring that at least two narrators asked him, on and off the tape, to destroy their recordings. She remembers him saying so. There's no note, no letter, nothing in the accession file, and the finding aid affirmatively states that every narrator signed a release. The interviewer died in 2006. We can't verify the claim and we can't rule it out.

Meanwhile the neighborhood historical society — different people, some overlapping families, some not — has written asking us to keep everything up and to stop treating the Assembly as the only voice of the community. Our policy says collections touching identifiable communities should be managed "in consultation with that community," and says nothing whatsoever about what to do when the community is visibly split about what it wants.

One of our archivists has floated a fourth option I keep turning over: withdraw the individual recordings where there's a credible request, but leave a placeholder entry in the finding aid for each one — name, date, running length, and a note that the item was withdrawn at request on such-and-such a date — so the absence is documented rather than simply vanishing. Half the panel thinks that's the only honest solution. The other half thinks its a more sophisticated silence, because we'd be cataloguing an erasure in categories the institution picked, and making a withdrawn narrator's name more searchable than their voice ever was.

Draft me the memo I'll circulate — call it 1,000 to 1,300 words, something people will actually read before the meeting. I need: a bottom line up front with a concrete disposition covering the physical tapes, the online access, and the finding aid, plus a timeline we could commit to in public; the reasoning, written so that the two archivists who disagree with each other could both answer it in the room; a genuine engagement with the strongest case for taking everything down, not a strawman; a section on what we do about the widow's unrecorded claim given that we can neither confirm it nor ignore it — including whether an unverifiable oral request can obligate an archive at all, and what we owe the narrators whose names come up in it; and a paragraph on whether the placeholder-entry idea survives the objection that it just documents the silence in a nicer format. No citations or reading list, please — I want arguments, not a bibliography. And don't invent facts about this case beyond what I've given you; if you need an assumption to get to a recommendation, flag it as an assumption.
