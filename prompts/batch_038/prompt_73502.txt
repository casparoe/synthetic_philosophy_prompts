Something I keep chewing on with the Condorcet jury theorem — can you build me a concrete example?

The way the independence assumption usually gets discussed is social: jurors shouldn't deliberate, shouldn't see each other's ballots, shouldn't herd. The worry is influence. But what the theorem's arithmetic actually needs is unconditional probabilistic independance of the votes, and that can break even when the jurors never touch each other at all. A common cause does it just as well: shared training, shared upstream evidence, or simply the fact that some of the questions landing in front of the panel are hard for all of them in the same way.

So give me an example that makes the gap vivid. A specific panel, with actual numbers — pick the size and the probabilities yourself — where all four of these hold at once:

1. no juror causally influences any other: separate rooms, simultaneous ballots, zero information about what anyone else voted;
2. taken one at a time, each juror is more likely to be right than wrong about the question;
3. if you take that individual reliability and plug it into the theorem the way a textbook would, the majority looks close to infallible;
4. but there's a real chunk of cases where the majority does worse than a single juror would have done — ideally worse than a coin flip — and nobody in the room could tell from the inside that they're in that chunk.

Show the arithmetic for the key comparisons, not just the punchlines. Then tell me which assumption is really doing the work when the guarantee fails, and whether the obvious repair — defining competence conditional on the type of case rather than averaged across all cases — actually buys you anything worth having. If it doesnt, say that plainly rather than hedging.

No citations or reading list needed, I just want the worked example and the reasoning. American English, please.
