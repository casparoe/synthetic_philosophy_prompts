There's a session I'm running at a workshop in the spring — two days on animal moral status, and the room is going to be a genuinely weird mix: a couple of comparative neuroanatomists, some welfare scientists who work on fish and on insects, and a handful of philosophers who mostly do population ethics. The session is "is brain size morally relevant," and I want to hand people a classification instrument before the arguing starts so everybody can see where they're actually standing.

The problem is that question is at least four different questions tangled together, and people in this room will answer yes and no to different ones while sincerely believing they disagree with each other. So build me the logical space, not a list of views with names attached.

What I need:

1. Start by pulling out the axes. I have three in mind, but tell me if these are the right cuts or if you'd slice it differently:
   - whether a neural metric is supposed to be *constitutive* of moral relevance or only *evidence* for it;
   - whether having more of the relevant capacity changes *whether* a being's interests count at all, *how much* they count, or *which* interests it has in the first place — a creature that can dread next Tuesday has an interest that a creature which can't simply lacks, and that's a different claim from "its suffering counts double";
   - which metric is even a candidate: absolute brain mass, brain-to-body ratio or an encephalization-style quotient, total neuron count, neuron density, amount of one particular kind of tissue, or nothing neural whatsoever.

2. Then the grid. Rows = constitutive / evidential / neither, columns = status / weight / inventory-of-interests. Nine cells. For each one: is it occupied, and if so what's the view called — if nobody has a name for it, say "unnamed" and describe it in a sentence. For the empty cells, diagnose why they're empty: incoherent, coherent but repellent, or just nobody's gotten around to it. I suspect at least two of the empties aren't really empty and I'd want you to say so rather than leave them blank.

3. For each occupied cell, one sentence on the commitment that's going to be hardest to defend in front of both neuroanatomists and insect people. Specific, please — not "vulnerable to counterexamples," but the actual counterexample: the pinhead-sized animal that behaves like it has an inner life, the enormous brain that's almost entirely motor control for a big body, the animal with a huge number of neurons crammed into a tiny volume, the lineage where size and organization come completely apart from anything phylogenetic.

4. This is the part I care most about. Take the metric axis and write out the case for and against each candidate the way a neuroanatomist in that room would actually make it — including the worry that brain-to-body scaling is mostly a story about running a body rather than about being clever, and the worry that our neuron counts were calibrated on one class of animals in a way that makes cross-phylum comparison shakier than people assume. Then tell me which two metrics are so entangled that my grid can't separate someone who picks one from someone who picks the other. I'd much rather flag that up front than pretend the instrument is sharper then it is.

5. Last: five yes/no questions for a slip of paper at the start of the session, each built on a stipulated creature. Invent the animals — that's deliberate, I dont want people answering from what they happen to know about octopuses, I want them answering from what they'd say about a brain and a set of behaviors. Give me the key too: which answer patterns land in which cells. No question should use the word sentience, and none should let someone answer by recognizing a label.

Length is up to you but I'd rather have a tight grid than a long essay. And if you think one of my three axes is a fake distinction that collapses as soon as you push on it, cut it and tell me what you'd put in its place instead of quietly building around it.
