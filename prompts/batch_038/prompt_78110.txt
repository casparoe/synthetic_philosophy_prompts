I attend, as a non-voting observer, the pharmacy and therapeutics committee of the health system where I work. Last Thursday the committee took up a request to list adult lactose intolerance as a covered condition for lactase enzyme therapy and for prescribed medical nutrition therapy. The cost analysis was uncontroversial; what consumed the meeting was a prior question, argued out between two voting members — Haddad, a clinical epidemiologist, and Lindqvist, a family physician who did philosophy before medicine — namely whether the condition is a disease at all. A friend of mine was in the room too, and the two of us have re-litigated the exchange at length twice since and gotten nowhere. Before I form a view of my own, I would like a neutral referee. From my notes, as accurately as I can render it:

(1) Haddad opened by arguing the request should be denied as a treatment for disease, because adult lactase non-persistence is not a disease. Most of the world's adults lose much of their lactase activity after weaning; that down-regulation is the species-typical pattern, and persistent lactase production is the derived variant that spread in dairying populations. On the Boorse-style account he endorsed — health as normal functional ability, disease as an internal state that depresses some function below the typical level for the relevant reference class — there is no dysfunction here. The patient is not ill; she is an entirely typical human being in a food environment her species did not evolve into.

(2) Lindqvist replied that this verdict is a reductio of the account, not a ground for denial. The patients in question present with abdominal pain, bloating, osmotic diarrhea, restrictive eating, and sometimes nutritional and social consequences. A theory of disease that sorts all of that under the heading of health has been purchased by giving up whatever connection the concept had to the reason medicine has it.

(3) Haddad said the theory's job is precisely to say which of the many genuinely bad states are medical, and it does that by looking at functional design rather than at amount of suffering. Misfortune is not illness: if distress sufficed for disease, then poverty, bereavement, short stature, and unattractiveness would be diseases. He added that his account handles environmental variation without appealing to value at all — sickle cell trait is deleterious in some environments and protective in others, G6PD deficiency is silent until someone eats fava beans or takes primaquine, phenylketonuria is catastrophic only against a diet containing phenylalanine. Same genotype, different environment, different disease status, no evaluation required.

(4) Lindqvist said that this concedes her point rather than answering it. If disease status turns on the environment, it turns on a description of the environment that only makes sense relative to someone's interests. Which environment fixes the reference class? Haddad nominates the ancestral one, but nothing in the biology nominates it for him. Her patient lives here, eats here, and her gut cannot do what guts in this food supply need to do; the selection of the reference class is a choice, and choices of that kind are where the values enter, unannounced.

(5) Haddad answered that reference classes are not selected by interest but fixed by age and sex, exactly as in any table of normal values in clinical pathology, and that frequency is not the operative criterion anyway — failure of a mechanism to do what it was built to do is. Lactase down-regulation after weaning is what the mechanism was built to do, so there is no failure. He contrasted hypertension: also common, also asymptomatic for years, but genuinely impairing a regulatory mechanism's capacity to perform its job, which is why his account treats it as disease and hypolactasia not. Lindqvist's talk of what guts here need to do, he said, quietly swaps an evolutionary notion of function for a causal-role notion, and once function means only what a part currently does or is currently needed for, nothing can malfunction, because whatever a system does is what it is for.

(6) Lindqvist said she would make the swap explicit and adopt the hybrid view instead: a disorder requires both a failure of function and a harm judged bad by the surrounding culture. On that view the condition qualifies, since the harm is obvious and treatable and the functional failure exists at the level medicine actually works at. She added that the pure naturalist position is either untenable inside a body deciding coverage, because it treats the harm as irrelevant, or it smuggles the harm back in through the ends it takes as given — survival and reproduction. Why are those the organism's ends rather than, say, the individual's continued flourishing or her capacity to pursue her projects? Because they suit the theorist's picture of biological success. That is a normative selection in laboratory dress.

(7) Haddad said the ends are nobody's selection; they are what function means in biology, since a trait is there because it contributed to the propagation of the lineage. He then granted the hybrid for argument's sake and pressed it: the dysfunction clause in Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis is still the naturalist clause, asking whether an evolved mechanism failed to perform its natural function, and on that view harms without dysfunction are explicitly not disorders. Hypolactasia involves no such failure. So Lindqvist, he said, is invoking the hybrid's name while discarding its naturalist half.

(8) Lindqvist said then the real dispute is over the level of description at which natural function is read, and that fixing it at the ancestral environment is a stipulation without a principled defense, since an organism's mechanisms admit of many levels and the level that predicts and treats is the one medicine has always used. She closed by pointing out what the committee is in fact doing: writing a benefits schedule, not settling a metaphysical question. The term disease in the plan document is stipulative, and the only defensible way to stipulate it is by reference to the interests the plan exists to serve — which is the normativist thesis, true in the one place where it needs to be true.

(9) Haddad's last remark was that if it is stipulation all the way down, he will stipulate narrowly, because a schedule that covers everything harmful is not a schedule, and he would like to hear why his stipulation is worse rather than merely different. Lindqvist did not take the question.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each numbered move, assign a rating on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with two or three sentences of justification tied to what was actually said. Then answer these specific questions: does the reference-class objection in (4) land against the position stated in (1), or does the reply in (5) block it; is the charge in (6) that survival and reproduction are normatively selected ends a genuine objection or a misdescription of how the biological notion of function works; is (7) correct that the hybrid account, applied consistently, delivers the verdict Haddad wants, or is Lindqvist entitled to read dysfunction at the level she proposes; and is the move to stipulation in (8) an escape or a surrender. Say finally who came out ahead overall, what neither of them addressed — including any error either made about the biology of lactase persistence — and whether any of this changes how the committee ought to have voted. I am not looking for a survey of the literature on the disease concept; I want these arguments weighed as they were made.
