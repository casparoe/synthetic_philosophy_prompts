Been going down a rabbit hole on the 1977 episode where Popper publicly backed off his long-standing verdict that natural selection wasn't proper science -- the tautology business, where fitness gets defined as differential reproductive success and "survival of the fittest" collapses into saying that those who survive, survive. He'd been arguing for a couple of decades that the theory was really a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable one, and then he gave the lecture that got published as Natural Selection and the Emergence of Mind, where he said he'd changed his mind about its testability and scientific status -- apparently after working biologists told him he was wrong about their own field.

I want this analyzed as falsificationism applied reflexively. Who are actually the players -- Popper, the evolutionary biologists in the room, the students who'd built softer versions of the criterion -- and what exactly was under test? Is the falsifiability criterion itself the hypothesis that got refuted by one bad application, or is that a category error, since a demarcation rule isn't the kind of thing an empirical case can falsify? Map out what the criterion said Popper ought to have done when the pushback came, what it says about the retraction (was he rationally required to retract, or was it just the prudent move for a man whose whole philosophy is about learning from mistakes), and where it goes silent.

Then run it the other way: which features of the episode does falsificationism explain well, which does it garble, and does Lakatos's research-programme framing -- progressive vs. degenerating, instead of asking whether a slogan is analytic -- handle it better? What I most want pinned down is whether the original charge was ever a claim about biology at all, or a claim about a catchphrase that no one in the field was using as their theory. Flag anything you're shaky on historically instead of filling it in.
