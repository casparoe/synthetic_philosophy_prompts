Eight months ago the government I served under for a decade was replaced, and I have been seconded to the interim panel that must decide, instrument by instrument, which of the old regime's enactments and administrative acts continue to be applied until a new legislature exists. One of those acts is the reason my brother lost his professional licence; I have declared the conflict and stayed on the panel anyway, which is part of why I need the procedure to sit outside my own intuitions.

Our brief is narrower than the famous version of this problem. We are almost never asked whether anyone should be punished for doing what was then permitted. We are asked whether to unwind: licences granted and revoked, property transfers, disciplinary findings, qualifications, benefits already paid. In every such case some third party relied in good faith on an act they did not endorse, and often still depends on it.

Please write a decision procedure the panel can run on a single instrument in under an hour, with two members, at least one of whom is not a lawyer, and no recourse to the legislature. I want the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the available dispositions — continue in force, suspend pending substantive review, treat as never having been valid — and the conditions that select among them; and an explicit stage at which reliance interests are weighed, kept separate from the stage at which the instrument's own character is assessed. Ground it in whatever the argument about the internal demands of legality actually yields about when a duly promulgated rule fails to be law in the sense that obliges obedience, and treat those demands as tests applied to a document, telling me which a non-lawyer can run in an hour and which she cannot.

Then apply it to three cases: (a) a published, generally applied licensing rule that excluded one ethnic group from a profession; (b) a vaguely worded public-order regulation under which a single official made an unreviewable determination that cost my brother his licence; (c) an ordinary building-code amendment, technically sound, on which a thousand completed private constructions now depend. Case (c) should show reliance doing independent work rather than the procedure merely tracking how objectionable a rule's content was, and case (b) should not come out in my favour simply because I want it to.

Finish by marking where the procedure stops being a procedure — where it frames a choice among values rather than resolving one — and by saying what this kind of triage systematically gets wrong.
