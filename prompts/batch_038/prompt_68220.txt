I sit on a twelve-person civic awards board and once a month we decide who gets the town's "above and beyond" medal. It's basically a supererogation commitee, though nobody there would use that word. The trouble is we've never written down a standard, so every vote turns into an argument about what our own past votes mean. Our record over about four years:

- Off-duty firefighter, stopped at a car fire on her way home, pulled two kids out, second-degree burns. Medal. The minute says her shift was over and nothing required her to stop.
- ICU nurse who stayed four unpaid hours through a mass-casualty intake. No medal, just a letter. Minute: "that's the job."
- Kidney donated to a stranger. Medal.
- Kidney donated to a stranger where a broker paid the donor $30,000. No medal. Minute: "we don't medal a transaction."
- 15-year-old who went into a flooded storm drain after somebody's dog and nearly drowned. Medal, and the minute specifically praises the risk he ran rather than what was at stake.
- Guy who had the only working boat in town during a flood, ran it for six days, brought out maybe forty people. No medal. Minute: "when you're the only one who can, it isn't optional."
- Woman who fostered fourteen kids over twenty years while working two jobs. Medal.
- Paramedic who ran three red lights and clipped a car getting an overdose patient to the ER; the patient lived. No medal. Minute: "the medal isn't for rule-breaking, however it turns out."
- Man who anonymously paid off every student's lunch debt in the district, around ninety grand. Medal, but 7-5, and the recorded dissent says that if we medal this we're telling the other two hundred people in town with the same bank account that they did nothing.

Here's the nomination we take up Thursday and I can't tell which way our own record points.

A line cook at an airport restaurant, twenty-five minutes into a Friday rush, notices an unaccompanied six-year-old being walked toward the exit by an adult who isn't holding his hand and whose story doesn't match what the kid is saying. She leaves the line without telling anyone where she's going, follows them into the parking garage, and stands in front of their car claiming to be the kid's aunt until an officer wanders over. The man turns out to be a non-custodial parent under a court order, mid-attempt to take the boy out of state. She was fired for abandoning the line and was out of work for eleven months. Two months after that a local station gave her a ten-thousand-dollar "hometown hero" award she never applied for. When people ask why she did it she says her own brother was taken from a store when she was nine and nobody stopped it.

What should we vote if we want to be consistent with ourselves rather than with how the story makes us feel? I want: the candidate rules our nine precedents actually support (there's more than one, and I think at least two of them point in opposite directions on this woman), what each of those rules predicts about her, which single precedent is the closest analog and where the analogy breaks, whether the boat case is doing any work here or whether people on our board are just misreading it as a diffusion rule, and whether the ten thousand dollars and the brother story are relevant at all given what we said about the paid kidney. If our record genuinely doesn't decide it, say so plainly and tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with a yes and what with a no, because I'd like to walk in knowing which one I'm signing up for. And then name the next nomination that would come along that would finally tell us which rule we've been running — I'd rather settle this on a hypothetical than in a public argument in front of the nominee's family.
