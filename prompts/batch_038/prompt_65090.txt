I am a fourth-year PhD student in macroeconomics working on automation and the functional distribution of income, and my professional vocabulary is fairly well defined: growth accounting and the Solow residual; aggregate production functions and the Cambridge capital controversy over reswitching and the coherence of an aggregate capital stock; the task-based framework in which capital and labor are allocated across a continuum of tasks, with automation understood as the migration of tasks from one factor to the other; endogenous growth models in which ideas are nonrival and partially excludable; and the recent empirical literature on the decline of the labor share and the rise of markups. My outside reader is a historian of economic thought who has been pressing me to engage Marx's account of machinery as an argument rather than as a period piece, and I would rather do that seriously than wave at it. So please take that account — both the third-volume claim that mechanization raises the ratio of constant to variable capital and thereby exerts a downward pressure on the rate of profit, subject to the list of offsetting influences he supplies, and the better-known notebook passage in the Grundrisse where he suggests that as accumulated social knowledge becomes embodied in the machine system, direct labor time ceases to be an adequate measure of wealth and production resting on exchange value is consequently undermined — and restate it, as faithfully as you can manage, in the terms I actually work in. I want the mapping made explicit rather than impressionistic: what plays the role of the organic composition of capital, what corresponds to the property of living labor that it can create more value than it costs, what the general intellect becomes if it becomes anything at all (a stock of nonrival knowledge? a shift in the task frontier? something with no home in my framework?), and how the alleged collapse of the value form would have to be phrased, if it could be phrased at all, in the language of factor shares, markups, and task displacement.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Item by item, say whether each element translates cleanly, whether it translates only at the price of a distortion (and specify what the distortion costs me), or whether it has no expression in my framework whatsoever. I have three specific worries. First, whether the distinction Marx needs between value and price survives the translation as anything more than an unmodeled assumption that does no work. Second, whether the offsetting influences he enumerates against the falling rate of profit turn out to be nothing other than my own productivity, reallocation, and cheapening-of-the-wage-basket effects under a different name — in which case the tendency and its counteractions are both just comparative statics I already have. Third, and most interesting to me, whether the two arguments can be translated consistently with one another: the third-volume claim is that mechanization erodes the rate of profit, while the notebook passage seems to say that mechanization dissolves the measure of value altogether, and my historian colleague insists the tension between these is only apparent. Tell me whether a faithful translation bears him out or exposes a real incompatibility. Finally, I want your verdict on the residue itself. My working suspicion, which you should attack if you think it is wrong, is that once the labor theory of value is removed, the machinery argument collapses into an endogenous growth story plus a claim about who appropriates the surplus, and that the collapse thesis asks for nothing my framework cannot already express. Either confirm that, or show me where it fails — for instance, whether growth accounting has any place at all for the difference between a productivity gain that enlarges output and a productivity gain secured by altering bargaining power over a given output; whether a model that takes final goods rather than labor time as its numéraire is structurally incapable of representing certain instabilities; or whether the concept of a cost that is paid by someone but never appears in any price is inexpressible in my terms. And keep the translation honest throughout: do not smuggle in my framework's commitments — competitive factor markets, well-defined marginal products, a stable aggregate capital stock, a representative agent — and then credit or discredit Marx for anticipating or missing their consequences. If one of those commitments is precisely what he would reject, say so, and say what happens to the translation at that exact point.
