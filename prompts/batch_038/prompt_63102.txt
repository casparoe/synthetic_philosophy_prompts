Every fall my phil-of-science seminar hits the week on multiple discovery — Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, Lobachevsky and Bolyai, the whole oxygen business with Priestley and Scheele and Lavoisier — and every fall, without fail, somebody says "well, it was in the air," everybody nods, and we move on. This year a grad student of mine decided the slogan settles two seperate questions at once, and I want to push back without just sounding like I'm defending great-man history.

Here's what he argued, in order: (1) since discoveries keep coming in pairs and threes, the discovery was inevitable — the air was loaded, so to speak; (2) if it was inevitable, the Newton–Leibniz priority dispute is a non-issue, just a squabble about who happened to reach the finish line first; (3) and further, since the content of the discovery was fixed by the problem situation, nothing about the individual shaped what got discovered — the discoverer merely transcribed what the air already contained — so we shouldn't be naming theorems after people at all. A senior colleague in the room muttered the same phrase a few minutes later, but I'm fairly confident he meant something much weaker, something about how the documentary record is a mess and we simply can't establish who got there first.

So: take "it was in the air" apart for me. I want a menu of the distinct precise claims it could be making — starting from whatever modest statistical observation Merton's multiples actually licenses, moving through conditional-probability claims about the state of background knowledge, counterfactual claims (and note that those need a time bound and a specified replacement: someone would have done it *by when*, drawn from *what pool*?), claims about convergence of content rather than mere simultaneity of discovery, and the epistemic reading my colleague was probably making. For each entry, tell me what exactly it asserts; whether it's a platitude everyone already accepts, a substantive empirical thesis, or something false or not really intelligible; what would count as evidence for and against it; and whether it gets my student to (2) or to (3), or to neither.

Also flag every place where the slogan's persuasiveness depends on sliding between the token discovery (this manuscript, that year, that notation) and the type (a formalism for rates of change; a theory of descent with modification), becuase I suspect that slide is carrying most of the weight. Leibniz's notation and Newton's fluxions are not obviously the same object, and neither are Darwin's and Wallace's later positions — say whether that helps or hurts each reading.

End with which reading, if any, lets me concede (2) to him while refusing (3) — or tell me that's not a stable place to stand and I should resist both.

One page, list form, no citations or reading list; I'm handing it out as the reading for the week.
