Right, so — I'm on the naming and monuments sub-committee of a fairly boring English district council (don't ask, it was a raffle at a residents' association meeting three years ago and I've regretted it ever since), and next month we have to recommend whether to rename Crace Row. It's named after Martha Crace, a Victorian philanthropist who raised most of the money for the town's waterworks and later chaired the Board of Guardians that ran the workhouse infirmary under a per-head contract; the local history society has dug up letters suggesting she knew the mortality returns were being massaged, since the payments didn't vary with outcomes. She did, though, genuinely bring clean water to the place. Cholera stopped. So the meeting is going to be grim and I want to turn up with my own thoughts in some kind of order rather than just reacting to whoever shouts loudest.

Could you write me a dialogue — call it 1,200 to 1,500 words — between two members of the committee who disagree about what to do? The thing I care about most is that they actually argue: each one responds to what the other just said, concedes the points that deserve conceding, and at least once lands something the other has no immediate answer to. No strawmen, no one being made to sound thick, and please don't let either of them win outright. Both should be well read and both should want the town to do the right thing.

The spine I'd like them to fight over is a distinction I came up with on the bus, and I've no idea whether it's standard in the literature or whether I've reinvented something, so treat it as my coinage rather than as a term of art: verdict-commemoration versus loan-commemoration. On the verdict view, naming a street is a one-off settled judgement about a person's standing, made at the moment of naming by the people who were in a position to make it, and thereafter it's just an address — reopening it turns every public name into an open case file. On the loan view, honour is a standing credit the public extends and can call in, so the name remains a live act of endorsement for as long as it's on the sign, and the question is never what Crace's contemporaries thought but whether we're willing to keep lending. Have one character hold each, and have them push hard on where the two come apart in practice: does the loan view collapse into a permanent renaming tribunal, does the verdict view hand a moral monopoly to whoever happened to be on the council in 1897, and — this is the bit I keep going round in circles on — what either view says about the compromise the history society has proposed, which is to keep the name and add a plaque mentioning the infirmary contract. Is a plaque a genuine solution or is it commemoration with a disclaimer attached, a footnote nobody reads at knee height?

I'd also like them to treat two unglamorous constraints as philosophically load-bearing rather than as background noise. One is the replacement problem: renaming means naming after somebody else, and any candidate we pick is also a person with a life and a paper trail, so are we just shunting the difficulty one street along? The other is the address problem: two hundred households, solicitors' deeds, deliveries, blue-light callouts, and a row of people who mostly don't care either way but resent the faff enormously. If one of the characters thinks the faff is itself morally relevant and not merely inconvenient, I'd like to hear the argument for that.

You're the expert here — someone who's thought seriously about monuments, street names, public apology and reparative renaming — but please don't turn it into a lecture or drop in citations and further reading, I can't chase any of that up anyway. Before you start writing, do a bit of visible brainstorming: sketch three or four genuinely different positions a thoughtful committee member could take (at least one of which shouldn't just be "keep it" or "get rid of it"), say which two you reckon will produce the best friction, and then write the dialogue. Finish with a short paragraph on which side you found more persuasive once you'd argued both, and what specific thing would move you — I know you're meant to be even-handed, but I'd rather have your actual view than a shrug.
