I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am reporting a story about proposals to write individual protections directly into automated decision systems — scheduling software, content platforms, benefit-eligibility engines — so that each user has at least one binary choice the system is forbidden to override. The economist I interviewed most closely told me, with evident satisfaction, that the entire enterprise is formally incoherent. He said this follows from Sen's Paretian liberal result, but the version he gave me was generalized well past anything Sen argued for, and it is the generalized version I cannot shake. Here it is, as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

(1) To say that person A has a right over a binary issue of his own, with alternatives x and y, is to say: in every admissible profile of preferences, if A prefers x to y, then society ranks x above y — whatever anyone else prefers, and whatever else is at stake.

(2) Unanimity: if everyone prefers x to y, society ranks x above y.

(3) Whatever society's verdict is, it is an ordering: complete and transitive.

(4) Domain: every logically possible profile counts as admissible. No preference is disqualified in advance, including preferences about other people's protected issues.

(5) Take the smallest society capable of hosting a right at all: two people, A and B, one binary private issue each. A alone decides the colour of his own door, B alone decides the colour of hers. Four states: RR, RB, BR, BB.

(6) One admissible profile does all the damage — the nosy one. Let A rank BR > RB > BB > RR, and B rank BB > BR > RB > RR. A's right governs the pair {RB, BB}, the states in which B's door is blue; he prefers RB, so society ranks RB above BB. B's right governs {BR, BB}; she prefers BB, so society ranks BB above BR. By transitivity, society ranks RB above BR. But both of them rank BR above RB, so unanimity ranks BR above RB.

(7) Hence no ordering of these four states satisfies (1)–(4). Two people, each with one trivial private decision, admit no rights-respecting, unanimity-respecting ranking at all.

(8) Notice what (5)–(7) never used: the content of the issues, the stakes, the number of alternatives, the sophistication of the agents. The construction is purely structural — it concerns the logical form of a protected pair. Any assignment of any binary protected issue to any two agents generates it, provided only that preferences about other people's protected issues are admissible.

(9) A scheme that assigns no protected issues is not a candidate for the description, and unanimity is the weakest efficiency condition on offer, since abandoning it licenses outcomes everyone agrees are worse. So (8) is not a curiosity but a general result: no arrangement whatever, however small or trivial, can both vest protected choices in individuals and respond to unanimous judgement. Rights are not in tension with the common good at the margins. The very idea of a protected individual choice is incompatible with collective rationality.

That last sentence is where I stop being able to follow him, and I want to know whether my unease has a principled source rather than a merely temperamental one. My two flatmates paint their own doors. In the nosy profile one of them ends up with a door he ranks below the alternative, which strikes me as regrettable, even self-defeating, but not incoherent, and certainly not a refutation of the claim that each of them had a right to decide. Yet by (8) the same structure governs a household, a constitution, and a platform's terms of service, and I am not prepared to print that.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (1) and (2), because they look like what the words right and improvement mean, and I would like to keep (4) as well, since I suspect that disqualifying nosiness by definition is a stipulation wearing a premise's clothes — but tell me if I am wrong about that. If the escape instead lies in re-describing a right, as A's authority to select from a menu from which B's door has been removed rather than as a guarantee about where society ranks things, say whether that redescription genuinely dissolves (6) or merely relocates it, and say what does the selecting once a right has narrowed the menu. Finally, tell me whether anything substantive is lost in the crossing from "no ordering satisfies these conditions" to "no possible arrangement does," because (9) appears to make that crossing without noticing, and I suspect it is where the argument earns its absurdity.
