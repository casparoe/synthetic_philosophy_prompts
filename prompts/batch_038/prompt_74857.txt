In three weeks I am presenting a dissertation chapter at a comparative constitutional law workshop. The room will be mostly French public lawyers who treat laïcité as the benchmark of state neutrality, two German Staatskirchenrecht scholars who are protective of the cooperative model but distrust Anglo-American free-exercise reasoning, and nobody who works on English establishment.

My thesis is that the German church-tax regime is a better model of separation for religiously plural societies than the French one: affiliation there is a matter of publicly registered individual choice with a workable exit mechanism, and support flows to every body granted public-law corporation status rather than being withheld from all. The centerpiece is a concept I call exit friction — what it actually costs a dissenter, in fees, lost sacraments, and lost access to church-run welfare provision and employment, to leave a recognized community, weighed against what it costs a community that laïcité declines to recognize at all. I expect the sharpest fire on the churches' privileged position as employers, on the historical state payments that have never been redeemed, and on the gap between the parity principle and how few Muslim bodies hold that status.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, phrased in the idiom of the tradition each would come from and ranked by how damaging they are; for each, the hinge it turns on, a ninety-second reply, and the premise I should concede; a flag on any objection that turns only on a different sense of separation or neutrality; and finally the two questions I am hoping nobody asks, with an account of why they would be hard to survive.
