She works on pain and its phenomonology, and her view is roughly this: what makes a pain bad for you isn't the nociceptive signal, and isn't some felt quality of unpleasantness stapled onto that signal either, but what she calls the pain's grip — the extent to which the experience commandeers your attention and your planning and makes you want it to stop. Her favorite exhibit is pain asymbolia, the lesion patients who'll tell you a burn feels painful while smiling and reaching back toward the thing that burned them. On her account those people have pains that have lost their grip, so their pains aren't bad in the sense that matters, and she thinks this buys her a unified treatment of a lot of other cases: the chronic pain patient after a cingulotomy who reports the pain is still present but no longer troubles him, the person on opioids saying more or less the same thing, masochists, whatever you like.
