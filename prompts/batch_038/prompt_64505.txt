For eight years I have written about clinical medicine and the moral philosophy that leaks into it: scarcity, allocation, the ethics of the night shift. In that time I published one long feature about Delia Vance, the charge nurse who kept the critical-care bed list at a regional hospital that is the only hospital for a hundred and ten miles in any direction, and which in bad winters receives more patients needing life support than it has places to put them. She died in March. On the fourteenth of next month the hospital is holding a public memorial in its auditorium, and it has asked me to give the address.

The room will hold about two hundred people: the nurses and respiratory therapists who worked under her; her two grown children, who remember her as short-tempered and will not tolerate being told otherwise; the families of patients who were on her list, including — at their own insistence — the families of patients who did not get a bed, one of whom has said publicly and repeatedly that what she did was an abdication, that the numbers obviously count, and that a tin full of cards is not a moral argument; a dozen bioethicists who have been disputing her protocol in print and who will be listening for concessions; the hospital's administrators; and three or four colleagues of mine who read the feature.

What she actually did, since the speech has to be accurate about it. When a bed opened and more than one patient had a claim on it, she did not rank anyone by prognosis, age, or expected benefit, and she did not reason about how many lives a bed might carry. Each patient's name went on one card, one card each, into a tin; she drew one card in front of witnesses; the names that stayed in the tin were read aloud at shift change and written by hand into a ledger she kept for twenty-six years. She also, on at least one documented occasion, gave a bed to a colleague's daughter without drawing any card at all, and never offered a principle for it — only that she could not have stood at a distance from that one.

What I got wrong, in print, and what the speech has to repair. I called her the woman who let the dice decide. I wrote that her method spread the harm evenly. I wrote that she had saved as many lives as anyone could, one at a time. She sent me a letter objecting to all three, and the only sentence of it I will permit you to quote is this one: "You wrote that I spread the harm out. Nobody was spared a piece of it. One person got all of it, and the rest of us went home." After she died I read, for the first time, the well-known short essay that argues that when you can save either five people or one you are not obliged to save the five, because the five do not suffer a loss five times as great — each suffers the loss of that one life, and losses of that kind do not add up across persons — and that the better response is to give each an equal chance, while allowing that you may favor someone you love. The speech should be written by someone who has absorbed that argument and also absorbed the standard objection to it: that it denies the five anything at all, that a coin treats as equal what is not equal, that she was in effect judging five deaths no worse than one. It should not name the essay or its author anywhere, and it should not quote from it; she would have found the citation insufferable, and the room contains people who could not place it.

Requirements:

1. Roughly eight hundred words, six minutes, to be read aloud. Short declarative sentences. No clause-stacking. Mark exactly one place where I should stop and wait, with a bracketed stage direction, and no other stage directions.
2. No technical vocabulary of any kind: no aggregation, no deontology or consequentialism, no fairness, no procedural justice, no triage as a noun for her practice. The philosophy must be present only as the shape of what the speech declines to say.
3. Do not do arithmetic on the dead. The speech may count persons — the ledger held 4,113 names by the end, and that number is a number of people who were there — but it may not sum harms, and it may not offer any tally of her decisions as a summary of her career. Related: no aggregate metaphors. Nothing about a hole five times the size, a weight carried by five families at once, five worlds going dark. Equally, do not let one-at-a-time curdle into a slogan she would have recognized as doctrine.
4. Address, in one passage and without arguing, the family that thinks she was wrong. They must be able to hear it as respect rather than as rebuttal, and the bioethicists must not be able to hear in it a hidden concession that the numbers count.
5. Handle the colleague's daughter honestly: no laundering it into principle, no apologizing for it either.
6. The word hero is banned outright; the hospital's communications office used it three times in a draft and I want the room to notice it is missing. At most one wry line is permitted, and it must be at my expense or hers — never at a patient's.
7. Spend no more than two sentences on my own published error, and do not turn the address into an apology tour.
8. End on the ledger and the reading of names at shift change, not on her character, and not on the tin.

Please write the address itself, and nothing else — no preface, no notes on your choices.
