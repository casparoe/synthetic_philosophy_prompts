Okay so I'm about 60% through my third novel and I need a self-contained short story that could double as one of its chapters, roughly 2,500–3,000 words. The book is set in a small invented free port on the Adriatic, sometime in the late fifties, and the one institution I've built out most is the Office of Attestations, a state bureau where no claim of the form "there is something such that…" can be entered in the public register unless a named clerk personally carries out the construction that witnesses it. Some of those constructions are infinite. They get farmed out to "witness clerks" who supply one term per day, sign for it, and are genuinely free in their choice, so the sequence doesn't exist as a finished object anywhere — not in a vault, not in a formula — it only ever exists up to today's entry. The clerks aren't discovering these sequences, they're making them, and when a clerk dies the sequence dies mid-construction and the file is sealed. I want that to be the ordinary, unremarked-on background of the story, the way a customs house is ordinary background, not a twist.

Here's the seed I've been chewing on. Protagonist: a witness clerk in her late forties who has been feeding one sequence for twenty-two years and whose only son went out in a fishing boat and didn't come back, body never recovered, and the harbour office has left the case open because it can't close it. The engine of the story is that she walks into her own bureau wanting one ordinary sentence entered in the register — either my son is alive or he isn't — and the ledger will not take it, because nobody has produced the thing that would decide either side. What I really want you to nail, and what I keep failing to write myself, is that the clerk *is* allowed to inscribe the doubly-negated version of that sentence: it can't be that it can't be that he's alive or he isn't. Formally she gets that for free, on the spot, no witness required. But it gives her nothing, and I want the reader to feel the specific shape of that nothing — that she has been handed a proof about a proof, a floor two levels below the sentence she came in for. Then, later, an inspector arrives from the eastern branch of the service with a shortcut his people accept and hers doesn't: if it can be ruled out that no witness exists, then a witness exists, full stop, and under that rule her son's file could be closed tomorrow. She has to decide whether to sign for it, and I want the decision to be about her integrity as a clerk and her need as a mother at the same time, not a debate staged in dialogue. There should also be one quiet, positive beat, because I'm tired of stories where this stuff is only deprivation: she works something out about a restricted class of sequences — one where every path gets stopped in finitely many moves — and discovers that inside that class she really can extract a uniform, bounded answer, and for a few pages that feels like the first solid ground she's stood on since the boat went out. Let her find out what the bound costs her.

Craft notes, please honor these. Nobody in the story says the words intuitionist, constructivist, or excluded middle, and nobody explains anything — the logic has to show up entirely as procedure, paperwork, oath formulas, and the physical habits of the bureau (the ledger's refusal to accept an entry is a thing a clerk can *feel*, like a lock that won't take the key). Keep the invented institution internally consistent: no magic, no prophecy, no one produces a witness by wishing. Don't resolve the son's fate and don't tip your hand about whether the eastern inspector is a con man or an honest man — I want the ending to land on the entry she makes or refuses to make, with the last image being bureaucratic and small. Also: no author's note afterwards explaining the maths, no reading list, no real mathematicians name-dropped as characters, and no meta commentary about what the story means. I'll know if you've got the logic right because the double-negation beat will hurt instead of reading like a technicality. One more thing — I've got a typo in my own draft of the outline ("attestion" for attestation, I've been fixing it for weeks) so don't stress about matching my spelling, use the correct one in the story.
