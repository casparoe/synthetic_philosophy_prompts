Below is a draft of a paper I am preparing for a political philosophy workshop. I would like detailed critical comments on it. I am deliberately not asking for references or suggestions of further reading, so please do not supply any; work only with the argument as I have set it out, and where you think a claim of mine about the existing literature is wrong, say so in your own words rather than by citation.

---

**Owed, Not Owned: Dirty Hands and the Location of Moral Residue**

**§1.** The problem of dirty hands is usually posed as a paradox about justification. A political actor, on Walzer's familiar telling, does something that is wrong — deceives, tortures, authorizes a killing — in order to prevent a greater catastrophe; the wrong is not erased by the prevention; and yet he was right to do it. Nagel's version emphasizes the character of the actor rather than the act: public life requires a ruthlessness that private morality forbids, and the decent public man is the one who remains capable of moral feeling about what he has become capable of doing. The orthodox moral of both stories is that something survives the justification. Call it the residue: the apology that is still owed, the compensation that is still due, the grief that is still appropriate, the guilt that is still real even though the act was all-things-considered right.

**§2.** There are three standard ways of handling the residue. The first denies it: once we take seriously that the actor occupies a role with its own normative powers, the act is not wrong at all, and there is nothing left over; the feeling of guilt is a species of moral squeamishness. The second accepts it and celebrates it: the residue is real, ineradicable, and the anguish it produces is precisely what keeps the political actor human; a politics without residue would be a politics of monsters. The third is consequentialist: if the act was right, the residue is an illusion generated by our inability to hold two true beliefs at once. I want to set these aside, because all three share a premise I mean to attack. Each assumes that if the residue exists, it belongs to the actor — that it is *his* guilt, *his* anguish, *his* to express, confess, or suppress. Call this the ownership premise. It is what makes the paradox look tragic rather than merely administrative: the residue attaches to a soul, and souls cannot be indemnified.

**§3.** Reject the premise. What survives a justified wrong is not a stain on the agent but an unextinguished claim, and claims have claimants. If A wrongs B in the course of doing what was on balance right, what remains is not a free-floating debt to the moral universe but B's entitlement: to be told, to be compensated, to have the act acknowledged as a wrong rather than excused as a necessity, and — crucially — to decline the acknowledgment if B would rather be left alone. On this picture the residue is not a property of the agent at all. It is a standing liability *owed*, and the agent is merely the debtor.

**§4.** Something follows that I think the dirty-hands literature has systematically missed. If the residue is owed, then the agent has no standing to choose the form in which it is discharged, any more than a debtor gets to choose the currency. The form is fixed by what the claimant has use for. And this means that the anguished confession — the repentant memoir, the televised apology, the insistence on being held to account in public — which is routinely offered as evidence that the actor has not become a monster, can be a further wrong. Confession characteristically demands something from the person wronged: attention, re-experiencing, and eventually absolution. It converts a liability owed to the claimant into a performance whose real beneficiary is the debtor. I will call this *residue capture*: the agent's appropriation of an owed residue as a credential of his own moral seriousness. The dirty-hands literature treats the capacity for anguish as the safeguard against ruthlessness. On my account, anguish is at best evidence and at worst a motive.

**§5.** Consider a case. (It is invented; the details are adjustable.) H. directs the water authority of a mid-sized city. Routine sampling suggests a contaminant in one district's supply at concentrations whose health effects are unknown; confirmatory testing will take eleven days. H. judges that immediate publication will trigger hoarding, the collapse of the district's property market, and — because the same authority runs the following year's vaccination campaign — a general loss of confidence in official statements that will cost more lives than the contaminant could plausibly take. She delays publication, and in the interval quietly reroutes the district's supply and installs filtration, so that on any plausible model nobody is exposed. The confirmatory tests come back showing that the original reading was an instrument artifact. There was never a hazard. The concealment is known to six people.

Twenty-three years later, retired, H. intends to publish a chapter of her memoirs disclosing the episode. Her stated reason is not personal: she thinks citizens are entitled to know that officials once operated under an unwritten practice of withholding sampling data, and that this practice, not the artifact, is what deserves scrutiny. Her former colleagues object that the disclosure will (a) destroy the standing of the authority's current leadership, and (b) convince several thousand people that they were poisoned, generating litigation and health anxiety where there was in fact no exposure.

**§6.** The ownership premise says H. has a reason to confess that is independent of its effects: she carries something, and confession is how one puts it down. My view says the opposite. The residue here — if there is any, which §7 questions — is owed to the district's residents and to the polity in its capacity as the party that was kept in the dark. Neither is served by the memoir. The residents have no use for a disclosure that installs in them a belief about their own bodies that is false; the polity has a use for something much narrower, namely the rules under which officials may delay publication of sampling data. What H. owes is therefore the disclosure of the practice, delivered institutionally, stripped of the first-person anguish that makes it a memoir — and, plausibly, silence about her own state of mind. On my account this silence is not evasion. It is the only form in which the debt is actually payable.

**§7.** Two difficulties, which I will concede rather than solve. First, the case may not be a dirty-hands case at all: since no one was exposed, it is not clear that anyone was wronged, and if no one was wronged there is no residue for the ownership premise to mislocate. The wrong, if there is one, is epistemic — the withholding of information from people entitled to it — and I am not certain that residue attaches to that kind of wrong in the same way. Second, where the claimant is dead, dispersed, or (as here) not determinately harmed, the view needs to say to whom the residue is owed. My proposal is that the polity holds it in trust, which makes the trustee's judgment decisive; I recognize that this reintroduces, at the institutional level, exactly the paternalism I objected to in the confessing agent.

**What I would like from you.** Please comment on the following, and rank your objections by severity rather than listing them as equally damaging:

1. Does the central inference in §3 hold — that because a residue is a claim, it has a claimant, and therefore the agent cannot choose its form? I suspect I am moving too quickly from the existence of a claimant to the claimant's authority over *how* the debt is discharged. If the inference fails, say which step fails and whether a weaker version of the thesis survives.

2. Does the distinction between residue that is *owed* and residue that is *owned* actually cut against the three positions in §2, or is it compatible with all of them? In particular: is my §2 taxonomy a fair paraphrase of the field, or a straw man built for convenience?

3. Does the coinage "residue capture" earn its keep, or is it a relabeling of a distinction already available — for instance, the distinction between apology that serves the victim and apology that serves the apologizer? If it is a relabeling, is it a useful one, and what would make it non-redundant?

4. In §6 I claim that the same act — public disclosure — can be required of the institution and forbidden of the individual. Is that stable? I worry that once the practice is disclosed institutionally, the residents will have the belief I said they should be spared, so the distinction buys nothing. If so, does the case collapse, or does it merely force me to a more restrictive conclusion about *what* gets disclosed?

5. §7 concedes two problems and moves on. Tell me whether either is fatal, and whether the paternalism I flag at the end of §7 is a problem the account can live with or one that reduces it to consequentialism about other people's feelings.

6. Finally: is §5 over-engineered? I built in the instrument artifact specifically so that nobody is harmed, in order to isolate the question of who controls the residue; but the artifact may be doing more work than I admit, since it lets me treat the residents' prospective distress as merely factual rather than as a genuine injury. Propose the smallest change to the case that preserves its function, or tell me to replace it.

Please be blunt about whether there is a paper here at all. If your judgment is that the thesis is true but uninteresting, or interesting but false, say which, and say what the cheapest revision is.
