I spent 35 years in an analytical chemistry lab, mostly making sure other people's measurements were traceable and arguing about detection limits, and since I retired I've been running a Tuesday-evening group at our library with the grand title "Arguing About the News." Between 18 and 25 people show up, ages about 25 to 80, a mix of retirees, a couple of carers, two or three shift workers, and honestly a fair number of them come for the biscuits. We get 90 minutes and I want to spend one session, in the next month or so, on benefits: conditionality, sanctions, fraud, the whole business of who deserves what. The local paper runs a story most weeks about people who could work if they wanted to, and my group has swallowed it more or less whole, including several people who have never claimed anything in their lives and are quite sure they never would have. I don't want a debate and I don't want me lecturing at them. I want them to run into the thing themselves.

So: design me a runnable 90-minute activity for that room. Full rules, roles, what paper or cards or envelopes I need to prepare, the actual numbers in the payoff scheme, a timeline for the session, and a debrief. Here's the shape I've been sketching, which you should improve or throw out if it's wrong. Phase one, about 25 minutes: they sit as a committee running a small closed benefit scheme with a fixed pot, and they have to set three dials — how fast the benefit is clawed back as earnings rise, how harsh the sanction is for missing a required appointment, and how big a slice of the pot goes on checking up on claimants. They score themselves on two things only: how much need gets relieved, and how much cheating gets caught. Before they vote I want each of them to write one sentence on a card justifying their setting in the language of being fair to people who play by the rules, and I want to keep those cards and read them back aloud at the end. Phase two, with no warning and no reset: every one of them is dealt a claimant dossier and lives three months under exactly the policy they just wrote, with the hidden attributes on the card — a van that won't start, a rota that changes weekly, a kid's inhaler, an ex-partner's income they don't know about, one genuine undeclared cash job — determining what happens to them round by round.

The three things I want them to feel, and this is where I need you to be careful with the arithmetic because the arithmetic is the whole point:
- that there's a band of earnings where working more leaves you strictly worse off, so the choice that looks like laziness is the correct one on their own numbers, and they should hit that band in phase two rather than be told about it;
- that sanctioning people for missing appointments sorts them by capacity to comply rather than by willingness to work, so the ones it lands hardest on are precisely the ones it was meant to spur on — again, out of their dossiers, not out of my mouth;
- the base-rate trap on the checking budget. I spent a career on this and I want it as the spine of the debrief: if the real rate of cheating in the scheme is low, then even a very good screening test throws up mostly false accusations, and the money spent hunting them would have relieved more need than the cheating cost. I'd like them to vote a big checking budget in phase one because it feels righteous, and then be shown in cold numbers what they bought. And I'd like the whole game run twice at two different cheating rates, a low one and a high one, so that they watch their own verdict flip on a quantity nobody in the room could possibly know. That's the bit I care most about: I don't want anyone leaving convinced they've refuted anybody.

Then give me the debrief, with the questions in order and rough minutes for each; the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do in the moment (I'm already worrying about the one person in the group who is currently claiming and may not want to play, and the one who'll turn phase two into a speech); whether the fixed-pot framing is a simplification I ought to confess to up front, since a real welfare state isn't a closed box and the committee never sees the tax side; and a blunt list of what this activity simply cannot reproduce, that I'll have to supply myself. Keep every number in the game invented and self-contained — I don't want any real-world statistic in there that you'd have to look up, and no reading list at the end, they'd never read it and I've no time to chase it. Last thing, and please don't skip it: tell me where you're least sure of yourself. Which of the three mechanisms might not actually fall out of the rules as you've written them, what I'd observe in the first run that should tell me to redesign a phase, and — most importantly — what argument or evidence would change your mind about the substance, i.e. under what conditions a regime of heavy conditionality and heavy verification genuinely is the right call. If you can't say that, then I'll know I've just built an expensive machine for printing my own opinions in other people's heads.
