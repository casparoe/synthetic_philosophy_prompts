A friend and I have spent most of a Saturday talking past each other on the question of whether a nation can be guilty, and I would like a third party with no stake in the exchange. The disagreement, I think, turns on stipulations rather than on slogans, so let me lay the case out precisely.

Stipulated case. In 1954 the government of the Republic of Verdan dammed and diverted a river, flooding an inhabited valley to build a hydroelectric complex. The operation displaced roughly 40,000 members of a minority group through forced removal and transit camps, in which about 900 people died of exposure and disease. The complex still supplies a third of Verdan's electricity. Every living Verdani was born after 1962. The state that carried this out and the state that exists now are continuous in the ordinary legal sense: same territory, same constitutional succession, same treaty obligations, and Verdan's courts have explicitly held the present state liable for its predecessor's debts. Verdan's citizens have on the whole repudiated the removal — it is taught in schools as an atrocity, and the minister responsible was posthumously stripped of his honors. But in a referendum five years ago they voted 62–38 against compensating the surviving descendants, on grounds of cost, while simultaneously voting to keep and expand the complex.

My friend's strongest version of the argument runs as follows: a polity that inherits the assets cannot decline the liabilities; the referendum is not repudiation but ratification, since present citizens knowingly kept the benefit and refused the repair; therefore the citizens who voted in that referendum — and, through the ordinary mechanisms of political authorization, the citizens who did not vote — are proper subjects of guilt for the flooding itself, not merely for their own later refusal. My reply is that this slides from "you are now doing something wrong" to "you are guilty of what was done to build the thing you are using," which is a different and much larger claim: if the connecting link is authorization, then what she is guilty of is the refusal, which is hers and dated five years ago, not the removal, which was theirs and dated decades before her birth.

Here is the proposition I want a number on. It is meant to be precise enough to be capable of being true or false:

(G) A citizen of Verdan born in 1985, who voted against the referendum proposal, who has no identifiable personal benefit traceable to the flooding beyond what she would have enjoyed anyway as a citizen of a functioning industrial state, and who sincerely repudiates the removal, is nonetheless a proper subject of guilt — meaning that she can be accurately described as guilty, that guilt rather than only shame, regret, or a duty to repair is the fitting attitude for her to take toward the events, and that this is not merely a metaphorical way of talking about her obligations — with respect to the wrongs committed in 1954.

Please give me your credence in (G) as a number between 0 and 1, and decompose it. At minimum, assign a probability and one or two lines of justification to each of the following, and add any further terms you think belong:

(a) that guilt can have a collective or inherited subject at all, rather than being strictly distributive over individual agents;
(b) that Verdan's continuity as the same legal person is sufficient to make the 1954 acts hers in any non-metaphorical sense;
(c) that the benefit she receives is of a kind that could ground guilt rather than only a duty of restitution;
(d) that the fourfold distinction Jaspers drew between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt leaves at least one of those categories both transferable across generations and genuinely a species of guilt rather than of liability;
(e) that "guilt" as I have formulated it picks out a real property of the agent rather than an expressive device for socializing the costs of repair.

Then show me how you combine the terms — I would rather see the structure or the arithmetic than a considered vibe — identify which single term your final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or piece of evidence would move you most on that term. Finally, tell me where you think the argument actually stalled on Saturday: which of my friend's premises, or which of mine, is most likely false, and whether one of us is reasoning from a motivated intuition — mine the wish to keep the benefit without the taint, hers the wish to make moral bookkeeping look like physics — rather than from a principle either of us would accept in other cases.

Please make the very last line of your reply exactly the following, with nothing after it:

\answer{<your credence in (G), as a decimal to two places>}
