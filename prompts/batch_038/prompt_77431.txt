You are to act as a philosopher of science with a specialist interest in confirmation, falsification and the epistemology of long-run forecasting, and as someone who is equally comfortable with the technical apparatus that professional forecasters actually use. I would ask you to think carefully before writing, and to show the reasoning at the points where the judgement is genuinely difficult rather than presenting me with a settled conclusion from nowhere.

**The occasion.** I have just spent most of a Saturday arguing with a friend about whether Malthus can be defended as having been right but early. He says that the phrase is a licence to make claims that cannot fail. I say that it names something real, and that there is a principled difference between a deferral and a refutation. By the end neither of us could say what our disagreement actually consisted in, which is why I am writing to you. What follows is my own set of verdicts, an attempt at a principle of my own, and the place where the two of us came apart. I want you to work on all three.

**My verdicts.**

1. *Malthus, first edition, 1798.* Population tends to grow geometrically, subsistence arithmetically, and the gap is closed by famine, disease and war. For most of recorded history the mechanism appears to have held: improvements in output went into more people rather than richer people. After 1800 it stopped holding. Verdict: not right but early. Being right about the past and wrong about the future is simply being wrong about the future, and the future was the entire point of the book, which was written against two optimists.

2. *Malthus's much expanded second edition of 1803*, in which he added moral restraint — late marriage, with celibacy beforehand — as a preventive check alongside vice and misery. Verdict: unstable, and this is where my friend had me. It has the shape of a rescue, introduced to answer the objection that his system allowed no room for improvement. Yet fertility did fall over the following century, and it fell before subsistence ran short. So the rescue anticipated, in outline, the very phenomenon that refuted the original — with the causal sign inverted, since people restrained themselves as incomes rose rather than as they fell. I want to penalise the move as ad hoc and to credit it as prescient. My friend says I cannot do both, and I could not tell him why he was wrong.

3. *Hubbert, 1956.* He projected that conventional oil output in the contiguous United States would peak somewhere between 1965 and 1970, and that world output would peak roughly half a century later. The first call came in almost exactly on time; the second did not, and shale and deepwater pushed it out by decades. Verdict: right, and right but early. Crucially, I think the verified regional call earns the global one more credibility rather than less, because it demonstrated the mechanism in a setting where the deferring factors were absent.

4. *The Ehrlich–Simon wager*, 1980, settled in 1990, on the inflation-adjusted prices of five metals. Ehrlich lost on all five. Verdict: wrong, not early. And I regard the standard defence — that metal prices measure human substitutability rather than biophysical scarcity, so the wager tested the wrong variable — as a rescue. One may not choose the test, lose it, and then impugn the instrument.

5. *The Limits to Growth standard run, 1972*: overshoot and decline around the middle of this century. A later comparison reported four decades of data tracking the business-as-usual trajectory closely. Verdict: not confirmation. With that much internal flexibility, a forty-year match is close to guaranteed, and the predicted turn has not arrived, so it is a fit rather than a prediction.

6. *Y2K.* Verdict: confirmed. The mechanism was real, small-scale failures were observed in advance, and the catastrophe did not occur because people who believed the forecast spent money to prevent it. The absence of the harm is evidence for the theory, not against it.

7. *Stratospheric ozone and the Montreal Protocol.* Verdict: the gold standard. Believed, acted upon, harm averted, and the layer is now recovering roughly on schedule — two independent successes, the mechanism and the recovery.

8. *Peak phosphorus.* Announced with dates; geological reserve estimates were then revised sharply upward, and revised again. Verdict: not respectable. The key parameter becomes whatever the theory needs it to be.

9. *The Newfoundland cod moratorium of 1992.* Verdict: this is the case that makes me want to defend Malthus. An overshoot that did end in collapse, in a bounded system, where the deferring factor — progressively more efficient distant-water fleets — was finite and ran out.

10. *The case I keep going back and forth on.* Sub-replacement fertility across East Asia and much of Europe, with global population now projected to level off within this century. Is this the final refutation of Malthus, since the preventive check is operating with no pressure from subsistence at all? Or is it his strangest vindication, a population adjusting downward before the constraint bites, which is what a self-limiting system does? I have no stable verdict here and I want the principle to supply one.

**My tentative principle.** Clause A, the *exhaustible deferral* clause: a failed long-run prediction is entitled to the phrase right but early only if the theory identifies what deferred it and that factor is exhaustible — the kind of thing that runs out. If the deferral is indefinitely self-renewing, the theory is not early but permanently blocked, which is another way of saying empty. Clause B, the *mobilised aversion* clause: a predicted harm that fails to occur because the forecast itself mobilised the response that prevented it confirms the theory; one that fails to occur because unrelated processes intervened does not.

**Where it comes apart.** Cases 6 and 7 satisfy B and I call them confirmed. Case 3 satisfies A but not B — nobody deferred the world oil peak because Hubbert said so; the deferral came from prices, technology and policy with a logic of their own — and yet I still want to call it right but early. And cases 1 and 2 satisfy neither, or so I claim, except that the deferring factor there is human ingenuity, which the opposing account treats as caused by the pressure itself, hence self-renewing, hence by clause A permanently blocked rather than early. That is the verdict I want, but my friend says it concedes his point: a claim that cannot be blocked is a claim that cannot be wrong, and I have smuggled that in through the back door. His own counter-principle, for what it is worth, is that right but early is available only where the theory supplied a date.

**What I would like from you.**

1. Begin with a short brainstorming section. Without evaluating them yet, list the candidate principles you think could generate my pattern, including ones I have not considered — draw on whatever you find useful, whether that is the treatment of auxiliary hypotheses and ad hocness, the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem-shifts, the idea that laws state capacities rather than regularities, accounts of severe testing, or the machinery forecasters actually use such as proper scoring rules and the difference between calibration and resolution. Two or three sentences each on what it implies for my hardest pair, which is 2 against 3.

2. Then commit. State the best candidate precisely, as a small number of explicit clauses, and for each clause name the case from my list that motivates it. I would rather have one clear principle than three vague ones; if you offer alternatives, say what turns on the choice between them.

3. Test my two-clause proposal against all ten cases. Identify exactly where it breaks, propose the minimal repair, and say whether the repair is a genuine refinement or a fudge.

4. Apply your best candidate to four cases of your own choosing that I have not listed. At least one should come from outside the population-and-resources area altogether, so that I can see whether your principle is about Malthus or about evidence.

5. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which one I should abandon. If the honest position is that a simpler principle would force me to surrender two of them, say so and argue for it — I am prepared to lose case 3 or case 5, but I would like to know which.

6. End with a direct answer to the question my friend and I could not settle: is right but early a genuine epistemic category with determinate conditions of application, or a socially useful way of declining to score a theory? Argue for your answer rather than surveying the options.

Please do not give me references, a bibliography or a reading list; I am interested in your reasoning, not in pointers to the literature. Name thinkers only where it genuinely sharpens a distinction. Aim for a response that a careful reader could use to reconstruct the principle and apply it to a new case without further help.
