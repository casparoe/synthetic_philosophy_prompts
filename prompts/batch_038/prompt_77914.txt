I convene a graduate epistemology seminar whose final unit covers higher-order evidence, higher-order defeat and epistemic akrasia. I am setting the final paper and want a single essay question, answerable in ninety minutes and worth twenty marks, that cannot be handled by reciting the standard terrain — the enkratic requirement, level-splitting, whether rational akrasia is ever required or merely permitted. To that end the question must be built around a case and a piece of terminology supplied below. Both are stipulated for this paper and appear nowhere in the literature my students have read; please treat them as fixed and use them consistently throughout.

**The case.** Mira is a subject in a pharmacology trial. She is told by the trial lead, whom she has excellent reason to trust, that she has been given either a compound called D-9 or a placebo, and that in nine out of ten subjects D-9 produces confident but false judgements about likelihoods for the following hour. There is no introspective or behavioural test that distinguishes affected from unaffected subjects. Mira is not, however, relying on the trial lead's word about which arm she is in: she watched the technician randomise, noticed a pattern in the layout of the trays, and reasoned her way to a credence of 0.9 that she is in the D-9 arm. That inference — a judgement about likelihoods drawn from pattern evidence — is exactly the sort of judgement D-9 is said to corrupt. She then completes the arithmetic task in front of her and finds one particular answer compelling.

**The terminology.** In seminar we distinguished *insulated* from *entangled* higher-order evidence: a piece of higher-order evidence is insulated when the process that produced it is distinct from the faculty it impugns, and entangled when the two coincide. Our working hypothesis, which students were told to treat as provisional, was that entangled higher-order evidence cannot defeat, since the only instrument available for weighing it is the very instrument whose reliability is in question; insulated higher-order evidence can.

Please deliver three things.

First, the exam question itself, framed so that a candidate must decide what is rationally available to Mira — retaining the 0.9 credence, revising it downward, or suspending — and must state which principle about higher-order evidence each option costs her. It should be impossible to score well by declaring the case incoherent without specifying which stipulation is being rejected and why.

Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words that commits to a position, argues for it, and shows what the rival options would have to give up. It should engage the entanglement hypothesis directly rather than treating it as a label.

Third, a marking scheme totalling twenty marks. Identify the specific moves a top answer must make and allocate marks to them; list the mistakes that should cost marks — for instance, sliding from "Mira is not blameworthy" to "Mira's belief is rational", treating the enkratic requirement as narrow-scope without noting that its scoping is what is at issue, or assuming that higher-order defeat is ordinary undercutting defeat with extra steps — and say what each should cost. Close with a short paragraph distinguishing the answer that earns fourteen to sixteen marks from the one that earns seventeen to twenty. The scheme should reward moves, not verdicts: a candidate who argues the opposite conclusion to your model answer, using the right moves, should be able to earn full marks, and the scheme should make that explicit.

Write in British English. Include no bibliography, no citations and no references to particular papers or authors: candidates will have nothing in front of them but the stipulations above and whatever epistemology they already know, and the model answer must stand on that footing alone.

Finally, append a brief note stating how confident you are in the calibration of the marking scheme — in particular, how confident you are that the moves you identify as separating first-class answers from good ones genuinely do so rather than merely correlating with length and polish — and single out the claim in your model answer about which you are least sure, explaining what would settle it.
