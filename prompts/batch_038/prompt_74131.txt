I'm auditing a formal epistemology reading group this term and have to hand in a short essay at the end of it; the group has spent the last several weeks on the accuracy-first program, and I want to write about a problem in it that I don't think we've resolved. The framework, as we've been developing it, says that the epistemic value of a credence function is a matter of its accuracy — how close its degrees of belief are to the truth values of the propositions they concern — and that the measure of accuracy we adopt should be strictly proper, meaning that whenever you evaluate candidate credence functions by your own lights, your actual credences come out best. Concretely: if I use the Brier measure, where the inaccuracy of a credence c in a proposition is (c − v)² with v the truth value, and I currently assign 0.6 to a coin landing heads, then my expected inaccuracy is 0.6 × 0.04 + 0.4 × 0.36 = 0.24, whereas adopting a flat 0.9 would give 0.006 + 0.324 = 0.33, and even retreating to 0.5 gives 0.25. Strict propriety is what buys the accuracy-first epistemologist the two headline results — that incoherent credences are accuracy-dominated, and that only conditionalization on new evidence is expected to improve accuracy — so giving it up is expensive.

The difficulty is that strict propriety builds immodesty into the framework at the ground floor. Since any alternative opinion set now available to me is expected by me to be worse, I cannot regard myself as probably mistaken in any sense the framework recognizes; and since I know my future self will conditionalize on evidence I don't yet have and will therefore make exactly the same self-assessment from its own standpoint, the immodesty is inherited by every later stage of me. One camp says this is fatal or near-fatal: a plausible theory of epistemic value should leave room for the thought that a rational agent expects, at any time, to be wrong about some things, and a theory that structurally forecloses that thought has misdescribed the phenomenon it was meant to capture. The other camp says the intuition is being misread: it is a mathematical triviality that your current credences maximize expected accuracy relative to themselves, and this is perfectly compatible with acknowledging that a better-informed agent — or you, later, having seen the evidence — would score your present opinions poorly; the modest thought is always indexed to a standpoint other than your own, and once that is spelled out, nothing has been lost. I should note for the essay that the conditionalization result cuts across the dispute: by my present lights, planning to conditionalize does raise expected accuracy, so it isn't true that I expect my future self to be less accurate simpliciter — what I expect is that my future self will be immodest in the same self-sealing way I am now.

A friend in the group has floated a repair that I want the essay to test properly rather than dismiss. She proposes a two-measure view: keep a strictly proper measure for everything substantive — evaluating credal states, establishing coherence and conditionalization norms — but introduce a separate, deliberately non-proper self-assessment measure, one whose maximum sits at a credence shrunk partway from the agent's own toward 0.5, used only for the agent's expectations about the quality of its own opinions. Her pitch is that this delivers modesty without touching any of the accuracy-based arguments, because those arguments only ever appealed to the first measure. I'd like the essay to ask, and answer: is a measure of that shape coherent as a norm-governed attitude, or does having two accuracy measures that disagree about which credences are better reintroduce exactly the dominance problems the program was built to avoid; is the appeal to shrinkage toward 0.5 motivated by anything other than a desire to manufacture the modesty intuition, and if it is ad hoc, is ad hoc enough to disqualify it given that the alternative is abandoning strict propriety altogether; and does the two-measure view even capture what modesty is supposed to be, or does it encode something else — a bias toward uninformative opinions — that no defender of epistemic modesty should want?

Write the essay as a self-contained argumentative piece of roughly 1,500 words, aimed at a reader who knows probability and the basics of the framework but who has never encountered this particular dispute, and who is offline: define any technical term you use, and don't include a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind, since a reader with no access to sources can't check them. Weigh the immodesty objection, the deflationary reply, and the two-measure repair against each other on the merits, but do not end in a truce — I want a stated verdict about which of these positions the accuracy-first epistemologist should adopt, and about whether the modesty intuition survives that verdict, together with the strongest consideration on the losing side, stated in a form its proponents would recognize and then answered rather than set aside.
