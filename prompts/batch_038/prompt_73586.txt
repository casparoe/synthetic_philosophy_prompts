I chair the editorial standards committee of a famine-relief NGO. Before any report of ours may describe a food crisis as something other than a natural disaster, the committee votes on whether the phrase "man-made famine" is permitted in the text. Our record so far:

- Bengal, 1943 — permitted. Recorded grounds: the administration had been warned, held shipping and grain options it declined to use, rationed by occupational priority, and destroyed boats and coastal rice stocks under the denial orders.
- Ireland, 1845–52 — permitted. Grounds: the blight was the trigger, but food kept leaving the island and relief was run on a doctrine that the hungry ought not be fed at public expense.
- China's Great Leap famine, 1959–61 — permitted. Grounds: procurement quotas were set from inflated yield claims, peasants could not leave their communes, and there was no press through which the starving could make themselves heard.
- Ethiopia, 1984–85 — permitted. Grounds: the drought opened the gap, but the government forcibly relocated people out of the starving provinces, militarized relief, and fought a war there.
- North Korea, 1994–98 — permitted. Grounds: distribution followed political loyalty, the army was fed first, outside help was delayed, and the population was told nothing.
- Bangladesh, 1974 — permitted. Grounds: aggregate food supply was roughly normal; the failure lay in hoarding, distribution, and official delay, and the state could have imported and released grain.
- Niger, 2005 — refused. Grounds: drought and locusts destroyed the harvest, the government appealed for help early and lacked the fiscal means, and no one chose who starved; the report said "chronic vulnerability" instead.

The case now before us concerns a country with competitive multiparty elections and a vigorous domestic press, where the harvest failed after a severe drought; where the ministry of agriculture warned the cabinet fourteen months in advance; where the government held ample foreign-exchange reserves and standing import options; where it refused import licenses to a province governed by the opposition while allowing contracted grain exports from that same province to continue; and where it issued a national-security order barring reporters from the worst-hit districts.

Should we permit the phrase? Please answer in American English. Tell me which decision rules are jointly consistent with our entire record — I can see preventability, political voice, and identifiable-chooser principles all in the running, or something else — and which of them our precedents have already ruled out. Then say which surviving rules the new case satisfies and which it fails, which single precedent is the closest analog and why that analogy does not settle the vote, and what principle we would be committing ourselves to under either outcome, including anything we would have to revise about the Niger report. Close with one further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the surviving rules.
