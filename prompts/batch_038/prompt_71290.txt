I teach a 24-person upper-division seminar called "Collapse" — mostly history and anthropology majors, one 75-minute session, and by that point we've already done the diminishing-marginal-returns-to-complexity argument (Tainter) plus the objection that "collapse" is a label outsiders slap on what was really just reorganization, or migration, or people deciding they'd rather not pay taxes anymore.

Here's the thing I want them to feel instead of being told: the two definitions of collapse come apart. From the polity's point of view it's a catastrophe — the state, the roads, the script, the long-distance trade, all gone within a couple of generations. From a household's point of view it can be a relief — the collector stops coming, the corvée stops, and people eat better and work less. I want them to notice, late, which of those two they'd been rooting for, and to realize they were tracking the wrong unit of analysis the whole time.

So: design a simulation. Something like an invented river-valley polity where each student (or pair) runs a village household across maybe six rounds. Each round the center offers or imposes some complexity-increasing project — canal expansion, a standing garrison, a scribal bureau, a temple complex, a census — that costs the villages surplus and returns a benefit that shrinks over time. Build in an exit option: households can quietly slip out to the periphery and stop participating, at some cost in security and trade access. I want the payoff scheme to have a round where exit is individually rational for a majority of households even though total output drops hard, and I want that round to land before anyone in the room has said the word collapse.

Give me the materials list (nothing requiring real money), a round-by-round table with three columns — what I say out loud, what they see on their sheets, what I'm hoping they realize — plus how scoring works and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the definitional dispute rather than just re-explaining it. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time you run one of these and how to handle it: my guesses are that the winners gloat, or that it degenerates into spreadsheet arithmetic and nobody feels anything. And be honest about which features of real collapses this can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself — I'm thinking violence, disease, and the fact that archaeology only ever sees snapshots centuries apart. If it can be resized for 12 or for 40 without becoming a seperate game, say how.
