Help me break an argument a colleague dropped into a draft memo for our county's pretrial reform working group. It's meant to show the fairness debate is already over, and I think it proves way too much, but I can't locate the bad step.

(1) A risk score is fair only if it's calibrated across groups and its errors are balanced across groups: same score means same observed event rate for both groups, plus equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates.
(2) The impossibility results show that when base rates differ, no score that isnt trivially degenerate satisfies all three at once.
(3) So whenever base rates differ, every score is unfair.
(4) Base rates always differ. Even where the underlying behavior didn't differ, the recorded outcome is produced by earlier policing and earlier scoring, so the measured base rates carry that history with them.
(5) Therefore every score applied to a real population is unfair.
(6) And any decision procedure about people either uses a score or can be read off as one: rank the people it favors and you have a score, which (5) covers.
(7) So no procedure, algorithmic or human, can be fair, and we should stop trying to build fair ones and argue only about who gets hurt and how much.

I want to keep (2), it's basically a theorem. But (7) isn't something I can sign onto — by the same route it condemns ER triage, college admissions, and a judge deciding who gets bail. Which step do I reject, why did it look fine going in, and what does each candidate rejection actually cost? In particular: does giving up the conjunctive requirement in (1) solve anything, or just relocate the problem to wherever we choose the threshold and the outcome variable? And if (4) is doing real work, what would it take to show the base-rate difference is itself the unfairness rather than an input to it?
