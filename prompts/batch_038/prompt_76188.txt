I'm a clinical pharmacist sitting on a quality-improvement committee at a community mental-health clinic, and I've been having the same arguement with our prescribers for months, so I want it laid out properly by someone who can hold both halves at once.

The episode is the DSM's bereavement exclusion. As I understand it, DSM-IV said that when the low mood, sleep and appetite stuff showed up after the death of a loved one, you couldn't call it a major depressive episode unless it dragged on beyond a couple of months or came with the heavier features — suicidal ideation, psychotic symptoms, that kind of thing. DSM-5 in 2013 deleted the exclusion outright, so grief-related depression gets diagnosed on the same criteria as any other depression. Critics said this was medicalizing ordinary grief; the defenders said grief-related depression is the same disorder and the old rule was just undertreating people. Then a few years later the manuals went the other way again and added a separate prolonged grief disorder, with a minimum-duration clock on it. So inside roughly a decade the same symptom pattern went: not a disorder, a disorder, and then its own disorder with a stopwatch attached.

Run that sequence through the naturalism/normativism debate about disease — a Boorse-style biostatistical account on one side, the value-laden/normativist accounts on the other. I know both positions in outline. What I want is the mapping done carefully: what counts as the function at issue, what the relevant subpopulation is, where the harm clause is supposed to come from, and which specific editorial decision each account predicts or has to swallow. Then tell me honestly where each account goes quiet, and whether the sequence ends up confirming one of them, being an anomaly for one of them, or being a case neither was ever built for.

The wrinkle I want you to actually wrestle with, because I think it's the crux and I've never seen it stated cleanly: Boorse fixes the reference class by age and sex and then asks whether the person's functioning is statistically subnormal for that class. But nothing stops you narrowing the class to people who lost a spouse in the last eight weeks, and at that point the grief response is statistically dead typical and so not pathological by construction. Widen it back to all adults and the same response is a spectacular functional impairment and looks pathological. I've started calling this refrence-class shopping, and my suspicion is both sides of the DSM fight were quietly doing it — the exclusion-era crowd had picked the bereaved class, the DSM-5 crowd had picked the general population, and prolonged grief disorder is a third pick (people still grieving a year on). Is that diagnosis of the diagnosis right? Can the naturalist fix the class on principled grounds, or does the fact that you have to settle the comparison population before you can settle the disease status show that the naturalist account is normativism wearing a lab coat?

Spend them wherever you think matters, but the reference-class question has to be in there somewhere and one of the five should be a straight verdict on which account comes out of this episode better.
