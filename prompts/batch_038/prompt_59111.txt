For a departmental reading-group event, I want a piece of sustained deadpan satire in the form of a printed auction-house catalog: the annual Retrospective Ontology Sale, at which an invented firm sells off the theoretical furniture of superseded sciences. Invent the house, its premises, and its two regular bidders — one who buys only the mathematical relations and refuses anything with an ontology attached, one who insists that every lot is scrap and says so at tedious length in the bidding notes. The subject being satirized is the pessimistic meta-induction and the scientific realist's attempts to survive it; the form of the satire should be an inventory of things that once did real explanatory work and are now being knocked down.

Target length: 650–800 words. Structure it as a real catalog would be: a short front-matter paragraph announcing the sale and its terms; six to eight numbered lots, each with a lot title, a description, a provenance line, a condition report, and an estimate; a closing Conditions of Sale paragraph. Estimates may be quoted in a currency of your own invention, provided it is used consistently. Hold the voice throughout — restrained, commercial, faintly snobbish — and never break it to explain a joke. The comedy must come from the philosophy being exactly right, so that a reader who works in this area laughs rather than winces, and finds both bidders caricatured fairly rather than one of them strawmanned.

The catalog must contain, distributed across the lots rather than flagged as such:

1. A lot whose condition report enacts the structural realist's salvage operation: a mechanical medium described as structurally sound but ontologically vacant, with the equations that once rode on it offered as a separate lot. The house should note that the second lot has never once failed to sell and the first has not sold since the turn of the last century. Provenance may name real historical scientists such as Fresnel or Maxwell; the point is that the relations survived and the furniture did not.
2. A lot the house is quietly embarrassed by — something cataloged in an earlier sale as a triumphant success, which a historian's letter (summarize it, do not quote it at length) now claims never produced the kind of novel predictive success the house's own inclusion criteria require. The house's hedged reply should let the reader see that the force of the entire pessimistic induction depends on which past theories are allowed to count as successes in the first place.
3. A lot that cannot be described. The house holds it in reserve, knows only that it will eventually occupy the whole of the remaining floor, and cannot say what is in it, because nothing so far conceived fits the label. It should be the one lot for which neither regular bidder will raise a paddle.
4. A final lot consisting of the catalog itself, or of the general maxim underwriting the sale, offered as is and with all faults and no warranty as to reference, and withdrawn while the room is still disputing whether the withdrawal vindicates the maxim or merely postpones it.

Two constraints. Do not reproduce any real passage from the philosophical literature: all quoted matter — letters, house notices, calls from the rostrum — must be invented prose. And do not turn any living philosopher into a character. By the end, a reader should be able to say what the house itself stands for, why the first bidder keeps paying up for structures, and why the second thinks the whole inventory is worthless, without the piece ever naming the positions it is enacting.
