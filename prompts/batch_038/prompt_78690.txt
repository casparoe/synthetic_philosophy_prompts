I have no academic training in philosophy. I spent twenty-eight years with a municipal transit agency, first as a scheduler and later in capital planning, and since retiring I have been reading on my own about whether technologies have politics. I have read Winner's essay asking whether artifacts have politics, and I have followed enough of the arguments around it to know the general shape of the controversy. What I want to ask about is not that controversy. It is something that keeps happening inside it, and I have never seen anyone name it or step back from it.

Here is what keeps happening, both in what I read and in the public meetings I used to attend.

First instance. An overpass built in the early sixties has a clearance that will not take the newer forty-foot low-floor buses with roof-mounted cooling units, so a route that would connect a neighbourhood on the east side directly to the hospital has to detour. Someone at the meeting says the overpass is a political structure: it decides, today, who can reach that hospital. Someone else says the overpass is concrete and steel and nothing more, that the politics was in the clearance standard adopted decades ago and in the people who adopted it, and that calling the concrete political is a way of letting those people off the hook. The first replies that the standard was repealed long ago and the committee that wrote it is dead, whereas the concrete is there every day, excluding buses without anyone having to decide again. The second replies that concrete only excludes anything given a bus fleet, a route network, and a set of alternatives, all of which are human arrangements. And so it goes, round after round. Everyone in the room accepts every fact: the dimensions, the dates, who approved what, what the effect on ridership has been. No evidence is offered or requested by either side. Yet neither sounds like it is merely quibbling. Each is making what feels like a substantive claim, about where responsibility sits and about whether the remedy is a redesign or a policy change.

Second instance, shorter. A shift-scheduling program used at a hospital whose default treats every employee as available seven days a week unless they opt out. The same slide follows: is the default itself political, or is the politics in the manager who chose it and the staff who never opt out, with the software merely recording that?

The pattern I think I am seeing is this. A disagreement that survives complete agreement on all the empirical and historical facts, because the two sides locate the property in question — being political — at different points in a chain that both of them accept, and every relocation is answered by a further relocation. The dispute is not about how the world is; it is about where in the world to place a predicate, and neither side will concede that this is what is happening.

So: does this have a name? I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list, which I could not chase anyway. I want to know whether this particular configuration has been carved out anywhere, in philosophy of technology or in some other field. My own untrained guesses, none of which seem quite right, are these: reification; Marx's idea that relations between people come to appear as relations between things; a merely verbal dispute; the standing controversy over whether artifacts can have agency at all; and technological determinism itself, though that looks like a claim about causation rather than about where a predicate belongs. Tell me how the thing I described differs from each of these, and which of them is closest.

Two further questions would help me decide whether I have identified one thing or three.

First, a case I am unsure about. There was an argument in my city over a stormwater culvert in which the two sides genuinely disagreed about what the original engineers knew and intended, so the facts themselves were in dispute. Is that the same phenomenon wearing a disguise, or a different kind of argument that merely sounds similar? I ask because if the pattern requires agreement on all the facts, that case should fall outside it — and I would like to know whether that requirement is doing real work or whether I invented it to make my observation look tidier than it is.

Second, does the same structure turn up outside technology? My hunch is that it does, in arguments about whether a market, a corporation, or a legal code is the sort of thing that can bear a property we normally attach to persons, and that the reason nobody names it in these debates is that it has a name elsewhere and nobody has carried it across. If that is so, tell me where it lives.

And if it has no name anywhere, give it one. Define it precisely enough that I could tell whether some argument I met tomorrow was an instance of it. State the conditions under which it arises, and the conditions under which an argument only looks like it. Then offer one or two borderline cases of your own choosing and give your verdict on each. The label by itself is of little use to me; what I want is the edges of the thing.
