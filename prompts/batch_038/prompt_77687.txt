I am in my final year and currently taking a metaphysics seminar whose unit on infinity ended, last week, in a dispute between two classmates that the class could not settle. I was taking notes rather than participating, and I have tried to report both positions fairly; I have no view of my own to defend here. The target of the dispute was this: Yusuf's thesis was that no actually infinite collection of concrete things can exist and that Hilbert's hotel demonstrates the point; Claire's thesis was that the hotel demonstrates nothing of the kind. Their exchange ran as follows.

1. Yusuf: the hotel is, by hypothesis, full — every room occupied — and yet one further guest can be accommodated, and so can countably many more, arriving on countably many buses. A collection that cannot admit another member and a collection that can admit infinitely many more have been described contradictorily. So the hotel is impossible; and since nothing in the case turns on hotels rather than on any other countable collection of concrete things, no such collection is possible.

2. Claire: there is no contradiction, only a suppressed premise. "Full" means no room is empty, and it means the same thing for finite and infinite hotels. What Yusuf needs is the further principle that if no room is empty then no further guest can be housed — but that principle is a consequence of the pigeonhole principle, which holds only for finite collections. A set equinumerous with a proper subset is Dedekind-infinite; this is a theorem, not a paradox. Yusuf's argument assumes finiteness in order to establish it.

3. Yusuf, two replies. (a) The theorem is internal to a stipulation: cardinality was defined in terms of bijection, so of course ℵ₀ + 1 = ℵ₀ comes out true. A consequence of a definition cannot settle a metaphysical question about whether reality contains infinite pluralities — that is exactly the question at issue — and finitists from Kronecker through Weyl denied that the stipulation answers to anything real. (b) Consider departures rather than arrivals. Guest 1 checks out, everyone else moves down one room, and the hotel is full again; on Claire's account the number of guests has not changed. But for a collection of concrete objects, removing one leaves fewer. That is not a framework-relative matter, and a bookkeeping scheme that cannot register the loss of a guest simply does not apply to concrete things.

4. Claire: (a) is a red herring and (b) begs the question. On (a), the relevant bijections are explicit and computable — the shift n ↦ n + 1, the interleaving of bus and seat indices — so the constructivists Yusuf appeals to accept precisely these constructions; Brouwer's and Weyl's objections were directed at non-constructive existence claims and at the classical continuum, not at recursive rearrangements of the natural numbers. On (b), the principle that removing a member leaves fewer is the finite principle in dispute; if Yusuf is entitled to it he is entitled to his conclusion, and the argument ends before it begins. She added that his position proves too much: quantum mechanics is formulated on separable Hilbert spaces, whose orthonormal bases are countably infinite. If actually infinite collections are impossible, that formalism is not merely non-literal but false, and Yusuf owes us a replacement physics.

5. Yusuf: that conflates two claims. He has no quarrel with infinite structures as mathematical instruments — an orthonormal basis is not a heap of concrete objects — and he is a fictionalist about such apparatus. His thesis is narrower: nothing concretely real can form a completed infinite plurality. If Claire may treat physical formalism instrumentally while he may not treat set theory instrumentally, the double standard is hers.

6. Claire: it is no double standard, because she is not making an instrumentalist move at all; she is denying that Yusuf has produced a single contradiction. And his narrowed thesis is no longer knowable a priori: whether some concrete system — spacetime, a field, a physical process — is infinite is an empirical question, and the hotel does no work in answering it. His original claim was that the hotel refutes actual infinity; what remains is the unargued assurance that physics will not deliver one.

Neither would budge and the instructor moved on. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — (1) through (6), treating the sub-parts of (3) and (4) separately — and for each say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, with a justification of the rating rather than a restatement of the move. Where a move contains a mischaracterization of a historical position or of the mathematics, say so explicitly and explain what the correct position is. Then tell me who is more right overall, and separately what the two of them appear to have overlooked between them: distinctions they collapsed, premises neither side surfaced, or resources available to either side that neither deployed. Judge the arguments as stated; I am not asking for references or further reading, only for your assessment.
