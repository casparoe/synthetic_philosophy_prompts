I'm the lay member on my hospital's determination-of-death panel — philosophy background, no MD, mostly here to ask annoying questions — and Thursday we've got a case that has our two neurologists openly disagreeing, which hasn't happened once in the three years I've sat here. Our state's statute is the usual one: an individual who has sustained either "irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions" or "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem" is dead. Our own record has always looked tidy to me, but going back through the minutes this weekend I've started to suspect it isn't, and that we've been papering over at least two different theories with the same vocabulary. So I typed the precedents up and I want an outside read before the meeting.

Here's the record as I've got it:

- **A.** 34-year-old after out-of-hospital arrest. No brainstem reflexes, no respiratory drive on a completed apnea test, but recurrent spontaneous flexion-withdrawal movements of both arms for six days, which badly upset the family. Death. Recorded reason: movements originating below the foramen magnum are not functions of the brain.
- **B.** 7-year-old, devastating anoxic injury. Isoelectric EEG, no awareness and no prospect of any, but intact brainstem reflexes, spontaneous breathing off the ventilator, sleep-wake cycles. Alive. Recorded reason: the statute says the entire brain, and the brainstem was still doing its work; the panel explicitly declined to write that loss of consciousness is by itself enough.
- **C.** 22-year-old pulled from near-freezing water after maybe fifty minutes, asystolic, apneic, isoelectric EEG on arrival — resuscitated over three hours, full neurologic recovery. The panel wrote a guidance note afterward, and it says two things that don't obviously go together: that hypothermia, sedatives and paralytics must be excluded before any determination because a determination has to track the fact of cessation rather than our inability to test for it, and, in the same note, that what "irreversible" can sensibly mean is "not reversible by the means available to us here."
- **D.** Patient on VA-ECMO with an unrecoverable heart — the circuit doing all the work, no native output — who then infarcted the entire brain. Absent intracranial flow on nuclear study, no brainstem reflexes, apnea test performed with the circuit adjusted. Death. Recorded reason: the brain prong, not the circulatory one; the panel wrote that circulation was in fact ongoing, by machine, and that this did not prevent death because the failure that mattered was the brain's.
- **E.** Our OPO proposed shortening the post-asystole observation window in controlled DCD from five minutes to two. Rejected. Recorded reason: autoresuscitation has never been documented after five minutes.
- **F.** A patient maintained for four months after a determination — healed a dehisced abdominal wound, mounted fevers to infection, stable electrolytes and near-normal counts on replacement hormones. Family said his body was still doing what bodies do. Panel reaffirmed death, on the ground that none of it is brain function and continued body chemistry is not the organism's integration. One member dissented, saying we had no principled way to tell the machine-driven integration in D apart from the pharmacology-driven integration here. I've never gotten that dissent out of my head.
- **G.** Locked-in patient after a basilar artery stroke — fully aware, communicating by vertical eye movement, ventilated three weeks and then weaned. Alive. Recorded reason: the criterion isn't about the capacity to move or even to breathe; the presence of meaningful brain function settles it.
- **H.** Two rulings from the same year: that we will not record a determination whose battery was never actually completed, however unmistakable the clinical picture, and that a family's consent or refusal regarding donation plays no role in whether or when death is determined.
- **I.** A high cervical cord injury made the apnea test impossible. Panel accepted an absent-flow study in its place. Recorded reason: where a function cannot be tested directly, its substrate may be tested instead.

Now the new case. A 55-year-old man who got a continuous-flow left ventricular assist device as destination therapy three years ago, and who for about eight months has been in uninterrupted ventricular fibrillation. His native heart contributes nothing; the pump does all the work. He was warm and perfusing and ambulatory — walked the corridors, argued with us about baseball — with no palpable pulse anywhere, an unrecordable cuff pressure (Doppler mean of 74), and an ECG that has shown VF for months without consequence. Two days ago: large intraparenchymal hemorrhage with intraventricular extension and herniation. He is now comatose, pupils fixed and mid-position, no corneal, gag, cough or oculocephalic response, no motor response above the neck. We attempted the apnea test twice and aborted both times on desaturation and flow alarms, so there is no completed apnea test, and the radionuclide study came back in a configuration the neuroradiologist won't sign as definitive. The family consents to donation and the OPO wants controlled DCD: withdraw device support in the OR, observe, proceed. Two things about that plan keep me up. The device stops when somebody presses a button on a controller, and unlike a native heart after five minutes of asystole it could be running again in seconds — so I can't tell whether the five-minute floor from E even means anything here, since the reason we gave for it was a statistical fact about native hearts. And the family has asked, reasonably, why their relative, whose skin is warm and pink and whose blood is moving, is being described as dead before anything has been switched off. Meanwhile one of the neurologists wants to declare on the brain prong right now, citing D and I; the other says D is no help because there the brain criterion was actually completed, and that I doesn't license resting a determination on a study the reader won't stand behind.

Can you write this up as something I can hand around before Thursday? Please use these sections, in this order: **Bottom line** — one sentence, because our chair reads nothing else; **What the record commits us to** — a restatement of A through I in about 150 words, no new facts, flagging where the stated reasons already pull apart; **Candidate rules** — two or three readings of our own precedents that each fit all of A–I about equally well (roughly 200 words each), saying for each which rulings it strains to accommodate and which it makes central; **This case under each rule** — about 400 words total, with each rule's answer to whether we can determine death now, and if not now, what happens after device withdrawal and what observation would count as cessation of circulatory function when the circulation was a pump; **My recommendation** — about 250 words: the ruling that best coheres with our own record, which of A–I is the closest analog and why it's closer than the ones that look closer, and an explicit statement of the principle we'd be adopting if we went the other way instead; and **What would settle it** — around 120 words describing one concrete further case on which the candidate rules come apart. My hunch is that the discriminating case involves a patient with a perfectly intact brain, but tell me if that's wrong and give me the one you'd actually pick. Target about 1200 words overall. I took a philosophy of mind course a decade ago and I still think about the higher-brain standard a lot, so if you think B and G already foreclose it, say so plainly rather than hedging — the panel responds badly to hedging, and honestly so do I.
