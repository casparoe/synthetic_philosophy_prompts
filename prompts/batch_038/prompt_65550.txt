So me and my friend Rui — he's two years into a statistics PhD, I did maths and then sort of drifted sideways into philosophy — got into it last night about Bertrand's chord problem and didn't stop for something like four hours, to the point where the bar staff were stacking chairs around us. He's a full-on Jaynesian: thinks "a chord chosen at random" is well-posed enough once you take the symmetry group of the problem seriously, that scale and translation invariance actually pin down a unique distribution, and that people who say the whole thing shows the principle of indifference is bankrupt are just refusing to do the maths. I'm the other camp: I think there's no such fact as the random chord until somebody names a mechanism, that the three textbook answers are three different problems wearing the same costume, and that van Fraassen's cube-factory business shows "I see no reason to prefer one" isn't a reason. We've agreed to run a proper adversarial collaboration and co-sign a joint statement, and he's drafting his half — I want a full draft of the document itself so I can see in advance where I'd get pushed into concessions I shouldn't make. There's a bet attached: whoever's position survives the drafting buys the other's coffee for the term, which is not nothing on a stipend.

I want the deliverable, not a survey of the debate. Start with a numbered section of claims we can both genuinely sign — I expect some of these will be about what the three standard randomisers do give (1/3 from random endpoints, 1/2 from the random radius, 1/4 from a midpoint uniform over the disc), about what Bertrand was trying to demonstrate with them, and about which moves are illegitimate no matter whose side you're on. Then a disagreements table with columns for: the issue; the statement of it in wording the Jaynesian would accept for his own view; the statement in wording I'd accept for mine; whether what's left over is substantive or only words; and which crux it turns on. Then the cruxes proper — sub-questions where a settled answer would actually move one of us — and for each a concrete case, calculation or experiment we'd both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and numerically wherever numbers are possible. Finish with, for each of us, what would count as giving up rather than as an annoyance. Two bits of seed material so the cruxes aren't generic. First: I've got a machine that interpolates between two of the textbook randomisers — pick an angle uniformly, pick a distance r from the centre with density proportional to r^α on [0,R], take the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius. α=0 is the midpoint-over-the-disc method, α=1 is the random radius, and I can already do the integral, it's 2^-(α+1), so the arithmetic is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what either of us is entitled to say about a chord from this machine when we've been told nothing at all about α: is that a well-posed question with no answer yet, or a badly posed one, and do our two positions really differ here or only sound different? Second: we are actually going to do the physical version — chalk a circle on my driveway, toss wooden skewers at it from shoulder height, extend the line of every skewer that lands across the circle and score whether the chord beats a side of the inscribed triangle. He says 1/2 because the tossing is scale and translation invariant; I said I'd bet the observed frequency sinks when the skewers are short relative to the circle, since a short stick mostly makes short chords, and that a skewer several diameters long should behave like an infinite line. Get both predictions on the record for two or three specific skewer lengths, and say which outcome would refute each of us.

Two more cruxes I want forced, because they're where I think he's weakest and I'm not sure. One: is the transformation-group machinery even determinate? I've seen it argued that you can recover all three of Bertrand's answers out of the very same rotational, scale and translation symmetries, just by writing the invariance requirement down for a different generating procedure — and I dont know how much of that sticks or whether it's a cheap trick. Put it to him in a form he'd accept as a fair challenge, give his best reply, and record my prediction about whether that reply works. Two: make us confront whether the chord case is the same shape as the cube factory at all, or whether there's a difference in kind between invariance under reparametrisation (side length vs face area vs volume, which seems to me to prove nothing about anything) and invariance under a group acting on the plane (which he thinks does real work). And if you conclude some of this is a verbal dispute — I suspect "well-posed" and "the probability" are doing a lot of quiet smuggling — say which part and why, inside the document, not in a footnote where it can be ignored. Last thing, and it matters: in the agreed-claims section especially, flag clearly which items are actually settled among people who work on this and which are just local concessions we'd make to keep the collaboration moving. My suspicion is that neither his line nor mine is the default view in philosophy of probability or in statistics departments, so if the draft seperates out the real consensus and then quietly dresses one of our positions up as it, that's a failure of the document even if everything else in it is good.
