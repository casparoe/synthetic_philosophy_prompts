I'm sitting in on a social choice seminar this term (not for credit, I'm in a different dept) and the last couple of weeks have been on what the prof calls safe Pareto improvements — deals where nobody has to compare their utility against anybody else's, you just need each party to be able to say "I'm not worse off in any situation I take seriously, and I might be better off." He spent most of Tuesday on why it's surprisingly hard to build a case that's a Pareto improvement but not safe, because he says nearly every student example cheats in one of four ways: (a) you quietly leave out a third party who gets hurt, so it was never Pareto in the first place; (b) you lean on adaptive preferences, where somebody has stopped wanting the thing they're losing, so by thier own lights they're fine; (c) you compare against a baseline that was never actually on the table; or (d) you sneak in a harm you didn't describe — "trust breaks down," "they feel used" — and then count that as the thing making it unsafe.

I want to build one that doesn't cheat, and I want it to actually be hard, so here are the specs. Two or three parties in an ordinary institutional setting: a hospital system and a physician group, a port authority and the boats that use it, a university and the town it sits in, a platform and the people who make stuff for it, whatever — no sci-fi, no thought-experiment furniture, no islands and no demons. Preferences are informed, stable, and not adapted to anything; nobody is fooled; there's no hidden fourth party getting squeezed offstage. The status quo is a live option that both sides would accept as the comparison point. And the proposed arrangement is one each party can honestly call safe at the moment they sign: in every scenario they consider live, they're at least as well off as they'd be without it, and in some scenarios strictly better off. Yet it turns out unsafe — at least one party ends up worse off than they'd have been had they never made the deal at all. The constraint I actually care about: whatever makes it unsafe has to be something the deal itself does, not a risk that was already sitting in the status quo that the deal merely fails to insure against. Keep the scenario to one paragraph. No perfect predictors, no magic commitment technology, nothing beyond ordinary contracts, reputation, and the fact that some moves can't be un-made.

Then do the checking work out loud, because that's the part I want to see. Give me each party's ranking of the outcomes so I can verify the Pareto computation myself, spell out exactly which scenarios they had in mind when they judged the deal safe, and then show the state where the safety claim fails and why the failure traces back to the arrangement rather then to the background risk. Name the single structural feature of the case doing the work, and tell me what happens if I delete it — does the whole thing collapse into "risky deals can go bad, thanks"? Finish with the strongest reply available to someone who thinks a Pareto improvement should always be taken, and be straight with me about which of my prof's four cheats your case comes closest to, if any. I'd much rather hear it's borderline from you than get taken apart in section.
