I am the secretary to a statutory review board in a mid-sized jurisdiction. We decide whether a public agency may put an automated decision system into production. The statute sets a three-part test, and we record a separate finding on each part: (1) the system's error rate for the population it affects falls within the tolerance the board itself has adopted; (2) a person facing an adverse decision can get a human being to reconsider it before the consequence lands; (3) the benefit to the people the agency serves outweighs the harm to the people it flags. We approve only if all three findings are affirmative. Our minutes publish the vote on each finding as well as the verdict, because an appellant is entitled to know which finding she is contesting. Nine members, sometimes eleven: lawyers, software engineers, two clinicians, two lay members appointed by advocacy organizations, and one nominee of the agency whose system is under review. The chair has a casting vote and may defer a case for further evidence. I record the votes and may not cast one. The board cannot amend the statute, but it may adopt standing orders governing how votes are taken. A full session disposes of six to eight cases in a day.

Fourteen months of minutes have turned up a pattern I cannot fix by better drafting, and I have come to believe it is a known problem with a name I do not know. Concretely, in a recent case on a benefits-fraud flagging system the findings were: criterion one affirmative 6–3, criterion two affirmative 5–4, criterion three affirmative 6–3 — and the approval itself was defeated 4–5. The five members who voted against approval were not a bloc: three of them had accepted criterion three, four had accepted criterion one. Every published finding was carried by a majority, and the verdict those findings entail was rejected by a majority. The losing agency has since written to the chair observing that our own minutes show the board voting to refuse a system it had just found met every statutory test, and asking which document is the decision.

Four features of our situation make it worse than the textbook shape of the problem, and any procedure you write has to survive them:

- The findings are not independent in members' minds. The two lay members' votes on criteria one and three move together; so do the engineers'. The pattern above is therefore structural and recurs, rather than being a coincidence of a particular ballot.
- Criterion one is graded, and the board set the tolerance itself. Two variants of the same system differing by a rounding change of four-tenths of a percentage point came before us two months apart; we found the criterion met for the first by 6–3 and not met for the second by 5–4, after a third of the membership had rotated. A one-third turnover each year means last year's findings function as precedent for a differently composed body, and appellants have begun citing the contradictions.
- On a second recent case, criterion two was carried 6–3, but three members accepted it because a caseworker rechecks every flag before it takes effect, and three because there is a fast appeal afterwards; the three dissenters accepted neither ground. The minutes must state a ground, and no ground commands a majority, though the finding does.
- Two members are instructed by the organizations that nominated them. The statute does not distinguish them from the rest, and the chair has no power to discount a vote.

What I want is a written procedure, not an essay about the general theory. Please produce:

1. A voting and recording sequence I can run in the room, and a pre-session template I can circulate the week before: the order in which questions are put, what exactly is voted on at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and what the default is when the board cannot produce one.
2. The points at which the procedure stops with a refusal — where the findings are jointly inconsistent, where no ground carries a majority, where a case contradicts a finding the board has already published — and the points at which it hands the question to the chair or to the board as a whole because what remains is a choice of institutional values rather than arithmetic. In particular, I want it settled once, at the adoption of standing orders, whether we aggregate by findings or by verdict, and never revisited case by case, since choosing the rule to fit the case is exactly what appellants will accuse us of.
3. A treatment of the graded criterion and the rotating membership: how the board can keep its published findings from contradicting one another across cases and across years, given that it cannot bind future members and cannot express a tolerance as anything other than a vote.
4. Honesty about misfires. Say plainly which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, where the rule will produce verdicts that look indefensible in the minutes, and which of the properties we want — decisiveness, consistency over time, a publishable rationale, faithfulness to member opinion — cannot all be had at once, so that the procedure has to say which one it gives up and why that is the right one to give up.
5. A short note, in language a non-specialist appellant could follow, explaining to someone who lost why the rule we used was adopted in advance and is not rigged against her. Define any technical term you need in a clause as you go; I cannot look anything up, so I do not want references.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the benefits-fraud case with the numbers above; the pair of near-identical variants decided two months apart by differently composed boards; and the case where criterion two carried on two incompatible grounds. At least one of the three should come out in a way the board will not like — if the procedure forces us to publish a verdict a majority voted against, or to refuse to certify and thereby let the agency operate under a statutory default while an appeal runs, say so explicitly and say why that cost is worth paying rather than burying it.
