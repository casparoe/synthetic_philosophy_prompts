I sit on the macroprudential policy committee of a national central bank. We are eight voting members, chaired by the deputy governor, and each month we publish a single collective probability for a standing forward-looking indicator — at present, the probability that at least one of the six largest clearing members in our jurisdiction fails to meet a margin call within the next twelve months. Market participants read that number, and the press quotes it to one decimal place.

Our governing by-law was drafted seven years ago by outside consultants and has never been revised. It contains what I will call the convergence requirement. In substance: every member must deposit all models, datasets, and private information bearing on the estimate in a shared repository, after which members exchange figures in successive rounds until all eight lie within five percentage points of the median. If that is not achieved after six rounds, the published figure is the median, and any member outside the five-point band must either file a written statement identifying information material to the estimate that is not already in the repository, or withdraw their figure and adopt the median. The consultants' rationale, recorded in the drafting note, is that rational agents who share a prior and who have made their judgments fully known to one another cannot coherently persist in disagreeing, so that residual dispersion is itself evidence that someone is withholding information or is failing to update in good faith.

This month the clause has bitten for the first time. After six rounds, five of us sit between 0.44 and 0.58. Two do not: the head of banking supervision is at 0.68 and the external academic member is at 0.31. Both have signed the standard attestation that they have disclosed everything bearing on the estimate. Both have walked the committee through their models line by line. Neither can identify any datum absent from the repository, and neither has invoked supervisory information they are legally barred from sharing. What separates them is structural: they model the propagation of stress through margin spirals differently, they disagree about how much of the observed strain in the clearing data is idiosyncratic rather than systemic, and they read the same three series that all eight of us have been examining for four months in opposite directions.

The secretariat's calibration log is relevant and unhelpful to both camps. Neither of these two members has been a persistent outlier in the sense of being directionally wrong: on the two prior occasions when each fell outside the band and was quietly left alone, the event they were more pessimistic or more optimistic about resolved in a way that made their figure no worse than the median in hindsight.

The chair now proposes to convert the clause into a standing rule, tightened in two respects. First, an outside-the-band member who files nothing identifying undisclosed information must adopt the median, with no discretion for the committee to waive this. Second, a member who adopts the median in three consecutive quarters is recorded as having contributed no independent judgment, and that finding is transmitted to the appointing authority at the time of reappointment.

We meet Thursday. The committee will vote on the chair's proposal and must also settle this month's published figure. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion beforehand, and I would like you to draft it in my name, addressed to the committee. Aim for 900 to 1,100 words, with the recommendation stated in the first paragraph and short headings thereafter, in a register that could be tabled as a document rather than circulated as correspondence.

The opinion should do the following.

Take a clear position on the chair's proposal — adopt as drafted, reject, or adopt with specified amendments — and state what should happen to this month's figure and to the two outliers, with a concrete procedure and dates that the secretariat could implement before the next cycle.

Explain, in terms a member without formal training could follow but without softening the analysis, which premises of the drafting note's rationale actually obtain in our procedure and which do not, and what that implies about how much convergence a protocol of this kind can be expected to deliver as opposed to how much it can demand.

Give the chair's case its strongest formulation rather than a straw version: that a rule tolerating unbounded dispersion entrenches factions, that an appeal to differing starting assumptions can function as an unfalsifiable excuse for refusing to move on evidence everyone has seen, and that a published number the market can act on requires a single figure whose authority depends on the committee visibly having tried to reach it. Then say plainly what of that case survives your recommendation and what does not.

Supply criteria the secretariat can apply mechanically, without exercising judgment about the merits, to separate non-convergence that is legitimate from non-convergence that is evasion; specify exactly what a member must file in order for an out-of-band figure to stand, and what happens to a member who files it.

State the cost of your own recommendation explicitly: what we lose, in credibility and in internal discipline, if we stop treating residual disagreement as a defect to be eliminated, and what new failure modes your alternative opens up that the chair's rule would have suppressed.

The argument should rest on reasoning about our own procedure and about what the drafting note assumes; do not attach references, a reading list, or attributions to particular authors or papers, and do not include any quotations.
