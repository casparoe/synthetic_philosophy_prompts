I help run an undergrad reading group on the philosophy of perception, and we just spent two weeks on disjunctivism. Half the group has talked itself into thinking the view is bulletproof — or worse, unfalsifiable. Their move is always the same: you describe a hallucination, they say "sure, but the subject cant tell it apart from really perceiving the thing, and that indiscriminability is the entire story about what the hallucination is," then they shrug and tell you that you haven't described anything that actually touches the view. The other half thinks the disjunctivists are just cheating with words. I want to break that loop by handing them a case they can't shrug off.

So build me a hallucination case to spec. The target is the negative, epistemic characterization of hallucination — the idea that a hallucinatory state is nothing over and above a state that is indiscriminable from a corresponding veridical perception, so that there's no positive inner episode shared between the good case and the bad one. I want a case that makes that characterization come out false, or at least badly incomplete, and I want it to do that without any sci-fi scaffolding, because the group immediately discounts anything with electrodes in it. Two ground rules about what counts as cheating here. First, the pressure on the disjunctivist has to fall out of features of the scenario they'd accept as described — if it comes from a line like "assume there is no reply available" or "assume the state matches the perception in every intrinsic respect," the case fails. Second, please don't just re-run the bent stick in the water or the perfect hallucination of a yellow lemon; we've done those to death and everyone has their canned responses ready.

Constraints on the scenario itself:
- One paragraph, real world, ordinary setting. No brains in vats, demons, VR, hypnosis, direct brain stimulation, or drugs the subject doesn't know they've taken.
- It has to be a hallucination rather than an illusion: what the subject is aware of is genuinely absent, not present-and-merely-looking-to-have-a-property-it-lacks.
- At the moment of the experience, the subject knows — or at least has strong justified reason to believe — that what they seem to be aware of isn't there. Crucially, that insight has to be earned inside the scene by evidence or reasoning available to them, not imported from a diagnosis, a doctor's warning, or the narrator just telling us so.
- The experience can't be faint, imagery-like, or under the subject's control. It needs the same presence and force as actually perceiving, and it has to be unbidden, so the disjunctivist can't immediately reclassify it as imagining or as an appearance the subject isn't committed to.
- If you borrow a real clinical phenomenon, fine, but don't let the name of the phenomenon do the work — spell out the mechanics so the case stands on its own two feet.

Then, after the paragraph, give me:
1. A constraint-by-constraint check showing each one is satisfied and how.
2. What the negative-epistemic disjunctivist says about the case, and the precise point where their usual move jams. If it doesn't jam, say so straight out rather than forcing it.
3. What a common-factor theorist (sense-datum or representationalist, your pick) makes of the exact same case, and why they'd count it as evidence for their side.
4. Which single feature of the case is doing the work, what the case collapses into if you delete that feature, and the best disjunctivist reply that isn't just "you've mis-described it."
5. Your verdict: genuine problem, survivable complication, or dud. If it isn't fatal, tell me the smallest change that would make it hit harder.

No citations or reading list needed — I'm not asking you to source anything, just argue it out. I'll be the one fielding the objections in the room.
