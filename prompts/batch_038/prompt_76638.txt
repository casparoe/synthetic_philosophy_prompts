I sit on a small panel that's writing a retrospective on long-run resource-and-population forecasting for a policy body, and before we start scoring anything I want the criteria pinned down — partly because two of us allready disagree about Malthus and I suspect we're just running different philosophies of science at each other without saying so.

So: what does a theory of the Malthusian kind have to deliver to be called anything other than unfalsifiable? The core claim, in his own words, was that "the power of population is indefinitely greater than the power in the earth to produce subsistence for man", with population doubling every twenty-five years on a geometric ratio while food crept along an arithmetical one. He published six editions between 1798 and 1826; the second, in 1803, is over four times the length of the first and quietly adds moral restraint — postponed marriage — as a check alongside vice and misery. Then the world did roughly the opposite for two centuries: real wages rose, food output outran population, and fertility fell because people chose to have fewer children rather than becuase misery beat it down. Our draft list currently runs:

1. it must have been risky — it must have ruled out outcomes that were genuinely on the cards at the time;
2. it must beat a naive extrapolation of the existing trend over the same window;
3. it must have specified the time window in advance;
4. if the prediction failed, the theorist must have said beforehand what would count as premature rather than wrong;
5. getting the shape of the relationship right with the parameters out by an order of magnitude counts as confirmation, not failure;
6. it must have got the mechanism right and not just the trajectory — see moral restraint versus famine above;
7. it must not have been patched after the fact;
8. it must have been statable and checkable with the methods available when it was made;
9. any auxiliary it retreats to must do independent work of its own;
10. an author who revises in the face of criticism is running a progressive programme if the revisions yielded new predictions and a degenerative one if they only absorbed the damage.

Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are one camp's prejudices wearing a neutral name — I want the camps identified, not the isms recited — and give the consideration or the concrete case that motivates each. Then the bit I most want: which subsets can't be satisfied together. I have a hunch about 3, 4 and 8, but I want your own account. And I suspect something worse lurking under all of them, namely that a long-run social theory can be true and yet refuted by its own publication, because the alarm it raises changes the very behaviour that would have vindicated it. If your list needs a clause for that, say what the clause says and what it costs you elsewhere.

After that, briefly — a paragraph at most — how Malthus himself, the 1970s limits-to-growth system dynamics models, and Julian Simon's cornucopian counter-thesis come out against whatever list you land on, and which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. To fix what 'adequate' even means here: we need a theory good enough to be cited in an official assessment as a live long-run constraint, not good enough to be declared vindicated. Those aren't the same bar and I don't want them quietly merged.
