Six weeks ago I left academic work for a small research team at a firm that publishes a country-risk index used by institutional lenders, and my assignment has been the governance variables. Last month the team split over a single coding decision, and two senior colleagues argued it out at length in writing. I was copied on everything because the original proposal was mine, and I have been asked to lay out where the disagreement actually lies before a methodology review that is now five weeks away and at which I will have to defend the proposal under my own name. The case is Hungary. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it, in order.

(1) My proposal was to keep the country coded as a democracy but to flag it as backsliding, on the grounds that elections remain genuinely contested and have changed hands at the municipal level, while the retirement age for judges was lowered, the constitutional court was enlarged, the electoral map was redrawn, and most of the privately held news media was consolidated into a pro-government foundation.

(2) Marta, a comparative constitutional lawyer, said the flag was an act of cowardice dressed as nuance. Her argument: backsliding is a process concept, and a process concept only makes sense if there is a status concept at the end of it — something you can slide back from and something you can slide into. If the court and the media have already been captured, the honest code says so; if they have not, there is nothing to slide from and no direction to record. On her account, keeping the word democracy while listing all of those changes is a way of deferring a judgment one does not want to make, and she added that the field already has a term for the halfway house I was describing, competitive authoritarianism, which would at least commit me to a claim about what the regime is rather than what it is becoming.

(3) Dev, who built the quantitative side of the index, went the other way. He said the two-track proposal was right and that Marta's demand for a threshold was exactly the problem, because thresholds are arbitrary and a component score that moves continuously tells a client more than a binary that flips. Then he raised what he took to be the fatal objection to the whole vocabulary: everything on my list was done by ordinary legislation, passed by a majority that won a free election and signed into law without incident. If the definition of democratic backsliding reaches lawful acts, he said, it has no content at all, since every governing majority anywhere does lawful things its opponents experience as damage to the system. If it excludes them, it is useless, because almost no one stages coups now. Either way, he concluded, the concept is empty or partisan.

(4) Marta answered that legality was beside the point, and that the real question is whether the rules are being used to entrench the rule-makers so that the next contest is not a contest. She said the entrenchment here is asymmetric in a way that ordinary legislation is not: an incoming government could reverse a tax change with a simple majority, but reversing a packed court or a consolidated media market requires a supermajority it is unlikely ever to win, which means the change is functionally one-way. She also said that a definition requiring illegality is a definition tailored to the 1970s.

(5) Dev conceded the asymmetry had some force but said it did not answer his deeper objection, which is that sliding is always relative to a baseline, and nobody can say which baseline we are using. Relative to the country's own past? Then any country that had an unusually good decade is coded as backsliding the moment it returns to its ordinary self. Relative to a minimum standard of democracy? Then we have to say whose minimum, and ours was written by people in three capitals with a particular view of what courts and broadcasters are for. His claim: we are not measuring backsliding, we are measuring divergence from a liberal template, and we should say so plainly instead of burying the template inside a verb.

(6) Marta said that was a category mistake about measurement, since every index has a template including his, and the difference is that a directional concept tells a client which way things are moving rather than only where they stand, which is what a lender actually needs to know. She added that his professed neutrality is not neutral: refusing to name a direction shifts the burden of proof onto anyone alleging harm, and shifts it there until after the harm is complete, which is a gift to whoever is inflicting it.

(7) Dev's last move was to grant that direction may matter but to insist that once we admit the baseline is chosen, the choice is political, and we are a private company selling a number to clients who hold positions in the countries we score. He wants the word removed from the report language entirely and replaced with a table of raw component movements, letting clients draw their own conclusions.

I am not going to tell you which of them I found more persuasive, partly because I am still unsure and partly because I would rather the arguments be judged as made. So please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of (2) through (7), say whether it is right, wrong, or right about one thing and wrong about another, and indicate roughly how strongly — something like clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — and justify each rating in a sentence or two. I am especially interested in three questions your adjudication should bear on. First, does Marta's claim hold that a process concept like backsliding is parasitic on a status concept, so that one cannot coherently record sliding without also committing to a threshold? Second, is Dev right that a definition covering lawful acts is either empty or partisan, or does Marta's asymmetry argument supply a non-partisan criterion after all? Third, on the baseline problem, is there any principled way to fix a reference point, or is Dev correct that every choice of baseline is a political choice that a private index publisher has no standing to make? Finally, tell me what both of them are missing, and whether, after all this, the concept of democratic backsliding amounts to anything more than a label that partisans attach to whatever their opponents do.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables. Work through the moves in the order I numbered them, referring to them by number as you go, and let the structure of your reply come from the sequence of the argument rather than from any formatting.
