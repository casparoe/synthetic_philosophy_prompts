Hey, so I'm in a reading group that's been grinding through the arguments about affirmative action and quotas, and by week four everyone had just restated there priors at each other and nobody was learning anything. I want to shake it up by bringing a story instead of another position paper. I'm going to hand you a real policy situation and ask you to write literary fiction out of it, and I want you to work like someone who's actually published short stories but has also spent years in corporate governance — so the boardroom textures come out right instead of reading like a summary of a news article.

The setup is real. Norway passed a law in 2003 requiring that roughly 40 percent of the board members of its public limited companies (the ASA form) be of each gender. It came into force on January 1, 2006 with a two-year transition period, so the deadline landed at the end of 2007, and it covered a few hundred companies — essentially all the big listed ones. No special fine was written for it; the sanction was the ordinary machinery of Norwegian company law, which in the last instance could mean a company gets forcibly dissolved. Worth knowing too: the quota fixed board composition but not the hierarchy inside the boardroom, and after the dust settled almost all the chairs were still men. Set your story in that last stretch, autumn 2007 into early 2008, in Oslo. Here's the thing I actually care about, the thing the story should be about rather than merely mention. A friend of mine in investor relations keeps saying the quota is harmless because it's just a floor and the competent women were always there anyway. But I think something weirder is going on epistemically. Take a woman who is on any honest reckoning extremely good — real sector experience, nobody's diversity hire by anyone's standard. Before 2006, an offer of a board seat was information: it told her how she looked to people in a position to judge, and she's the sort of person who calibrated her self-assessment on signals like that rather than on her own opinion of herself. After the law, the same offer carries no information at all, because the company would have extended it to someone of her gender regardless of who she turned out to be. So the story is a careful person losing an instrument she trusted, and having to figure out what to do about her own confidence in the meantime.

Constraints, please honor all of these. 1000 to 1500 words. Nobody gets to make a speech for or against the quota — no op-ed dialogue, no character whose job is to articulate the other side; the argument has to be carried by what people do and fail to do, and by what they talk about instead. Include at least one scene that's boring the way real board life is boring (a nominating committee meeting, a fee schedule, a badly catered lunch) and let the philosophical pressure come out of that scene rather than out of a confrontation. Add one small detail that shows the shape of the incentive without anyone explaining it — pick just one: a company weighing whether to convert out of ASA status entirely rather than comply; a male director quietly declining re-nomination so a seat frees up on schedule; the financial press starting to keep score of how many seats a handful of women have racked up, with whatever nickname you like for them. End on something unresolved and concrete — no epiphany, no lesson, nobody understanding themselves better in the final paragraph. Before you draft, spend eight or ten lines brainstorming out loud: two or three candidate protagonists and their situations, which one makes the epistemic point land hardest without turning her into a thesis, and how you'd stage the exact moment she notices the signal has gone dead. Think carefully about that choice, I'd rather see the reasoning than have you sprint to prose. Then write the story, and afterward add two or three sentences on what you'd change if I'd asked for the chair's point of view instead.
