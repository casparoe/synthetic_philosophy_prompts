I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a column about a style of reasoning that has become indispensable wherever systems are too large or too opaque to analyze directly: you decompose the system into parts, attach to each part a plausible probability of some undesirable behavior, and multiply, on the default that in the absence of any specific information about correlation the expectation of a product should be estimated as the product of the expectations. The researchers I have interviewed are refreshingly candid that this default is informal and unjustified in general, and that there is an ongoing effort to turn it into something principled — a rule that says when such an argument is licensed and when it merely looks compelling — but nobody has handed me a case I can actually put in print. One told me the default only fails in toy examples no engineer would ever build; another insisted that toy examples are precisely what real systems look like from the inside, and that the difference is only visible afterward. My editor wants arithmetic a reader can follow, and my fact-checker has a statistics background but no patience for notation she cannot verify by hand. My readers know elementary probability and none of the technical literature, so every term must be defined where it first appears; I am not asking for references or suggestions for further reading, because the example has to carry the entire weight itself.

So please engineer a system and an argument about it, to the following specification. The system should be ordinary and recognizable — a redundant monitoring ensemble, a chain of automated checks guarding an industrial process or a data pipeline, a set of separately designed and separately maintained safeguards — with nothing science-fictional about it, no adversarial intelligence, no exotic physics, and no self-reference. The failure of interest must require every component to fail at once, so that multiplying is the natural form the argument takes, and the per-component failure probabilities should be the sort of round figures a real analyst would write down. A careful, competent analyst then produces a heuristic argument concluding that the joint failure is negligible; I want the step at which the default about independence does its work flagged explicitly. Two further constraints are what would make the case worth printing. First, the analyst must run a correlation audit that checks every pair of components, and that audit must come back exactly clean — not within sampling error, not because the analyst stopped early, but because the pairwise independence genuinely holds. Second, the true probability of the joint failure must nonetheless exceed the analyst's estimate by at least a factor of a thousand, with the exact sample space small enough that I can enumerate all of it by hand. If you conclude that this last requirement cannot be met without distorting the setting, tell me what the largest honest factor is and which constraint binds, rather than quietly lowering the bar.

To be clear about what would count as failure here: the gap must be generated by the structure of the system and by the ordinary, well-intentioned use of the default, not by a stipulated blunder, an analyst defined into carelessness, or components that turn out to be the same component wearing different hats. If the only way to hit the numbers is to make the shared dependence obvious at a glance, say so plainly. Then give me the demonstration. Keep the scenario itself to two paragraphs and let the analysis run as long as it needs. Lay out the true joint distribution explicitly; establish that the pairwise audit passes, stating the general reason and working at least two pairs through in full, including whichever pair a suspicious reader would choose; compute the true probability of the joint failure and the ratio to the estimate; and name the structural feature that is doing the work. Run the counterfactual — remove that feature, for instance by letting each component draw on a private source of variation rather than a shared one — and show that in the resulting variant the heuristic argument is exactly right, so that I can explain to readers what changed. Tell me what minimal additional assumption would have licensed the original conclusion, whether that assumption is the kind of thing anyone could realistically verify for a system of this size, and why an audit confined to pairs is structurally incapable of detecting its absence. Close with the strongest objection a defender of heuristic arguments would raise against my example — that it is rigged, that no real components behave this way, that a competent analyst would have found the shared cause simply by reading the design documents — together with your honest assessment of how much of that objection lands.
