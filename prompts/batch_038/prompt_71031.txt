ok so i run a logic reading group, four of us, and we're ending the term on Lewis's 1976 triviality paper — the one where he shows there can't be a proposition whose probability always equals the conditional probability. i want to close the last night with a poem instead of a discussion question. half the group is allergic to formalism so it can't read like a lecture, but it has to actually be about the argument, not just vaguely about "if".

here's what i'm after. form: six quatrains, no fixed meter, and every one of them ends with the exact same line, word for word — a refrain that's an ordinary domestic if-then, the kind of thing two people say to each other in a kitchen (ferry, weather, a harvest, a doctor's appointment, whatever feels right). the refrain never varies, not one word, in any of the six. the work it does is that the identical line should carry a different weight in each stanza purely because of what sits above it — that's the point of the paper, that what governs saying a conditional is the conditional probability, not some fixed value the sentence hauls around by itself.

then the argument needs to land in order:
- stanza 1: naive confidence, the refrain feels like a plain report about the world
- stanza 2: same words, new situation, and you can feel that what you'd bet on has moved while the sentence hasn't
- stanzas 3–4: the squeeze, and this is the part i need right — Lewis's trick is that if the identity holds for both "if A, B" and "if not-A, B" (so P(A→B) = P(B|A) and P(~A→B) = P(B|~A)), then P(B|A) = P(B|~A), and the consequent comes lose from the antecedent. show that detachment happening in one human pair of claims, then show it spreading: once you demand the identity everywhere, everything drifts free of everything else
- stanza 5: the trivial world, where the refrain finally means exactly the same in every context because nothing is connected to anything. should read as a victory that costs everything
- stanza 6: refrain verbatim again, but the three lines above it have quietly drained the if-clause — nothing turns any more on whether the antecedent holds, so all that's left standing is the bare consequent

after the six quatrains, leave a gap and give me one or two free lines for the concession: we keep saying the line anyway, and we're right to, because assertion tracks the conditional credence; there just isn't a proposition sitting there with that probability.

hard constraints: the words probability, credence, conditional, trivial, Lewis and Adams don't appear anywhere in the poem, and no symbols in it either. then, separately, four or five plain sentences mapping each stanza onto the step of the argument it's carrying, and flagging any spot where you had to fudge the philosophy to keep it a poem — i'd much rather know. a philosopher of language should be able to read it and nod at the reasoning, not just the mood.
