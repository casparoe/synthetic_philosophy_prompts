So I'm giving the departmental colloquium here in about three weeks — 50 minutes plus a half hour of questions — and the paper I'm presenting is my attempt to defuse the Principal Principle rather than defend it. My line is that Lewis's principle was never a bridge from objective chance to rational credence at all. Strip away the metaphysics and what's left is an explanatory deference principle: your credence in A should equal whatever value is assigned to A by the chance-positing hypothesis that best systematizes your total evidence, and — this is the part I actually think is new — by one that would keep being the best systematizer no matter what further evidence comes in. I call that the stability clause. The payoff I'm selling is threefold: the principle no longer commits you to chances as worldly features, so a Humean and a realist can both sign on; the undermining problem goes away, because an undermining future is just one in which the incoming frequencies would knock my current best systematizer off its perch, and it's the stability clause doing the ruling-out, which lets me define admissability non-circularly instead of the way Lewis had to; and it explains why the principle feels a priori without making it a piece of metaphysics.

Here's the room, and this is where I need your help. It's mostly metaphysicians and epistemologists, several of them Lewis-trained and firmly committed to chance being a real feature of the world rather than a posit, and they will be polite but they will not like being told their favorite principle is a symmetry principle wearing a costume. There are two philosophers of physics who think the whole best-system/data-fitting framing is beside the point because chances are fixed by the quantum state, and I've been warned they'll go straight at stipulated cases: a coin I simply tell you is fair, with zero data, and the Born-rule chances that are read off a theory rather than inferred from frequencies. Then there's a visiting statistician who is a subjectivist about probability, cares only about whether a principle can be checked, and will ask me what it predicts that plain calibration doesn't. And a senior person who works on centered evidence will want to know why a principle whose content is fixed by "my total evidence" is a principle about chance at all and not just about me. I know where I'm exposed: best-systematizes is vague and I have to lean on likelihood to cash out fit, which is arguably circular; my principle is a constraint on credence conditional on a hypothesis I may never know to be true, so it may give no unconditional guidance whatsoever; equally good systematizers that disagree about the reference class blow it up; and the stability clause smells tailor-made.
