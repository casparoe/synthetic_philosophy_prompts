I am assembling a problem set for a graduate seminar on the units of selection, and I need a case that does real work rather than one students can dissolve on sight by invoking inclusive fitness. The failure mode I keep getting is that someone proposes an altruistic sterile soldier in a nest, and the entire discussion collapses into a relatedness calculation before it has started. I want a scenario that is quantitatively tractable, biologically ordinary, and engineered so that the three candidate levels — the gene, the organism, and the group — return genuinely different verdicts, with those verdicts falling out of the life-history parameters rather than being asserted. The point of the exercise is that the conclusions must be generated by the arithmetic, so please do not report a verdict you have not computed.

Here is the raw material I would like you to work with; you may tune it, but please do not replace it wholesale. Suppose a fictional but unremarkable weevil whose larvae develop inside individual fallen seed pods of a salt-marsh legume. Each pod holds a clutch of roughly a dozen larvae, the pod desiccates and dies by late summer, and there is one generation per pod. A mutant allele causes a fraction of larvae to stop feeding and instead pack frass into the pod wall and seal the emergence holes; the sealing makes it far harder for a parasitoid wasp to reach the brood and raises whole-pod survivorship substantially, but the sealers that do emerge are stunted and about 60 percent as fecund as their non-sealing pod-mates. Under the baseline life cycle, overwintering adults mix into a single panmictic population and females locate pods essentially at random in spring, so pod-mates are no more related than chance. Under the variant life cycle, everything is identical except that each pod is colonized by the daughters of a single female — equivalently, by a bud from an adjacent pod — so pod-mates are close kin.

Deliver the following, in American English. First, write the scenario itself: a tight description of the organism and of both life cycles, in a paragraph or two, stating the concrete numbers you intend to use. Second, run those numbers for at least two generations in both variants — pod sizes, sealer frequencies, within-pod fecundities, whole-pod survivorship, and an explicit split of total change into within-group and between-group contributions — and confirm by arithmetic that the sealing allele declines in the baseline and spreads in the variant; if the parameters I sketched do not produce that pattern, change them and tell me exactly what you changed and why. Third, give the verdict each of the three perspectives renders in each variant, and say what each perspective has to make of the fact that the trait is costly within pods yet beneficial to pods. Fourth, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the explanatory work, and run two removal tests: what happens if the parasitoid is deleted from the ecology entirely, and what happens if the baseline life cycle is kept but pods are made much smaller, say four larvae each. Finally, pose and then argue the interpretive question I actually want the seminar to fight over — is the difference between the two variants categorical, or is the variant merely the baseline pushed to the far end of a continuum of relatedness and group size? Give me the strongest case for each answer, and say which one survives once your numbers are on the table. Keep the whole thing self-contained: no bibliography, no citations, no invented references, and no page numbers; assume the reader knows the general shape of the levels-of-selection debate but has never encountered this organism.
