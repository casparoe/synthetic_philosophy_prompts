I'm in a graduate seminar on the history of educational thought, and each of us has been asked to bring one early modern theorist to bear on a piece of contemporary instructional technology. My partner took Rousseau and the outdoor-science curriculum; I drew Locke, and the object I have chosen is the literacy-and-drill platform our district adopted for grades three through six this year. It is worth describing precisely, because the details are what generate the puzzle. Each child logs in for about fifteen minutes a day and works through bite-sized exercises with instant right-or-wrong feedback. Correct answers earn points; points accumulate into badges; badges are displayed on a class-wide board. A consecutive-day counter, visible both to the child and to the rest of the class, rises with each day of use and resets to zero after a single missed day. The interface is bright, animated, and lavish with praise, but the praise is generated by the software: no adult need notice that a child has finished a unit, and no adult is available to be asked. Parents receive a weekly dashboard showing minutes, completion rates, and streak length. The district's stated justification, in the rollout memo, was that the platform gives every child the individual pacing that a teacher with twenty-eight students cannot supply, and that it works without anyone ever being sent to the office or threatened with detention.

Nearly everything I retain from Some Thoughts Concerning Education seems to point in two directions at once. On one side, Locke wants a child's instruction made pleasant; he wants learning to feel like play rather than a task or a burden; he cautions against pressing a child at moments when he is out of sorts; and he is happy to teach the alphabet through toys and games rather than through the rod and the grammar-school drudgery he despised. That is a reasonably fair description of the platform. On the other side, Locke is scathing about buying a child's compliance with money, sweetmeats, or fine clothes, on the ground that rewards of that kind install a mercenary and covetous temper where virtue ought to be, and he wants the actual engine of education to be the child's love of esteem and dread of disgrace — worked up by the approval and disapproval of parents and tutor, and eventually by the wider company of reasonable people whose good opinion the child has been brought to want. So my question is whether a badge is a bribe or a form of esteem. If it is esteem doing the work, does it matter that the approving party is a program with no judgment to render, and that the classmates who can see the badge have evaluated nothing themselves? And is a streak that erases itself after one missed day a reward mechanism, or a pain-and-shame mechanism in disguise — the very thing Locke wanted kept out of the schoolroom?

What I want from you is a reasoned prediction of Locke's verdict, not a summary of what he wrote. Please take these up in order. First: on his own account of motivation, is the platform an instrument of esteem or a species of mercenary reward — and if you think neither category fits, say what third thing it is and why he lacked the concept. Second: whether something essential is lost when the audience for a child's performance is an algorithm and a wall chart rather than persons whose opinion the child has been taught to care about, and whether Locke's mechanism can survive being detached from a household. Third: how he would weigh the platform against what it displaced — a 1690s parish or grammar school with its hours of Latin declension, its corporal correction, and its thirty boys moving at one pace — since it is not obvious to me that he would prefer a tutor's method at a scale it cannot reach to a machine's method at a scale it can. Fourth: whether he would reach the same verdict for the children in the working-school scheme he put before the Board of Trade in the late 1690s as for a gentleman's son, or whether his framework licenses a two-tier answer, and if so whether that is a concession to circumstance or a consequence of the theory. Where the texts genuinely leave a matter open, say so rather than forcing a determination, and keep separate the conclusions his stated principles commit him to from the ones you think his audience and station would merely have produced. Finally, if Locke were asked not to accept or reject the platform but to redesign it, tell me what he would strip out, what he would keep, and what he would add that no product team would think to build.
