I am a novelist, and each autumn I am invited to lead one evening session in an interdisciplinary seminar on memory at a small liberal arts college: fourteen students, mostly writers with a handful of cognitive science majors, ninety minutes, no budget. This year I want the session to accomplish something I cannot accomplish by talking. I want them to feel the force of the claim that cognition does not stop at the skull — that a card file can be a constituent of a thinking process rather than an instrument one consults. They will all have encountered the canonical thought experiment beforehand: the man with the memory impairment whose notebook plays for him the role that biological memory plays for the rest of us, and the argument that if the same information were carried in his head we would not hesitate for a moment to call it belief. On the page they found it tidy and unconvincing, which is how arguments arrive when one has not been made to live inside them. I want them to run into the discomfort before anyone names the thesis.

So: design the session as a runnable activity, not a demonstration. Constraints, all of them deliberate. Phones are confiscated at the door, which I realize removes the most obvious example and forces the scaffold to be paper. Materials are limited to index cards, pens, masking tape, one wall, and a stopwatch. No prizes, no scores that require arithmetic. The students do not know one another's work and will not have met before that evening.

I have a seed I would like you to develop, sharpen, or discard in favor of something better. Students first build, under time pressure, an external scaffold for a task that is genuinely too large for working memory — I have been thinking of a family chronology of some forty members with cross-cutting debts, grudges, and inheritances, though you may propose a better one — and then generate prose from it. Midway, while they are writing, the facilitator silently exchanges one file for another: a transplant performed in paper. The student must continue producing coherent text from a structure she did not build. I want to know whether that produces a felt difference of kind, and not merely of familiarity. I would also like a second beat in which some of the cards carry assertions the writer never verified, so that we can observe whether she endorses them automatically or with visible resistance.

What I need from you: the rules and any roles; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits ninety minutes including the debrief; the exact specification of the task the cards must support, and why it defeats unaided memory rather than merely inconveniencing it; what the students physically produce and leave with; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto particular parts of the thesis — the conditions under which an external resource becomes part of the process rather than a cause of it, such as ready availability, automatic endorsement, constant accessibility, and the ease with which its contents are consulted. The debrief should say where the activity makes those conditions vivid and where it merely flatters the view.

Two honest notes I will hold you to. First, I suspect the emotional payload of the transplant — the flare of violation when a stranger's hands are on your cards — is an affective confound that evidences attachment rather than constitution. Tell me whether you agree, and if so either design around it or show me how to spend it deliberately in the debrief rather than let it do unearned work. Second, state plainly which feature of the thesis no paper activity can reproduce, and which I will therefore have to supply by argument in the final ten minutes; and give me the strongest objection the students will be able to raise afterward — the one that says any criterion permissive enough to admit a card file also admits the doorknob, the library, and everyone else in the room, until the word mind has stopped doing any work at all.

Please write in American English. Send me a session I can run on Thursday, not a bibliography: no reading list, no citations, no attributions I would have to verify.
