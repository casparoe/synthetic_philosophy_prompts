I do incident investigation for an NHS trust, and I've been going back through about four years of our post-incident reports trying to pin down a pattern that I cant find a word for. I'd like to know whether one exists.

Here's the pattern. When something goes wrong with a bit of automated or semi-automated kit, the report almost never concludes that nobody is at fault, and it almost never concludes that fault is smeared across the whole system. It concludes with a finding against one named person, and it is nearly always the person whose job was to watch the machine. The monitoring clinician, the controller on nights, the person sat in front of the screen. That person gets the human-error finding, and sometimes the disciplinary, and once or twice the sack.

Now, the bit that actually bugs me: everyone writing the report knows this is a fudge. The background section will say, in the same document, that the person had no realistic way to intervene, that the alarm threshold was set somewhere nobody could distinguish it from the general noise, that one individual was covering four wards because of a staffing decision taken two levels up which the report then declines to comment on anywhere. So it isn't ignorance or a rush to judgement. The finding gets made with the full knowledge, shared by everyone present, that it isn't really about that person.

And then the second half, which is what I actually want to know about. The finding seems to do work. Once there's a named human failure in there, the system itself gets signed off as sound: the recommendation is retraining, or a new checklist, or a poster, and the equipment stays in service and the procurement decision and the vendor's design never get reopened. Compare that with the cases where there is no plausible human to point at. Those go the other way entirely — kit gets pulled, contracts get reviewed, the whole thing is treated as a system failure and stays under review for months. So it looks like the availability of a fall guy is what allows the automation to carry on being certified as safe. The blaming isn't a defect in the investigation. It's load-bearing for the sign-off.

I'm fairly sure something has been written about this, but the closest thing I've stumbled across myself is the idea that once a machine is doing the acting there's nobody left to hold responsible — and that's practically the reverse of what I'm seeing in our reports. There's always somebody. The somebody is produced on purpose, and it's the producing of them that keeps the machine in service.

So: is this a named thing? If it is, tell me what it's called and how the standard account of it actually goes — and in particular how it deals with the point about the blame being a precondition for approval rather than a substitute for it. If it's adjacent to something named but not the same, tell me which and mark the difference precisely. I don't want it folded into a general conversation about scapegoating, because scapegoating is about protecting a group's self-image, whereas this is a technical finding that unlocks a signature. And if nothing fits, then give it a name and define it properly: what has to be true of a case for it to count, where the edges are, and whether an operator who genuinely could have intervened and didn't is an instance or a near-miss that should be kept out.

Two extra things if you can. If the existing concept only covers the blaming half, what would you call the mirror case — the one where no human is available so the system gets grounded? And how would you tell, from outside, whether an organisation is doing this deliberately or has just drifted into a reporting template that makes it happen on its own? I ask because our own answer to that question keeps changing depending on who you talk to.

Don't bother pointing me at papers, I can't get hold of them from behind the trust firewall anyway. I just want the concept and the carving.
