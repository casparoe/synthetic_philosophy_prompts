I'm putting together the opening exercise for a session of my graduate seminar on ontological and verbal disputes, and I've grown tired of the standard illustrations — the statue and the clay, the heap, the ship rebuilt plank by plank — because by week three the students recognise the shape of the case before anyone has finished reading it. This year the session is about whether some of these arguments are merely verbal, and specifically about the old trick of banning a contested word and seeing what, if anything, is left to fight about. I want to open by projecting something that stages that trick without announcing it. What I'd like from you is a sonnet, and then a second version of the very same sonnet with one word replaced. The scene is an invented border valley of your own construction: a river running down from a high pastoral village to a port town at the mouth, with a string of settlements along it, and speech drifting a little at every bend so that each village follows its neighbours comfortably while the two ends of the valley need to slow down and repeat themselves. Two of these people — one from up-valley, one from the delta — are arguing at a market about whether they speak one tongue or two. Both should be given their best case; neither is a bumpkin and neither is a professor, and it should read like an argument that could go on for forty years between people who rather like each other.

Version A has to satisfy the following. Fourteen lines of iambic pentameter in whichever sonnet form you prefer, stated up front, with an identifiable turn: the turn should be the moment one speaker stops pressing the word and proposes some test that doesn't use it. The lines alternate between the two voices with no typographical marking at all — no speaker labels, no italics, no indenting — and a careful reader should be able to work out whose line is whose from the logic of the exchange alone. At least four of your rhyme pairs should be near-rhymes rather than perfect ones (shared vowels, shared consonants, off-rhymes), and exactly one pair should rhyme perfectly: I want the near-rhymes to be the sound of two people who can follow each other but cannot quite repeat each other, and the single perfect rhyme to land in the closing couplet. The two voices must share a single contested monosyllable — one syllable, used by both, applied by each on a different criterion, appearing at least five times across the poem and never once as a rhyme word. Finally, keep the philosophical vocabulary out of the poem entirely: no talk of identity, essence, meaning, or definition. The poem should be made of the valley — the ford, the toll bridge, the way the word for grandmother or for a particular kind of rain is different on the third bend, the child who answers both of them.

Then produce Version B: the identical sonnet, line for line, with every occurrence of that contested monosyllable replaced by a coinage you invent. The coinage must also be one syllable with the same stress, and it should sound unmistakably minted for the occasion — a word somebody made up in a corridor, not a synonym anyone would actually use — so that the reader can hear the stipulation. Everything else stays fixed: meter, rhyme scheme, the near-rhymes, word order, line breaks. Wherever the substitution forces a repair beyond the word itself (an article, an agreement, a stress shift, a hyphen), make the minimal repair and list every one of them afterwards, because those repairs are precisely where the original word was showing its grammar. Retitle each version with the relevant word. After the two poems, give me a diagnosis of roughly three hundred words in which you name the contested term and state the criterion each voice was applying to it; say what would have to be true in the valley for each speaker to be right, and whether both conditions can be satisfied at once; and say what the two voices agree on in that perfect final rhyme, and whether that agreement is a fact about the valley or a fact about the word. We sort these outcomes into three buckets in the seminar, and I'd like you to use them: a dispute **dissolves** if banning the term leaves nothing to argue about; it **splits** if the one word turns out to have been doing two jobs, so that the ban leaves behind a trivially answerable question and a hard one; it **hardens** if the disagreement survives the swap intact and is now visibly about the world rather than about the word. Say which bucket this case falls into, and defend that in a sentence or two. Please don't cite or name anyone — no bibliography, no references, no appeal to what some philosopher or other would say; the diagnosis should stand entirely on the poem and the valley.
