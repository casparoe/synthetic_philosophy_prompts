I run a philosophy-of-science seminar series and the first speaker next term defends a fairly aggressive thesis about chaos. Roughly: because no measurement can ever pin down a state exactly, and because chaotic dynamics amplify tiny differences exponentially, the set of states compatible with anything we could in principle know fans out into macroscopically different futures. So for a big class of real systems the future isn't fixed by what's knowable, and a strictly deterministic chaotic world is observationally indistinguishable from an indeterministic one. She likes to add that this is why Laplace's demon couldn't exist even in a universe whose laws are deterministic all the way down — and she has a habit of answering any technical objection with 'you're conflating the model with the world.'

Write me eight to ten questions that force her to actually engage with the technical stuff rather than restate the thesis. I want each question to come with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Make sure the set covers at least these pressure points:

- The horizon objection: the precision you need only grows logarithmically with the length of the prediction window, so what chaos limits is how far ahead you can look, not whether you can look at all. Get her to say precisely what 'in principle' is doing, and press on whether her claim amounts to anything more than 'predictions degrade,' which nobody disputes.

- The shadowing results: in a hyperbolic system a numerically computed pseudo-orbit stays close to some genuine orbit, so the computation isn't garbage. Have her say what shadowing does and doesn't rescue — my guess is she'll say it finds an orbit but not the orbit, and doesn't recover the initial condition — and then ask whether that makes her complaint about identification rather than prediction, i.e. an epistemic point wearing metaphysical clothes.

- The observational-equivalence results running the other way: there are deterministic systems isomorphic to a process of independent coin flips, and stochastic models that mimic deterministic ones. If the two descriptions can't be told apart even in idealized limits, ask her why she's entitled to use indistinguishability as a premise for a conclusion about what the world is like — and whether her view survives as anything other than a recommendation about how to talk about models.

- Where the amplification comes from. Exponential divergence is a property of the equations; the imprecision is a property of us. Make her say which one is doing the metaphysical work and whether she's smuggling our ignorance into the structure of the world. If she reaches for a finite-information-content move, point out that a demon reading the actual state has no imprecision problem at all — so does she need a non-pointlike ontology, and what does it cost her?

- The climate case, which is her most awkward evidence: trajectory-level weather forecasting fails past a couple of weeks, yet statistically useful projections run for decades. Ask how her view accounts for that, and whether, if the durable predictive content lives in invariant measures and attractor geometry, the fanning-out only defeats the least interesting kind of prediction.

- Quantum mechanics: no classical trajectories, correspondence breaking down on an Ehrenfest-type timescale. Does her thesis even get off the ground in a world that's fundamentally quantum?

- Free will bait. People hear 'open future' from chaos and infer room for agency. Ask whether she means that — and if she says no, ask in what sense a future that is still entailed by the present is open at all, and whether the popular uptake exposes something loose in how she states the view.

- And finish with the discriminating question: what observation, argument, or result would show her wrong? What would count as evidence that we're in the deterministic camp rather than the indeterministic one, given that she's argued we can't tell them apart? If she says nothing could, don't let her rest there — make her say what the world is like on her view, in a sentence that would be false if the world were otherwise.

No softballs, no 'what got you interested in dynamical systems' filler. Order them so the conversation escalates rather than repeats, and mark which two you'd cut if the room starts running short.
