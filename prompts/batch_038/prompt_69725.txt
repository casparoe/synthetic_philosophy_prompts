I am a novelist, and for the past year I have held a residency at an old college, ostensibly to work but in practice to watch. Once a term the fellows process into hall behind a hand bell, and two of them recite a handful of words in a language nobody present can render into English; when I asked the dean what the words meant, he said that nobody knew, and that this was precisely why they could not be dropped. In the same year the college abolished a system of tutorial reports that had run for ninety years, at a single meeting, on the ground that it wasted time, and no fellow supposed the reformers owed anyone a justification. Later the college voted to restore a candlelit ceremony that had lapsed in the 1920s, on the ground that it was traditional, though nobody living had performed it and it was rebuilt from an antiquarian's notes; and a junior fellow's proposal to add one new rite of his own devising was refused as inauthentic.

What does the first practice presuppose about the past's authority over the present that the others plainly do not? Reconstruct the implicit theory: must it hold that a practice can carry knowledge its own practitioners cannot state, that the burden of proof always lies with whoever would change a thing, or only some modest claim about the cost of being wrong? Say which rival views of reasons and reform it thereby rules out, and where it is underdetermined, so that quite different theories would license the same behavior. Then tell me whether the revival and the refusal of the new rite are continuous with that theory or have quietly substituted another one — the testimony of the past in place of the survival of a habit. Finally, what would have to change in how the college decides anything if it adopted the opposite presumption: that a practice must justify itself to the living, or lapse?
