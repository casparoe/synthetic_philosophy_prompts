I'm prepping a talk for a grad seminar on the timing of experience and I want to build a thought experiment, not just rehash the standard one, so bear with me on the setup. The raw material is color phi. Two dots, say red then green, separated on the screen by a few degrees, the second one flashed a couple hundred ms after the first. At the spacings and timings where apparent motion reliably shows up, people don't report two static flashes; they report one dot that travels and then abruptly changes colour somewhere around the midpoint of its path. Which is to say they report having experienced something — the midpath change — at a time when, on any physical account, it hasn't happened yet. Dennett's two ways of absorbing this are the Orwellian and the Stalinesque revisions. Orwellian: the subject really does see red, then a gap, then green arriving at the second location, and the record gets rewritten afterwards, so when they're asked they sincerely say they saw the change halfway along. Stalinesque: consciousness is served late, the brain holds the trial open until it has enough information to compose the whole move-and-change episode at once, so the subject never had the unrevised sequence to begin with and there's nothing to rewrite. The stock line is that in the simple case you can't tell these apart from outside, because both terminate in the same report, and that this equivalence is a big part of why Dennett thinks asking when exactly the experience became conscious is a bad question.

What I want from you is a case where they do come apart. Not a verbal argument about which story is more parsimonious — an actual experimental thought experiment, one a real vision lab could run, in which the Orwellian and the Stalinesque accounts make different predictions about some measurable behaviour on some identifiable class of trials. The point of the case is to test whether the distinction is empirically empty, so a design that quietly assumes the answer is a fail.

Constraints, and these matter, so check each one explicitly rather than quietly violating it. First, ordinary psychophysics only: dots, masks, probes, buttons, eye movements, a secondary task, whatever you like, but no drugs, no implants, no TMS, no sci-fi machinery. Second, you may not seperate them just by fiddling with the interstimulus interval or the dot spacing. That's the obvious move and I'm pretty sure it fails; if you think it works, argue for it, but the deliverable I actually want is a design that doesn't lean on it. Third, the response that separates them can't be a judgement about how the experience seemed, since both accounts predict the same eventual report; the divergence has to surface in a channel the subject isn't introspecting — a saccade, a pointing movement, a speeded guess about something else, a bias leaking into an unrelated task. Fourth, that separating response has to be collectable inside the window where the two accounts disagree about what is already conscious; if it can only come after the trial is over it's just the report again with extra steps.

I have a candidate and I suspect its broken, so tell me straight. My idea: run color phi as normal, but during the gap between the dots flash a very brief neutral probe — a grating tilted left or right, near threshold — and have the subject guess the tilt by button press as fast as they can. My hunch was that on the Stalinesque story the probe gets swept up into the not-yet-composed episode and biases the guess, while on the Orwellian story the probe is processed by machinery that will get rewritten anyway and so shows no such bias. Does that work? If it does, name the mechanism. If it doesn't — and I expect it doesn't, because I can't see what would stop the Orwellian rewrite from covering the probe too — then either repair it or throw it out and give me something better.

After the scenario, I need the analysis: what each account predicts for the separating response, step by step, and why; which single feature of the design is doing the separating work, named rather than gestured at; what happens to the case if I delete that feature — does it collapse back into an observationally equivalent pair, or into something else; the best comeback available to whichever account your design makes look bad, including whether they can escape by adding another layer, another revision or another delay or another draft, and whether that escape is a legitimate refinement or an unfalsifiability cheat; and every place where my constraints or your design already presupposes one of the two accounts. I'm especially worried that talking about a fixed composition window is a Stalinesque commitment smuggled in as neutral vocabulary, so take that head on.

Open by telling me flat out, in your first couple of sentences, whether you think a genuinely separating case is buildable under these constraints or whether I should give up and talk about something else. Don't bury that verdict at the end.
