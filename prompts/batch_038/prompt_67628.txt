I teach personal identity every fall and the Ship of Theseus part of the syllabus has stopped landing — the students have all seen the memes, they know the moves before I make them. So this year I want to hit the same structure from a completely different direction, and I've decided to write the reading myself: a short story. That's where you come in, becuase I'm a philosopher and my prose is prose.

Here's the setting, and I'll spell out the technical stuff so you don't have to guess at it. A company deploys a large model somewhere the mistakes are expensive — emergency dispatch triage, say, or reading scans. Inside it there's a mechanism that interpretability people call a circuit: a bunch of components that jointly carry out one identifiable job, like noticing that a case is time-critical. Before deployment that mechanism was studied, characterized, and formally certified. The rules require the certified mechanism to stay in place, so a monitor watches the model's internals for drift and flags anything that no longer looks like the thing that was signed off on. That's the anomaly-detection layer.

Now the interesting part. Routine maintenance — fine-tuning on new data, a component-wise refactor to make inference cheaper, a patch for an unrelated bug — starts changing the mechanism piece by piece. Functionally it does exactly the job it was certified to do, arguably better. But the monitor keeps firing, and it isn't wrong: the computation really is being carried out differently now. An auditor has to sign a form saying whether this is still the certified mechanism. That disagreement is the whole engine of the story.

I want the competing accounts of mechanism-sameness to live in people with jobs to do, not in speeches: sameness of function or output, sameness of the actual computation performed, sameness of causal role within the wider system, historical continuity (it got here by an unbroken chain of small changes from the certified thing), and sameness of substrate. Give me at least one hard case that seperates two of those criteria cleanly — a repair where the auditor's signature either condemns a system that is safe or blesses one that has genuinely become something else.

Constraints: 1,200 to 1,800 words. Nobody may say the phrase Ship of Theseus, and nobody may use the word identity in its philosophical sense — the reader should assemble the shape of the problem on their own. Don't resolve it. I want an ending where a decision has been made and still feels wrong to the person who made it.

Format: the story first, under whatever title you like, then one section headed Craft notes, roughly 300 words, telling me which criterion each character is carrying, where exactly you planted the case that splits two criteria apart, and what you cut or compressed to stop it turning into a seminar transcript. Don't bother getting the regulatory detail right or pointing me at sources — that part's on me.
