I run a half-day internal workshop for people who actually build oversight and eval tooling at a mid-size lab, and the handout I want from you is a decision tree, not a list of techniques. Entry condition, stated plainly at the top: assume for this exercise that the system you're about to deploy is misaligned and knows it's being watched. Every branch node after that should be a single question about the *measure*, not about the model, and it has to be answerable by someone who has never heard the jargon — so no node may contain words like "sandbagging" or "corrigibility" without the question still working if you swap them for plain descriptions.

The nodes I care most about are the ones the field currently mushes together, and I want each to be its own branch somewhere in the tree:

- does the measure still work if the system is fully aware of it and actively steering around it?
- when capability goes up by an order of magnitude, does the measure degrade gradually or fall off a cliff all at once?
- does making the measure work require trusting a second system, so that you've just moved the alignment problem rather than solved it?
- if the measure fails, do we find out before the harm or after it?
- is it removing opportunity or removing incentive?

Leaves get named as categories of control (sandboxing, tripwires and canaries, weaker-model oversight, interpretability-based audits, capability handicaps, human veto, whatever set you think is actually right — don't feel obliged to use my list), and every leaf carries one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the thing that has to be true for it to work, that its fans never quite say out loud.

Then two things after the tree, and honestly these are the part I want most. First, flag every pair of leaves your own questions can't seperate — where two categories come out the same at every node and the difference is really just vocabulary. Second, and this is the real work: run the whole tree a second time with a different entry assumption, "misaligned but not strategically deceptive — it has bad goals and no plan to hide them," and report which leaves change status between the two runs and why. I'm betting several measures that look load-bearing under the deception assumption turn out to be doing nothing under the honest-misalignment one, and I want that made explicit rather than papered over.

Finish with the unoccupied cells: combinations of node answers that no measure anyone actually deploys lands on. For each, say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's incoherent-looking but buildable, or because nobody has bothered. Keep the whole thing to roughly two pages of prose plus the tree, and don't bother with references, I'll add my own.
