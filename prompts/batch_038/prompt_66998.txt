social choice PhD here, mostly matching markets, and there's something about the residency match that I can't get my advisor to engage with, so I'm throwing it at you.

The setup: applicants submit a rank order list and are told, basically, that truth-telling is a dominant strategy — rank them the way you actually want them and you can't be hurt. Everyone in my program knows G–S says you can't get that in general without a dictator, and the reason matching markets get to have it is the restricted domain: two-sided, responsive preferences, the whole trick. Fine. But then the guarantee is lopsided — it holds for the proposing side, which is why the NRMP moved to an applicant-proposing algorithm in the late 90s after years of complaints that the old one favored programs — and it evaporates entirely for couples, where there are preference profiles with no stable matching at all, so the guidance to couples amounts to telling them to think carefully about how they construct their lists rather than to just be honest.

So the practice says one thing to everybody and the theorem behind it covers some people and not others. What does the instruction actually presuppose? Not the game theory, the philosophy. Does it assume preferences are fixed and fully known before the mechanism opens, so that filling out a list is reporting rather than deciding? It can only carry order, never intensity, which seems to rule out any view where how badly you want somthing is part of your claim — and yet the same hospitals triage by urgency all day in the ER. Is strategy-proofness a norm about honesty (make lying pointless) or about protecting whoever can't compute a best response? The literature treats those as the same demand and I don't think they are.

And what would change, philosophically, if we dropped it as a design goal and handled manipulation the way we handle tax evasion — permitted, audited, sanctioned?

If you think I've mis-set this up — the couples case isn't the counterexample I take it to be, or presupposition is just the wrong lens for a piece of engineering — say that plainly rather than answering the question I asked.
