Last week my friend — a postdoc in condensed matter, so not a string person at all — killed our reading group's whole discussion of the landscape and eternal inflation with three words: "that's not even wrong." Everybody laughed, we moved on to the next paper, and I couldn't figure out afterwards what he'd actually claimed. It bugs me because he's smart and I don't think he was just dunking, and also because two days later he said the opposite-seeming things: he's totally fine with inflationary cosmology even though "obviously we'll never see the other bubbles," and separately he said he'd take the multiverse more seriously if there were "literally no other way to get the constants we measure," which is not a Popperian criterion at all. So the slogan is doing some work in his head that isn't just falsifiability, and I want to know which work.

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be standing for? Stuff like: not testable even in principle; not testable by any technology we could plausibly build; makes no predictions that weren't reverse-engineered from what we already knew; its central terms aren't defined sharply enough for anything to be true or false of them; it's compatible with every possible observation and therefore says nothing; it's a category mistake rather than a bad theory; and, as a last option, it's not a claim about the theory at all but an insult about the people doing it. For each one, tell me whether it's a platitude everybody already accepts, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, and — the part I care about most — whether that reading would actually license waving off the whole enterprise the way he did, or whether it would also sweep up parts of physics he plainly likes (I have a feeling some of these readings condemn early inflation, or Everett, or the measure problem, or whatever). Then say which single reading, if any, is consistent with the other two things he told me, since that's probably the one he was using.

Please don't give me the provenance of the phrase or who supposedly said it, and no reading list — I'm asking you to think, not to cite. Numbered menu, three sentences per item at most, and cap the whole thing at 700 words. American English, pls.
