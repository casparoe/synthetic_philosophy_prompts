I'm a third-year graduate student in philosophy working on a dissertation chapter about the epistemology of set theory, and I'm also presenting at our department's logic seminar in a few weeks. I know the textbook moves here well enough: the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem gives us a countable model of any consistent first-order axiomatization of set theory; that model satisfies the sentence asserting that there are uncountably many reals, or uncountably many ordinals; and the apparent contradiction evaporates once we notice that uncountability is not absolute between a model and the metatheory, since the bijection witnessing countability from outside simply is not among the model's sets. What I can't get a clean grip on is how much philosophical work that standard reply actually does, and last week our seminar got into a dispute about it that I have not been able to resolve on my own.

The dispute ran as follows. One of my colleagues argued that Skolem's paradox is nothing more than the familiar failure of first-order theories to pin down their intended structures, and that it therefore adds nothing to the general problem we already have with arithmetic. On her account, a countable model of set theory that believes its reals are uncountable is exactly parallel to a nonstandard model of Peano arithmetic that believes arithmetic is inconsistent because it contains a nonstandard number coding a finite proof: in both cases the model gets things wrong for structural reasons we understand perfectly well, and in neither case does anything surprising follow about the subject matter. A second colleague objected that the two situations differ in a way that matters, namely in the direction of the failure. The nonstandard model of arithmetic is inflated: it contains spurious elements that ought not to be there, and it satisfies a false sentence because of what it has too much of. The countable model of set theory is deficient: it satisfies a true-in-the-model but externally false sentence because it lacks a set that exists in the universe, and it is precisely this deficiency, she said, rather than any surplus, that gives the paradox its bite. Neither of them would budge, and I confess I find both positions attractive and cannot say what turns on the difference.

So what I would like is an explanation, pitched at readers who are comfortable with first-order logic and elementary model theory but who have never met forcing, of whether the assimilation of Skolem's paradox to the nonstandard-models-of-arithmetic phenomenon is correct, and if not, where exactly it breaks down. Please make explicit whether the inflated-versus-deficient contrast my colleagues were trading in is a real conceptual distinction or a red herring: does it matter, for a realist about sets, whether a model errs by containing a bogus witness or by omitting a genuine one? Please also address one further wrinkle that I think bears on the question but that neither side engaged. Set theory can formalize much of this internally: by combining reflection with Löwenheim–Skolem and the collapse lemma, the theory proves for each finite fragment of its own axioms that there is a countable transitive model of that fragment, even though it cannot, on pain of inconsistency, prove that it has a model at all. I want to know whether that internalization changes the character of the puzzle, since it suggests the embarrassing result is a theorem of the very theory it seems to embarrass rather than a fact about the theory read from outside by a metatheoretic observer. Finally, please say something about how the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem on its own relates to Putnam's later model-theoretic argument against metaphysical realism: my impression is that Putnam took the ordinary countable-model diagnosis to miss his point entirely, that he thought the real problem was not the existence of unintended models but the absence of any fact that could make one of them the intended one, and that his permutation argument was meant to show this in a way that the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem by itself does not. If that impression is wrong or oversimplified, correct it. Please give me your answer as continuous prose only, in the manner of a letter rather than a handout: no headings, no bullet or numbered lists, no bold or italicized emphasis, and no tables. I would rather you state your verdict on the seminar dispute in the first couple of sentences and then work through the reasoning, and I would rather you be candid about the places where the distinction between the two colleagues' positions turns out to be merely verbal than that you manufacture a sharper contrast than the material supports.
