I teach early modern at a state school, and for a couple of years now I've been the unpaid "philosophy person" on a city committee that screens proposals for local-money schemes — a use-by voucher for one neighborhood, a stamp currency for another, an e-cash pilot, that sort of thing. The person who actually has to make these calls is a program manager with a public-policy master's, no economics, and about ninety minutes per proposal; two to four come in a month, and whatever she recommends goes up to a council subcommittee that has never in its life read anything longer than a page. After teaching The Querist this semester it struck me that Berkeley is weirdly the right patron saint for this job, and I want to hand her something she can actually run instead of my seminar notes.

Here's the raw material, in my own paraphrase — my Querist is sitting in the other office, so please don't worry about exact wording or which numbered query says what, and don't send a reading list either, she'd never look at it. The claims I have in mind: money isn't wealth, it's a device for exciting industry and for recording and transferring claims on other people's labor, closer to a ticket or a counter than to a thing with value in itself; a place can be poor because its money doesn't move rather than because it lacks gold and silver; the amount of money in a country should be proportioned to the industry and commerce it's there to circulate, and too much is as bad as too little; what matters is quick circulation, so hoarded money is dead weight; paper and credit can do everything specie does provided they're anchored to something real like land or expected future produce; and — the bit everyone skips — money operates on people's wants, so stirring the appetite to consume is part of what makes it work at all.

Turn that into a decision procedure she can follow: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each when she has essentially no data (tell me which proxies are acceptable and what to do when she can't get a number at all), the steps where it stops at a flat no, and the steps where it has to hand the thing back to the council because its a question of values rather than facts. Say which of Berkeley's distinctions have to survive the simplification and which I can let her drop, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire — including where Berkeley is simply silent or, frankly, wrong, and where I'd have to bolt on something non-Berkeleian to make the thing usable; flag those patches visibly so I can see them. Then run it on three live proposals: an expiring voucher redeemable only at businesses in a district with high retail vacancy and a lot of idle capacity; a demurrage stamp currency in a town whose two main plants closed, where the organizers are counting on the holding fee to force spending; and e-cash for a few hundred displaced families in a camp where nearly everything for sale is trucked in from outside. Fair warning, I'm fond of Berkeley and I'd like to tell the committee he earns his keep here, so if the procedure kills one of these — or worse, blesses the one I think is a gimmick — say so plainly and show me which step is doing the work. I want a seperate one-pager she can hand up at the end of each run, and mark anything that's really a judgment call dressed up as procedure.
