I'm in my second year of a PhD program that runs a joint seminar between the archaeology department and political theory, and for three weeks we have been going around in circles on whether "collapse" picks out a real process or is a normative verdict dressed up as a description. One side of the room — call them the systems people, working in the tradition that treats collapse as a rapid and substantial loss of sociopolitical complexity — argues that something genuinely went wrong: the marginal return on further investment in complexity eventually falls below the cost of maintaining it, polities therefore shed hierarchy, and this shedding is in principle measurable. The other side, mostly people trained on Mesoamerica and Oceania working in the resilience-and-transformation idiom, says the framing is an artifact of the evidence: we notice the cessation of royal inscriptions and monumental construction because elites leave those behind, and then infer the end of a society from the end of its elite's media, when in fact divine kingship was abandoned in some Maya regions while persisting in others, and Maya-speaking populations continued unbroken through the present. Then there is Rapa Nui, where the parties do not even agree about the chronology, since whether the demographic and ecological decline substantially predates European contact is itself contested; and Cahokia, where the standard story has shifted from abandonment to dispersal and reorganization, and where whether the surrounding region was depopulated or merely rearranged is argued about as well.

Here is my working diagnosis, which I want you to test rather than endorse. I think the two sides are implicitly tracking different bearers of continuity — the polity, the population, the elite cosmology, the subsistence system, the built landscape — and each declares collapse or continuity as a function of which bearer it happens to track, so the argument never actually connects. But I suspect a second, deeper fault line that I cannot quite name: that one side's claims are about actual outcomes (did the people survive, did complexity fall), while the other's are about fragility and counterfactuals (could this arrangement have persisted given what it cost to maintain), and that a claim of the second kind is compatible with everything the first kind affirms. If that is right, the impasse is neither merely verbal nor substantive in the ordinary sense; it is a mismatch in what kind of claim is being made, and neither side can refute the other because neither is answering the other's question. I also wonder whether a third thing is doing work underneath both: whether "collapse" is supposed to carry an evaluative charge at all — whether a fall in complexity that leaves ordinary people better fed is a collapse or a liberation — and whether that question is answerable or is simply where each side's intuitions bottom out.

So diagnose this. Tell me which of these fault lines is load-bearing, and reconstruct the two sides' commitments in enough detail that I can see whether your diagnosis holds; I want you to characterize what each side is committed to and what it treats as evidence (epigraphic and monumental cessation, settlement survey, paleoclimate proxies, osteological and ancient DNA data), not to name authorities or hand me a reading list, of which I have plenty. Then tell me what would count as progress. Would an operational definition settle it — some agreed threshold on a measurable variable that deliberately decouples loss of political complexity from population decline and from any evaluation of how the change affected ordinary people — or would that merely relocate the dispute into arguments about the threshold and about which variable gets to be the variable? And is there a case, actual or constructed, that would discriminate between the readings: one where the polity vanished, the population persisted and dispersed, and material conditions arguably improved, forcing the two sides to say different things about the same facts rather than different things about different facts? If your honest conclusion is that no such progress is available, that the disagreement is bedrock about what the concept is for, say so, and then explain why the field keeps arguing anyway rather than splitting into two non-communicating vocabularies.
