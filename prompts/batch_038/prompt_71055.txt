You are a decision theorist who has also spent years teaching game theory to clever undergraduates whose mathematics goes no further than high-school algebra. I am running a fourth-year seminar on the foundations of rational choice, and this week we are on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies. My students have grown suspicious of the standard textbook examples, in which the first round of deletion is visually obvious and the whole procedure looks like a rigged puzzle; two of them have gone further and claimed that once you allow a strategy to be dominated by a *mixture*, the method "proves whatever the analyst wants," because you can always cook up weights that make some honest option look irrational. I want a case, built to order rather than borrowed from a book, that answers them by showing the mixed-strategy step doing real and irreplaceable work. Please think carefully, show every inequality explicitly, and begin with a short paragraph of design notes — which player owns the doomed strategy, what the payoff comparisons will turn on, how you intend to verify that all my constraints are jointly satisfiable — before you write anything else.

Construct a two-player, simultaneous-move, non-zero-sum game with an everyday setting (a pair of competing small businesses, two municipal departments fighting over one grant, a landlord and a tenant, a clinic and an insurer — anything concrete and recognisable, but with no communication, coordination, or side payments between the players), three strategies per player, and payoffs in thousands of dollars of annual profit. The scenario must *generate* the following verdicts from the numbers rather than assert them:

1. At the outset, no strategy of either player is strictly dominated by any pure strategy of that player. A student who refuses on principle to consider mixtures gets stuck immediately, with all nine cells still live.
2. At the outset, exactly one strategy in the whole game — call it the pivot — is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy of its owner. State the mixture with explicit weights, verify the domination inequality cell by cell, and then give the *entire interval* of mixing weights for which the domination holds, not merely one witness.
3. Confirm, independently of the domination arithmetic, that the pivot is not a best response to any belief about the opponent's play, including mixed beliefs.
4. Deleting the pivot unlocks a cascade: at least one further strict dominance becomes available, and continued elimination terminates in a single cell, which is the game's unique Nash equilibrium.
5. Add a bitter twist: that surviving cell must be Pareto-dominated by some other cell of the original game — ideally by a cell in which the pivot itself is played — so that the two players end up jointly worse off precisely because the elimination logic marched them away from it.

After the scenario, do the analysis in numbered stages, giving the reduced comparison at each stage, and then explain in one intuitive, quasi-geometric sentence how a strategy can be beaten by no pure alternative yet be beaten by a lottery over two of them. Say whether the order of elimination matters here and why; explain why deleting a strictly dominated strategy cannot destroy a Nash equilibrium, and what goes wrong if the domination is weak rather than strict. Then confront the objection my students will actually press: a real person facing a one-shot decision cannot literally "play" a mixture, so what is the randomisation supposed to represent, and what does the argument assume about the agent's attitudes to lotteries over her *own* actions? If an agent is not linear in probabilities over her own options and so finds the pivot strictly better than any coin-flip between the two alternatives, does that rescue the pivot, or merely relocate the disagreement? Say how your case relates to rationalizability, noting whether the equivalence between surviving this procedure and being rationalizable rests on any assumption that holds for two players but might fail when there are more. Finish with an honest note on the engineering: which single feature of the payoff structure makes the mixed-strategy step indispensable, what the case collapses into if I remove that feature, and how a student defending the pivot as a perfectly rational choice would best attack your construction — together with the reply I should give her. If any of my specifications turn out to be mutually unsatisfiable, say so, show why, and then build me the closest case you can. Aim for roughly a page and a half: a compact scenario paragraph, the stage-by-stage elimination, and the conceptual discussion. No reading list, no references, no citations — everything I need must come from your own reasoning.
