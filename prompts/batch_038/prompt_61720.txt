I write literary fiction. Chapter twelve of my current draft is a house with nobody in it for about forty pages — the family's away, so I just track light moving across the floorboards, a tap, a cat that belongs to no one. My beta reader, who did a philosphy degree, says that's exactly why Berkeley's master argument is garbage: Berkeley's whole move is that you can't frame an idea of something nobody's perceiving, since the framing is itself a perceiving, but novelists frame unperceived rooms for a living, so the argument must prove too much. He also said my narrator is basically Berkeley's God, holding the whole town in mind at once so nothing blinks out between chapters.

Take the analogy apart for me. What's meant to map onto what — narrator, manuscript, reader, the described room — and where does the mapping snap? A lot of the book is free indirect, so the perceiving is smeared across characters and narrator anyway; does that help him or hurt him? Tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for his claim and which are just noise, whether his deflation of the master argument survives, and if the analogy's the wrong one to begin with, what comparison would fit what chapter twelve is doing better.
