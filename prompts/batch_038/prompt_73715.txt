Our alignment reading group has been chewing on HCH — Christiano's setup where a human consults a copy of themselves, who consults copies of themselves, recursively, so a question that's too hard for one unaided person gets unpacked into a tree of easier sub-questions that some human somewhere in the tree can just answer on the spot. The gloss we've been working with, borrowed from the factored cognition side: a task is *perfectly factored* when you can write it as a DAG all of whose nodes are "basic" — things a single unaided human can do in one sitting, like "is this claim consistent with that one," "which of these two summaries is more faithful," "does this code recieve the expected inputs and return the right output on these three cases" — and whose root node's output is the answer to the original question.

One of us came in with an argument that seems to prove way too much and I can't find the exit. (1) If a task admits a perfect factoring, HCH can carry it out, since by construction every node falls inside a single human's unaided competence. (2) Big collaborative science admits a perfect factoring: the division of cognitive labor in something like a particle-physics collaboration is already a DAG of basic operations — calibrate this channel, write this analysis note, referee this paper, check this simulation against that one — each performed by an individual human doing a bounded job. (3) So HCH could in principle carry out whatever such a collaboration carries out, including results no unaided human could reach. (4) Some of those results aren't merely outputs but known truths; we have overwhelming independent grounds for believing in the Higgs boson. (5) Yet in the HCH run no node grasps the whole result — each only answers its own basic question — and the same holds of the actual collaboration, where nobody has the whole thing in their head. (6) So a system can be reliably right that p while nothing inside it understands p, and adding more components can't repair this, since every component is basic by hypothesis. (7) Therefore understanding isn't necessary for reliability, and the HCH/iterated-amplification picture of alignment — which is supposed to let us ask "what would a human conclude about this, given enough time and help" — is keyed to the wrong quantity: what actually scales with tree depth is reliability, and there's no reason to expect it to stay coupled to the human perspective.

I want to reject (7) but I cant tell which earlier step licenses it, or whether any of them do. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine when we read it out loud, and what does rejecting it cost? I'm most attached to (2) honestly — the science case is the intuitive pump for the whole factoring idea, and if it doesn't work there I'm not sure what factoring is even for. One warning: don't just tell me the tree accumulates errors, because (6) is supposed to hold even for a tree that gets the right answer, and error rates feel like an engineering detail rather than a principled objection. And if your diagnosis is that (2) trades on two senses of "factoring," spell out what a genuine perfect factoring of even a modest scientific claim would have to look like that the collaboration's DAG isn't — I want the positive account, not just the diagnosis.
