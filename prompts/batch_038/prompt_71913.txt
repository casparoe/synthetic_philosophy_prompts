Question 3 (suggested length 500–600 words; 100 points). This question concerns the two chapters from Dennett's Elbow Room (1984) set in week nine. Dennett distinguishes the sort of freedom that libertarians say they want from what he calls the varieties of free will worth wanting. Explain the distinction and what it is meant to establish. Your answer should (a) say what, on Dennett's account, an agent must have in order to be a proper subject of praise and blame; (b) explain how he thinks the objection that a determined agent could not have done otherwise is best answered; (c) explain how his compatibilism differs from Frankfurt's; and (d) offer at least one piece of critical evaluation of your own. Marks are distributed as follows: 30 points for part (a), 30 for part (b), 25 for part (c), and 15 for part (d). A score above 85 indicates an answer that is accurate in its exposition of the position and that identifies at least one genuine difficulty for it; 70–84 indicates an answer that is broadly accurate but imprecise in places, or that misses a complication; below 70 indicates material misattribution, or an argument that does not hold together.

The student's answer, reproduced exactly as submitted (538 words):

---
Dennett is a compatibilist: he holds that the truth of determinism would not by itself take away the freedom that matters for moral responsibility. What the libertarian wants is something much stronger — a will that is not caused at all, a capacity for choice standing outside the causal order and originating action from nowhere. Dennett's objection is that this is not a coherent demand. If a choice were genuinely uncaused, it would be a random event, and a random event is no more mine than a twitch or a spasm; so the libertarian is asking for something that would not be worth wanting even if it could be had. In its place Dennett offers an inventory of the things that are worth wanting and that we can actually have: self-control, the capacity to foresee what is coming and to take steps against it, and a general responsiveness to reasons rather than to shoves and pulls.

What an agent must have, on this view, in order to be a fit subject of praise and blame is a certain kind of control architecture. Dennett's illustration is the digger wasp, which stings and paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, goes inside to check that all is ready, and then hauls the cricket in. If the cricket is pulled a few inches away while the wasp is underground, the wasp emerges, drags it back to the entrance, goes in again, and repeats the sequence indefinitely. Nothing is interfering with the wasp; it is doing exactly what it wants, unimpeded, and yet it plainly has no freedom worth the name. Freedom, for Dennett, is the opposite of this sphexishness: the plasticity to register that the world has changed and to do something different. Human beings have a good deal of that, and it is what makes us responsible rather than merely complicated.

Against the objection that a determined agent could not have done otherwise, Dennett deploys the conditional analysis. To say that she could have done otherwise is to say that she would have done otherwise had she chosen or wanted to; and determinism leaves that true, since it remains the case of the ordinary competent agent that had she wanted to keep her promise she would have kept it. What determinism excludes is only the further thought that she might have wanted something else out of nowhere — which, as the first paragraph showed, is not a freedom anyone should want.

Frankfurt's account differs in being structural rather than dispositional: for Frankfurt a will is free when the agent's second-order volitions endorse the first-order desires that move her, so that those desires are properly her own. Dennett agrees that this mesh matters but thinks it cannot be the whole story, since identification of the right kind is itself a capacity that had to be designed, and since what Frankfurt leaves out is the agent's grip on the future — the ability to represent how things are likely to go and to steer accordingly. It is also here, I think, that Dennett needs a limited element of chance: on his model of deliberation the agent's final choice is itself partly undetermined, and it is this that keeps the decision from being fixed in advance by her character and her circumstances.

My own reservation is that the wasp cuts against Dennett rather than for him. If freedom is the absence of a fixed routine, and determinism means that every action is the fixed product of a routine laid down by the past, then a determined agent is sphexish by definition, and Dennett has described away the very freedom he set out to preserve.
