For several years I've been a more or less daily reader of the rationalist blogosphere, and one habit I've absorbed there is a standing suspicion of explanation-shaped stories: if a hypothesis can be stretched to absorb any evidence that arrives, it probably explained nothing in the first place. I want to turn that suspicion on a case I care about in the philosophy of social science — whether culture can be a cause at all — using the Protestant ethic debate as the running example. I've drafted a short essay for a reading group I attend with some friends; rather than paste the whole thing, here is the skeleton, and the comments I want are on the skeleton.

The essay opens from the observation that the standard statement of Weber's thesis (doctrine inculcates inner-worldly asceticism, asceticism produces saving and reinvestment, saving produces capitalism) is attacked by at least three families of critics who are not, in fact, attacking the same proposition: the Samuelsson-style archival deflation of the historical record, Tawney-style arguments about the direction of influence between religious movements and economic change, and modern quantitative reanalyses such as Becker and Woessmann's Prussian county study, which attributes the Protestant economic advantage to Bible-driven literacy rather than to an ethic. My central claim is that these critiques target three distinct causal roles that get collapsed into the single phrase culture causes X: (1) culture as preference shifter — it changes the psychic price of a life-conduct, so that diligence and deferred consumption stop being costly and conspicuous leisure stops being rewarding; (2) culture as belief shifter — it changes what agents take the probabilities and payoffs to be, as with worldly success read as evidence of election; (3) culture as equilibrium selector — it leaves motives and beliefs intact but moves a population from one self-sustaining pattern of conduct to another, the way internal monitoring within a dissenting congregation changes which of several feasible commercial arrangements is stable. I argue that Weber's own switchman image, on which interests supply the motive force while ideas merely fix the tracks along which that force runs, is really a claim in family (3) with an admixture of (2), and that most of the famous objections are aimed at a family-(1) thesis he never asserted.

The deliberately provocative step comes next: I claim Becker and Woessmann do not refute Weber, they relocate him. Literacy was demanded because of doctrine — the obligation to read Scripture, the priesthood of all believers — so the causal chain still begins in theology and merely runs through skill formation instead of ascetic psychology. I then try to preempt the obvious charge of unfalsifiability with a criterion of my own: a doctrine-first account loses content exactly when the specific content of the ethic becomes dispensable, that is, when you could substitute any other theology generating the same demand for literacy and the historical record would look identical. A shorter closing section argues, following Hart and Honoré, that singling out culture as the cause is partly an exercise in causal selection against a background of normal conditions, and that culture usually behaves less like an intervention variable and more like a parameter one cannot turn — which is why it attracts identification worries it may not deserve.

What I'd like from you is criticism rather than encouragement, on four points. First, are the three causal roles genuinely distinct, or is (3) just (2) wearing a game-theoretic costume, since equilibrium selection plausibly operates by changing what each agent believes about what others will do? If you think the boundary collapses, show me the collapse rather than asserting it. Second, is the relocation move a real argument or motivated reasoning — and, more usefully, what empirical finding would count against a doctrine-first account, given that any discovered mechanism can be redescribed as downstream of doctrine? Third, how accurate is my gloss on the switchman image, and would Weber himself accept being read as claiming (3)? Fourth, does the causal-selection section earn its place, or is it decoration I should cut; if it can be load-bearing, tell me what it would have to do to become so. I should add that I'm not looking for further reading suggestions or a bibliography — assume I've read the obvious things and tell me where the reasoning is weak instead. Finally, please flag anywhere I've slid between using culture to mean shared representations and using it to mean local practices, because I suspect I've done it at least twice.
