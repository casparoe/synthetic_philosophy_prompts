I'm a second-year doctoral student in cultural psychology, and this term I'm working as a coder on my supervisor's project, which pushes the tightness–looseness framework below the level of the nation-state: rather than rating countries, she rates individual institutions — firms, congregations, expeditions, message boards, orchestras — on how much rule-following they demand. She is well known in our department for refusing to state her coding rule; she issues verdicts and expects us to recover the rule from them, the way one recovers a grammar from sentences. I would like you to adopt the standpoint of a measurement theorist and comparative social scientist who has spent a great deal of time worrying about construct validity, and to reason carefully rather than quickly. Please begin by brainstorming the full space of candidate rules — including ones I have not thought of — before committing to a prediction. You will not have access to the literature here and I do not want references, a bibliography, or citations of any kind; everything you need is in the descriptions below, and the interesting work is inference from them.

Her verdicts on the first ten institutions, with the marginal notes she made beside each, are as follows:

1. A Tokyo trading company — tight. Note: norms govern dress, speech register, gift-giving, overtime, seating at dinners, and the handling of business cards; sanctions are almost entirely informal, but no deviation goes unremarked.
2. A Dutch industrial-design studio — loose. Note: near-universal agreement about what counts as good work and good taste; dissenters simply go unrewarded, and no one is ever told anything.
3. An unmoderated anonymous imageboard — tight. Note: subject matter is completely unrestricted, but register, formatting, and reference culture are rigid, and a post that gets them wrong is demolished within minutes.
4. A Hasidic congregation in a secular metropolis — tight. Note: no coercive power whatsoever; sanctions operate through school placement, matchmaking, and who is invited where.
5. An Antarctic winter-over station — tight. Note: every hour is allocated by protocol; deviation is treated as a safety threat; the environment kills people if coordination fails.
6. A Big Law litigation department — tight at work, loose at home, and she refuses to average the two. Note: the same group can be two cultures in two rooms, and a single number per group is a category error.
7. An anarchist social centre — loose. Note: heavy mutual surveillance, since everyone knows everyone's business, and total consensus about values; but, as she puts it, there is almost nothing one must do.
8. A symphony orchestra — tight. Note: what may be played is fully determined and deviations are instantly audible, yet the only sanction is not being re-engaged next season, and members leave freely and often.
9. A university faculty — loose. Note: hundreds of pages of statutes and handbooks, which everyone has read and agrees about the meaning of, covering almost nothing that faculty members actually do from day to day.
10. An early kibbutz — tight. Note: no formal punishments existed, the whole community could see everything, the founding generation had lived through famine and war, and conformity was total in every domain.

The six candidate rules I have been arguing about with two other coders are: (a) coverage — the fraction of the group's own activity space that norms determine; (b) sanction certainty — the probability that a detected deviation draws some response, with severity irrelevant; (c) scriptedness — how few behavioural options the situation affords, regardless of whether anyone ever responds; (d) internalization — whether members would comply if enforcement were removed entirely; (e) normative consensus — agreement about what the norms are; and (f) threat history — the distal ecological and historical threats the group has faced. Here is what I need from you. First, predict her verdict on each of the three new institutions below, with an explicit confidence for each: an intentional community of traditional homesteaders whose written rule of life governs dress, grooming, courtship, agriculture, schooling, media, and speech, on which everyone is fluent and in agreement, but which has formally renounced sanction, so that a violation triggers only an invitation to talk, violators retain full standing, and by our own fieldwork estimate a third of members break a major rule every week without consequence; a long-running private high-stakes poker game with no rules beyond the game itself, total latitude in style, and nothing at all constrained about members' lives away from the table, but where breaches of table etiquette produce immediate, certain, and permanent exclusion, applied without exception and without discussion; and a biohacking community whose norms reach into sleep, diet, supplements, training, spending, screen time, and even friendships, each stated as an optimization target with indefinitely many acceptable implementations, and where failure is met with encouragement rather than censure. Second, tell me which of the rules she is running: state which of (a) through (f) survive all ten verdicts, which are refuted, and by which specific verdict each is refuted — I suspect at least two survive, and if they are near-equivalent on the first ten I want to know exactly why. Third, identify which of the three new cases is the single most diagnostic one, and say which surviving rules it pries apart. Fourth, suppose she codes the homesteader community loose and the biohacking community tight; what would that commit her to, and is it coherent with verdicts 3, 7, and 9 in particular? Finally, a question that came up in seminar and that I would like you to answer honestly rather than diplomatically: the framework is usually invoked normatively, in arguments about how much rule-following a society ought to demand, and the surviving rules do not have the same normative shape. Under one of them a group can make itself tighter simply by adding punishments while nobody's behaviour changes at all; under another it can make itself looser merely by lengthening the menu of permitted options even if everyone keeps choosing exactly what they chose before. Say which rule has which property, and tell me which one you would adopt if you were building an index intended to inform policy, and what that choice costs you.
