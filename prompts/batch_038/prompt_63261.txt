I am a third-year PhD student in the history of economic thought, writing on eighteenth-century Irish monetary debate, and I have been asked — because I am the only person from the parish with a university connection — to give the opening remarks at the launch of a community credit union in a town of about four thousand on the west coast of Ireland. The hall seats two hundred, the acoustics are poor, and everything will be read from a lectern by someone who does not speak in public. In the room will be the founders (a retired national-school principal who will be chairing, several farmers, the publican who has been keeping the books informally for two years), the county councillor who secured the premises, some thirty members who have been out of work since the medical-devices plant closed and who are the actual reason the institution exists, a youth savings scheme staffed by secondary-school students, and one economic historian from Galway who has published on Berkeley's Querist and will catch every allusion I make. My supervisor regards the commission as a month of writing life lost, which is a further reason I want it to be genuinely good rather than ceremonial.

What I need is a speech of roughly nine hundred words that runs on Berkeley's monetary thinking rather than merely name-checking it, and I want to hand you the material explicitly so that you are not guessing at it. The claims I take from the Querist and want built into the structure are these: that money is not itself wealth but a contrivance for setting labour in motion, so that its entire usefulness lies in what it lets people do with one another's work; that a town does not really want money so long as everyone's labour is sure of finding something to do and someone nearby to buy it, and that the genuine scarcity in a depressed place is of occasions to work and to exchange rather than of coin; that paper issued on good security by an institution the public trusts, and can hold to account, can perform the work of specie better than specie does; and that the cautionary case Berkeley kept in view was a paper-money scheme in France that collapsed once the bills outran the security standing behind them. Please render all of this in your own words. Do not place any sentence of Berkeley's inside quotation marks: I would be reading aloud something I could not check, and I have the texts at home if I want a line from them. Do not recruit immaterialism either — no joke about money existing only in the mind. The room has heard that pun, and it is not what the Querist is about.

Three structural demands. First, somewhere in the opening third, a run of six to eight numbered questions of my own composing in the Querist's manner: not rhetorical throat-clearing, but questions somebody in that hall could actually answer about this town, each of which sets up something the speech later collects on. The historian should recognise the form at once; everyone else should simply hear a list of pointed questions. Second, the speech has to carry the awkward fact that Berkeley's version of the industry thesis was made about national character, and sometimes in terms about the Irish poor that are brutal and would not survive contact with a modern audience. It must use the thesis without letting it function, for one second, as a reproach to the thirty people in the room who cannot find work — and I would like a sentence or two after the speech, not inside it, telling me how you arranged for that. Third, this credit union's security is not land: it is the members' own shares and the fact that everybody in the parish knows whether everybody else works. I want that substitution made explicit and treated as the interesting part of the proposal rather than as a technicality. The speech should end on the institution and not on its eighteenth-century antecedent — Berkeley gets named once, twice at most — and it should be sayable aloud in eight minutes without a single sentence I would have to interrupt myself to explain.
