I've been living inside a novel for about two years now that's set in a place where interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing have been fully bureaucratized, and frankly I'm going a bit soft in the head from being the only person in the world who knows this place. Before I commit to another two hundred pages I want to see the conceit handled by someone who isnt emotionally invested in it, so this is basically a commission: a short story, 1,200 to 1,800 words, your voice and not mine. Treat it as a standalone piece rather than a sample of my book — I'll steal whatever survives, which is the whole point of asking.

Here's the furniture. There's an office, call it the Office of Commensuration or whatever you'd call it, that settles one narrow kind of dispute: when some limited good has to go to one person and the governing rule says it goes to whoever has suffered the greater uncompensated loss, somebody has to produce an actual number comparing two people's welfare. Their instrument is an anchor protocol. Every claimant is exposed to the same calibrated reference experience — short, standardized, mildly nasty, fully reversible, identical for everyone — and then interviewed about what in their own life they'd trade to avoid it, and how many copies of it would take to outweigh some other experience they describe. The official story is that this gives you a common unit, the way you'd convert two currencies through a third. In practice the anchor lands differently on everybody: a claimant who's been in pain for eleven years barely registers it, a claimant who's lived a cushioned life comes apart, one of them finds it faintly pleasant and is embarrassed about it, and there's a child in the queue who can't do the protocol at all, so his mother answers on his behalf and everyone in the room knows her answers are contaminated by her own history.

The case you write should be one afternoon, two claimants, one slot: a restorative treatment that only works if given now and only works on one body. One claimant lost the use of her hands in an industrial accident three years ago; she's furious, articulate, and scores high on every instrument they hand her. The other has had, on almost any outside description, a considerably worse life — you invent the specifics — but reports being happy, genuinely rather than defensively, and has been happy for some years now. Taken at face value the numbers say he lost less. Give me the moment the figure comes out, show me the actual digits, and let them be wrong in an interesting way rather than obviously wrong. First person from the technician works, close third on the clerk works, whatever gives you the most friction.

The part I actually care about is this. I do not want a story whose thesis is that measurement is a lie and the clerk weeps into his paperwork — I've read that one, I've nearly written it. The person running the anchor should have a real argument for the practice, something like: we make these comparisons every single day when we decide whose pain to relieve first, we've simply never written them down, and refusing to produce a number doesn't avoid the comparison, it just makes it unexaminable. And the claimant who's happy despite everything should get a real argument back, not a shrug — ideally one that turns on whether adapting to a bad life is a kind of triumph or a loss of information about how bad it was. Let the two collide properly, let the decision get made, and let it not be obviously the wrong decision. Keep it small-bore and in the weeds: the calibration paperwork, the squabble over whose reference session counts as valid, the exact wording on the form, what the room smells like. No lectures, no named philosophers, and nobody explains to a layman what a utility function is.
