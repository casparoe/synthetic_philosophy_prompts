I'm in the second year of a master's programme in environmental history, and this term's seminar is on the historiography of societal collapse. For our next session each of us has to bring in a piece of general-audience writing and report to the group on whether it can be trusted. The one I chose is the companion essay to a recent documentary series about ancient catastrophes — the sort of thing that runs in the culture section of a weekend paper and gets read by a lot more people than our seminar list does. Its central paragraph runs as follows:

"Civilisations collapse when they outgrow the environment that supports them. Joseph Tainter gave the standard definition — a rapid and substantial loss of sociopolitical complexity — and the three clearest cases show how it works. On Easter Island, a few thousand Polynesian settlers cut down the last of the great palms in order to move their statues; with the trees gone the soil washed into the sea, famine followed, and the survivors fell into warfare and cannibalism, so that the society had already broken down completely before the first Europeans arrived. In the southern Maya lowlands the kings stopped raising dated monuments around 900, the great cities were abandoned, and the Maya disappeared from the region for centuries. Around 1200 BCE in the eastern Mediterranean, palace administrations from Mycenae to Ugarit were burned, the Hittite empire came apart, and writing disappeared from the region for four hundred years. The mechanism is the same in each case: rising complexity demands ever more energy, until the marginal return on further investment falls away and the system can no longer meet its own overheads. Collapse, then, is not an accident that befalls complex societies but a stage they pass through — which is why ours is unlikely to be exempt."

My own inclination is that the last sentence is where it fails, but a classmate who works on resilience thinks the whole paragraph is fair and that I would be splitting hairs: she points out that it at least names Tainter and locates the Maya abandonment in the southern lowlands, which most popular treatments don't bother to do. I can't tell which of us is closer to the mark, and I would rather not spend the seminar hedging between her reading and mine.

So I'd like a claim-by-claim audit of that paragraph. For each distinct assertion, give me a definite verdict — something a specialist would accept without qualification, a defensible simplification for a general audience, a live controversy being reported as settled, or simply false — and for the latter two categories tell me what the corrected version would look like and what evidence or argument makes the difference. Pay particular attention to whether the opening sentence and the appeal to Tainter are stating the same theory or two rival ones, since that seems to matter for everything downstream. Then identify which single move is load-bearing for the conclusion: if only one error were fixed, which fix would most damage the final sentence? Finally, tell me plainly whether the closing inference — that collapse is a stage complex societies pass through and that ours is therefore unlikely to be exempt — survives the correction of the paragraph, and if it does not, what the strongest defensible version of the essay's thesis would be. I don't want references or a reading list, just your own judgment about the claims. And I want that judgment committed: where you think the essay is indefensible, say so rather than noting that some scholars disagree; where you think my classmate has a real point, say that too; and if you end up siding with neither of us, say what the third position is and why you hold it.
