so i'm a third year and i run the recording for my department's little podcast — basically me, one other guy, and a borrowed mic. next month we've got an economic historian on who's spent the last several years arguing that Malthus was right but early. and i mean it in a technical sense, not the vague "well he noticed a real tension" sense. she defends a thesis she calls delayed vindication, which as far as i can follow is this: a theory whose prediction fails can still come out ahead evidentially if the failure gets explained by interfering causes that are independently corroborated, and she says the two big ones here — the jump in yields from industrial agriculture and synthetic fertilizer, and the voluntary fertility decline — are exactly that kind of thing, corroborated completely separately from anything Malthus wrote. her stock line when people push back is that the theory didn't fail, the background conditions did, or some variant of "the ceteris paribus clause was never satisfied."

i get to pick the questions and i've got maybe 35 minutes with her. can you write me nine to eleven of them, in an order that escalates — open with stuff that lets her actually lay the thesis out on the table, then progressively make the deflection harder to reach for, and land on the ones that are genuinely uncomfortable to sit through. for each question give me a follow-up keyed to the answer i think she's most likely to give, so i'm not improvising live on tape.

stuff i definitely want in there:

- the 1803 rewrite. the first edition only had misery and vice as checks, and the later editions add moral restraint — delaying marriage — as a third one. i want a question about whether that addition was a legitimate refinement or the original immunizing move, and then, harder: why her own rescue isn't just that same move repeated two centuries later by a friendlier author. she'll say the difference is that hers is independently testable. chase that.

- the window problem. the Simon–Ehrlich bet, 1980 to 1990, five metals, Ehrlich lost and mailed a check — but people have since pointed out he'd have won over most other start and end dates in the century, and that the 80s were close to the worst possible decade for him. she uses this kind of thing to argue scarcity predictions are about tendencies and any single window is nearly a coin flip. i want a question that asks how she stops that from making the theory compatible with every window you care to pick, and whether the window can only ever be identified after the fact. if it can, what's left?

- the track record asymmetry. the trap basically held from the neolithic until around 1800, so on her reading the theory has an absurdly good record and exactly one big exception. is that exception a refutation or a boundary condition, and who has the burden of proof there. she's going to say a law with a ten-thousand-year run and one anomaly is a law with a domain restriction. push on why the restriction isn't just "except when it doesn't happen."

- Boserup. population pressure driving agricultural intensification rather than collapse — the same demographic history that she says vindicates the trap also feeds the rival story where pressure is the engine of innovation. so is the escape predicted by her account or only accommodated by it afterwards? and if both theories explain the long stagnation and both can explain the breakout, is there any observation left that separates them?

- content. if the mechanism was real but masked for the entire period in which we could have observed it, what's the difference between saying the tendency was there and blocked and saying there was nothing there at all? get her to say what a masked tendency commits you to that an unfalsifiable one doesn't.

- the genre question, which i think might be my favorite. the Essay was written as a polemic against the perfectibilists — Godwin and Condorcet. if it's partly an argument against a claim about human improvability rather than a forecast with a date on it, does "right but early" even apply? can you be early about a rebuttal?

- what would make her just say false. not "right but early," not "the clause wasn't satisfied" — actually false. i want her to name, on tape and in advance, an observation that would do it: sustained below-replacement fertility, another couple of centuries of yield growth, whatever she picks. and then ask her to commit to it before we publish.

- the licensing worry. whatever criterion separates delayed vindication from plain immunization has to be statable before the fact, or the phrase can rescue anything — astrology, the second coming, a stock tip. ask her for the rule, and ask whether her own case survives being run through it by a hostile reader.

a few practical things: keep them short enough that i can say them out loud without rambling, i'm bad at this. nothing that can be answered with a yes and a shrug. no softballs and no "what first drew you to Malthus" filler — my co-host already asked if i wanted that one in and i said no. mark the two questions that are most likely to blow up and eat the whole episode so i know where to be ready to cut. and flag any question where a listener who's never read the Essay would be lost, with a half-sentence of setup i can read into the mic first.
