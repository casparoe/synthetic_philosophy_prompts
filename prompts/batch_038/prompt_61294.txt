I am a final-year undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on value theory and the foundations of practical reason. As part of the course, our instructor has asked each of us to draft one practice essay question for the departmental revision sessions held before the exam, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, all of which the instructor will vet and then circulate. I would like assistance producing mine, and I should explain the constraints carefully, since the point of the exercise is to write something that cannot be answered from the standard summary.

The unit under assessment concerns what our seminar has been calling indirect normativity: the family of views on which the content of morality, or of an agent's reasons, is fixed not by directly specifying principles or values but by specifying an idealized deliberative procedure and taking its output as normative. On such views what matters is what the agent would want, or would endorse, or would decide were she fully informed about the relevant non-normative facts, fully coherent in her instrumental reasoning, and vividly acquainted with the experiences at issue, among other idealizations. I would like the deliverable to be usable by students who know this schema but have not thought hard about it.

Please write:

1. One essay question, answerable in forty-five minutes by a final-year undergraduate, that cannot be discharged by defining idealized deliberation, listing the idealization parameters, and reciting the familiar objections.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words.
3. A marking scheme totalling 25 marks, which states explicitly what an answer in the top band must do that a solid but second-class answer typically omits, and which identifies two or three characteristic mistakes that should cost marks, saying why each is a mistake rather than merely an omission.

I want the question built around a specific crux, which I will state so that you have something definite to work with and so that the difficulty is not generic. An idealization specification normally requires the deliberator to be fully informed; but a fully informed deliberator's information would appear to include facts about the output of the very procedure whose output we are trying to determine. The procedure therefore looks self-referential, and it is not obvious that it has a determinate output at all: several mutually inconsistent outputs may be equally admissible, or the output may shift with what the agent expects it to be, in the manner of a fixed-point problem. The obvious repair is to restrict the idealized agent's information, so that she knows all non-normative facts except facts about the procedure's own verdicts. But that repair appears to concede the deeper objection, since deciding which information to exclude is itself a normative decision made in advance, so that direct normativity re-enters at the stage of specification rather than being avoided.

The question should force students to determine whether this is a fatal difficulty, a technical one with a technical solution, or a case of demanding something the view never undertook to supply. A strong answer should engage at least with the following resources and say why they do or do not help: an appeal to stability or convergence across reasonable specifications instead of uniqueness of output; the denial that a determinate output is required, provided the procedure narrows the field sufficiently for practical purposes; and the claim that the self-reference is benign because access to the output cannot alter what the idealized agent would endorse. It should also face the converse pressure, namely that if the defender retreats to convergence, the view risks losing its distinctive claim that the procedure, rather than our antecedent normative commitments, is what does the justificatory work.

Please do not include citations, a bibliography, a reading list, or attributions to named authors, and do not guess at any. Refer to positions and arguments in generic terms; I will supply the attributions myself from the seminar reading list.

One epistemic request. At the end, please tell me how confident you are in the philosophical claims the model answer makes, in particular in the claim that the specification is genuinely self-referential rather than merely loosely described, and in the assessment of the information-restriction repair as conceding too much. Please also single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure, and explain what would settle it.
