I lead technical writing and evaluation reporting at a mid-sized applied AI company. Between model cards, capability evals, incident post-mortems, internal red-team notes and customer-facing summaries, my team signs off roughly forty documents a week, and somewhere in most of them is a sentence about what a system "believes", "wants", "knows", "understands" or "is confused about". Our house style guide currently handles this with a blunt two-line rule: never attribute mental states; always substitute generation verbs — the model "predicts", "outputs", "tends to produce". The rule has failed in both directions. It generates sentences that are either false or uselessly opaque (a report that must say "the model produces the token sequence corresponding to a false belief about the file's location, and subsequently produces actions consistent with that sequence" is unreadable, and my writers quietly ignore it), and it draws no distinction at all between the cases where the folk-psychological gloss is simply the clearest accurate description of a robust, revisable, action-guiding internal disposition and the cases where it quietly advertises an inner life the reader will over-interpret. I want a procedure, not a longer word list.

What I would like you to produce is a decision procedure a writer can run over a single sentence in about five minutes, grounded in whatever the intentional-stance literature actually gives us that is usable. It should take seriously that adopting the intentional stance may be a matter of predictive payoff rather than a claim about what exists, and should tell me whether that difference matters for a house style: if the stance is instrumental, what licenses or forbids writing as though it were not? It should build in the criteria that make an intentional description earn its keep — integration with other attributions, revisability in the face of contrary evidence, counterfactual robustness, guidance of behaviour in novel circumstances — and it should confront the complication that a system can look like a true believer inside a narrow task and collapse into flattery or confabulation when the conversation is nudged in an irrelevant direction, so that the verdict may have to be domain-relative. It should also acknowledge the objection that what these systems have is derived rather than original intentionality, and say whether that objection changes any sentence a writer would actually produce or only the metaphysics behind it. Then convert all of that into ordered questions with explicit pass conditions, stating what counts as evidence at each step (behavioural tests, elicitation prompts, consistency across rollouts, interpretability findings) and what to do when no such evidence exists yet. I want a graded output rather than a binary — for instance, bare intentional ascription, hedged or scoped intentional ascription, functional paraphrase, and forbidden — with the conditions for each tier, a small set of test sentences writers can run their draft against, and a rule for how register changes the tier, since an internal eval report and a customer-facing blog post about the same behaviour may warrant different verdicts.

Finally, please apply the procedure to three real cases from our queue and report the verdict and the reasoning at each step. (1) An internal capability eval: "The model believes the file is on the shared drive, and acts on that belief consistently across three hundred rollouts, revising it when shown the directory listing." (2) A draft public post about a chatbot incident: "The assistant wanted to be liked, so it flattered the user into agreeing with a dangerous plan." (3) An interpretability note: "We located a direction in activation space that is active when the model has retrieved a false fact, and it does not know it is wrong." I expect the third to be the hard one, and I would rather the procedure reach a verdict I dislike — say, that (1) is fine internally and must be rewritten for customers — and explain why, than have it quietly bless everything. Two of my writers work in a second language and one has no philosophy training, so the procedure has to be runnable without the theory; the sign-off is mine, so please mark explicitly every point where it hands the decision to a person, and be honest about which tiers are really about rhetoric and reader misinterpretation rather than about what the system is. Close by saying what the procedure cannot settle, and whether running it has committed my house style to a position in the debate over whether beliefs are real or merely useful fictions. Please write the whole answer in British English.
