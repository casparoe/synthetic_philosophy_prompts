Been going back and forth on s-risks for like a decade now, and I still cant get my own intuitions to line up no matter how many times I read the arguments on both sides. So I'm going to dump my case verdicts and see what principle, if any, actually fits them. Assume throughout I'm talking about futures with an astronomically large number of minds, so the usual "just think about your own neighborhood" framing doesn't get a grip.

1. Future A: N minds, each living a rich happy life containing no genuine suffering at all. Future B: the same N minds, exactly as happy, except each also goes through a period of real grief once every few decades — losing a close friend, say. B is worse than A, and I want to say worse by a lot, not by the small amount of grief involved.

2. Same setup, but now B just has slightly less happiness per mind and no extra suffering. Here A is better than B only very slightly. So a unit of suffering seems to count for way more than a unit of happiness in my head, and it isn't squeamishness about the extreme stuff — I feel it in case 1, which is pretty mild grief.

3. One person tortured for a year vs. a billion people who each get a mild headache for a day: the billion headaches are worse. Confident, never budged.

4. But 10^100 people who each get a papercut vs. one person tortured for fifty years: here I flip and say the fifty years is worse. I genuinely can't tell you what changed between 3 and 4 other than that the number got bigger and the per-person amount got smaller.

5. Mind X suffers horribly and mind Y is delighted, net positive, vs. a single mind Z who suffers the same amount and is delighted the same amount, net identical. Z's world is better. Compensation only counts if it happens to the same subject. I hold this even when the totals are enormous and even when X and Y never interact.

6. A machine runs the exact computation that would generate agony in a mind, but stipulate there's no experience in there — no lights on anywhere. Zero disvalue, I say without hesitation. Now change it so there IS experience, but nobody ever observes it, reports it, or remembers it, and the whole thing gets deleted right after: I judge that exactly as bad as a version broadcast to N witnesses. So it's the experience doing the work, not the being-known or the cosmic significance.

7. 10^20 torture-years that eventually end, followed by a long good recovery, vs. the same 10^20 torture-years that just keep going forever with no recovery. The second is worse, and worse by more than the difference in total suffering, because the first one has an ending. Which I know is incoherent on a straight sum view.

8. Causing suffering is worse than failing to prevent the same amount, even when prevention is basically free. But when I picture a superintelligence that could abolish factory farming and most wild-animal suffering at trivial cost to itself and just... doesn't bother, I want to call that a catastrophe of the same magnitude as if it had built the farms itself. So maybe I don't actually believe the first half of 8.

The principle I've been kicking around: net badness = (total suffering × some finite k) − (total happiness), with k large but not infinite, plus a clause that shuts off aggregation above some severity threshold. Problems I can already see — it's two unrelated mechanisms stapled together, 3 and 4 pull k in opposite directions, and I have no idea what "above some severity threshold" is supposed to mean operationally.

So: is there a principle, or a small family of them, that gets most of these? I want it stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it to a new case, and for each clause tell me which of my cases forced it in. Then run it on stuff I haven't listed. Does a future with N happy minds plus one mind in permanent unspeakable agony beat extinction? Does 5 survive when the two minds are very different in kind — a human and something not human at all — or does "same subject" quietly do a lot of work I'm not entitled to? And does 7 give me a real reason to weight irreversibility beyond the sum, or am I just double-counting the future suffering I forgot to include?

Also be straight with me about which verdicts have to go. I'd much rather lose 4 than lose 1, but tell me if that trade isn't on offer.

The one I keep flip-flopping on: is a future with nothing in it — no minds, no suffering, no happiness, heat death with nobody around — better or worse than a future like ours that keeps going a very long time with all the misery that implies? I notice I answer "better" when I imagine the empty future as a kind of relief and "worse" when I imagine it as a loss, and I can't tell which of those two imaginings is the honest one.
