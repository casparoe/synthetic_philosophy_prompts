You are a philosopher of mind who has also worked through the clinical neuropsychology of commissurotomy, and I would like you to treat what follows as a reconstruction problem rather than a survey. Please think carefully before answering. I am in the second year of a PhD and for the past eight months I have spent two afternoons a week volunteering in the presurgical evaluation unit at an epilepsy center, observing the assessment of candidates for corpus callosotomy and for resective surgery. I have read the standard philosophical literature on brain bisection and the unity of consciousness, so I am not asking for a digest of it, and you do not need to cite anything: I want the argument, not the bibliography. What I want is an audit of the practices I watch every week, which seem to me to embed substantive commitments that nobody on the team would state aloud — and to embed commitments that pull against one another.

Let me describe three of them concretely. First, the lateralized testing sessions. A word is flashed to one visual field, or an object is placed in one hand out of view behind a curtain; the patient looks at the examiner and says he felt nothing and saw nothing; his left hand is then allowed to reach behind the curtain, and it picks out the correct object from a grab bag. The technician records all of this on one line of one data sheet for one patient. The sheet has a single column headed "patient response," and entries are annotated "LH" or "RH," but there is no field anywhere for who is answering. Second, intermanual conflict. In the classic reports, and occasionally in front of me, a patient's left hand unbuttons a shirt that the right hand has just buttoned, or reaches for a cup the other hand has pushed away. This is treated entirely as a management problem — sleeves, mittens, patience, reassurance to the family — and never as anything with ethical standing. The attending's stock formulation when relatives are alarmed is that we do not have two patients, we have one patient with a very bad internal communication problem; and our center's consent paperwork acknowledges that the operation may produce temporary conflict between the hands while promising that it will not change who the patient is. Third, the Wada test. The patient is awake; a barbiturate is injected into one internal carotid artery so that one hemisphere is silenced for several minutes while the other keeps working. The arm on the side opposite the injection goes limp, and if the silenced hemisphere is the speech-dominant one the patient becomes mute. During those minutes the team continues to address him in the second person, continues to take responses from the hand controlled by the awake hemisphere, shows him objects and pictures to remember, and then, once the drug wears off, accepts whatever he says about the interval as his own report of it. Meanwhile the go/no-go criterion for the eventual resection is explicitly a sufficiency judgment: whether the hemisphere that will be left standing can, on its own, carry his language and his memory well enough.

The tension I cannot resolve is this. In the first protocol a hemisphere by itself is treated as less than the subject: its knowledge is partial, it cannot be spoken, and its testimony about its own experience is not to be trusted. In the third protocol either hemisphere by itself is treated as sufficient to carry the whole person, and the entire surgical decision is built on that sufficiency. And the same speech-dominant hemisphere is simultaneously discounted and enthroned: in the testing room its denial of awareness is overruled by what its own body's other hand goes on to demonstrate, while in the consent conversation and the post-anesthesia interview its verbal assent simply is the person's assent and its verbal account is the only account there is of what happened while it was mute.

Working through that material, please give me the following:

1. For each of the three practices separately, reconstruct the view of the unity of consciousness and of personal identity that the practice requires. Distinguish sharply between what the practice strictly presupposes — what would make it incoherent or self-defeating to run it otherwise — and what it merely sits comfortably with.
2. Say whether the three practices can be licensed by a single view, or whether the unit is genuinely of two minds. If you think there is a real inconsistency, locate it precisely, and consider whether something like a non-branching clause in a psychological-continuity account, or a view on which subjecthood comes in degrees, or a view on which there are two streams of experience but only one person, dissolves it.
3. Identify which rival positions the practices quietly rule out, and be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where two or more incompatible theories would license exactly the same protocol and the same data sheet.
4. Tell me what would have to change on the ground if the "two subjects" reading were adopted officially, and what would have to change if a strict unity view were adopted. I want operational consequences: consent procedures, who is interviewed and how, the ethics of restraining a hand, whether a hemisphere's silence during anesthesia counts as a subject being absent, whether the "LH/RH" annotations survive.
5. Finally, take a position on the testimony problem. If the speaking hemisphere routinely produces fluent accounts of behavior it did not initiate, what does the practice of recording "the patient reports" presuppose about the authority of verbal report, and can the same institution coherently treat that authority as defeasible in the testing room and conclusive in the consent conversation?

Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list the candidate theories that might license each practice and note which details of the protocols discriminate between them, before you commit to your reconstruction. End with your own verdict, stated plainly, on whether this unit's practice is philosophically coherent, where you are most confident, and where you think the evidence I have described simply does not decide the question.
