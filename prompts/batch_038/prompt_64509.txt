For a decade I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections on whole-brain emulation, substrate-independence, and the general question of whether cognition just *is* computation, and I have absorbed the standard objections to computationalism in a scattered, unsystematic way. Last month I tried to compress one of those objections — Putnam's triviality argument — into a single self-contained derivation for a local meetup group, and I succeeded too well: the argument now appears to prove a great deal more than I want it to, including something that undermines the argument itself. I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind and computation who has worked seriously on the metaphysics of implementation, and to treat me as a competent amateur rather than as someone who has made an elementary slip. Please begin by sketching the dependency structure of the derivation — which steps are doing independent work, which are load-bearing for more than one conclusion — and only then diagnose. I want a diagnosis, not a survey: no bibliography, no reading list, no name-dropping beyond what is needed, since I cannot verify any of that offline. Here is the argument as I currently have it.

(1) To say that a physical system S implements a computation C is to say that there is a mapping from S's physical states onto C's formal states under which S's state-transitions line up with C's transitions; nothing further is required, because if anything further were required, the further ingredient would have to be non-computational, and then the substrate would not have dropped out after all.
(2) Putnam's triviality result: for any ordinary open physical system S — one that keeps changing rather than cycling through a short list of states — and for any finite-state automaton C, such a mapping can be constructed, by taking S's successive physical states over a suitable interval and assigning formal states to them so that S's trajectory traces out C's state-sequence.
(3) So every ordinary open system — the granite paperweight on my desk, a beaker of water warming on a bench — implements every finite-state automaton, including, if computationalism is true, the automaton that is the computation underlying your mind and mine.
(4) Computationalism: what it is for a system to have a mind of a given kind is for it to implement the right computation, with no further ingredient, since the whole selling point of the view is that the realization medium is irrelevant.
(5) Therefore the paperweight on my desk has a mind of my kind.
(6) That is plainly false: there is no fact about the paperweight's counterfactual profile corresponding to any mental state — you cannot ask it what it is thinking, and nothing it does tracks anything it believes.
(7) Therefore computationalism is false: implementing a computation cannot be what mindedness consists in, and substrate-independence is a mirage.
(8) But (2) proves more than that. If the existence of a suitable mapping is all there is to implementation, then for any physical system there is no fact of the matter about which single computation it runs, since it runs all of them; my own reasoning about this very argument is then no more a truth-tracking computation than an arbitrary one, and the belief I express in these premises has no determinate content. So the argument, if sound, removes my standing to assert its conclusion.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (4) and (6) than (5) and (7), because abandoning (4) means abandoning the upload project I have spent years thinking about, and I have no sympathy for crediting rocks with experience; so I expect the damage to fall somewhere in (1) through (3). But tell me whether that triage is right rather than assuming it. Specifically: is the derivation invalid, and if so at which step and by what kind of slip — an equivocation on "implements," a scope error in the generalization from (2) to (3), a strawman in (1), or something else — or is it valid, in which case which plausible premise is cheapest to surrender? Explain why the faulty step looked perfectly respectable to me when I wrote it, and then price each alternative rejection honestly: what has to be added or given up if I reject (1), if I reject (2), if I deny (3), if I bite the bullet on (5), or if I try to block (8) while conceding everything above it. One worry I want addressed explicitly, because I suspect it is where my own account is soft: any strengthening of the implementation relation that is firm enough to exclude the paperweight has to remain permissive enough to let a silicon emulation of my brain count as a mind, and I do not know how to state the line without either re-admitting triviality or smuggling in exactly the substrate-dependence the computationalist was trying to avoid. Think carefully before answering, and do not reassure me that both horns are comfortable.
