I'm putting together the final for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar — about twenty juniors and seniors, all of whom have read Lakatos's 1970 paper on falsification and the methodology of scientific research programmes, plus a good chunk of the expanded 1978 book version. I need one essay question on research programmes that can't be answered by reciting the vocabulary. The problem I keep running into is that if I ask anything like "distinguish progressive from degenerating problemshifts," I get the definitions handed straight back to me with a ritual nod at Newton on one side and Marxism on the other, and I still can't tell who actually understands the machinery from who memorized the labels.

So I built a synthetic case for them instead. Invented field, invented timeline, no real science involved — nobody can look it up, and nobody needs any biology or physics background to work through it. Here's the case handout:

> **The thermin programme.** From the 1890s, a programme in thermal physiology holds (hard core, H) that warm-blooded animals regulate body temperature by controlling the concentration of a single circulating substance, thermin, whose target level is set by one central gland; every temperature deviation is a mismatch between thermin level and the gland's setting, and nothing outside the gland sets that setting. The programme comes with an assay technique for measuring blood thermin and a well-corroborated set of initial predictions (cold-adapted animals run high thermin, gland-removed animals fail to regulate, etc.).
>
> **Anomaly A1, year 12.** A diving bird holds normal temperature at thermin concentrations far below anything H predicts.
>
> **Auxiliary h1, year 14.** Diving birds lose less heat through a countercurrent exchanger in the legs, so they need less thermin. h1 was built using only the blood-assay data from A1. It also implies a specific quantitative temperature gradient along the leg, steeper than in the bird's non-diving relatives. Nobody measures this until year 19, when a different lab, working on an unrelated question about limb blood flow, happens to record exactly that gradient, to within the predicted range.
>
> **Anomaly A2, year 25.** In healthy animals with no change in gland activity and no change in ambient temperature, measured thermin oscillates on a roughly daily cycle.
>
> **Auxiliary h2, year 26.** Thermin circulates bound to a carrier protein that releases it rhythmically; gland output is constant, so H is untouched. The carrier's release rate was fitted to the very oscillation records that produced A2. h2's one further consequence — that destroying the carrier abolishes the rhythm — was never tested, because the carrier could not be isolated.
>
> **A rival, from year 20.** The distributed programme (R) holds that regulation is the joint product of many local effectors with no central setting substance. R handles A1 and A2 with no extra machinery, and in year 31 predicts, and finds, that gland-removed animals still regulate within a narrow band when ambient temperature changes slowly — something H's proponents had repeatedly said was impossible.
>
> **Years 28–30.** The thermin camp finally isolates the carrier protein and discovers it also explains a standing puzzle in their own assay: readings come out systematically low at night. That consequence was not part of h2's original rationale, and an independent assay method confirms it in year 30.
>
> **Year 33.** R's people announce a crucial experiment and demand that H be abandoned.

What I want from you:

1. The exam question itself, built on that case, in three or four parts with point values adding to 25, written for a 75-minute slot at the end of a two-hour final. Frame it so a student can't get through it just by defining hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic.

2. A model answer of roughly 750 words, written the way a very strong student would write it under time pressure, not as a lecture.

3. A grading rubric that actually seperates an A from a B by naming the moves each has to make. I want the specific cruxes flagged: I think the question really turns on what counts as a novel prediction (why the year-19 gradient result does work for h1 and why h2's fitted release rate does no work for h2), on whether saving the core with an auxiliary is itself a defect at all, on the fact that degeneration is a property of a sequence judged retrospectively so the year-26 verdict has to be limited without being empty, on what the years 28–30 discovery does to the classification you'd have given h2, on why a "crucial experiment" is the wrong way to frame year 33, and on why R's existence matters to any verdict at all. Tell me where you'd put the points and, for each, the common wrong answer that should lose them — for instance treating every auxiliary as automatically ad hoc, treating the immunization of the core as showing the programme is unscientific, sliding into a Kuhnian crisis-and-revolution story, or concluding that H had to be dropped in year 26. Say explicitly what a B answer looks like: which of these it gets right and where it stalls.

Two housekeeping things: no bibliography, no reading list, no suggestions for further sources — I know the literature. And don't put anything in quotation marks unless you're certain of the exact wording; paraphrase Lakatos rather than quoting him.
