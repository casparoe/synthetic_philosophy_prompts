I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized company, and for the past year I've run an optional Friday session for our engineering organization: 75 minutes, 14 to 20 people, mixed seniority, one whiteboard, a shared document, and a room that reliably contains two very senior engineers and at least one person who openly dislikes games. None of us has any philosophy training, including me — I read around the edges of things, badly, and I'm used to specifying deliverables rather than arguments. I want to run a session on a distinction I keep colliding with in code review, and I'd like you to design the activity.

The observation that started this. There are two very different kinds of pressure that can be applied to a judgment of the form "this design is good." One is a consideration that bears on whether the design is in fact good. The other bears only on whether it would be good for me to be someone who judges it good: a VP who needs the launch to look sound, a teammate whose morale is wrapped up in their patch, a deadline that makes shipped-cheaper better than shipped-right. Our review culture is supposed to be governed by the first kind only, and yet the second kind shows up constantly wearing the vocabulary of the first. Nobody writes "approve this because I need the win." They write "this is more maintainable than it looks" or "it scales fine at our volume," and the incentive has been smuggled inside a claim about the artifact. I've since learned philosophers have a name for something like this involving wrong-kind reasons, but I couldn't tell you more than that.

Here is the part I find genuinely strange, and the part I want people to feel rather than be told. When the pressure is of the second kind, I usually cannot act on it directly. I cannot decide to find a design elegant because I will be rewarded for finding it elegant; the reward does not reach the judgment. But I can reach the judgment indirectly — by choosing to look hard at the parts of the design that support the verdict, by loosening what "maintainable" is going to mean today, by concluding that the objection was pedantic all along. From the inside, that indirect route feels exactly like changing your mind on the evidence. I have a further hypothesis I want the session to put under pressure rather than assume: for people who leak their judgments — in tone, in how hard they push back, in what they quietly decline to sign off on — the line between saying it's good and believing it's good is partly illusory, because the only reliable way to say it convincingly is to come to believe it. If that's right, second-kind pressure gets some of the grip on belief that evidence has, and I don't know whether that's an insight or a confusion on my part.

What I need from you:

1. A complete, runnable activity: roles, rules, materials I can produce in an afternoon, and a timeline broken down to five minutes. Payoffs must be explainable in under three minutes. Real money is available — I'll put $50 in a jar, or make the losing side buy coffee for a week — and I'd rather exploit the fact that engineers game a two-dollar payoff than pretend otherwise.
2. The distinction must emerge from what happens, not from narration. Participants should run into it before anyone names it; the name arrives in the debrief. Please get the terminology right there, and give me one or two canonical illustrations you'd deploy at that point. No bibliography and no reading list — I'll chase down sources myself.
3. Two structural features I want if they can be had without bloating the game: a round in which the pressure to judge favorably is explicit and visible to everyone, so participants hit the wall of not being able to comply directly; and a round in which the identical pressure arrives disguised as a claim about the artifact. By the end they should be unable to tell, from the record of their own reasoning, which round they were in.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto specific parts of the distinction, and that closes with something usable on Monday: a test an engineer can apply to a comment on their own pull request.
5. An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. At minimum, address: someone bright diagnosing the mechanism in round one and announcing it; the senior engineers dominating; the person who won't play along; and a mitigation for each.
6. How this session could backfire into the cynical lesson — all reasons are just incentives, review is politics — and what to build into the rules to prevent that. Sending eighteen engineers back to their teams convinced of it would be a worse outcome than not running the session.
7. A frank statement of which feature of the idea a game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself, plus your assessment of whether my leakage hypothesis above is something an activity can genuinely test or only illustrate.

If some of this cannot fit in 75 minutes, cut something and tell me what you cut and why.
