I cover research methodology for a science desk, and two norms in clinical evidence keep striking me as sitting uneasily beside each other. Guideline bodies rank evidence in a pyramid: randomized trials near the top, observational studies of the very same drug below them, and reasoning about how the drug works mechanically at the bottom, barely above expert opinion. Then, inside the trial, the analysis that counts groups patients by the arm they were assigned to rather than by whether they actually took the drug — even when a third of the treatment arm never filled the prescription and everyone concedes the resulting estimate is diluted toward zero. The analysis restricted to those who followed the protocol is set aside as biased, because adherence is settled after randomization and carries prognosis along with it.

What conception of causation does that combination presuppose? It seems to license the thought that assignment, not ingestion, is the cause — as though a cause is whatever a policy could be built around — and yet the quantity being estimated is defined in frankly counterfactual terms, as the difference between the outcome observed and the outcome those patients would otherwise have had. Does ranking trials above observational work commit anyone to a view on which causes are only revealed by interventions, or is it just a practical worry about confounding that a counterfactual account motivates equally well? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely suits it, indicate which parts of it several incompatible theories would license anyway, and say what guideline-writing would look like if mechanistic inference were given the standing that the same tradition's insistence on results carrying over to new populations seems to demand.
