Background: I'm not a philosopher, I coordinate a church food pantry in a town where the stamping plant shut down in the spring, and as of about six weeks ago I'm also the "community rep" on a county working group rewriting the rules for the winter emergency shelter. So this went from abstract to my actual calender very fast.

Here's a thing I keep running into and I don't have a word for it. Act like you're a moral philosopher who has also sat on a hospital ethics committee or a disaster triage board — somebody who has watched this happen in practice, not just in papers. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming broadly: list maybe six to eight candidate concepts or terms from anywhere (ethics, political theory, economics, org sociology, disaster studies, whatever you like) before you narrow down. I can't look anything up, so no reading lists or citations, just explain each candidate in your own words.

The pattern. A group controls a resource. They set a number — how many families get boxes, how many cots get opened. Then they describe that number as though it were a law of nature instead of something they picked: "the kitchen can only do 120," "we physically cannot go past 40 cots." Once it's phrased that way, two things follow. First, nobody blames the people who set the number; they get to feel bad right along with everybody else, and the anger of the families turned away goes at the situation rather than at the board. Second, anybody who says "well then raise the number" gets treated as emotional or unserious, like they're arguing with gravity.

But here's what makes me think this isn't just the ordinary fact of limited resources: the number isn't actually fixed. When somebody sympathetic shows up — a family with a kid in a wheelchair, a case the pastor takes personally — we quietly go to 125, or a 41st cot appears in the fellowship hall, and nobody revisits the official cap or admits it moved. The limit is rigid in public and stretchy in private, and it seems to stretch precisely when the stretching can be kept invisible. The same building that "cannot" hold more than 40 for shelter held 150 for the Christmas concert.

And the strangest part is that the people getting turned away mostly accept the framing. They apologize to my volunteers. They say "I know you guys can't help it."

I've heard "lifeboat ethics," "carrying capacity," and "tragedy of the commons" used somewhere around this territory, but none of them seem to name this specific move, which is less about there being limits than about who gets to turn their own decision into a fact about the world.

So: does it have an established name? Which nearby concepts come closest, and what does each one miss? If nothing really fits, coin a term and give me a defintion plus the edges — when is a limit genuinely external, so this charge doesn't apply, and where does this shade into plain hypocrisy or everyday rationalizing instead? Be specific about whether the public/private asymmetry is a separate feature in its own right or just evidence of bad faith, and whether it changes anything morally that the excluded buy into it.
