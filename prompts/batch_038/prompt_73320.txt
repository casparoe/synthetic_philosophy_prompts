The assignment question was:

"In his short essay arguing about suicide, Hume takes up three duties that suicide is supposed to violate — a duty to God, a duty to our neighbours, and a duty to ourselves. Explain how he handles the first of these, and say whether his argument depends on his own religious views."

One student's answer:

"The usual objection Hume is answering is that killing yourself usurps God's authority, since God governs everything and so deciding to die is taking a decision that isn't yours to take. Hume's reply is that the objection proves far too much. If interfering with what providence has arranged is a crime, then the farmer who drains a marsh, the doctor who cures a fever and the engineer who turns a river are all criminals too, because they're all changing a course of events that God set in motion. God works through general laws, and nothing that happens falls outside them, so there's nothing specially defiant about a human being ending his own life. Hume adds that we're already trusted with enormous power over life and death anyway — we choose whether to bring children into the world, we rescue people from accidents, we keep the sick alive with medicine — so it would be strange to call this one exercise of that power a sin. On that basis I think we can see that Hume's argument only holds if you share his scepticism about religion: he's quietly assuming God isn't the sort of being who issues particular decrees about individual lives, so a genuinely devout reader can just refuse that premise and the whole thing collapses. Once the religious objection is out of the way, Hume goes on to argue positively that suicide is the right course of action whenever a life contains more misery than happiness, since on his view we should always take whichever option gives the better balance of good over evil, and there's no reason to keep an existence going once that balance has turned against it."
