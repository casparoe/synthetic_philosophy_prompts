I teach philosophy of cognitive science, and for the past several years I have been working my way through the free energy principle and the predictive processing literature that grew up around it. I have now been asked to contribute a short position statement to an interdisciplinary workshop organised jointly by my department and a computational neuroscience group; the organisers want each participant to set out their substantive commitments in advance, in a form brief enough that a modeller in the room can object to it precisely, so that the sessions are adversarial rather than expository. I have drafted mine and, before I circulate it, I want a proper audit of it. Not a survey of the debate — I know the debate, and my colleagues on both sides of it — but a determination of whether this particular package hangs together as a single position.

Here is the package. (1) I take the free energy principle to be a substantive empirical hypothesis rather than a redescription of what we already knew: for any candidate system, the claim that it persists by minimising variational free energy relative to some generative model is the kind of claim that could be shown false, and the breadth of its intended scope is a point in its favour, not a symptom of vacuity. (2) At the same time I concede that the inequality bounding surprisal by free energy is just mathematics once a model is given, and that a free-energy gloss on a system already described in other terms adds nothing by itself; whatever is testable is testable only at the level of a specified model — these states, these priors, these precision weightings. (3) About Markov blankets I am an instrumentalist: the partition into internal, external, active and sensory states is not read off nature but adopted by the modeller in light of the description under which we want the system's persistence to be intelligible, and there is no blanket-independent fact about which variables belong inside. (4) I am nonetheless confident about the extension: the principle applies to a bacterium, to a plant, and with due care to an insect colony, but not to a candle flame or a hurricane, whose persistence is not self-evidencing in the relevant sense. (5) The states doing the minimising are representations with genuinely probabilistic content; the brain performs approximate Bayesian inference over hidden causes, and I reject both the deflationary reading on which the so-called model is nothing but a causal structure with no semantic properties and the suggestion that the framework collapses into an enactivism without inner states. (6) Models are nevertheless selected for what they do for regulation rather than for correspondence: a generative model that misrepresents the hidden causes systematically, in a way indexed to the organism's own ends, may be optimal for control, and I do not find that possibility troubling. (7) Epistemically, though, I want the stronger claim: predictive processing rehabilitates perception, because a system that reliably anticipates its own sensory flux thereby tracks environmental structure, so the framework answers a sceptical challenge instead of deepening it. (8) About motivation I am a reductionist: there are no intrinsic values and no irreducible desires, what an organism wants is exhausted by the preferences built into its model, and the decomposition of expected free energy into an epistemic and a pragmatic term is all the normativity the framework requires — which is why I insist the principle is normatively inert, telling no one what to value and smuggling in no conception of the good. (9) The dark room problem is a non-problem: an organism confined to an unchanging dark room would indeed score well by the formalism, but no actual organism's model encodes such a preference, and the fact that animals seek out informationally rich environments is something the framework predicts rather than struggles to accommodate.

What I want back is an audit. Go through the list and tell me which pairs of commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentified inventory. For each genuine tension, identify the exact locus of the incompatibility — I want to know what in one commitment rules out what in the other, not merely a label for the disagreement — then state the minimal revision that would remove it and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list, since I suspect most of the fixes are not free. For the pairs that only appear inconsistent, say which distinction does the dissolving, and be careful there: I would rather you explain why a tension is merely apparent than list it for completeness. Finish by naming the single revision that would buy the most consistency across the whole set, and by telling me plainly whether what is left would constitute a coherent position or an unstable coalition of views borrowed from camps that cannot be occupied simultaneously. Two provisos. First, I have deliberately not indicated where my own attachments lie, and the numbering is not a ranking of importance; please let the severity ordering follow the structure of the commitments rather than any guess about which I would defend to the last. Second, no reading recommendations and no literature review — I will handle the citations myself, and the entire value of your answer lies in how precisely it traces what commits me to what.
