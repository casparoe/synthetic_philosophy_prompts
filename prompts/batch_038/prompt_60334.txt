Got into a two-hour thing with a friend last night about how norms like dueling and slavery actually die, and we somehow ended up fighting about Huckleberry Finn instead, which neither of us planned. Background: she's a big fan of Appiah's honor-code idea, the claim that British dueling and Chinese footbinding and the Atlantic slave trade collapsed once they became dishonorable rather than merely immoral, and that the arguments were mostly downstream of that. I said fine, but Huck Finn is a test case she's reading wrong. She said no, you're the one reading it wrong. Now I want an actual reading to settle it, or to tell me we're both wrong.

My side: what turns Huck isn't a syllogism about personhood. He never revises the belief that helping Jim escape is theft and a damnable sin — he tears up the letter to Miss Watson while fully expecting hell for it. What he can't stand is being the kind of person who'd sneak on a friend, and the earlier moment that sets that up is after the fog trick, where he goes back and humbles himself to Jim. To me that's a recognition/status move, not a belief change, so the norm doesn't get argued out of him, it gets out-honored. Her side: that's just sympathy or affection, nothing to do with honor, and anyway it changes nothing structural — Jim winds up free because of a will, a piece of paper, and the whole last stretch is Tom turning Jim's captivity into a game with rats and inscriptions. She also points at Pap's rant about the free Black professor up in Ohio who dressed well and could vote, and says that's Twain showing the norm propped up by resentment about standing rather than by property — which she thinks cuts against me, because if it's status anxiety for Pap it's status anxiety for everyone.

So: read the novel through this debate, seriously. Which channel is the book actually staging, and is there a real difference between giving up a norm because you've come to think it unjust and giving up participating in it because you can no longer stomach it while still thinking it unjust — and can that second kind ever scale into a revolution, given that revolutions seem to require people to justify the new norm in public? Does the ending, with the paperwork and Tom's game, refute the honor reading or complete it? Is Pap a counterexample, or the same mechanism seen from the defender's side? And be honest about where the novel resists all of this, including what my framing probably distorts. Don't quote the book at me and I don't want a bibliography — I just want the arguement.
