I'm writing a novel about a family where one sibling makes it big and the others don't, and I've realised I keep making quiet judgment calls about which of my characters the reader should admire and which should get cut down to size. I know there's names for this stuff — the American Dream on one side, Jantelagen and tall poppies on the other — but I don't want the labels, I want the principle underneath my own verdicts. Here they are, mostly arrived at by feel:

- The kid who grows up in a rusted-out town, works two jobs, gets out, becomes a surgeon: admire, fully, no asterisks.
- That same surgeon twenty years on, who can't stop mentioning where he came from at every dinner party: something curdles. I don't want the reader admiring him anymore, but I dont want them despising him either.
- A woman who inherited the business and is genuinely better at running it than her father was: I want her admired, but the admiration comes out thin, like it doesn't land in the chest.
- The guy who worked exactly as hard as the surgeon and got cancer instead: I want the reader to feel something closer to anger on his behalf than pity.
- A neighbour who resents the surgeon and says so loudly at the hardware store: contemptible.
- A neighbour who resents the surgeon, says nothing, and quietly stops inviting him over: I want the reader to understand him almost from the inside.
- The surgeon's brother, who sabotages him a little out of that resentment: awful, and the most alive person in the book.

Is there a principal that gets all seven, or most of them? My guess is something about who did what with what they were handed — but that has to be wrong, because it demotes the woman who inherited the business, and I don't actually want to demote her. So state the principle as precisely as you can, tell me which clause each verdict is doing work for, and then tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed: a teenager from a rich family who's extremely good at something and has visibly worked at it. And if it can't hold all seven at once, say which verdict I should throw out and why — I'd rather lose one than build a principle with seven epicycles on it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back at me. Also be straight with me about how confident you are in the principle you land on, and single out the part of it you're least sure of.
