I'm auditing an epistemology seminar this term, and the week on skepticism moved considerably faster than I could follow. To fill in the gaps afterwards I took out a trade book on the topic by a professional philosopher writing for a general audience; I've since returned it and no longer have the page, but I copied out the central passage because it seemed to be claiming substantially more than the seminar leader had claimed in class, and I can't tell whether that's because the book is sharper or because it is looser. The passage runs as follows:

'Nozick's answer to the skeptic is that knowledge is belief that tracks the truth. He analyses knowing that p in four parts: p is true; you believe that p; if p weren't true you wouldn't believe that p; and if p were true you would believe that p. The third clause is the famous one, sensitivity, and the picture behind it is that a knower's beliefs stay in step with how things are: they are responsive to the evidence, so that when the evidence changes the belief changes with it. That is what tracking amounts to — the belief follows the truth the way a stylus follows a groove, correcting itself whenever things are otherwise, which is why the theory is advertised as one on which you could not easily have been wrong about what you know. The fourth clause, that you would believe p if p were true, is what does the real work against lucky guesses, and it is the clause later epistemologists have mostly kept when they streamlined the analysis into the slogan that you know something only if you couldn't easily have got it wrong. Now apply this to skepticism. If I were a handless brain in a vat, I would still believe I had hands, because the vat is arranged to deliver exactly the experiences I am having; so my belief that I am not a brain in a vat fails the test, and I do not know it. But my belief that I have hands passes the test, since if I had no hands — if they had been crushed in an accident, say — I would not believe I had them. And here is the bombshell. Everyone assumes that knowledge is closed under known implication: if you know p, and know that p entails q, and believe q on that basis, then you know q. Nozick denies this, and the vat case is his counterexample: I know I have hands, I know that having hands entails not being a handless brain in a vat, and yet I do not know that I am not one. So skepticism gets everything it asked for and nothing that matters: we do not know we are not brains in vats, but that fact does nothing to disturb our knowledge of hands, tables, and other people. Once you see that closure was never part of what knowledge is, the skeptic's demand that we rule out the vat turns out to rest on a confusion about the concept, and any theory that preserves closure in order to protect our intuitions is simply refuted by the skepticism it delivers.'

Parts of this match what I remember from the seminar, and I'm inclined to think the brain-in-a-vat reasoning is right, but a member of my reading group — who is considerably further along than I am — said flatly that the passage has the two counterfactuals doing each other's jobs, and I could not follow her objection well enough to evaluate it. She also mentioned a case involving a grandmother and a grandson that is supposed to be a problem for the analysis, and I genuinely cannot see how it differs in any relevant respect from the vat case, where the analysis seems to me to come out right. A third person said the view commits you to a pair of claims that sound straightforwardly contradictory when you say them aloud, though she could not remember what the charge is called.

Please work through the passage claim by claim and tell me what to keep and what to discard: which sentences a defender of the view would accept as they stand, which are defensible simplifications for a general reader, and which are misrepresentations — and for the misrepresentations, what the view actually says and what changes downstream once the correction is made. I'd especially like to know whether the gloss on the third clause (responsiveness to evidence, the stylus-in-a-groove picture, the "couldn't easily have been wrong" slogan) captures the condition the account needs, and whether the vat result would still follow if that gloss were correct. Which of the two counterfactuals has the subsequent discussion emphasized, and what stops working — or starts working — if you drop the other one? Does denying closure commit the view to closure failing across the board, or only in cases of a particular shape, and does that distinction matter for whether this is an answer to skepticism or a capitulation to it?
