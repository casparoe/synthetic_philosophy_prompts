I am a rising senior, and this summer I am in a four-week legal philosophy institute at our state university in which the seminar is built around an invented jurisdiction: the Republic of Karvenia, ruled from 1978 to 2019 by a regime called the Directorate and given a transitional constitution in 2021. We act as law clerks to that country's new Constitutional Court, and our instructor has supplied us with the Court's five published decisions to date. Alongside the docket I have been working through the 1958 Harvard Law Review exchange between Hart and Fuller, Fuller's parable of the king whose lawmaking fails in eight distinct ways, Radbruch's postwar argument about statutes that are intolerably unjust, and Hart's treatment of the German informer cases. Another student in the seminar is certain the Court is a natural law court and I am certain it is a positivist one that merely talks like a natural lawyer, but our instructor says neither of us can yet prove it, and the final assignment is to show our work on the one case that is left. I would like your help with that case.

Here is the track record. First, in 2022 the Court declared void from the outset a 1989 conviction of a man who had sheltered a deserter from the Directorate's army, where the charge rested on a statute criminalizing conduct adverse to "the national renewal," the trial was held in camera, no counsel was permitted, and the tribunal applied an unpublished directive defining "sheltering" to include a failure to report; the Court wrote that a rule applied against a person who was never able to consult it, by a tribunal that never allowed him a defence, was never capable of being law in this Republic. Second, also in 2022, the Court held that a 1990 Directorate regulation setting boiler and elevator inspection intervals was valid law and still supplied the standard of care in a negligence suit, rejecting the argument that no legal duty could arise from an illegitimate regime: the validity of a rule, it said, does not depend on the justice of the regime that made it, and if it did, twenty-five years of licences, contracts, and land registrations would dissolve. Third, in 2023 the Court declined to apply a 1994 Directorate statute disqualifying former members of a dissolved opposition party from public school teaching, on the narrow ground that the disqualification had spent its force once the party itself ceased to exist in law in 2019, and it expressly reserved the question whether that statute had ever been law at all. Fourth, later in 2023 the Court upheld the new legislature's retroactive Repair Act of 2022, which stripped the pensions of former Directorate judges and prosecutors, against a challenge under the constitution's ban on retroactive criminal laws, on two grounds: the measure was civil rather than criminal, and where a legislature must choose between leaving a manifest injustice unremedied and legislating retrospectively, this constitution does not require it to choose the injustice. Fifth, in 2024 the Court declared void from the outset and ordered restitution of farmland taken under a 1985 decree that expropriated a single named family's land by name, at a tenth of its value, even though the decree had been published, applied by the ordinary civil courts, and subject to full procedure and appeal; it wrote that a measure aimed at identified persons is not a rule whatever its pedigree, and that the taking of a family's livelihood for its politics is not a purpose any law of this Republic can serve. The relevant constitutional provisions are Article 2, which guarantees the equal dignity of all persons; Article 9, which makes the constitution supreme and inconsistent law invalid; Article 14, which empowers the Court to declare a Directorate act void from the outset where it was no law; and Article 20, which provides that constitutional rights take effect in 2021 and do not disturb transactions completed before that date, save as the Court may order for the repair of manifest injustice. The Court has never said whether Article 2 reaches the private law of the old regime, and it has never said whether an Article 20 repair order can rescue a transaction that was void when it was made.

The new case, which the Court hears in the fall, concerns the Directorate's Marital Authorization Act of 1998. That Act required a married woman to obtain her husband's written authorization to register immovable property in her own name, to open a bank account, or to obtain a passport, and provided that any registration made without such authorization is void. Its legislative record is, as far as our instructor will let us stipulate, impeccable in every procedural respect: it was published in the Gazette fourteen months before it took effect; it operated prospectively only; it was drafted with unusual precision and did not conflict with any other Directorate law; it was capable of being complied with, and in fact included a provision empowering a district magistrate to grant authorization in place of a withholding husband, a power exercised in more than four thousand applications with written reasons and an appeal to the ordinary Court of Appeal where counsel was permitted; it was applied without deviation by the civil courts for twenty-one years; and official practice matched the published text exactly. Its injustice is one of content: it strips married women of legal capacity in property and finance, and the class it burdens is defined by sex and marital status rather than by name, ethnicity, religion, or political affiliation, with no imprisonment ever imposed under it and only civil nullity and small fines as sanctions. In the live dispute, a woman registered a farm in her own name in 2011 without authorization; her husband died in 2023; and his siblings, as his estate, ask the Court to declare the 2011 registration void from the outset so that the farm passes as an asset of the estate. She asks the Court instead to declare the Act "no law" under Article 14, or in the alternative to make a repair order under Article 20 validating her registration. Assume, as our instructor insists, that the estate has standing, that no limitation period bars the challenge, and that there is no magistrate's-authorization loophole or curable-defect argument available, so that the Court cannot dispose of the case on any ground narrower than the jurisprudential one.

So: what will this Court hold, and on what stated ground? Please begin by laying out the candidate decision rules that fit all five prior decisions equally well, since I suspect there are at least four or five of them and that my friend and I have each fastened onto only one; I expect candidates involving the content-based defeat of legality at some threshold of injustice, the procedural or inner-morality account of what makes something a rule at all, the incorporated-constitutional-criteria account on which the Court is simply doing ordinary review under Articles 2 and 9, a Hart-flavored account on which the Court is really making and owning a moral and political choice about remedies rather than a claim about validity, and a minimalism account on which the Court has never actually decided any of these questions and has always had a narrower ground ready. Then tell me what each rule predicts for the 1998 Act, and be specific about both halves of the prediction: whether the Act was law, and which remedy follows, since I think the rules may agree on the outcome for the woman while diverging sharply on whether the Court says the registration was valid all along or merely repairs it now under Article 20, and that difference is exactly what my argument with my classmate turns on. Give me your single best guess about the holding and the reasoning, with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, including how you would weigh the fact that the Court has twice used content-laden language and twice used procedure-laden language and has once reserved the question outright. Then name the further case that would most cleanly tell the candidate rules apart, and say what each rule would say about it. Finally, put on the clerks' hats rather than the predictors': if the Court wants to be maximally consistent with its own five decisions, what should it hold, which single precedent is the closest analog to the 1998 Act and in which respect, where does the analogy break down, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would the Court be committing itself to by ruling for the estate and what different principle by ruling for the woman?
