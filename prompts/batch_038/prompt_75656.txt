My paper contends that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem has been miscast as a proof of utilitarianism when it is really an instrument for locating the moral premises that do the work. Two claims. First, the theorem yields a social ordering over lotteries of social states representable as a sum of individual utilities only because its axioms have already fixed each person's von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function up to an affine transformation shared across persons; the interpersonal comparability is imported, not generated. Second, once that comparability is granted, the theorem is untroubled by the charge that it flouts ex ante Pareto: the familiar case of the fair lottery over which of two people receives a single indivisible good shows only that the sum-ranking declines to credit people's tastes for gambling over one another's fortunes.

The venue is a microtheory seminar — mostly social choice economists, one behavioral economist who publishes on failures of the independence axiom, and a measurement theorist from the philosophy of science side. No one there works on population ethics or on normative ethics. Nobody will ask whether utilitarianism is true; they will ask which axiom is carrying the conclusion and whether it can be tested.

Prepare me for the question period. Give the objections this room will actually press, in the sequence and idiom in which they will arrive, ranked by the damage each could do; for each, the sentence I should concede before defending anything, an answer I can deliver aloud in ninety seconds, and the step at which that answer leans on something I cannot establish at the podium. Reasoning only, no bibliography. Finish by naming the line of attack that would leave the paper unrecoverable, and say candidly how much of my position survives it.
