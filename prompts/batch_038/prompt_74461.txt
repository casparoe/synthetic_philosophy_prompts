so I'm presenting my views at a decision theory reading group next week and I want a sanity check on whether the package is even coherent. Setup, in case it matters: the absent-minded driver (Piccione–Rubinstein). Two intersections X and Y he can't tell apart and can't remember passing; exit at X pays 0, exit at Y pays 4, driving past both pays 1. Here's where I've landed:

1. I'm an evidential decision theorist — you act on what your act is evidence of.
2. I'm an evidentialist about credences: your degrees of belief are exactly what your total evidence supports, and nothing else gets to move them.
3. At an intersection his only evidence is "I'm at an intersection," and that's symmetric between X and Y, so alpha = 1/2.
4. The ex ante best plan is clearly continue-at-X / exit-at-Y, and a rational driver adopts it before setting off.
5. I reject resolute choice as gerrymandering — you can't rationally follow a plan just because you made it, if from where you now sit it looks worse.
6. I also insist a good decision theory be dynamically consistent: whatever's optimal from the start should still look optimal from inside the situation.
7. Randomizing is a legitimate object of choice, and I think the right answer here is something like exit with probability 1/3 at each intersection.
8. None of this needs exotic machinery — its just ordinary uncertainty about which intersection he's at, not some special self-locating thing.

Audit it. Which of these actually pull against each other and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than dumping them all on me as equally bad, and for each say what has to go and what it costs. Then tell me if one revision buys most of the coherence. I'm deliberately not saying which one I'd rather drop — I honestly don't know yet.
