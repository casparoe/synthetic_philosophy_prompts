Our philosophy society is closing the term with a little staged reading — two people at a table with scripts, ten minutes, and an audience of mostly undergrads who have actually read Rousseau's 1758 letter to d'Alembert on the theater (the one answering d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on Geneva, where he floats the idea of a comedy theater for the city). I've been asked to write the script and Im stuck, because every version I draft collapses into either a censorship debate or a history lecture. So: write me the dialogue. Two speakers, labels up to you, but make them a Citizen who is arguing the letter's case and a Playwright who wants the theater built and is no less a republican for it.

The Citizen has to actually deploy the machinery rather than wave at it: that theater doesn't shape a city's morals so much as track and flatter them, which is why it can't serve as an instrument of reform; that the purging story gets things backwards, since the passions a play works up are borrowed and spend themselves in the dark while the ones the spectator brought with him come out fed; that pity bought with a ticket costs nothing and so leaves him free to be exactly as hard on the walk home as he was on the way in; that comedy's engine is ridicule, which means it has to make the intransigent upright man funny and the easygoing worldly one look like the sane option — Molière's misanthrope is the exhibit; that the actor's trade is the selling of one's own person and the simulation of feeling for pay, with a separate and sharper complaint about women on stage; and that a small republic simply can't carry the idleness and expense a standing theater needs. Let him be pointed and a bit funny about it, not hectoring.

The Playwright is where I need the most help, because I can't write a good one. Constraints: no appeals to free expression or individual autonomy — argue on the Citizen's own terrain, i.e. what spectacle does to the character of a citizenry. Grant him the strong premises and then go somewhere else with them: if theater only mirrors manners, it's the one place where a city's manners get fixed in a repeatable, public, arguable form, a shared repertory of types that citizens can recognize and dispute; the pity charge can be met by distinguishing a passion rehearsed from a passion spent; and the misanthrope point misses, because Alceste is ridiculous for being indiscriminate — virtue with no judgment — not for being virtuous. Then have him turn it on the open-air festivals the Citizen offers instead, where the people are supposed to be their own spectacle: who organizes it, who's effectively directing, is a crowd watching a crowd any less a spectacle, is unanimity an achievement or a threat, and can a festival go wrong in just the way a play can. I'd like one exchange built around a specific invented tragedy — something like a magistrate who must choose between exposing a wildly popular general and starting a civil war — where they disagree about what an audience actually takes home from it, plus one moment where they agree on a factual premise and split on what follows from it. Equal competence throughout, and nobody gets to restate a point the other side has allready answered. Around 1,200 to 1,600 words, and end not with a resolution but with each of them saying in one line what they take the narrowest remaining disagreement to be.
