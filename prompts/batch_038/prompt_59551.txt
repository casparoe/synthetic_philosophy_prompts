I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy. I spent thirty-odd years as a process engineer in a chemical plant, and I have read philosophy of science on my own, in the evenings, more or less continuously since I stopped working. One argument has occupied me for years, and the longer I sit with it, the more I doubt that the two sides are disputing what they think they are disputing.

The argument is over whether we are entitled to believe our best scientific theories are true, or at least approximately true. On one side: science predicts and intervenes with a reliability nothing else in human life approaches, and that reliability would be inexplicable if our theories were wildly false, so we should believe them — or at any rate those parts of them that are actually doing the predicting. On the other side: look at the record. Caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether, vital force — theories that predicted well and were used to build things, and were later thrown out. From which the pessimist concludes that our present theories belong on the same list; we simply have not reached that page yet. Each side has its standing counters. The realists say the historical list is padded: that several of the theories on it never delivered the novel, quantitative predictive success the induction requires, and that what survives theory change is the mathematical structure, so that believing only in structure was the right posture all along. The anti-realists answer that retreating to structure is retreating until there is nothing left to be a realist about, and that approximate truth has never been given an account that does the work the realist argument needs it to do. Neither move ends anything; the exchange restarts in the same place.

My own suspicion — a hypothesis, not a conclusion, and I would like it attacked — is that the argument has stalled because both sides are running an inductive generalization over a sample whose membership they cannot agree on. To assert that most successful theories of the past turned out to be false, one must first fix a reference class: which theories count as scientifically successful at all, which parts of a theory count as its claims, and by what standard a discarded theory counts as false rather than as merely superseded. My guess is that the two sides are quietly assembling different lists and then presenting the resulting base rate as though it were a shared fact, so that what announces itself as a disagreement about the strength of an inference is in substance a disagreement about the data set. If that is right, there may be no argument here to win — only a sample to be agreed upon.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is my hypothesis correct? Is this a dispute about the membership of a reference class, a dispute about what truth and approximate truth mean when applied to a whole theory rather than to a sentence, a dispute about the attitude being recommended — belief, acceptance, or merely working as if — with each side answering a different question under one word, or some tangle of these that has been mistaken for a single argument? Work the diagnosis out from what each side actually says; do not give me a survey of the positions or a report on who is presently ahead. Then tell me what would count as progress, given the diagnosis you reach: a criterion for the reference class that both sides could accept in advance and that would decide the hard cases the same way; a formal treatment of how much pessimism a given base rate licenses; a specification of what approximate truth would have to be for the realist argument to run; a change of question; or nothing at all — in which case I want the reasons for expecting no movement, and what that would imply about whether the question was ever well posed.
