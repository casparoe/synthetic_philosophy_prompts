I'm a software engineer, never taken a philosophy class, so bear with me on the vocabulary. I work at a company that makes simulation and sensor-fusion software sold to militaries in a bunch of countries, including a couple whose current wars I think are plainly wars of aggression. There's a long-running argument in our internal ethics channel. One of our staff engineers (ex-military) says the work is morally neutral because of what he calls the moral equality of combatants: a soldier fighting for the unjust side isn't committing murder when he shoots at an enemy soldier, he's permitted to fight just like the guy on the other side is, so selling tools to both sides doesn't make us complicit in anything.

I like debugging things, so I tried to write his reasoning out the way I'd write out a proof. I think it's valid and the conclusion is way stronger then anything I can accept. Here's what I got:

1. Killing someone is morally wrong only if it violates a right of theirs not to be killed.
2. Soldiers on both sides of a war are permitted to kill each other — that's the whole point of saying war isn't just mass murder, and it's why we don't put captured enemy soldiers on trial for fighting.
3. If A is permitted to kill B, then B has no right that A not kill him. Otherwise the permission would be a permission to violate a right, and by 1 that makes it wrong.
4. So enemy soldiers facing each other have no right not to be killed by each other.
5. You can only lose a right not to be killed by something you do — being an unjust threat, or culpably taking part in wrongdoing. You don't lose rights just by falling under a label.
6. But plenty of soldiers on the unjust side do nothing wrong: conscripts, people who reasonably believe their own government, people who pose a threat only because they were told to stand there. By 5 they haven't lost anything.
7. That contradicts 4, so one of 1, 2, 3 or 5 is false.
8. If the false one is 5 — you lose the right just by being a combatant, no culpability required — then I can't find where the rule stops. A 19-year-old draftee asleep in a barracks 500 miles from the front, a navy cook, a whole standing army in peacetime, arguably anyone in uniform anywhere. And it makes the cause you fight for permanently irrelevant to whether killing you is wrong, so an invasion and the defence against it are morally identical acts of killing.
9. I can't accept 8. Especially not "the cause is never relevant," because if that were true I don't see how individual conduct in an aggressive war could ever be worse than the same conduct in a defensive one.
10. So 2 is false: soldiers on the unjust side are not permitted to kill. Which kills my colleague's neutrality argument, and also implies that every soldier fighting for an unjust cause is doing something like murder every time he fights — which I don't believe either. My uncle was drafted into a war I think was unjust and he is not a murderer.

So it walks me from "our software is neutral" to "war is a zone where nobody has rights" to "draftees are murderers," and at least one of those has got to be wrong, but every single step looks fine when I stare at it. I can't even tell whether the argument is valid and I just have to swallow one of these bullets, or whether some word is doing two jobs somewhere and I've got an equivocation on my hands.

I already tried two patches and both feel broken. Making 3 conditional on the war being just turns 2 into the thing under dispute, so the argument stops engaging my colleague's claim and just assumes he's wrong. And someone in the channel said rights can be overridden rather than lost, but I couldn't tell how that differs from saying there's no right in the first place, or why the override should fire exactly in wars and exactly against combatants.

What I want from you:

- Tell me which step you reject, and whether the flaw is a hidden equivocation or a premise that's just false. If it's an equivocation, name the two readings and show me the exact line where it switches.
- Explain why that step looked so plausible to me. I want to understand the trap, not just be told it's a trap.
- Put a price tag on giving up each of 1, 2, 3, 5 and 8 separately: what stops working, and who pays the cost. I'd rather keep some version of 2 and keep rejecting "draftees are murderers" than keep anything else, so if the bill lands there, tell me what I'm buying with it.
- Say whether there's a non-dodge way to keep something like my colleague's equality as a legal or conventional status rather than a real moral permission. If there is: who makes that convention, why is it binding on me, and does it actually get him back to "selling to both sides is neutral"? Or does it just move the problem?
- End by telling me plainly whether the neutrality conclusion survives any of the exits you've given me, because that's the part I actually have to act on.

Plain language, define any term you need on first use. And please don't hand me a reading list or a tour of which philosophers say what — I can't look anything up right now, and a summary of the two camps wouldn't tell me where my argument breaks.
