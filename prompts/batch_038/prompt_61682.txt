I teach a fifteen-student upper-level seminar on formal epistemology, meeting once a week for one hundred minutes. About two-thirds of them have had an introduction to probability and one term of logic; none has taken a computability course, though all have met the informal notion of a Turing machine. Next week's topic is the attempt to convert Occam's razor into a precise inductive rule: a prior over candidate hypotheses weighted by the length of their shortest descriptions, together with the mixture over all computable predictors that this weighting generates. I want the students to collide with the central difficulties during the session, not to be told about them afterward, and I would prefer that no names or formal apparatus appear until the debrief.

Please design one self-contained activity filling the entire session that makes three things felt rather than explained.

First, that weighting by description length is not a stylistic preference but something that pays off. Participants should be scored in a way that makes hedging across long and short hypotheses carry a real cost. The scoring must run on paper tokens or points, not money or grades, and I must be able to compute it aloud on a whiteboard.

Second, that description length is only defined relative to a language for writing descriptions down. I want the room split in two and issued different notations for recording candidate rules, deliberately arranged so that the rule actually generating the data is compact in one notation and cumbersome in the other, while a planted decoy rule runs the opposite way. The two halves should end up sincerely disagreeing about which hypothesis is simpler while agreeing perfectly about all the evidence, and should then discover over successive rounds what does and does not shift as the data accumulate.

Third, that some stretch of the data stream may be one for which no rule expressible in either notation beats chance, and that this should be survivable within the scoring system rather than catastrophic.

Data should be revealed in rounds, a few bits at a time, with participants committing to predictions in writing before each reveal. Tell me precisely how I generate the stream in advance without a computer, and how I prevent it from being inferrable from the room's reactions.

Include a materials list I can print or pull from a supply closet; a minute-by-minute timeline; the complete scoring rule with worked arithmetic for two or three sample rounds; both notations written out far enough that I could hand them to students cold; and a debrief mapping particular moments of play onto particular claims about the position.

Then tell me candidly what will go wrong the first time I run it — where students will game the scoring, where the arithmetic will stall, where the intended discovery simply will not arrive — and what to have ready for each failure. Close by stating which features of the actual view this activity cannot reproduce, and what I must supply verbally so the students do not leave holding a distorted version of it.
