I was a plant physiologist for thirty-six years — mostly drought tolerance in small grains, with more statistics in it than my colleagues liked — and I retired four years ago and promptly fell into philosophy, which my wife finds hilarious. Right now I'm working through Ian Hacking, especially his stuff on making up people and the looping effects of the human kinds, and I want to try an experiment I never had time for when I was running a lab. I read Ken Kesey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest as an undergraduate around 1970 and saw the Forman film when it came out, and I have never been able to shake that ward. So I'd like a real reading of the novel — the novel, not the movie, though say something about the adaptation if it matters — through the looping-effects idea, and I want you to be at least as hard on my reading as on Kesey's.

So we're aimed at the same target, here's what I take Hacking to be claiming. It is not merely that labels stigmatize people; that's older sociology and it's not interesting on its own. It's that when the human sciences put a new category into circulation, the people sorted into it begin to understand and enact themselves under that description, and so the category's own target moves — kinds of people are interactive in a way that quarks are not, because quarks don't read the literature about quarks and then revise their behavior, whereas a teenager who is told she is anorexic has, whether she accepts it or fights it, a new way of being a person available to her. What I can't figure out is how much of Cuckoo's Nest is actually about that, and how much is about plain old institutional power wearing a white coat.

Here's what I want you to work through. First: which classifications in the book genuinely loop? Billy Bibbit seems to me the clearest case — he stops stuttering for a few hours when he's living outside the description of himself, and then Nurse Rachet puts the description back on him with one sentence about his mother, and he goes straight to the razor. Is that a looping effect in Hacking's sense, or is it just cruelty, and how would you tell the difference? Second: the Chief. I seem to remember the narrator dividing the ward into the chronics, who've been given up on, and the acutes, who are considered fixable, and the Chief himself is sorted as barely there at all — a big deaf-mute who's treated as furniture, which is exactly what lets him watch everything and eventually tell the story. He's docile because he's classified as docile, and then at the end he speaks and lifts the thing McMurphy couldn't lift. Is that a loop running to completion, or is the Chief's whole taxonomy — the Combine, the machinery he hallucinates behind the hospital — a symptom that makes him too unreliable to be evidence for anything? Third, and this is my own idea so please attack it: McMurphy is the case the theory can't handle. He's classified for administrative reasons, a prison transfer under evaluation, and he adopts the description on purpose, without being made by it. He's a strategic entrant into a kind. Does Hacking's picture have room for the malingerer, and is McMurphy a counterexample to looping or a weird confirmation of it — the loop being something you can walk into backwards?

And fourth, the bit I actually care about. The lobotomy. My hunch is that it's the institution's way of exiting the loop: once a classified person refuses to be made up by the classification, the ward stops trying to reshape a self and instead removes the capacity to reshape back, turning an interactive kind into an indifferent one — a body you can chart and never have to argue with. The shock treatments earlier are a rehearsal for it. And notice that it runs the other way too: Rachet comes back from her week off unable to speak, the classifier deprived of the one instrument that made classification stick. The Chief then smothers McMurphy, which I read as a refusal to let the classified body stand in for the person. Is that a genuine extension of Hacking, or am I just doing literary embroidery on a philosophy of science? If it's genuine, what would the extension consist in — does looping presuppose that the classified can answer back, and what does an account owe us when the answer is surgically abolished?

Two more things. Please keep looping effects cleanly separated from the neighbors it gets confused with, because I suspect Kesey confuses them: labeling theory and stigma, the Foucauldian power-knowledge story, and the anti-psychiatry claim that the illness isn't real at all. Hacking's dynamic nominalism is supposed to be compatible with the condition being real — that's part of why it's interesting — and I want to know whether the novel is compatible with it too, or whether the book really wants us to think these men aren't sick, only oppressed, in which case using it to teach looping effects is a trap. For calibration, compare it against a documented case you trust more, like the way the American schizophrenia diagnosis changed character in the 1960s so that hostility and aggression entered the criteria and the diagnosis became attached to young Black men in protest — there's a history of that at a Michigan state hospital, published in 2010, that I've only read about secondhand. Does a novel published in 1962 support the looping thesis better or worse than that history does, and what would each be evidence of?

Last thing, and it's the one I'd argue with my book group about. Finish by telling me what the novel contributes to the debate that Hacking's essays can't — something about who has standing to apply a description, or about needing an audience to loop in front of, or whatever you actually find. And be straight with me about where I should stop pushing. My memory of the book is more than fifty years old, so if I've misremembered a scene or invented one, tell me flat out rather than playing along with it. No bibliography and no reading list, I can't chase down references anyway — I just want the thinking.
