The examination question, as set, was as follows.

QUESTION (25 marks total)

(a) [8 marks] State the rule of strict Bayesian conditionalization. Identify precisely what it assumes about the agent's relation to her evidence, and explain what fails, in technical rather than merely intuitive terms, when that assumption is not satisfied.

(b) [9 marks] State Jeffrey's rule for revising on a finite partition. What does the rule require beyond the agent's new probabilities for the members of the partition? Show how strict conditionalization arises as a special case, and say what the rule implies about the possibility of acquiring certainty.

(c) [8 marks] Two properties of strict conditionalization are often advertised as virtues: that the posterior is conservative with respect to the prior, and that the order in which items of evidence are taken up makes no difference to the final posterior. For each property, say whether it carries over to iterated Jeffrey updating, and make precise what "conservative" is supposed to mean.

CANDIDATE'S ANSWER

(a) Strict conditionalization says that if an agent with credence function P learns E with certainty, her new function is P⁺(H) = P(H|E) = P(H∧E)/P(E) for every H. Two assumptions are built into this. The agent must come to assign probability 1 to E; and P(E) must have been strictly positive to begin with. When these fail, two different things go wrong. If the agent merely becomes more confident of E without becoming certain of it, the recipe simply does not apply: P(H|E) is the credence to adopt on the supposition that one has become sure of E, and it says nothing at all about how to revise on a shift of confidence short of certainty. If instead P(E) = 0, the conditional P(H|E) is undefined and there is no number for the rule to output. The first failure is a failure of scope; the second is a failure of definition. Only the second is really a technical breakdown, since the first is just a case the rule was never meant to cover.

(b) Let {E₁, …, Eₙ} be a finite partition, so the cells are pairwise incompatible and jointly exhaustive, and suppose P(Eᵢ) > 0 for each i. Let experience move the agent's probabilities over the partition to q₁, …, qₙ, which sum to 1, without making any single cell certain. Then Jeffrey's rule is

P⁺(H) = Σᵢ P(H|Eᵢ) qᵢ.

Beyond the new partition probabilities the rule needs rigidity: P⁺(H|Eᵢ) = P(H|Eᵢ) for every H and every i. This is not an independent assumption that has to be checked case by case. It is secured by the fact that the new experience bears only on which cell of the partition obtains and not on how the cells bear on H; once that is granted, rigidity is just what it means for the experience to be about the partition. Given rigidity the rule is immediate: expand P⁺(H) by the law of total probability over the partition and substitute.

Strict conditionalization is the limiting case in which some qₖ = 1 and every other qᵢ = 0, since then P⁺(H) = P(H|Eₖ). Two consequences are worth noting. If the new partition probabilities equal the old ones, the rule returns P unchanged, so an experience that leaves the partition alone licenses no revision whatsoever. And since P⁺(H) is a weighted average of the P(H|Eᵢ), it cannot exceed the largest of them: if none of the conditionals was already 1, no posterior credence can be 1 either. The rule can redistribute certainty that is already latent in the conditional structure, but it cannot manufacture certainty out of inconclusive experience.

(c) Conservativeness carries over, and Jeffrey's rule can be characterized exactly in these terms. Among all credence functions that satisfy the new assignment over the partition, it selects the one with the greatest Shannon entropy. It is therefore maximally conservative: it commits the agent to as little as possible beyond what the experience forced on her, and it moves her away from her prior no further than it has to.

Order-independence does not carry over. Revising first on partition A and then on partition B generally differs from revising first on B and then on A. The reason is that the second revision is applied to a distribution whose conditionals on the second partition have already been disturbed by the first revision, whereas with strict conditionalization both revisions are just restrictions of one prior to a growing body of evidence, and P(H|E∧F) does not depend on the order in which E and F are learned. So the non-commutativity here is not an artefact; it reflects the fact that a Jeffrey revision on A is not merely the acquisition of information about which cell of A obtains, but a change in the whole function, including its conditionals on B.
