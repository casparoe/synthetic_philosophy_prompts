I am working through the early sections of Frege's Begriffsschrift in English translation — the stretch in which he introduces the judgment sign and then the conditional sign — and I have run into a difficulty I cannot resolve on my own. Because the translation's phrasing is dense and I want to be sure I am reasoning about the argument rather than about my own recollection of it, let me first set out what I take the relevant claims to be. These are my own compressed renderings, not quotations, and if any of them misstates the text I would want to be told so:

(i) Frege separates the content that is judged from the act of judging it. Truth and falsity attach only to what is judged, and the assertion sign exists to record that a judgment has been made; it contributes nothing to the content asserted.

(ii) A content can be entertained without being judged. In a conditional, the content of the antecedent and the content of the consequent both occur unasserted, and the single judgment falls on the whole.

(iii) He glosses the conditional sign by running through the combinations of the two contents and their negations: the conditional rules out exactly one of these combinations, the one in which the antecedent holds and the consequent fails. On this gloss, the content of a hypothetical judgment is something like that ruling-out.

(iv) He also indicates that contents occurring in unasserted position are nonetheless determinate, and are the very same contents that could be judged separately on their own.

Two questions about what this means, and then two about what it is worth.

1. On (iii): if the content of a conditional is simply the exclusion of one combination out of four, then Frege is already offering a truth-functional account of the conditional at this early stage, and the object of the judgment in a conditional is a content of the same general kind as the content of a bare assertion. But (ii) and (iii) seem to pull in different directions. (ii) suggests that judging is a further step added to a content that is already complete and self-standing, whereas (iii) suggests that the content of the whole is constructed out of components that have, at that point, no claim to truth at all. Is there a genuine tension here, or am I reading (ii) as a stronger claim than the text supports?

2. On (iv): does Frege anywhere commit himself to the principle that a content occurring unasserted has the same content it would have if it were asserted — call it invariance across asserted and unasserted positions — or is that principle a later reconstruction, supplied by readers who needed it for a different argument? I want to know whether the passage licenses invariance or merely fails to contradict it, because for my purposes the difference is decisive.

3. Suppose a non-cognitivist about moral vocabulary wants to borrow this apparatus. In a conditional whose antecedent is a moral sentence, they say, the antecedent records a disapproval that is entertained and withheld, while the whole conditional expresses a compound attitude; since the very same disapproval is entertained in the antecedent as would be expressed by the bare assertion, no equivocation occurs and the embedding objection simply dissolves. Is that borrowing licensed by (i) through (iv)? My suspicion is that it is not, because Frege's machinery appears to require that each component content be the sort of thing that could itself stand as the content of a judgment — that is, that it be capable of being true or false — and that is exactly what the non-cognitivist denies of moral sentences. If my suspicion is right, what precisely is doing the work in that requirement, and is it detachable from the rest of the account? If it is detachable, how would the remainder have to be reformulated so that the components are not truth-apt while the whole still has a determinate content? If it is not detachable, what ingredient would the non-cognitivist have to supply that Frege does not?

4. A colleague of mine proposes a deflationary reading: (ii) is not a semantic thesis at all but an instruction about how to read the notation — Frege is telling us which sign governs which, not claiming that unasserted occurrences share their content with asserted ones. On that reading, the embedding problem rests on mistaking a notational stipulation for a claim about meaning. Is this defensible? What turns on it: if my colleague were right, what exactly would be lost, and would the non-cognitivist's difficulty in 3 disappear along with it, or survive intact?

I am not after references or a reading list. What I want is the reasoning worked through step by step, and above all a definite verdict on 2 — whether the passage gives us invariance or only permits it — since the answer to 3 seems to hinge on it.
