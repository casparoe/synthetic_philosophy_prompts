Twice a day I tick a box declaring that I am not a robot, or pick out the squares containing a traffic light, before a website will sell me a ticket or let me reset a password. Two features of this practice strike me as philosophically loaded, and I want the commitments dug out of them.

The first is that the widget is a Turing test run backwards. It settles whether there is a person at the keyboard purely by finding a task a person can do and a machine cannot — no testimony, no introspection, nothing about what it is like to be the clicker — and treats that performance gap as decisive for whether an agent with standing is present. Yet the same widget doubles as a collection device: the labels I supply while proving my humanity are precisely the sort of data that erases the gap, so the test is engineered to decay by its own operation.

The second is that in checkbox form the widget tests no capacity at all. As I understand it, it reads the wobble of my cursor, my cookies, my browsing history, the length of my hesitation — the causal pedigree of the click rather than anything the clicker can do.

So: which criterion of mind is the practice actually committed to, and is it one criterion or two? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and say which rival accounts of mind it rules out and which it merely fails to notice. Tell me what it assumes away about the standard objections to behavioural tests — the lookup-table machine that passes by brute storage, the room that produces fluent output without understanding — and whether the assumption is defensible or simply convenient. Then say whether a test whose discriminating power is designed to expire can be a test of anything other than the current state of engineering, and what the institution is thereby committed to about the human/machine asymmetry: a permanent fact about minds, or an empirical accident that keeps moving. Finally, what would have to change in the widget — and in what we owe whatever is on the far side of it — if we adopted the criterion the practice implicitly rejects, namely that passing a conversational test is what having a mind consists in?

Please answer in British English.
