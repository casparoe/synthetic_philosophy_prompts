Our group is trying to write down, in a form that could actually be audited, the inference behind a monitoring protocol for a deployed model. The claim it needs to support is roughly this: we have identified some mechanism in an earlier or smaller version of the system, and we want to conclude something about whether that mechanism survives post-training — whether it was removed, merely suppressed, or displaced by something else that produces the same surface behavior. Every version of that argument trades on an account of when two mechanisms, in two checkpoints or at two scales or in two model families, count as the same mechanism, and nobody in the group has been able to say what that account must look like. Before we read another proposal, I want the specification.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of mechanism identity across training, scale, architecture, and post-training modification has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case that motivates it — the concrete interpretability situation that an account failing the requirement would misdescribe — rather than the requirement in the abstract. The list should cover at least: gradual drift over the course of training, where consecutive checkpoints differ negligibly but the endpoints differ a great deal; transformations that preserve the computation while changing the implementation, such as permuting hidden units, quantizing weights, pruning, or adding an adapter; two models that are behaviorally indistinguishable on every evaluation we can run yet differ internally; polysemantic components that take part in several circuits at once, so that parts do not map cleanly onto wholes; one capability implemented one way at small scale and another way at large scale; the level at which identity is being claimed — a feature, a circuit, an algorithm, a behavioral disposition — and whether the account is level-relative or level-committing; whether identity here is an equivalence relation or a graded similarity; and what the account must stay consistent with from how these systems are actually trained, including the fact that different seeds yield different implementations of the same capability.

State also the distinctions the account must not collapse — among them, a mechanism being absent versus being present but unreachable, sameness of function versus sameness of implementation, and identity versus mere similarity — and mark, for each requirement, whether it is non-negotiable for the purpose above or a preference imported from a particular research camp, with the reason for the classification.

Then answer two questions about the list itself. Which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts? And is any subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable — my own hunch is that interpreter-independence (identity must not depend on the analyst's chosen vocabulary or decomposition), epistemic tractability (identity must be determinable from data we can actually collect), and determinacy of level cannot all be had at once, but I want the argument rather than my hunch, including whether the tension is real or dissolves once one of the three is weakened. Close with a brief assessment of how a causal-role account, a structural account, an algorithmic-level account, and a graded family-resemblance account fare against the list; keep this short, since the requirements are what I am after.

Be explicit about your confidence throughout: separate the requirements you think any account must meet from those that reflect your own theoretical taste, and finish by naming the single point in your list you are least sure of, what would settle it, and which way you would bet if forced.
