I'm in my final year of a philosophy degree and taking a course on knowledge and scepticism, and honestly our seminar group has been going round in circles on the same issue for about five weeks now. Its the closure principle — whether knowledge is closed under known implication — and specifically whether Nozick's tracking picture can survive it.

Roughly where everyone sits. First faction, following Dretske and Nozick: you can know the animal in the pen is a zebra without knowing it isn't a mule cleverly painted to look like one, and you can know you have hands without knowing you aren't a handless brain in a vat; so closure just isn't a constraint on knowledge, and yes, you end up happily asserting a pair of claims that DeRose finds abominable, and they think that's a price worth paying for blocking the sceptic. Second faction: that price is a reductio. Closure is constitutive of the very idea of knowledge, so if sensitivity forces you to give it up, sensitivity is the wrong modal condition — knowledge requires safety, not sensitivity, and safety doesn't wreck closure. Third faction, the contextualists: both sides are right in their own contexts because the standards shift with what's salient, so there's no single relation for the first two factions to fight over. Fourth, one guy who thinks the whole thing turns on which method of belief-formation you hold fixed when you evaluate the counterfactuals, and that there's no principled way to pick the method, so the debate isn't hard but literally undecidable.

My own contribution made things worse rather than better. I asked everyone to consider a case, they all agreed on the verdicts, and nobody moved an inch. Case: a city archive digitises its old residents' registers. You want to know whether your grandmother was registered as living in the city in 1952. You ask the archivist, she queries the digitised index — a faithful scan of the paper register — and says yes. You come to believe it, and the whole seminar agrees that you know it. They also all agree that you can't tell, from where you're sitting, whether the index is a faithful scan or a reconstruction put together decades ago after a fire, and they all agree that your subsequent reasoning is competent deduction. Where they explode is the description. One lot say the belief is sensitive but not safe. Another lot say whether it's safe depends entirely on how you individuate the method, so the description begs the question. The contextualists say you know it in the seminar room and wouldn't know it once the fire story has been mentioned. Then you deduce: I'm not in the reconstruction situation. Closure says you know that. Tracking says you don't, since if you were in the reconstruction situation you'd believe exactly the same thing. And we just restarted from the beginning, each side absorbing the agreed case into its own vocabulary.

Here's my hunch, and I want you to attack it rather than be polite about it. I think this disagreement was never about which knowledge claims come out true. Cases can't settle it, because all four factions agree on the verdict for almost every case anyone has ever cooked up and each of them has machinery for explaining away any new one. What they're actually arguing about is what the concept is *for*: whether a knowledge claim certifies an information link that can be relied on and passed along to others — in which case closure should fail, because information doesn't transmit through deduction the way truth does — or certifies a modal achievement that's creditable to the believer, or fixes conversational standards, or none of these. On that reading they aren't offering rival analyses of one relation at all; they're competing for one job description, and no number of zebras will decide it.

So: is my diagnosis right, partly right, or a cop-out that flatters my own side? Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word knows, four groups answering different questions under a single heading, or is the closure question ill-posed in some more specific way? Ground the diagnosis in what each side positively claims, not just in their verdicts on cases. And tell me what would count as progress given whatever diagnosis you land on — a distinction nobody has drawn, a formal or impossibility result about what any relation can do while playing more than one of those roles, some completely different kind of evidence, a change of question — or argue properly that no progress should be expected and why. Please write in British English, and don't hand me a reading list: I can't look anything up this week and I want your reasoning, not references.
