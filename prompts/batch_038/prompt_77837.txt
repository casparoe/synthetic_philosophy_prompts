I'm the science lead on a small mission-review panel. Roughly twice a year an instrument team or a lab comes to us with something in the "we think we found life" family, and we have to decide whether what they're reporting actually counts as evidence of it. The panel is geochemists and engineers, nobody who's read a page of philosophy since undergrad, and the last three times we went around in circles for a full day and then split the vote on vibes. Our funder's rulebook only has two boxes — detected, not detected — and half the panel thinks that's a joke, so I'd like the procedure to let us report something graded when the evidence is graded, without turning into a numbers theater exercise.

So: write us a decision procedure we can actually run on a claim, grounded in whatever the conceptual work on defining life really has to say — that old NASA working definition of life as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution and the standard complaints about it, the metabolism-first vs replicator-first disagreement, and the usual mess of borderline cases (viruses, prions, dormant spores, mules, flames, crystals, that sort of thing) — but translated into an ordered list of questions a non-specialist can answer about a specific claim. For each question tell me what counts as a good enough answer, what a yes or a no does to the case, and, crucially, flag the ones where the question isn't factual at all but a decision about what we want the word to mean; those are the ones we should escalate to a vote instead of arguing about for six hours. Constraints: one 90-minute meeting per claim, so if it can't be run in about an hour it's useless to us, and whatever it outputs goes into a press release, so a false positive is a lot worse than a shrug. Also be honest about where the procedure will misfire and what it flatly cannot settle.

Then run it on three claims sitting on my desk right now:

1. A plume sample from an icy moon. Mass spec shows a 4% excess of one amino-acid handedness over the other, plus a polymer made of repeating units that don't match anything in our contamination catalog. Imager: nothing that looks like a cell. Team says life, and says the chirality number settles it.
2. A lab report on a flow reactor running a set of mutually catalyzing reactions. It's been self-sustaining for about 400 days, it recovers when perturbed, and its composition drifts over time — but nobody has ever seen it produce a catalyst that wasn't already in the network. The authors want to call it protocellular life; their reviewers say it's chemistry in a fancy box.
3. An Earth claim about a newly described infectious protein particle that templates its own folded shape onto normal copies of the protein. No nucleic acid, no metabolism of its own, no lineage you could put a mutation in. One of our members is convinced this is the case the whole debate has been hiding from.

Give me the procedure first, then a worked pass on each of the three, including the graded output for each, and make sure at least one of the three comes out with an answer that's going to annoy half my panel. Where the procedure punts to a vote, say so plainly and tell me what the vote is actually about.
