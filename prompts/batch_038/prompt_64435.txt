I write a weekly-ish column on science and ideas and this week I'm trying to do something a little different with the old unrealistic-assumptions chestnut — Friedman's 1953 methodology essay, the F-twist, seventy years of commentary piled on top. I don't want another recap of that debate; my editor has pushed back on the draft twice for exactly that reason. So instead: here's the position I've actually ended up defending, and I want you to tell me what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

The position is that realism-of-assumptions isn't a property of a model, it's a property of a model-use pair. The same frictionless-market, no-arbitrage idealization that is harmless when you're pricing an option can be actively distorting when that model gets recruited into an arguement that markets self-correct — and nothing about the model changed in between, only the question being put to it. From that I want to cash out a distinction I've been calling assumption portability: an idealization is portable iff how badly it distorts doesn't depend on which question you ask. On my reading Friedman's defense only ever licenses non-load-bearing idealizations inside the use they were tuned for, and working economists routinely assume portability for free whenever they carry a model from description into policy evaluation. The Lucas critique is maybe the one place the discipline noticed this pattern and then failed to generalize it into a methodological principle.

So: what counts against me? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions and formal results that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically — does my view just collapse into Musgrave's negligibility/domain/heuristic typology, or into the credible-worlds line Sugden pushes, such that it buys nothing beyond a relabeling? Does it predict anything the flat, use-insensitive positions don't predict? Give me one concrete case, real or constructed, where a model is epistemically fine in one use and defective in another with the assumptions held fixed, and then tell me what it would look like for that case to go the other way instead. And the uncomfortable one: is portability so easy to reassign after the fact that my position can't fail — if a model's policy use blows up, I can always say the idealization simply wasn't portable for that use, which starts to sound like insulation by qualification rather than a claim about the world. Say plainly whether that's true, and if it is, whether it's fatal for a methodological thesis of this kind or just the normal condition of one. Which of these defeaters would a committed use-relativist concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they wave off? What would most move me personally is a coupling result: evidence that the severity of an idealization tracks predictive accuracy and counterfactual accuracy together, so there's no gap left for use-relativity to live in. If results like that exist, or are even in principle available for a discipline as noisy as economics, tell me — that's the version of refutation I'd find hardest to squirm out of, and I'd rather hear it definately than be reassured.
