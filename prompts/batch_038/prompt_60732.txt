I clerked for Judge Marion Adley in 2009–10, and her three children have asked me to propose the toast at her retirement dinner next month, in the county bar association's rooms, immediately before a slideshow nobody is looking forward to. There will be about a hundred and twenty people: her family, three decades of former clerks, judges and lawyers from the county, and — because for twenty years she ran an informal Thursday-evening reading group at her kitchen table that outlasted two of her marriages — somewhere between eight and ten philosophers who have read considerably more philosophy than I have. She has asked that the toast run no longer than five minutes, that it concern her work rather than her hobbies, which she maintains she never had, and that it be funny at least once.

The difficulty is that there is one thing the toast has to handle, and I don't know how to handle it without either flattening it or turning a dinner into a seminar. In her early fifties she came to accept an argument against moral responsibility — the regress argument, the one that says that if you are to be truly responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for the way you are, at least mentally, and since the chain of "and who made that?" never terminates in anything you authored, there is nothing left over to charge. She told me once, in chambers, that it was the only piece of philosophy that had ever made her cry. She then went back on the bench and sentenced people for another eighteen years, and she never regarded that as a contradiction. She was savage about two misreadings. The first is that the argument is an excuse: she used to say that an argument which tells you nothing about what to do tomorrow morning has been misunderstood rather than refuted, and that anyone who thinks it changed her sentencing had not been paying attention. The second is that the argument rests on determinism: she never used that word, and she insisted the argument goes through whether or not every event has a cause, because what does the work is the regress and not the physics. She also had an emphasis of her own, which I would like preserved. As she put it to me, the argument is not that there is no self and not that people are illusions; it is that whenever you go looking for the self that made the choice, you find only more of the same material — more temperament, more history, more of the particular way she had been frightened as a child — and no surplus standing behind it. The person is exactly where all of that is, she said, and that is precisely why there is nothing further to indict.

Here is what I need from you: the full text of the toast, as I would stand up and say it. It must be affectionate rather than explanatory, and it must be followable by her grandchildren without a single term of art. It must state the argument accurately enough that the philosophers at the back table nod, and it must not name the philosopher or the essay — she asked specifically that we not drop a name at a dinner, on the grounds that a name dropped at a dinner is a way of not saying the thing, so everything must be conveyed by description. Do not quote from any philosophical text, and do not gesture at a literature. The words "free will," "determinism," and "just deserts" are banned outright: she said she had misused the last of them for thirty years and would not hear it at her own party. The toast should make use of one habit of hers and should make it carry the weight without ever announcing that it is doing so — for thirty-two years she would not let anyone leave her courtroom, defendant or juror or witness, without being told aloud the date of their next hearing, because early in her career a man missed one and lost two years of his life to a clerical silence. Finally, two things about the room. One of her clerks is now a prosecutor who has written publicly that arguments of this kind are irresponsible, and he will be sitting at the head table; the toast must not lose him, must not sneer at him, and should hand him something true he can accept. And it must not end on a resolution: she has told me she would walk out if the toast concluded with her "holding both things at once," or with any variation on that, or with the argument dissolving into warmth; but it is a party, and it must not end bleakly either. Find the ending that is neither.
