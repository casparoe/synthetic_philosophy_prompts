Our reading group is doing Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the Geneva theater, and I'm running a little game with it: I hand everyone a case Rousseau never actually ruled on and we have to write down a predicted verdict before we're allowed to argue. I want to sanity-check my own answer first, and I'd much rather get a prediction grounded in the arguments he actually makes than a generic "Rousseau hated the theater" take.

Here's the track record as I've got it, all from the letter:

- Molière's Misanthrope: bad, but not for the reason people assume. His complaint is about who the audience ends up siding with — the blunt, upright man is the comic figure and the polished man of the world is the likeable one, so the play trains you to find integrity embarrassing.
- Tartuffe: bad because the ridicule aimed at fake devotion spills over onto devotion as such, and ends up arming people who were never taken in by the hypocrite at all.
- Voltaire's Mahomet: bad because the impostor comes out looking grand; you're supposed to be indignant and you keep sliding into admiration instead, so the vice never actually gets degraded.
- Tragedies built on love, Racine above all: bad, and here the argument is about the audience rather than the plot. He doesnt buy the old purging story — a play can only purge passions you don't have, and staging love makes it more gripping for people already in its grip, not safer.
- Greek tragedy: much better than the French classical stuff, because it was a public, religious, national affair — choruses of citizens, stories the polis told about itself, everybody outdoors — whereas French tragedy is an indoor diversion about gallantry for a polished audience.
- Actors as a class: the contempt attached to them isn't just barbarous prejudice. The trade asks a man to sell himself and to stand continuously on display, judged for his person, his face, his voice.
- Paris vs. Geneva: he's explicitly not an absolutist here. He thinks theater flatters and reinforces a people's existing moeurs rather than reforming them, so what's tolerable in a big, already-corrupt, courtly city is corrosive in a small republic where citizens are supposed to be in public all the time.
- His own positive proposal: free outdoor festivals with no stage and no cast, where the crowd is the spectacle and the people watching become the ones on show.

Now the case I want your prediction on. Geneva, a couple of years later: some citizens want to mark an anniversary of the city's independence with a play written by a Genevan, about a Genevan magistrate who refuses a bribe from a neighbouring power and is ruined by it. No love plot, nobody seduced, no women on the platform. It's performed outdoors in a public square on a low raised platform by amateurs — a clerk, two butchers, a pastor's son — unpaid, in roughly their own clothes, free to anyone, with the crowd standing and sitting on three sides. Afterwards everybody drifts off to drink together and argue about it. But it is a script: the cast is designated, they've rehearsed, and they're up there being watched and applauded for an hour or two while everyone else watches them.

So — does he bless it, tolerate it as harmless, or come out against it? I want the reasoning, not a vibe.

Give me: (1) your predicted verdict with a rough confidence attached; (2) the single rule you think best explains all eight earlier verdicts, stated sharply enough that I could test it against a ninth; (3) at least two other rules that fit that record about as well but say something different about the new case, plus which of them you're betting against and why; (4) the minimal-pair case — one variable changed, everything else held fixed — that would separate your top two candidate rules, with each rule's prediction written down in advance; and (5) if the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine this one, say what principle Rousseau would be adopting, possibly without noticing, whichever way he lands.

Also be upfront about where you're least sure and what would change your mind: which of my summaries above, if it's wrong or overstated, would flip your prediction; and whether your best single rule is more likely to fail because the letter isn't running one rule at all — moral psychology about sympathy in one stretch, political sociology about moeurs in another, something closer to a theory of the person when it gets to the actors — rather than because I chose the wrong one.
