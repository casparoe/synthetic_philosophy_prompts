My dad's been a widower two years now and something keeps happening to him that I've never been able to find a word for. He'll be in the armchair with the paper and he'll say, without looking up, "your mother's in here." No image, no voice, no perfume, nothing at the edge of his vision — he's emphatic about that part, he says there's nothing there to see or hear. And its not that he thinks she's in here, either, and not that he's talked himself into it: he believes she's dead, he'll tell you so in the same breath, he never sets a place for her or turns around to speak to the chair. But he says the "she's here" arrives the same way "the lamp's over there" arrives — immediate, not worked out, the kind of thing you don't feel the need to check. Then it fades after five or ten minutes, like a smell going out of a room. I work nights in a care home and I've had four residents describe the same thing to me in the past year, always with the same two features: nothing sensory, and no change in what they believe or do.

I've been collecting instances because I think it's one thing and not several, but the instances pull in slightly different directions and I can't work out where the line goes. Mine: walking home down a street with nobody on it and being certain — not frightened, certain, the way you're certain there's one more step down in the dark — that someone is about two metres behind me. I turn around, nobody. Afterwards I realised I couldn't have told you anything at all about them, not height, not footfalls, not a shadow, and yet the certainty was stronger than most things I actually see. Then there's my roommate, who feels her phone buzz against her thigh several times a day when it hasn't. Hers obviously does have a feel to it, a little tactile event she's misreading, and my instinct is that hers is a seperate animal — but all I can say for the difference is that hers has content and mine doesn't, which doesn't seem like enough to make them different kinds of state.

The reason I'm asking you and not just shrugging is that I've been reading around the argument from illusion lately, the one where the premise is that a hallucination you can't tell apart from a real perception has to share something with it, and the reply that says no, the fact that the subject can't tell them apart doesn't license you to read off a common ingredient, the two cases are just different kinds of thing. I can't figure out where my dad's experience goes on that map. It isn't a hallucination, since there's no sensory character to hallucinate; it isn't a belief, since he denies it and acts like it isn't so; it doesn't look like a hunch or an intuition either, because those seem to be about propositions and his is about the room. So — is there an established name for a state that carries the force and immediacy of perceiving, with no perceptual content and no downstream effect on belief or behaviour? I don't want just the label, honestly, I want the carving: what's the nearest thing to it that it gets confused with, and how is it not that; what would something have to be like to fall outside the category; and does a state like this make trouble for the whole good-case/bad-case way of slicing things up, because from where I'm sitting it looks like a third thing wedged between them. Could you write the reply as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables?
