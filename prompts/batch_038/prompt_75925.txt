For three decades, operational weather centres have stopped running their models once. The daily medium-range forecast at a major European centre is an ensemble: a single unperturbed control run plus fifty further runs, each started from a slightly different analysis of the current state of the atmosphere and each carrying small deliberate perturbations to the parameterisations of sub-grid processes. The product that reaches users is a probability — a thirty per cent chance of exceeding twenty-five millimetres at some station — computed, in the simplest case, as the fraction of members that exceed it. Verification units then ask whether those fractions are calibrated: whether the threshold was in fact crossed on roughly thirty per cent of the occasions when thirty per cent was announced, and whether the ensemble's spread tracks its error.

Climate projection uses the same output form for a very different object. There the ensemble is not fifty perturbations of one model but several dozen structurally different models, written by independent groups with different parameterisations and different grids, and the headline statement about end-of-century warming is a range across them — sometimes weighted, sometimes a flat average of the members.

Yet the same institutions state, plainly and to the public, that the detailed state of the atmosphere is not predictable more than about a fortnight ahead, because the governing equations are chaotic and small initial differences grow until they saturate; they will not issue a forecast for a particular day four months out, and they treat anyone who does as a charlatan. So over two weeks, determinism together with imperfect measurement is said to destroy predictability, while over eighty years a probability distribution over specific future states is offered as knowledge.

I want to know what this pair of practices presupposes.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory of predictability and of probability. For an ensemble fraction to be a probability in any ordinary sense, what must be true of the set of members — that they are exchangeable draws from some population? What is that population, given that nobody samples model perturbations at random, and the members are constructed by hand to be plausible rather than drawn? What must "spread" stand in for, in order for spread–skill calibration to mean anything at all?

Second, what must be assumed about determinism, and about where uncertainty resides — in the initial conditions, in the equations and their parameterisations, in the modeller's choices — for the two-week limit and the 2100 range to be true simultaneously? Say whether the pair is consistent, and if it is, what distinction consistency demands; then say whether that distinction genuinely does the work or merely restates the puzzle.

Third, which rival views does the practice quietly rule out: for instance, a view on which a deterministic chaotic system admits no objective probabilities whatever, or a view on which long-range projection is nothing but very long-range weather forecasting?

Fourth, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Which parts of it could a frequentist, a subjective Bayesian, and a propensity theorist each license, and which parts can none of them? Mark explicitly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would permit exactly what is done.

Fifth, tell me what would have to change — in what gets published, in how members are weighted, in what counts as a verified forecast, in how the fortnight limit is explained to a lay audience — if the implicit view were replaced by one on which ensemble spread is not a probability but a measure of disagreement among modellers.

Since you are reconstructing a theory nobody has stated, please end by marking where your reconstruction is most likely to fail: which of the claims above you would hold most loosely, and what argument or what fact about how these ensembles are actually built, weighted, and verified would push you to a different reading.
