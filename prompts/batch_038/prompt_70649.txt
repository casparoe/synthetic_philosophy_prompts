Our tiny research group has maybe two years and two people on the evidential cooperation in large worlds line of work — you know, the argument that even if you can't causally affect far-away or many other agents, evidential decision theory gives you reasons to cooperate with them because your choice is evidence about theirs, and that this gets stronger the more similar agents are out there making similar decisions. I want to pick where to spend the two years and I keep going in circles. Candidate targets: (a) showing the argument still works when you drop the idealization that agents know each other's decision procedures and source code, and instead face self-locating uncertainty about which of many near-identical agents they are; (b) figuring out whether the view needs the cooperators to be able to condition their policy on something like a shared Schelling point, and if so whether that requirement smuggles in the coordination it's supposed to explain; (c) whether bounded agents with limited reasoning budgets can even run the relevant similarity judgements, or whether the whole thing collapses once you're honest about compute; (d) working out what the view predicts for agents running different decision theories in the same population, i.e. whether it's stable against defectors rather than just internally coherent. Which of these is most upstream — in the sense that the others' answers depend on it — and which are most tractable with philosophy and a bit of toy modeling rather than serious technical machinery? Defend the ordering, tell me explicitly what you'd cut and why cutting it isn't just deferring it, and say what kind of finding in the first six months would make you reshuffle the whole thing.
