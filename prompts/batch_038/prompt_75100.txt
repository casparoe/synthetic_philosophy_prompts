I live in Kestrel Ridge, an unincorporated development of about nine hundred households governed by a homeowners' association. The association owns and maintains the roads, runs a shared beach and trail system, levies assessments, and enforces rules through fines; there is no municipal government over us, and the county's presence amounts to the sheriff and the tax assessor. Voting in association elections and on amendments to the bylaws belongs to owners of record. Roughly a third of the households are year-round renters who have no vote at all, and some three hundred owners live out of state and vote by proxy.

Last month a petition circulated to extend the franchise to any adult who has lived in the development for at least three years, regardless of ownership. At the information session two neighbors — call them A and B — started arguing about the petition and ended up arguing about who has the standing to decide who has standing. I have reconstructed their exchange as best I can, and I would like an assessment of it.

(1) A: The association makes rules binding on everyone here, fines people who break them, and there is no competing authority to appeal to; the renters are governed without representation. Any body that exercises that much power over people owes them a vote, on the plain principle that those subject to the rules should have a hand in making them.

(2) B: That principle belongs to towns and states, where you are born into the polity and cannot negotiate your way out. This is a private development. People bought in voluntarily, on terms recorded in the covenants, and those terms say owners vote. The renters chose to live under a regime they knew excluded them, and they can leave. Nothing is being done to them that they did not agree to, so there is no representation gap to fix.

(3) A: Consent cannot carry that weight. The covenants bind the land, not the person: someone who has rented here for eleven years answers to the same fines and the same beach and road rules as an owner and has exactly as much say as on the first day. And "they can leave" is not a reply, it is the objection. Exit excuses a regime only where exit is genuinely available, and moving a family, changing schools, and giving up a lease you could not replace at anything close to the same price is not free. If you have to leave in order to escape the rules, the rules are coercive — and coercion without a vote is the problem, not the solution.

(4) B: Then you have changed the subject. Your principle says the subject of the rules gets a vote. But who decides which rules, and which people, fall within the association's reach? That is itself a decision about the boundaries of the electorate, and it cannot be settled democratically, because any vote about who is inside has already assumed who is inside. The only body competent to draw that boundary is the one the covenants already constitute, namely the owners. If the members vote the franchise up or down, that is still a boundary being set by somebody; you cannot escape that by insisting it be set by everybody, since "everybody" is precisely what is in question.

(5) A: Right diagnosis, wrong conclusion. Yes — a demos cannot constitute itself by its own vote; every democratic procedure presupposes a prior unit whose composition is not itself decided democratically. But it does not follow that whoever is currently inside gets to decide unilaterally. It follows that the boundary must be justifiable to those outside it on grounds they could accept, and "you are out because we say so" is not such a ground. Where a rule cannot be justified to the people it binds, its claim on them is weakened, and that weakness is a reason to include them rather than to lock them out.

(6) B: Justifiable to whom, applied by what procedure? You have dropped democracy as the ground of the boundary and substituted a moral criterion, which means somebody has to apply it — in practice a board, or a lawyer, or a judge deciding who is really subjected. That is an unaccountable few determining the composition of the electorate, which is less democratic than letting the members vote, not more. And your criterion does not even deliver an answer. Subjection comes in degrees: the county has an interest in our road standards, the town downstream is affected by our stormwater rules, and widen the net far enough and you are enfranchising people who have never set foot here. To stop at three-year residents you need a threshold, and a threshold is a boundary decision, so we are back where we started.

(7) A: A criterion that admits of degrees is not thereby a failed criterion. The people at the far end of the scale — living here full time, paying rent that covers the assessment, fined under the rules — fall inside any line anyone could defend, and the downstream town sits at the far margin, outside any line anyone could defend. Imprecision at the edges does not manufacture a difficulty where the cases in front of us are clear.

(8) B: Then tell me what your criterion says about the three hundred owners who live out of state. They are bound by the assessments, they pay them, they are fined for violations when they visit. On your principle they are at least as subjected as the renters, so they stay in. They should stay in — but notice what that shows: your principle adds several hundred people with no daily life here while you describe yourself as enfranchising the governed. What you actually care about is residence and stake. Subjection is doing decorative work.

(9) A: I accept the consequence. Nonresident owners are subject and stay in; three-year residents are subject and come in. A criterion that also enfranchises people I would rather not add is likelier to be a real criterion than a rationalization. Meanwhile, note what your own view commits you to: if the association petitions to incorporate as a town, the franchise will be fixed by state law for everyone living inside the new lines, owners and renters alike, and you will call that legitimate. The difference between us is not principle. It is only that in the incorporated case somebody else is doing the excluding.

(10) B: In the incorporated case the boundary is set by a body whose own composition was settled by a prior body, and so on upward. That is not an escape from circularity, it is circularity with a longer chain. Your incorporation example proves my point.

They stopped there because the meeting was called to order. I am an owner in good standing and will cast a ballot at the annual meeting, but I came away unable to say which of them had the better of it, and I do not want to settle the question by noticing who landed the sharper line.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, judge whether it is clearly mistaken, weak, defensible but inconclusive, strong, or clearly right, and say briefly what makes it so; where a single move contains more than one distinct claim, separate the claims and rate them individually. Then answer three further questions: (a) which of A's or B's arguments survives the other's best reply, and which does not; (b) whether the disagreement is substantive, or partly an artifact of the two of them using "subject to the rules" and "consent" in different senses; and (c) what the most significant thing neither of them says is — in particular, whether either overlooks a way in which the association's status as a private, unincorporated body, rather than a government, changes the analysis.
