I am a third-year doctoral student in the history of political thought, and the instructor for our first-year seminar has been pulled away for two weeks, leaving me to run next Tuesday's seventy-five-minute session on the essays Mill left unfinished at his death — the ones on socialism that appeared posthumously, in the late 1870s I believe, prepared by his stepdaughter. The group is eleven students of very uneven preparation: four from philosophy who have read *On Liberty* carefully and want everything reduced to the harm principle, three from history who know the 1848 context better than the arguments, and two from public policy who will attempt to translate every sentence into a contemporary left/right coordinate. They have been assigned the essays, but realistically half will have skimmed, and the room has a whiteboard and a projector that has been out of order since Michaelmas. I should say why I want to be careful here: my own reading of Mill has shifted over the past year, and a colleague in my cohort has twice asserted in front of me that Mill simply "went over to socialism at the end of his life," a claim I found I could not rebut cleanly even though I am sure it is wrong. I am therefore acutely aware of the risk that I will spend the session defending a position rather than teaching one.

So please do not explain Mill's late views to me; I have read the text. What I need is a plan for the explanation. Tell me the order in which the components must be introduced and, for each step, what depends on having been established already — my own hunch is that nothing else can be built until students hold two things at once, namely that the schemes Mill is weighing are associationist rather than the state-owned, centrally administered arrangement they will picture, and that Mill already treats the existing arrangements of property as a human contrivance open to revision rather than as a natural right; if that is wrong, tell me what the real load-bearing first step is. Allocate the seventy-five minutes explicitly. Then tell me which single concrete case should carry the whole session: I am drawn to a workers' cooperative that owns its own workshop and shares the surplus, but I suspect the more honest carrier is one that forces a comparison between the socialist scheme at its best and the existing order at its best rather than at its worst, and I do not know how to construct such a case at the whiteboard in fifteen minutes. Say what that case must include, what it must exclude, and where in the arc it should be introduced and then revisited. I would also like the sequence of questions and objections the students will actually raise, in the order they will raise them, and how the plan should absorb each rather than field it.

Here are the misconceptions I expect, and I want them corrected and completed rather than confirmed: that these essays record a conversion; that "socialism" here means what it meant in 1917; that Mill's principal reservation is about incentives and would dissolve if people were more public-spirited; that the utilitarian calculus settles the question mechanically once the facts are in, when I think Mill's point is precisely that the relevant facts are not in; that the essays either contradict *On Liberty* or are fully continuous with it, with no interesting middle position; and, in both directions, over- and under-reading the fact that the text is unfinished and posthumous. For each, tell me at what point in the sequence it will surface, why it is tempting rather than merely careless, what the minimal move is that defuses it, and which of my six guesses is actually a misdescription of a genuine difficulty rather than a student error. Finally, say what I should refuse to explain at this level — I suspect the population argument and the whole reception history are candidates — and have me say out loud that I am leaving it out. Close with a final line beginning "Thesis:" followed by exactly one sentence, of thirty words or fewer, stating the single interpretive claim the session is engineered to leave the students holding.
