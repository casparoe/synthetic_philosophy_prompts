I am a high school junior preparing a twenty-minute talk for our school's science and philosophy society on why time has a direction, and the backbone of my talk will be a passage from a popular physics book I borrowed from the public library. I have transcribed the paragraph the chapter builds toward, leaving the author's name out because I want your assessment of the claims rather than of the reputation:

'At bottom, every law of physics works exactly as well forwards as backwards. Film two billiard balls colliding, run the film in reverse, and nothing in the picture tells you that you are watching it the wrong way round; the same is true of the equations governing the atoms in those balls. There is no arrow of time at the microscopic level at all. The arrow appears only through statistics. A disordered arrangement can be realized in vastly more ways than an ordered one, so a system left to itself will almost certainly wander from order into disorder. Boltzmann made this precise in the 1870s with his H-theorem, which derives the second law of thermodynamics from Newton's laws of motion. That is why eggs shatter but never reassemble, why cream disperses through coffee but never gathers back into a droplet, and why we remember yesterday but cannot remember tomorrow. It also tells us how the story ends: the universe will settle into a lukewarm, uniform soup in which nothing happens. The one thing this picture does not explain is the beginning. For all of the rest of it to work, the universe had to start in an extraordinarily ordered state, and nobody knows why it did.'

Most of this sounds right to me, and I especially like the last two sentences, because they leave the subject open rather than settled. That is precisely why I want to check them before I say them out loud to an audience. Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a physicist working on the foundations of statistical mechanics, or a philosopher of physics, would accept without complaint, what they would regard as a tolerable simplification for a general reader, and what they would consider actually wrong or misleading. Where something is wrong, please give the accurate version rather than simply noting that the passage overstates.

Three points in particular I would like you to settle. First, the passage says the H-theorem derives the second law from Newton's laws. I have read that this claim was disputed almost immediately, by people Boltzmann knew, and I would like to know what the dispute was, what assumption the derivation quietly leans on, and whether those objections are regarded as answered today. Second, the passage says the early universe was extraordinarily ordered, but it also says that uniformity is the disordered, high-entropy condition the universe is drifting toward — and I cannot see how both can be true, since the early universe was extremely uniform. Is that a real tension in the passage, or am I missing something about how entropy behaves when gravity matters? Third, does "we remember yesterday but cannot remember tomorrow" genuinely follow from counting arrangements, or does it require something extra that the passage has not stated?

If there are standard technical names for the assumptions or the objections you discuss, please give them, since I would rather use the right vocabulary than invent my own. But I cannot look anything up, so please do not send me to sources or list references; explain each term in a sentence or two rather than assuming I have met it before. Finish by telling me whether the last two sentences of the passage survive the corrections, and if they do not, what an honest version of them would be.
