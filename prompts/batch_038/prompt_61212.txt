I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy of social science, and the middle chapter of my dissertation concerns indicator-based governance: what happens to the epistemic status of a measure once an institution begins steering by it. My working framework is Goodhart's law in its original monetary-policy sense — Goodhart's claim that "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes" — rather than the slogan version popularized by Marilyn Strathern's 1997 article on audit in the British university system, on which a measure stops being a good measure the moment it becomes a target. I have also been working with the four-way decomposition of Goodhart effects into regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial variants, mostly because it obliges me to say which mechanism I think is operating instead of gesturing at perverse incentives. My advisor's standing objection is that this taxonomy was built for situations with a single optimizer and a legible objective function, and that it degenerates into post hoc labelling when applied to a bureaucracy, where the regulator is a stack of nested principals and the goal is at best a statute's purpose clause. A classmate in our measurement seminar makes the sharper version of the point: Atlanta, she says, was simply fraud under incentive misalignment, and calling it a Goodhart effect is a category error. I want to test both objections against a case rather than argue about them, and the case I keep returning to is the Atlanta Public Schools cheating scandal.

So that your analysis doesn't have to rest on anything you'd need to look up, here are the facts as I have them. Through the 2000s Atlanta was a national showcase for test-based accountability: among the largest urban gains in the country on the federal NAEP eighth-grade reading assessment, and a superintendent named national Superintendent of the Year in 2009. The district governed by targets — annual score targets set school by school, reviewed in meetings with principals, with evaluations, bonuses, and continued employment all understood to depend on meeting them. In 2009 the local newspaper published a statistical analysis of the state's Criterion-Referenced Competency Test showing that at a number of Atlanta schools the rate of wrong-to-right erasures on answer sheets was far outside the state norm. The district's own review was widely judged inadequate, the state took the investigation over, and its July 2011 report found improper testing practices at 44 of the 56 schools examined, implicated 178 educators in altering students' answers, and described a district culture of fear and intimidation in which teachers said they were pressed to meet targets and threatened with adverse evaluations or dismissal if they did not. Thirty-five educators were indicted; most took plea deals; twelve went to trial in what became the longest criminal trial in Georgia history, and in April 2015 eleven of the twelve were convicted of racketeering under the state RICO act. The former superintendent — the state report concluded she knew or should have known — was indicted in 2013 and died before her trial began. Two further features matter for my purposes. First, under No Child Left Behind the accountability metric was the percentage of students reaching proficiency, a status measure rather than a growth measure, and it is well documented that proficiency-rate accountability concentrates effort on students clustered just below the cut score while largely abandoning those far below it. Second, the policy response that spread afterward was to institutionalize erasure analysis as a standing detection statistic run over every school's answer sheets.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, theory illuminating episode and episode testing theory. Begin by fixing the mapping explicitly: at each level — the federal government setting proficiency accountability, the state, the district setting school-level targets, the principal, the classroom teacher — say who the regulator is, what the proxy is, what the goal is supposed to be, who the agent under selection is, and where the selection pressure actually bites; then tell me which of these levels the single-regulator taxonomy can represent and which it cannot, and what is lost in the flattening. Next, sort the mechanisms honestly: how much of Atlanta is regressional (score noise plus selection on extreme performers producing miracle schools), how much extremal (the regime change at the proficiency threshold), how much causal (test preparation and reclassification raising the measure without raising the learning it was meant to track), and how much straightforwardly adversarial (adults altering sheets); say which of these do real explanatory work and which are labels stretched over material they don't fit. Then confront the central question. Goodhart's law is about a regularity that collapses under pressure, so it presupposes there was a regularity to collapse: if the proficiency rate was never a valid measure of the learning Atlanta claimed to care about, the episode is not an instance of Goodhart's law at all but of prior mis-specification, or of Campbell's law, or of my classmate's plain fraud. Tell me what would have to be true of Atlanta for the case to count as a genuine confirmation of Goodhart's law, whether that could be known from the available evidence, and whether a law under which every such failure is filed as confirmation is doing any work at all. I'd also like the second-order layer treated seriously rather than as an aside: erasure analysis is itself a statistic now facing a population that knows it exists, so ask what the agents do once they know — erase more conservatively, coach students to mark firmly, simply stop correcting errors in front of them — whether that is a Goodhart effect aimed at the auditor, and whether the implication that detection must be kept secret is compatible with the fairness demands that a racketeering prosecution resting on that very evidence has to satisfy. Finally, take up responsibility. Goodhart's law in ordinary deployment seems to relocate blame from persons to instruments, the metric rather than the people who served it; the racketeering charges look like a deliberate refusal of that relocation. Say what the theory implies about how responsibility should be distributed among target-setters, culture-makers, and the teachers who erased, and then say plainly where the theory goes silent — on magnitude, on timing, on how far a proxy can be pushed before it breaks, on what to substitute for it, and on whether the target-setters were themselves optimizing in good faith. End with a verdict: is Atlanta a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated, and does the four-way taxonomy earn its keep here or would the same analysis be available without it? Please don't restate the history back to me, don't supply a reading list, and flag any place where the facts as I have stated them fail to fit the theory's requirements rather than smoothing them over.
