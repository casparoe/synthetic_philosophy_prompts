I'm a third-year PhD student in a philosophy-of-technology group and our faculty has this tiny "risky ideas" pot — five months, a few thousand euros, no teaching relief, basically enough to buy me out of one TA shift a week and print things. Applications are due Friday and they want two pages. I've got a half-formed idea I can't quite get into shape and I'd like you to turn it into the proposal.

The idea: the responsibility-gap debate about autonomous systems — the one that got going off the back of the autonomous-weapons arguments — seems stuck because everybody is arguing about whether "responsibility" is present or absent, as if it were one thing you either have or run out of. My hunch is that the thing people call responsibility is actually a bundle of distinct normative burdens, and they don't gap together. So split it: (i) desert of blame, (ii) liability to compensate, (iii) answerability in the narrow sense — owing someone an actual justification, which an entity can owe even when it's not blameworthy at all, and (iv) the standing/authority to sanction or to register a grievance. Then, for each burden separately, ask whether any of it is left unallocated after you've run every ordinary allocator we already have: culpable negligence upstream, product and strict liability, vicarious and organisational responsibility, the many-hands move, no-fault compensation schemes. My prediction, which may be wrong, is that compensation and answerability basically never gap because there's always an institutional backstop with a name and an address, whereas blame genuinely does — and then the interesting question is whether unallocated blame is actually a loss, or whether the blame-allocator was doing no practical work in these cases anyway and we've been mourning something empty.

So write the two pages. I want the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and I want it stated as the four questions rather than one. Then: why the existing positions leave it open — both the "there's a real gap so don't deploy" camp and the "no gap, tort law handles it" camp, and why neither side has an answer to the disaggregated version. Then the method, and here I have a specific thing in mind that I'd like you to develop and stress-test: a case battery, maybe ten to twelve vignettes of concrete autonomous-system harms, built to vary along three axes — how far the system's behaviour outruns its designers' foresight (including whether it learns after deployment), how many human hands are upstream, and whether a solvent institution is standing behind it — with each allocator run over each case and scored for residue per burden type. Sketch three of the vignettes in the proposal so the reader can see what the method actually produces. Say explicitly why this beats the two obvious alternatives, which are (a) going back down to the level of theories of responsibility and arguing about control conditions versus quality-of-will versus capacities, and (b) running an x-phi study on who ordinary people blame, since intuitions about blame don't tell you what the allocators do or should do. Then what a result would look like — I'm imagining the ledger table is itself the deliverable, plus a paper arguing the gap is real but confined to one of the four burdens — and what would count as the project failing. Two candidate failure modes I want you to address head on: the disaggregation not cutting the cases at the joints (everything comes out the same on all four dimensions, so the split buys nothing), and the residue turning out to be fully absorbable by mechanisms already on the books, which would collapse the whole thing into "this was a compensation debate all along and it's solved." Give me a month-by-month plan for the five months, and be honest about scope — tell me what I should cut if the battery turns out to be more work than I think, and whether a single person can realistically do the legal side (I have a friend in tort who owes me a favour, but that's one favour, not five months of consulting).

No bibliography or reading list, I'll add my own references — just feel free to talk about positions and arguments in prose. Also don't worry about polishing it, the committee is three people who read forty of these, so I care much more about the framing being sharp than about it being pretty.
