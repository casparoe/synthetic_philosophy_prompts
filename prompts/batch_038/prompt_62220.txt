In our social choice seminar we have been working through Sen's liberal paradox, and I have been testing my intuitions against a case I know from the inside: the informal constitution of the eight-person house I live in. Here are my verdicts.

1. Whether R keeps a devotional poster on the inside of his bedroom door, seen only by people he invites in: his call, even though the other seven of us would rather he did not. I would not let the house outvote him.
2. Whether the kitchen radio is on while I am cooking: my call, and here too I think the house's dislike should simply drop out of the reckoning.
3. If all eight of us, R included, came to prefer the poster down, the house should say so and R should take it down. I feel no tension with (1), but I want that explained rather than assumed.
4. R offers to take the poster down if the house pays him £20 from the communal fund, and everyone accepts. I think the resulting state is better than the one in which he keeps both poster and money, and that the house may make the offer. Yet it bothers me: it looks as though the house has simply bought a vote inside R's protected sphere.
5. Where the house's preference concerns only R's private conduct and nothing in its own situation — distaste for what he does behind his own door — I want to say that preference carries no weight at all. But then (1) describes a right beating something that never counted, and I am not sure what work the right is doing.
6. The house wants smoking banned everywhere on the property, including inside R's room, because the smell reaches the corridor. Here I say the house may decide, though the conduct is physically inside his protected sphere.
7. R wants his door left open all night; I want it shut because of the draught. Neither of us cares about the other's conduct except as it affects us. I think this should be settled by bargaining rather than by rights, and I cannot say what has changed since (1).

Is there a principle, ideally one I could state as a condition on a ranking of the house's possible states, that delivers all seven? Please state it precisely, say which verdict each clause is there to rescue, tell me what it implies for two further cases of your own choosing in the same setting, and be explicit about any verdict it cannot save and which of mine I ought to give up. In particular, I would like to know whether recasting our rights as a filter on the states we are permitted to choose from, rather than as decisive power over pairs within the ordering, genuinely escapes the paradox or merely relocates it.
