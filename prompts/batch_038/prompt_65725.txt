I came to al-Ghazālī late and by an unusual route: I spent my twenties in analytic philosophy, left the field without finishing the doctorate, and became a Muslim about eighteen months ago. My imam handed me the Deliverance from Error, and from there I went on to the Incoherence of the Philosophers, where I have been stuck ever since on the discussion of causation — Problem XVII in the Kamali English rendering, the one that begins by denying that the connection between what we take to be cause and effect is necessary. I can follow the argument. What I cannot yet do is hold it at the same time as the way I actually live, and my brother, who still works in philosophy of science, has made that harder: his verdict is that occasionalism is not a theory of causation but a refusal to have one, and that a man who says the fire does not burn the cotton has simply stopped doing philosophy. I have been asked to introduce next month's screening at the community-center film series I help run, and the film is The Truman Show. The more I think about the pairing, the less sure I am which of the two of us it will embarrass.

Here is what I want from you: a serious reading of the film through Ghazālī's occasionalism, of the kind I could turn into fifteen minutes of remarks for a room that is half curious Muslims and half secular film buffs, with no one in it a specialist in kalām. The temptation is obvious and I have already half succumbed to it. Seahaven looks like a world in which the regularities everyone trusts — the sunrise, the commute, the weather, the neighbor with the dog — are not natural at all but willed moment to moment by a hidden agent who can and does interrupt them: a studio light falls out of a clear sky and the car radio instantly explains it as debris from an aircraft in trouble; a hotel corridor elevator opens onto crewmen having lunch; the same man with flowers, the same cyclist and the same car cycle past Truman's house until he begins counting them; a nuclear-leak scare empties a street in seconds; a storm rages over one small boat and not over the water a hundred yards away. And the studio is rebuilt every night and switched on every morning, which seems to me a better picture of the Ashʿarite claim that the world is re-created at each instant and that no accident persists for two moments than anything I have found in the commentaries. But my worry is that this flatters the film and distorts the doctrine. Ghazālī's claim, as I read him, is not that some powerful being manipulates a genuinely causal order from behind a curtain; it is that there is no necessary connection in the order at all, so that when the cotton blackens at contact with the fire, the burning is created at that contact rather than by it — in the Kamali translation, observation shows only that one thing is with another, not that it is by it. Christof looks much less like that God than like an extraordinarily powerful engineer operating inside an ordinary causal world, which makes him a secondary cause among secondary causes, closer to what Ghazālī would call a jinn or a clever servant than to the only true agent. So: does the film dramatize occasionalism, or does it dramatize the puppet-master caricature that Ghazālī's opponents have always accused him of holding? I want you to take that question seriously rather than resolve it in the film's favor, and to say plainly which sequences support the reading and which ones break it.

The other thing I need is the reply from the far side. Averroes' Incoherence of the Incoherence charges that if the connection between cause and effect is not necessary, then knowledge of anything is impossible, since to know a thing is to know its cause. Read the film against that charge: does Truman, once he understands that the sequence of his days is a decision rather than a nature, remain able to act — he still sails, still plots, still deceives the cameras by stuffing a dummy in his bed — or does his discovery confirm Averroes by making the world uninhabitable, which is why the only move left is a door cut into a painted sky and a staircase leading out of the cosmos altogether? Is the ending a vindication of Ghazālī's insistence that the custom is stable enough to live by, or a quiet concession that a world whose regularities are someone's choices cannot be lived in from the inside? And I want you to tell me if what the film is really doing is a Humean point rather than a Ghazālian one — constant conjunction without necessary connection, an anxiety about the warrant for induction rather than a metaphysics of divine agency — and what is lost when an audience collapses the two, because Christof's line on the interview, that we accept the reality of the world with which we are presented, sounds to my ears like an epistemology of custom and testimony and not like a claim about causes at all. Please describe scenes rather than quote the screenplay, since neither of us will have the film in front of us, and don't send me a bibliography; I know there is a live scholarly dispute about whether Ghazālī is a strict occasionalist or allows a real derivative causality to creatures, and what I want is for you to flag the places where your reading only goes through on the strict version. End by telling me what, if anything, the film shows about this doctrine that the arguments in Problem XVII cannot show — because if it shows nothing, I would rather know that before I stand up in front of the room.
