I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on the political economy of food crises, and our department's student-run podcast has asked me to conduct the interview with next month's guest. She is an economic historian who works squarely within the entitlement tradition: she holds that starvation reflects a household's failure to command food rather than a shortfall in aggregate supply, and that famines end where political accountability operates. I would like a set of questions that engages the distinctive part of her position, not the well-worn summary of it.

Her distinctive claim, as I understand it from her recent articles: what actually prevents famine is not democracy as a regime type but what she calls an audit chain, a sequence of links running from a hungry household to an actor who stands to lose by ignoring it — an observer who notices the distress, a publisher who can report it without penalty, an intermediary whose position depends on that report travelling upward, and a decider who pays a price for inaction. Famines occur, she argues, when any link is severed; and she maintains that certain one-party states had intact internal chains, which she treats as the hard cases that the simpler claim about elections and presses cannot accommodate. She also insists that the entitlement vocabulary explains acute famine but should not be stretched to cover chronic undernutrition, and when critics answer her with aggregate crop statistics she tends to say that they are reading national accounts instead of household budgets.

Please draft ten questions, arranged in three rounds.

Round one, on the framework: what the chain adds beyond the standard accountability claim; how she tells the difference between a link that is severed and one that is merely weak; and whether the concept can be applied before the outcome is known, given that any failure can be redescribed after the fact as a broken link.

Round two, on cases. Bengal in 1943: wartime inflation around Calcutta, speculative hoarding, and an administration unwilling to declare a famine — which link failed, and how does she rule out that the supply shortfall was doing the real work? Ireland in the 1840s: a metropolitan state with regular elections, a free press, and Irish members sitting at Westminster, in which roughly a million people died. China from 1959 to 1961: an internal reporting apparatus that existed and then began reporting what superiors wished to hear. For both Ireland and China, give me her most probable reply and my follow-up to it. Include also the charge, now common in the economic history of famine, that several major famines involved genuine supply declines that her account understates.

Round three, on mechanism and limits: whether information without cost can do anything, given a press that reports and a decider who simply reads; what her chain predicts about authoritarian states that have avoided famine; why chronic undernutrition falls outside the framework when it looks like the same failure of command, only slower; and, finally, what evidence would persuade her that the chain concept does no explanatory work at all.

Constraints: every question must contain at least one concrete claim she has to accept or reject, so that none can be answered by restating her thesis; one question should concede something to her before pressing, since I am willing to grant that the aggregate food supply in Bengal in 1943 was not catastrophically low; no question about her career, her influences, or how she came to the subject; and keep each question to four sentences or fewer, since I have to read them aloud.
