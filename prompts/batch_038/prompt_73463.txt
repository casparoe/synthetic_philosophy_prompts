ok so I've landed on a view about insect welfare that I want stress-tested before I go around saying it out loud. Call it the numbers-first view. Roughly: I put something like a 1-in-100 chance on any given insect being phenomenally conscious at all. But there are on the order of 10^18–10^19 arthropods alive at any one time, so the expected count of sentient ones is still mind-boggling, and if even a modest share of wild insect lives are net-negative — which the reproductive strategy of the whole group suggests — then the total expected suffering swamps basically every other welfare cause, farmed animals included. Three commitments I want to be explicit about, beacuse most of the standard replies assume I don't hold them:

1. the 1% is a credence about a mind-independent fact (an insect either has some phenomenal character or it doesn't), not a report of conceptual vagueness;
2. aggregation is fine — I don't buy that spreading a fixed amount of harm across a trillion tiny subjects makes it stop mattering, and separateness-of-persons style objections don't move me here;
3. the "this is just Pascal's mugging / fanaticism" line doesn't bite, since the numbers are actual empirical estimates rather than something a stranger can inflate at will, and the stakes are real suffering rather than a threat.

What would count against this? I want the exposure map, not the two objections everyone already reaches for. Specifically: defeaters aimed at the 1% itself (is there an argument that the credence should be near zero rather than merely small, and what would it turn on — substrate requirements, taxonomic distance, the absence of any behavioural marker?); defeaters aimed at the multiplication step (including risk-weighted approaches to decision theory of the kind Buchak defends, where you discount low-probability/high-stakes payoffs instead of multiplying them out — does that route undermine me, or does it stop applying when there are that many independent chances?); defeaters aimed at my aggregation commitment; defeaters aimed at the valence question (I've assumed net-negative wild lives — what would the world have to look like for that to be false, and how much of my case survives if most insect lives are neutral, or just extremely short?); and the self-undermining one — does the same arithmatic force me to weight nematodes, or bacteria, and if I say no, is my cutoff principled or does it also cut off the insects?

Separately: I keep seeing empirical claims about bee cognition and fruit-fly nociception (pessimism-like judgment bias after stress, sensitization to noxious stimuli after injury, that sort of thing) and I honestly don't know how much weight they carry or whether I'm stating them too confidently. Flag where I've been sloppy.

And what does my view predict that the rival view — insects probably aren't sentient, and it wouldn't matter much anyway — doesn't? I want to know which concrete findings would count as failed predictions for me, because if there are none I'd rather hear that plainly.

I've pre-empted the fanaticism objection and the vagueness objection and the aggregation objection, so am I still risking anything — and would that even be a problem for a view of this kind, i.e. a practical, action-guiding credence claim rather than a metaphysical thesis?
