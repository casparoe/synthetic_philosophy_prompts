I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory working on the grounds of affluent states' obligations to the global poor, and last month I sat in on a workshop jointly hosted by our department and the university's development policy centre. In the discussion of a paper on unconditional cash transfers, an economist across the table observed that philosophers were still litigating a settled empirical matter, since aid creates dependency, and that this was precisely why the interesting question is not what the rich owe the poor but how the rich might stop harming them — trade access, capital flight, the terms on which extractive industries operate. He got a general nod. Nobody pushed back, partly because he had data and we had intuitions, and partly because several of us, I think, privately accept his conclusion while finding his route to it incoherent. I'm now drafting the pushback as a response piece, and I want to begin by getting clear on the slogan itself rather than on the literature around it.

Could you lay out the distinct and precise claims that this slogan might be expressing, as a menu rather than as a single favoured reading, ordered from the merely banal to the genuinely radical? For each entry I would like the level at which the claim is pitched (individual labour supply and savings behaviour; household risk-management and the decision not to self-insure; the fiscal relationship between a recipient government and its own citizens; macroeconomic structure and competitiveness; the moral psychology of agency, standing, and being a recipient rather than an author of one's circumstances); the causal mechanism it posits; what would have to be true for it to hold; whether, so far as you can tell, it is well supported, weakly supported, or actually contradicted by what is known; and whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Please also mark, for every reading, whether it is specific to a particular instrument — in-kind food aid, general budget support, project aid, direct cash to individuals, emergency relief — or whether it is asserted of transfers as such, since I suspect the slide between those two registers is where much of the slogan's rhetorical force comes from. And if there is a reading that is not economic at all but concerns the dignity or political standing of those who receive, treat it separately: I think it is the one that actually moved the room, and it is the one I am least equipped to evaluate.

The part I most need is the last. Tell me which of these readings, if true, would license the inference my interlocutor drew — from the slogan to the claim that the affluent do not owe the global poor aid, and that whatever obligations remain are negative ones concerning harm rather than rescue. Which readings get him all the way there; which get him only to a conclusion about aid design and conditionality rather than about obligation as such; and which, if true, would cut against him, for example by converting the debt from one of rescue into one of restitution, or by making the failure of one instrument evidence about our institutions rather than about our duties. I'm not asking you to adjudicate his overall position, and I'd rather you didn't survey the aid-effectiveness literature as such; what I want is the disambiguation, a verdict on each reading, and an honest note about which readings are doing unacknowledged work in policy conversation while the others do the arguing.
