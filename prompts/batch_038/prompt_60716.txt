I serve on a municipal advisory panel reviewing our property tax code, and the panel's de facto intellectual leader is a real-estate lawyer who opposes the proposed land-value tax. He returns every week to the same analogy, and he has become quite effective with it: distributive justice, he says, is a title search. No conveyancer examining a chain of deeds asks whether the resulting map of ownership looks fair; she asks only whether each link was a valid conveyance, and if the chain is clean the endpoint is just, however lopsided. The operative institutional question is therefore not what pattern holdings ought to display but whether the registry is well kept. He attaches two further claims that seem to be doing most of the work. First, that this makes his position administrable in a way no end-state theory is: it is a procedure a clerk could actually execute rather than a contested theory of the good. Second, that he is only restating Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument in the vocabulary of property law — liberty upsets patterns, so any patterned principle must be sustained by continuous interference with voluntary transfers, and a recurring levy on land value is precisely such interference.

Two features of real title practice trouble me, and I cannot yet tell whether they are fatal to the analogy or merely distasteful to me. One: chains are routinely cured. Limitation periods, adverse possession, and marketable-title legislation let a jurisdiction stop looking past a designated root deed, so a chain that reads clean today may rest on an earlier link everyone has agreed not to examine — and where to stop looking is a policy choice about the distribution of security, not a finding of fact. Two: a registry is kept and backed by an authority with power to invalidate and re-record, and its reliability depends on that authority doing considerably more than night-watchman work.

Please audit the analogy itself rather than the tax proposal. I would like the response organized under the following headings, in this order:

1. **The correspondence** (a table plus roughly 100 words): what in title practice plays the role of what in entitlement theory — the deed, the chain, the search, the clerk, the root deed, the curing rules, the registry itself.
2. **Disanalogies that bear weight** (about 350 words): the mismatches that actually threaten the two claims my colleague attaches to the analogy, especially the claim that a purely historical test is administrable and the claim that the analogy is the Chamberlain argument restated.
3. **Disanalogies that do not bear weight** (about 150 words): the mismatches that look like objections but that a defender can absorb without cost, and why.
4. **What the analogy smuggles in** (about 200 words): features of the source domain that the argument depends on and that the target domain may not supply — including whatever the analogy assumes about the first link in a chain, and about who is entitled to declare a chain closed.
5. **Verdict** (about 200 words): whether each of his two attached claims survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a Nozickian could still legitimately say in defense of the comparison.
6. **A better source domain** (about 150 words): if the title search misleads, what comparison would carry the historical-principle idea without importing the extra machinery.
7. **Confidence and the weakest link** (about 120 words): how confident you are in the main claims of sections 2, 4, and 5, and the single point in your analysis you are least sure of, with a note on what would settle it.

Please treat my two worries as hypotheses to be tested rather than as conclusions I have already reached; if either turns out to be harmless, say so plainly.
