I work on interpretability tooling for large language models — activation probes, sparse autoencoders, circuit analysis — and I have no formal philosophy training beyond one undergraduate survey course a decade ago. I am not asking for a bibliography or for references I would have no way of checking. I am asking for an audit of a text I read, because I can tell that it is confident and well-argued but I cannot tell whether it is correct.

Last week a widely shared essay by a prominent technology commentator (not an academic) argued that Wittgenstein's private language argument shows machine understanding to be impossible rather than merely unverified. I have reconstructed the relevant stretch below as faithfully as I can. The wording is mine, but each claim is his and the order is his.

> Wittgenstein's target is a picture of meaning he wants to destroy: the idea that a word means what it does in virtue of standing for something in the speaker's head — an idea, an image, an inner object — with public language serving as a translation layer between one person's private stock of meanings and another's. Against this he holds that the meaning of a word lies in how the word is used, that use is a practice, and that a practice requires standards of correctness that are not set by the individual practitioner alone. It follows that a word means something only if others can in principle check whether it is being applied correctly.
>
> The test case is the diary in Philosophical Investigations §258. A man decides to keep a record of a certain recurring sensation, one nobody else could ever observe in him, and marks it with the letter S each time it occurs. He cannot define S in public terms and cannot point to it for anyone; he can only concentrate his attention on the sensation and impress on himself that this is S. The difficulty, Wittgenstein says, is that the diarist has no way of knowing whether he remembers the sensation correctly. Today's S need not be the same sensation as last week's S, and neither he nor anyone else can find out whether it is. So S does not mean anything.
>
> The conclusion generalizes. If not even a single sensation-word can be given a private meaning, there is no inner theatre whose contents give our public words their point. To be in pain is nothing over and above to behave pain-wise; the inner drops out of the account as irrelevant. Note that this is not scepticism about other minds but a grammatical point: it is not that we cannot know what is going on in someone else, it is that the question was malformed.
>
> Which settles the matter of large language models. A model's internal representations are private in exactly the sense at issue: no one else can observe them, and the model itself has no independent way of checking whether its report of its own states is accurate. By the diary argument, its internal symbols mean nothing, and whatever competence it displays is an artefact of its outputs having been fitted to our public language from the outside. Call a system Wittgenstein-private when a third party could not learn what its internal states mean except on the strength of the system's own say-so. Every current model is Wittgenstein-private. Machine understanding is therefore not an open empirical question waiting on better tests; it has been closed by an argument about meaning itself.

Some of this I find persuasive and some of it makes me uneasy, and I cannot separate the two. Specifically:

1. Go through the reconstruction claim by claim and sort each one into what a careful scholar of the Investigations would endorse, what is a genuine but contested reading being presented as settled, and what is simply mistaken. I would rather have the contested items named as contested than smoothed over.

2. On the diary passage: he explains the failure as the diarist's inability to be sure his memory is right. I have read elsewhere that this misses the point, and that the real difficulty is not that the diarist might be getting it wrong but that there is no such thing as his getting it wrong here. I cannot judge whether that is a substantive distinction or a pedant's quibble, and whether it matters for what he goes on to say.

3. I suspect his opening definition quietly does the work for the whole essay. He seems to characterize a private language as one nobody else happens to understand, but I would have thought the last surviving speaker of a dying language is an obvious counterexample to that gloss, since her words are still in principle learnable. Is my objection sound? If the definition has to be repaired, does anything downstream change?

4. Is the behaviourist upshot fair to Wittgenstein, or does it flatten something the essayist needs later? He seems to want both the strong claim that the mental is nothing over and above behaviour and the weaker claim that this is merely a point about how we talk.

5. On the machine-learning part, I am inclined to agree with him, though for reasons of my own rather than his: models' self-reports about their internals are demonstrably unreliable in our measurements, and no human can read a several-thousand-dimensional activation vector. Tell me plainly whether those engineering facts carry any weight in the argument he is running, or whether I am trading on two different senses of "private" — and note that his coinage makes the criterion turn on what a third party can learn, whereas the diarist's predicament seems to be about the diarist himself.

6. Does his final conclusion survive the corrections? If it does not, is there a weaker conclusion about model understanding that does follow from the accurate parts of the summary — and does it follow, or merely stay consistent with them?

Please commit to a verdict on each item rather than telling me that everything depends on which interpretation one adopts. Where the answer genuinely does depend on a reading, say which reading and what turns on it.
