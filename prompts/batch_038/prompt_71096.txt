so I'm in a seminar this term on population ethics and existential risk, and my prof has this thing where before each unit everyone has to post their actual views as a numbered list so the seminar can attack them — no hedging allowed, and you're not supposed to signal which ones you're least sure about. This week its the simulation argument and whether it should change how much weight we put on the far future. Here's my list, honestly held:

(1) I think Bostrom's trilemma basically works, and I put something like a third of my credence on us being in an ancestor simulation run by some posthuman civilisation. Not 50/50, but way above "basically zero."
(2) I'm a total-view longtermist. The reason I think existential-risk work matters is the sheer number of future people, and I'm comfortable letting astronomical numbers dominate — "big numbers feel scary" isn't a refutation.
(3) But I think the simulation hypothesis actually *cools* that down. If we're in a sim, our universe probably gets switched off or pruned long before the heat-death-timescale picture that longtermists use, whether for cost or safety or because the experimenters got what they wanted. So (1) is a reason to be less fanatical about the far future, not more.
(4) Simulated people are fully morally real to me. If you're simulated you still feel things, and I dont think being made of computations downgrades your welfare at all.
(5) I refuse to be moved by tiny probabilities of gigantic payoffs. Pascal's mugging gets no traction with me, and neither do "one in a million shot at 10^30 lives" pitches.
(6) I don't think there's anything we could do about the simulators. I put no weight on trying to be interesting or well-behaved enough that whoever's running us keeps us going.
(7) And I don't think the fact that we haven't been switched off yet is evidence about how long we have left. Any long-running sim spends nearly all of its existence in the not-yet-terminated state, so my being here now doesn't tell me much about when it ends.

Can you audit this for me? I don't want a lecture on whether longtermism is overrated in general — I want to know whether *these seven* hang together. Some of them feel like they're pulling against each other and I can't tell which pairs are genuinely in conflict versus which just look weird sitting next to each other, and I really don't want to walk into seminar and have someone find the inconsistency before I do. Please go in this order: "Bottom line" (3-4 sentences — is the set tenable, and if not, which commitment is the load-bearing one), "Real tensions" (one subsection per tension, worst first, roughly 200 words each: what exactly conflicts with what, why it's a substantive conflict rather than a stylistic one, how severe it is, and the cheapest revision plus what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list), "False alarms" (the pairs that look inconsistent and aren't, ~100 words each, with the argument that dissolves them), "The one change that buys the most" (~150 words — which single revision would clean up the largest share of the mess and what I'd be giving up), and "Where you had to assume things" (~100 words on any place my numbers or my vagueness did the work, such that a different filling-in would flip your verdict). Also, if two of my commitments are only in tension on one particular reading of one of them — like, whether a claim is about value in general or about my own causal reach — say which reading generates the tension and what happens on the other one.
