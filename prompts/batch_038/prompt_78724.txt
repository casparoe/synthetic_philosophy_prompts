I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine — mostly books, the occasional lab story, and whatever weird slot nobody else wants. This month the obituaries page has a hole in it and my editor, who's decided we should be the kind of magazine that obits concepts as well as people, has assigned me 700 words on the death of the truth value of the English indicative conditional. Cause of death: the triviality results, with the collapse theorem as an underlying condition.

Write it for me, completely straight-faced, in full house-obituary register: headline, a one-line standfirst, then the body — date and place of death, the long illness, career highlights, survivors, predeceased by, quotes from grieving colleagues, memorial arrangements. Invented names for everyone quoted; no real philosopher gets a line of dialogue, and no paper titles, dates, or citations anywhere (my fact checker has other fish to fry and I don't want anyone guessing at bibliography).

The jokes have to come from getting the material right, not from puns about philosophy, so build them out of things the literature actually turns on:

- the Ramsey test as the deceased's dazzling early promise, and what it cost him
- Adams's thesis surviving the death in a reduced form — a rule about when you may assert, or how much credence to give, rather than about what is true — plus one survivor who bitterly disputes whether that counts as surviving at all
- the embedding problem as the complaint he never shook: fine standing on his own, useless inside a negation, a disjunction, or another conditional, and the family's long embarrassment about it
- the subjunctive conditional as the hale, unfashionable sibling now being asked about the estate, together with the awkward fact that the two seem to share truth conditions without being interchangeable — use the familiar Kennedy-assassination pair, one indicative and one subjunctive, but describe it rather than quoting it
- a paragraph where two obituarists argue over what goes on the certificate: never had truth conditions, versus had them but not classical ones, versus was never one thing to begin with and the singular obituary is itself a category error

Name the triviality results and the collapse theorem in prose, as diagnoses, never as references.

I also want it to do one serious thing without ever saying so. Under the furniture, a reader who knows this material should come away feeling the live problem: if these sentences aren't in the truth business, what are we actually doing when we contradict each other with them, nest them, or reason from them? Pick a side quietly — someone in the know should be able to tell which side I'm on from what I chose to be tender about — and don't wink, not once.

Keep the genre in your sights too: the pieties of the form (he is survived by, colleagues remember him as, in lieu of flowers) applied to a linguistic expression is funny on its own account. But don't let it decay into a list of gags; it should read like something that genuinely ran, flat and respectful, with the odd bathetic detail. 650–750 words.
