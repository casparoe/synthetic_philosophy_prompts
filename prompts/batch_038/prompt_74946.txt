Ok so I'm working through Hume's essay Of Money from the 1752 essay collection on my own, not for a class, just interest, and I've hit a wall partway in. I'm paraphrasing rather than typing the actual sentences out because I'm reading a scan on my phone in bed, so please tell me if my paraphrase has mangled something important. The bit that's got me is the sequence where he first imagines what would happen if all the coin in Britain were multiplied fivefold overnight, and conversely if all the money were annihilated overnight, and concludes that in neither case would anybody be a penny richer or poorer, that the real stock of labour, goods and shipping wouldn't change at all, that money is really just a representation, a convention people have agreed on for exchanging one commodity for another, more like the oil that makes the wheels of trade turn smoothly than one of the wheels itself. Then a few paragraphs later he swings round and says that nevertheless the good policy of the magistrate is to keep the quantity of money increasing if he possibly can, because that keeps a spirit of industry alive in the nation, and the mechanism he gives is something like: while the money is still coming in and prices haven't caught up, the extra coin gets into the hands of the enterprising sorts, merchants and farmers and manufacturers, who then work harder and employ more labourers, and the community's real gain happens in that interval before the general dearness overtakes the new money.

What I can't work out is what's actually bearing the weight in that second part. I've got two readings and I can't choose. Either he's leaning on people being systematically fooled, i.e. labourers don't notice their real wage slipping while the price of what they buy rises faster than what they're paid, so the industriousness is just money illusion doing the work; or he's making a purely distributional point, that a slow inflow shifts relative prices and moves purchasing power from creditors and people on fixed incomes toward producers and debtors, and it's the transfer that gets output moving rather than anybody's mistake. Which one sits better with the rest of the essay? My worry is that the error reading looks bad against the representation metaphor, since he's also explicit that things becoming dear merely because money is plentiful is a disadvantage to a trading nation, and if money is just oil you'd think fooling people about the amount of oil couldn't produce anything real.

Second thing, and this is the one I most want sorted out. If the fivefold thought experiment genuinely establishes that the quantity is neutral for domestic happiness, isn't the keep-it-increasing advice in flat tension with it? Or is he restricting the neutrality claim to a comparison between two settled end points, so that the recommendation rests entirely on the transitional path — which would mean the policy only pays off as long as the inflow never stops, and he doesn't think it can go on forever, since later in the same essay he expects commerce eventually to stagnate once the poorer nations catch up on prices. Tell me whether that tension is real or whether I've got the scope of the thought experiment wrong. For what it's worth my own guess is that this is an early version of what later people called the Cantillon effect, but I honestly don't know whether that's anachronistic or whether Hume would have rejected the comparison, so push back on that hard if it's wrong.
