I teach a junior-level seminar called Language & Mind, 15 students, one 75-minute block a week, and the linguistic relativity session is coming up in a couple weeks. The room has a document camera, a whiteboard, and basically no budget beyond printing and index cards. About half the section are psych majors who've already decided Whorf was debunked decades ago, and the other half are convinced their bilingual cousin literally perceives the world differently. Every year I lecture at them about the strong vs. weak versions and about how the modern evidence is mostly small, fragile lab effects, and every year I can watch it slide right off. So this year I want them to run into the thing themselves instead of hearing me describe it.

What I want is an activity where you split the class into two groups, hand each group a different artificial mini-language (you build them, with a one-page grammar handout each), and then give both groups the *same* non-linguistic task — and the design should be arranged so that they hit, on their own, the difference between an effect that only shows up while you're actually formulating an utterance (Slobin's thinking-for-speaking idea) and an effect that survives when language is knocked out or made irrelevant. Ideally the knockout is something they do to themselves rather than something I announce — an articulatory suppression condition, a speeded condition with no time to covertly label, a recognition-instead-of-recall version of the task, whatever you think is cleanest. Please don't build it on color terms and please don't build it on absolute vs. relative spatial frames of reference. Those are the first two things they'll find online and honestly they're the two I have the least left to say about. Pick some other gramatical domain — obligatory evidentials? a language whose causative system won't let you mark an accident as agent-caused? a tenseless language? no generic sentences? something better I haven't thought of — and tell me why that particular domain is one where an online-only effect and a durable effect would genuinely come apart, so the two conditions aren't just decoration.

Concretely I need: the two mini-languages spelled out far enough that I could hand them out tonight without inventing anything, including how much of the 75 minutes the students get to learn them and how you make sure both groups learn theirs to the same depth; the task or tasks, with materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in 75 including setup and debrief; the debrief script mapping specific moments in the activity onto the strong/weak distinction and onto whatever you're using as the interference control; an honest plan for what I do if the group difference doesn't show up at all, which with n=15 I assume is the likely outcome, so that the null result becomes the lesson rather than a busted class; and the standard list of what predictably goes wrong the first time you run something like this and how to head it off. Also be straight with me about which feature of linguistic relativity the activity simply cannot reproduce, that I'm going to have to supply by talking — I'd much rather know going in than discover it in the room.

Start with your bottom line in the very first sentence: the one thing the activity is engineered to make them feel. Then give me the rest of it, with no preamble and no restating my question back at me. American English, please.
