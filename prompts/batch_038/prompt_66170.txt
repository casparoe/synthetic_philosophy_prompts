For two years I have been facilitating a free Thursday-evening reading group at our public library. We are a mixed bunch: a retired nurse, two schoolteachers, a couple of university students, and several people who simply enjoy the argument. This season we have been reading the Mencius and the Xunzi in translation. I have no academic training in any of this; I read the same translations the group reads and very little else, so I am in no position to judge how well anyone has understood the texts.

Last month I set the group a short written exercise, and one member has asked me to mark it. I would like you to act as the grader.

The exercise question was:

"Mencius holds that human nature is good; Xunzi holds that it is bad. How far is this a genuine disagreement about human beings, and how far is it a disagreement about what the word nature is being made to mean? Say what, if anything, turns on the answer for the way a person ought to cultivate themselves."

The submitted answer ran as follows.

They agree about far more than the slogans suggest. Both work inside one tradition, both take ritual and the old sage-kings seriously, both think the point of the enterprise is to become a certain kind of person rather than to be restrained from outside, and both think almost anyone can be cultivated into something.

The difference lies in what they mean by nature. Mencius seems to use the word for what is distinctively human, the part that sets us apart from other animals, and on his account that part consists of beginnings: a feeling that cannot bear the suffering of others, a sense of shame, a tendency to give way to others, and a sense of when something is or is not right. He calls these sprouts, and sprouts grow. Left alone and undamaged they develop in a fixed direction, the way a plant develops, so calling nature good amounts to a claim about where our growth is headed. That is what his mountain is for: the trees have been hacked away and grazed down until the slopes look bare, but bareness was never the mountain's own condition, and the same holds for a man who has turned cruel.

Xunzi uses the word for the whole of what one is born with rather than taught — appetite for food, liking for sound and colour, wanting to get ahead, resentment — and observes that if that material is left to run by itself the result is contention and disorder. Nature in his sense is therefore bad, and whatever is good in a person has been put there afterwards, by teachers, study, practice, and the standards the sages worked out and handed down. His image is crooked timber that must be steamed against a frame before it will hold a straight line.

A good part of the quarrel thus looks verbal. Fix the word to mean the capacities that make us human and the verdict comes out good; fix it to mean the untrained inheritance we share with beasts and the verdict comes out bad. They are describing one person from opposite ends.

It is not purely verbal, though, because they disagree about what then has to be done. On Mencius's picture the sprouts only need to be sheltered and given room, so cultivation is largely a matter of not losing what is already there; on Xunzi's, the raw material has to be got rid of and replaced. That is why Xunzi's cultivated person is one in whom the appetites have at last been worn away rather than redirected, and why Mencius's cultivated person still feels along with other people, only across a wider circle. It also explains their different attitudes to authority: if the standard is already inside you, you can afford to say that a ruler who has lost it may be set aside, whereas if goodness only ever arrives from the tradition, the teachers who carry it become much harder to argue with.

Please give the answer a mark out of ten, and then justify that mark claim by claim. Work through the answer in order, saying which of its statements about the two thinkers hold up and which do not, flagging anything that has been attributed to the wrong person, anything that does not follow from what precedes it, and anything that is true but overstated; and identify any substantial part of the question the writer has left untouched. I want the reasoning behind the mark rather than an overall impression, and I would like it to end with a short list of the specific comments I can hand back to the writer.
