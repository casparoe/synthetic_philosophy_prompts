Okay, so — I write a loose, twice-a-month column about science and ideas for a US magazine, and this month I'm doing a piece on why consciousness researchers keep fighting about *timing*: experiments where people report seeing something change at a moment when the relevant information couldn't possibly have been available yet. My editor wants to hang a sidebar off it about Dennett and the whole "there's no screen in your head" argument, and he's floating the headline "The Philosopher Who Thinks Your Consciousness Is an Illusion." Before I spend political capital fighting him on it, I want to know if it's actually fair.

Problem is I read the book once in college and retain approximately nothing. My friend R, who reads way more philosophy then I do, sent me this as a sanity check:

"Dennett's point is that everybody pictures a little theater in the head — a place where all the senses come together and get presented to someone who's watching. He says there's no such place, and that looking for it in the brain is a mistake. What's actually happening is the brain churns out a bunch of parallel drafts of what's going on and keeps revising them, so there's no single moment where the lights come on; a bit of content becomes conscious once it's settled enough to be reported. His cleanest example is the two-lights illusion: a red light flashes here, a green one flashes there, and you see a single dot travel between them and change color partway across — except the second light hasn't even come on yet at the point where you seem to see the change, so the brain can't be relaying what happened, it has to be revising the record after the fact. Which is why I'd say his view is basically that consciousness is an illusion — there's no one home having the experiences, just machinery for reporting them."

So: how much of that holds up? Go through it roughly line by line and tell me what a Dennett-sympathetic philosopher of mind would nod at, what they'd call a simplification that's fine for a magazine, and what they'd insist actually gets the view backwards. Three bits I especially want pinned down. One: is "a content becomes conscious once it's settled enough to be reported" a fair gloss, or does it quietly put back the thing he's arguing against — a finish line, a moment of arrival? Two: does the two-lights case really force you into the "brain revises the record afterward" reading, or is that one option among several? I have a hazy memory of him running some Stalin/Orwell bit around there and R's paragraph never mentions it, which makes me think something load-bearing got dropped. Three: is "no one home having the experiences" Dennett, or the internet's version of Dennett?

Then the practical part: does the headline survive the corrections? If it doesn't, give me the strongest accurate one-liner I could actually run — something with a little punch, not just "he denies the intuitive picture of consciousness."

Write it in American English please (my copy desk is allergic to Britishisms), and don't bother with citations or telling me what to read next — I'm not after a bibliography, just the argument.
