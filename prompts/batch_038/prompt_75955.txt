Acting role first: you're a literary writer who also spent a few years working inside an institutional archive, so you know what accessioning, finding aids, controlled vocabularies and a mass-digitization push actually feel like from the inside — including the tedious parts, which is where I want this to live. Think carefully before you write anything. Give me a few bullet points of brainstorming first (what the story is really about, what the last image is), then the story.

Assignment: a short story, 1500–2500 words, about how archives manufacture silence. Not the dramatic kind where a regime burns the records — the quiet adminstrative kind, where the record gets made badly by nobody in particular and no one has done anything wrong.

Use this machinery (invent your own names, keep the mechanics): a regional public archive is three years into a mass digitization project. Scans go through an automated pipeline — deskew, binarize, OCR, confidence threshold — and anything the pipeline can't resolve as text gets sorted into a noise class and never makes it into the searchable text layer, which is the layer researchers actually use. The project's own QC reports a capture rate somewhere in the high 99s, and that number isn't a lie, because it's measured against the text the system already knows how to read. The collection on the belt is the administrative paperwork of a closed state residential institution. The one thing that shows up everywhere in it is faint pencil marginalia in the margins of typed reports — the only first-person material in the entire fonds, and exactly what the pipeline reliably discards as ghosting or artefact.

Your protagonist is a temp operating the scanner, on a contract that ends in six weeks. She's good at the job and mildly bored by it. Let her notice the drop rate as a technical annoyance before she notices it as anything else.

Things I want in there: at least two intercut documents — a QC log excerpt, an email thread, a line from the subject-heading list, whatever feels right — and one scene where a visiting researcher says something confident and reasonable about what the sources' silence tells us, and nobody corrects him. Ending: the loss does not get repaired. No whistleblower arc, no emergency grant. Something smaller and worse — either she can't file her own note because there's no authorized heading that would cover it, or she starts keeping a private parallel record that no future researcher will ever be able to cite as evidence. Pick one.

Craft notes: keep it concrete and sensory, the hum of the machine, the dust, the light table. Don't lecture me and don't name any theorists or cite anything, I want the argument buried in the work. Also skip the obvious twist where the marginalia turns out to be some code or confession. Give it a title.
