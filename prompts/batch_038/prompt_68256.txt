So my little brother Danny leaves tomorrow morning for a two-year formation program at a monastery upstate. He's 26, he was the most aggressively annoying atheist at our Thanksgiving table for like eight years, and then something happened on a hike in 2023 that he's never fully explained, and now this. My parents are throwing a goodbye dinner tonight, about 15 people: my parents, me and my two sisters, four of his old college friends (one of whom is convinced this is a cult and is coming mostly to size it up), and — this is the part I keep circling — two of the monks drove down with him and will be at the table. They're cheerful and totally normal, which is somehow worse for my stomach.

Ive been asked to give the toast. Standing up, glass in hand, call it four minutes.

The reason I need help is a conversation Danny and I had last week. I said the scared version of my actual worry: that he'll come back a different person and we'll have lost him. And he said — he'd been reading something about this, he had a term for it, reflection — that if the future Danny disagrees with me about whether future Danny is okay, that isn't a standoff between two people, it's evidence. His line was that the guy who comes out of this will know things about it that I can't possibly know from out here, so I should be deciding *now* to take his word for it later; he said he's going in expecting to be outvoted by himself, and he seemed pretty pleased about that. He also volunteered a limit, which surprised me: he allowed that if they were going to keep him up for three nights straight and just feed him a line, then whatever future Danny believed wouldn't be evidence about anything, it'd be the output of the very thing under evaluation. He's confident that's not what's happening. Obviously he is.

That's where I'm stuck, and it's why the toast has to actually do something rather than just be warm. If I stand up and say we'll trust whatever he comes back as, I'm committing my mother — 68, cried twice this week — to deferring indefinitely to a person none of us have met, on the say-so of a process none of us can inspect. And there's a circularity in there I can't get clean words for: future Danny is not a neutral witness on the question of whether the thing that made him was sound. But if I say the reverse — write down what you believe tonight and we're holding you to it — then I'm doing that in a house with two actual monks, treating them to their faces as diminished versions of themselves, and treating my brother's next two years as not counting.

So write the toast. It should land somewhere better than splitting the difference: some honest, sayable line between what a family can agree in advance to be outvoted about and what it can't. It needs to sound like a person who loves him rather than a person who has thought about it for a week, even though I have. No jargon, don't name the principle, and don't quote anything religious — half the room would take that as our side caving, and the monks don't need me borrowing their material. One joke, and it can't be at his expense or theirs. And bury two things in it: one that Danny will still be turning over in eighteen months when he's scrubbing a floor at 5am, and one that lets my mother breathe.
