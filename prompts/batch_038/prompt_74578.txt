Our philosophy department is co-hosting an evening with the engineering school on the theme of explanation across levels — reduction, emergence, and the question of when an account is too cheap to be acceptable — and I have been asked to contribute a short comic piece for the program booklet that the audience can read while the speakers are setting up. Most of the room will have encountered Dennett's crane-and-skyhook vocabulary, but few will have thought hard about what it commits one to, and I want the piece to do that thinking under the cover of a joke. What I would like is a parody of a municipal building-inspection report: a denial of a certificate of occupancy for a high-rise whose contractor claims to have raised the upper floors using lifting gear that was never anchored to anything already standing. Please write it in the deadpan voice of an official form, roughly 700 words, with a file header (parcel number, address, permit type, inspector, date), a numbered schedule of findings each tied to an invented but plausible code citation, a disposition, and, appended at the end, the contractor's brief request for a variance and the inspector's refusal of it. The contractor and the jurisdiction should both be invented; please do not name any real firm, code, city, or living person.

The humor has to come from getting the philosophy right rather than from bureaucratic absurdity for its own sake, so I would ask you to build three findings out of the actual structure of the distinction. First: lifting equipment is lawful precisely when it stands on a foundation that has already been built and is itself assembled out of materials delivered by earlier and smaller equipment — the inspector should approve several such devices without comment and clearly regard them as unremarkable, even essential. Second: a device whose anchor point is a floor that does not yet exist is not a defect of workmanship but a category error in the submission, since the load path terminates in nothing; the inspector should note, without heat, that the elevation drawings look magnificent and that this is not the issue. Third: the contractor has deleted the intermediate transfer levels to save money and insists the ground slab alone can carry the penthouse, which the report should treat as a violation not because the penthouse is impossible but because levels the code requires to be present and load-bearing have been assumed away. If you can, work in a fourth point about the contractor having specified a rigging supervisor capable of directing the lift as a condition of the works rather than a product of it, and the code's requirement running the other way. One further touch I would like: the report should acknowledge, in a single flat sentence, that the slab itself was certified in an earlier phase under an earlier inspector and that the present officer has no authority to reopen that determination — the regress stops somewhere unglamorous, and nobody finds this remarkable.

Two constraints on execution. The word crane may appear only in its literal construction sense — tower crane, mobile crane, crane operator — and the terms skyhook and greedy reductionism should not appear at all, nor should any philosopher's name; the reader who knows the material is meant to supply the pun and the diagnosis, and the piece should never explain itself or wink. Second, the engineering talk should hold together as engineering talk: load paths, anchorage, transfer levels, phase certificates, and inspection dispositions should be used consistently enough that a structural engineer in the audience enjoys it rather than winces, while a reader who has never heard of design space still follows a petty official denying a permit to a grandiose builder. Keep the inspector sympathetic and procedurally correct — the target is the contractor's style of justification, not the inspector, and not any real profession.
