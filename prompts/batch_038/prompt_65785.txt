I convene an informal reading group on how comparisons between whole societies are made and defended. I am not an academic and have come to the subject on my own, so before I return one member's answer with comments I would like an independent assessment of it.

The question I set was: People often say that Sweden and Norway differ in how liberal they are. Explain what would have to be the case for one of them to be more liberal than the other, and say what evidence would settle it.

The member's answer runs as follows.

"'Liberal' is doing two different jobs in that sentence, and the first task is to prise them apart. In one sense it names a package of permissive attitudes — towards immigration, gender roles, sexuality, drugs, and the state's claim to regulate private conduct. In the other it names economic liberalism in the older sense: low taxes, light regulation, open markets. The two countries do not line up the same way on both. Attitudinally the Swedes are usually presented as the more permissive; on the economic side, Norway's extensive state ownership of its natural resources and its sovereign wealth fund pull the other way. So the honest answer to the first half of the question is that there is no such thing as being more liberal simpliciter. The claim has a truth-value only once a sense is supplied, and once the sense is supplied it says something modest: that one population's average answers to a set of questions sit further in a given direction than another's. Most people who argue about which of the two is really more liberal are arguing past one another, and it is worth saying so plainly.

As for what would settle it: on a given sense, the comparison of the same items put to comparable samples in both countries. The standard instrument is a left–right self-placement question, and the natural procedure is to take each country's mean, set the two means side by side, and let the larger win. A mean is a mean; it is not thrown about by the handful of respondents at the extremes, and if the samples are large and properly representative, the gap between the two figures just is the difference the question was asking about. Where the two senses conflict there is nothing further to be said: any weighting of them would be arbitrary, so the defensible course is to report the two comparisons separately and decline to combine them."
