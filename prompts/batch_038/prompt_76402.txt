I'm auditing a philosophy of mind seminar this term and I keep getting hung up on Molyneux's problem — not the original puzzle so much as the modern attempts to settle it with actual patients. Last week we covered the congenital-cataract restoration work in India (Pawan Sinha's project), where people blind essentially from birth got surgery and were then tested on whether they could match an object they'd only ever known by touch to the same object now seen. The upshot, as I understood it, was that at first they're bad at it — the seen object doesn't obviously strike them as the same thing they know by feel — and they get better over weeks of looking, especially when they're allowed to move around and touch while they look. Half the seminar said this settles the question in favor of "no," the other half said it doesn't touch the question at all, and I couldn't follow either argument, honestly.

So here's what I want you to write: a dialogue between two people on opposite sides of Molyneux's problem, but staged in an invented near-future version of the experiment so it isn't just a rehash of the real studies. The setup: a lab has restored sight to one adult subject, call her M — blind since birth but with a totally normal spatial life otherwise. She's a sculptor, she's good at mental rotation, she navigates her city by cane and sound, she can tell you the layout of a room she's walked through once. Before her bandages come off, the same team runs a control condition: a haptic-to-vision neural net they trained themselves on paired touch-and-image data. With zero fine-tuning on the test images, that net picks the photograph of a cube out of a two-photo lineup (cube vs. sphere) at well above chance. M, shown the same two photos after surgery, can't do it — she says the pictures look like flat colored smears, and the only way she can get to the right answer is to trace the outline in the air with her finger while she looks at it.

The two characters: (a) a nativist-leaning cognitive scientist who thinks the right answer to Molyneux is "yes," on the grounds that spatial structure is represented in a way that isn't tied to any one sense, so what M lacks isn't the concept of a cube but the ability to *see* one — a calibration problem at the level of visual experience, not a conceptual gap; and (b) an empiricist-leaning philosopher who thinks the answer is "no," and who insists the AI control *supports* that rather than undercutting it, because what the net has is a learned statistical bridge built out of paired experience, which is exactly the empiricist story about how cross-modal matching ever comes to exist.

What I most want them to fight over is the AI control, since that's the part I can't get a grip on. Each should try to claim it as evidence for their own side, and then they should realize they disagree about what would even count as a Molyneux test — whether something with no body, no eye movements, and no history of learning to look can be a subject of the question at all, and whether "knowing by sight" is a capacity claim or an achievement claim. Please also have them go hard on the finger-tracing detail: is M using her new visual capacity there, or is she smuggling touch back in, and does it matter which? Both characters should be genuinely sharp — no strawmen, nobody caving at the end — and they should actually answer each other's points rather than trade speeches, including conceding where a point lands. End unresolved, but with the disagreement located more precisely than where it started. Maybe 1000–1400 words, casual register is fine, they can be colleagues who like each other arguing over bad coffee in the lab while M is still upstairs asleep. One more thing: don't have either of them name-drop books or papers or give me a reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references.
