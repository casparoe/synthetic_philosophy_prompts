I spent thirty-five years as a materials scientist in a government laboratory, the last fifteen of them chairing or serving on review boards of one kind or another: qualification panels, flight-readiness reviews, one accident investigation. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in a desultory but serious way, and for the past few months I have been working through the Condorcet jury theorem and the social epistemology that has grown up around it. I find that I keep wanting to test the theorem against a case I know from the inside rather than against the toy examples in the textbooks, and I should be grateful for a rigorous analysis of one such case.

The case is the decision to launch the space shuttle Challenger in January 1986. I will supply what matters, since I want the analysis to turn on the structure of the decision and not on disputed detail: the solid rocket boosters were assembled from segments sealed by rubber O-rings; on earlier flights those rings had shown erosion and blow-by, which the contractor's engineers had documented and flagged repeatedly; the rubber stiffened as temperature fell; the coldest launch previously flown had been somewhere in the low fifties Fahrenheit, while the forecast for the launch morning was in the twenties or thirties. On the teleconference the night before, the contractor's engineers recommended against launching. Their management pressed them to reconsider, reportedly urging them to set aside an engineering perspective and think as managers, and the recommendation was reversed. NASA was told that there was no objection to launching. The presidential commission afterward faulted the decision-making process as much as the hardware, and one of its members, a physicist, appended a personal statement arguing that the vehicle's reliability was two or three orders of magnitude worse than the figures agency management had been quoting.

Three things, then. First, map the episode onto the theorem with as much discipline as you can muster: who precisely constitutes the jury, what is the proposition on which they are voting, what were the individual competences, was the voting independent, was it sincere, and what aggregation rule actually operated. I suspect the last question is where the naive mapping comes apart, since what occurred was not the tallying of ballots but the suppression of a dissent and the substitution of a single institutional voice for a set of individual judgments; I should like that argued rather than asserted, and I should like to be told if I am wrong.

Second, distinguish the features of the episode the theorem genuinely explains from those on which it is simply silent. Does the case exemplify the mirror-image result, that when competence falls below a half, enlarging the assembly makes error more certain rather than less, given that adding managers to the call made the outcome worse? Or is that a misuse of the result? And what becomes of the assumption of a common competence when the physicist and the agency could not agree on the risk to within a factor of a thousand?

Third, and here I most want correcting: my working hypothesis is that this episode is not a counterexample to Condorcet so much as a demonstration that his theorem was never addressed to decisions of this kind. A jury pronounces on a proposition that is either true or false; a flight-readiness board pronounces on whether to accept a graded risk, and there is no fact of the matter of the sort the theorem requires. If that is right, the interesting question becomes what Condorcet's own stated conditions would imply about how such a board ought to be run, and whether those conditions are attainable or even desirable in engineering, where the communication among jurors that the theorem must forbid is precisely the means by which expertise is transmitted.

Please reason the mapping through yourself. I have no library at my elbow and no appetite for a reading list; what I want is your account of what the theorem says, what it cannot say, and what this episode does to it.
