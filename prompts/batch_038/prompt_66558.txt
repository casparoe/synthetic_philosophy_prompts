I work on AI policy at a think tank and I keep going around in circles on something — maybe you can help me break out of it.

When the big labs release weights "openly," the Llama community license being the clearest case, the release comes with a contract bolted on. There's an acceptable-use policy listing prohibited applications (weapons, certain kinds of surveillance, that sort of thing). There's the clause saying that if your products or services have more than 700 million monthly active users, the license doesn't cover you and you have to request a separate one from Meta, which it can grant or withhold at its discretion. If you fine-tune the model you have to keep the name in the title and label the result as built on Llama. And you're barred from using the outputs to train a rival model. Meanwhile the Open Source Initiative put out its own definition of open source AI in 2024 requiring, among other things, that the system be usable for any purpose without having to ask for permission, plus enough information about training data and code that a skilled person could rebuild something substantially equivalent — so basically none of these licenses qualify, which is why the labs say "open weights" rather than "open source."

Fine, that's all just factual. But here's the part that doesn't sit right with me. The same labs, when arguing that releasing is the right call, lean hard on the idea that diffusion is unstoppable: once a capability exists someone will leak it or replicate it, you can't put the weights back in the bottle, and the best you can do is get a lot of eyes on them. That argument treats control as already gone. The license treats control as very much retained — enforceable conditions, a revocation hook against the biggest deployers, trademark provenance that's supposed to survive fine-tuning. One of those two things has got to give.

So: what does the license-form of open release actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not a verdict on whether open release is good. Specifically:

- What view of harm does the acceptable-use-policy-plus-contract structure require? It seems to assume harms trace to identifiable agents who can be reached by a legal instrument and moved by it, rather than being diffuse or systemic. Is that a strict presupposition of the practice or just a view it happens to fit well with?
- What does a user-count threshold presuppose about danger — that the risk lives in concentration, in deployment scale, or just that Meta doesn't want a hyperscaler free-riding on its compute? Is there any coherent theory of risk on which number-of-users is the right lever at all, as opposed to, say, capability level or sector of deployment?
- What does the branding clause presuppose about provenance, given that a stripped checkpoint is a few seconds of work for anyone who cares?
- And what does the whole apparatus presuppose about the artifact itself — property, speech, knowledge, tool? Choosing a copyright license with conditions over, say, an unconditional public-domain dedication looks like it carries a commitment I cant quite articulate.

Also tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. My worry is that a purely cynical reading — these are just liability-shifting boilerplate terms written by lawyers with no theory behind them, chosen because they're the cheapest available hedge — licenses exactly the same documents, and if that's true then the whole exercise of asking what it presupposes is a bit of a fraud. I'd like you to say plainly whether it is, or whether there's a residue the cynical reading can't explain.

Finally, the counterfactual: if the irreversibility story were the operative commitment instead of the license story, what would the release practice have to look like? Give me two or three concrete changes, and say which of them a lab would actually resist and why, since I suspect the resistance is where the real commitments show up.

Bottom line in your first sentence — one sentence on what the license-form of open release ultimately presupposes — then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back to me.
