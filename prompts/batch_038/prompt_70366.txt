The thing I keep coming back to, and want you to work with. Two of our background assumptions. First, behavioral equivalence: the reward is only pinned down up to a shaping term, so there's a real sense in which the data can't tell apart reward functions that license the same policy — inference from behavior is underdetermined even in a clean, well-specified world. Second, the off-switch result: the reason a CIRL-style agent should let itself be switched off is that it treats the human's reaching for the switch as *evidence* about the reward, and that only works because we assume the human acts roughly rationally with respect to what they actually want. Now put the Hailsham students in that slot. An IRL agent trained on their behavior — they study, they trade tokens at the Exchange, they plan small futures, they become carers, they go back for a fourth donation, they essentially never run — would infer pretty confidently that this is what they want. And the assumption that their actions are informative about their reward is exactly the assumption the whole situation was built to falsify.

So here's what I want you to think carefully about:

1. Is Ishiguro dramatizing a failure of *inference* or a failure in the *thing being inferred about*? I mean: is the problem epistemic (the observer lacks data, or mis-models the agent, and better modeling would fix it) or is it that there's no stable preference there to recover, because the preference was deformed by the conditions that produced it? Give me a crisp version of that distinction, and then tell me which one the novel actually stages — because if it's the second, that's genuinely bad news for my field, since no amount of extra data or a fancier model solves it.

Elster's sour-grapes framing feels too broad here — it would classify almost every preference the characters have as suspect, which makes it useless.
