I am drafting an essay for a general-interest ethics venue and would like detailed critical comments on the draft below. Context first, then the draft, then a numbered list of what I specifically want from you.

**Context.** The essay concerns the ethics of insecticide use in agriculture. I am not writing either a defence of pesticides or an attack on them. My aim is narrower: to argue that nearly all existing discussion of the question — from ordinary animal-protection objections to the more recent literature on intervening to reduce wild animal suffering — shares an unstated assumption about what the unit of moral accounting is, and that the assumption is false. I have drafted about two-thirds of the piece. The final section is where I suspect the argument may collapse, and I cannot tell from the inside whether it does.

**Against the Event**

*1. The event model.* Most moral thinking about insecticide use takes a single application as its unit of analysis. A field is sprayed; a number of insects die; the deaths are harms; the harms are summed; the sum is the moral cost of the act. Call this the event model. It is not a position anyone argues for, which is part of why it is worth examining: it is a default, absorbed from the paradigm cases of animal ethics, which involve mammals.

Those paradigm cases do fit the model. If you shoot a dog, trap a deer, or confine a pig, there is an identifiable victim who existed before your act, a counterfactual in which that victim goes on living, and a harm that is local in time and attributable to you. Three features, then: identification, continuation, and boundedness.

*2. None of the three survive contact with insect populations.*

Identification. In a species that lays hundreds or thousands of eggs, most of the individuals whose fates a control decision determines do not yet exist when the decision is made. The decision is not about who dies. It is about who will be born. An accounting framed in terms of victims is counting the wrong population.

Continuation. For most insects, the counterfactual to a quick death is not a completed life. Juvenile mortality is enormous and its causes various: predation, parasitism, disease, desiccation, starvation, and, where relevant, the insecticide. The realistic comparison for an insect killed by a spray is not an insect that lives out its span but an insect that dies anyway, somewhat later, in a somewhat different way. The morally significant variable is therefore not death versus life but death-mode versus death-count: how painful, how prolonged, how many.

Boundedness. Because an intervention alters the population, its consequences do not stop with the individuals it kills. They propagate through every subsequent generation for as long as the alteration persists, which may be indefinitely. The aggregate of those downstream effects can exceed the direct deaths in either direction. Published estimates of insect decline illustrate the difficulty rather than resolving it: the widely discussed figure of a greater-than-seventy-five-percent loss of flying insect biomass over twenty-seven years across protected areas in Germany rests on one monitoring programme in one country, while broader syntheses find trends that range from steeply negative to roughly flat depending on which taxa, which regions, and which kinds of sites are pooled. Whatever the true aggregate, it is not a stable enough quantity to hang a moral verdict on.

*3. The trajectory model.* The alternative unit is the trajectory. Rather than comparing sprayed with not-sprayed at a moment, compare two population histories extended through time and ask which contains more disvalue summed over all the insects who will ever live along it.

On this model, interventions fall into classes the event model treats as interchangeable:

Acute lethal interventions kill existing individuals and typically permit rebound.

Suppressant interventions — sterilants, growth regulators, prophylactic seed coatings — reduce reproductive output rather than killing adults outright. As I remember it, neonicotinoid seed treatment now covers something like the overwhelming majority of maize acreage in the United States and perhaps a third to half of soybean acreage, applied as routine practice rather than in response to observed pest pressure; I need to verify the figures, though the shape of the claim is what matters. Suppressants change how many insects there will be.

Habitat interventions change carrying capacity — host-plant composition, field margins, overwintering structure — and so change everything downstream, including the fates of insects nobody has touched.

The upshot is counterintuitive in both directions. The acute spray, which is what the event model was built to condemn, may be the least consequential of the three. The intervention that merely prevents insects from coming into existence may be the most consequential, because it is the one that most directly sets the number of bad lives that will be lived. Its sign, however, is not knowable in advance: it depends on whether the life of a typical wild insect is net-negative, and on how the composition of deaths shifts when density shifts.

*4. What this does to two camps.*

To the conventional critique: the standard objection to insecticides inherits the event model. It counts deaths and infers harm. On the trajectory model the objection does not merely get a number wrong; it uses the wrong currency. I want to be careful here, because the point is easy to abuse. This is not a defence of insecticides. It is the claim that the familiar argument does not establish what it is taken to establish, and that a sound argument would have to be built on death-mode rather than death-count — not that spraying kills, but that it kills slowly, or badly, or in numbers a rebounding population cannot absorb.

To the interventionists: the trajectory model is their own logic taken seriously, and it bites them harder than it bites their opponents. If the aggregate over time is what matters, the calculation is dominated by a term whose sign nobody knows, and the uncertainty is not the sort that shrinks with more data, because the relevant data concern the welfare of animals we cannot observe in the states that matter. The usual reply — that the probability of net-negative lives is high enough to justify acting — appears to me to reinstate the event model by the back door, since it treats the conclusion as a claim about the insects one can point to rather than about the trajectory one cannot.

*5. The residue.* There is one place where I suspect the trajectory model condemns something it should not.

If death-mode matters more than death-count, then an intervention that kills a great many insects quickly may be preferable to one that suppresses a population gradually, leaving the insects never directly poisoned to starve, be parasitised, or desiccate over a longer period and in greater total number. Followed all the way, this licenses the conclusion that killing can be an improvement, and that a painless mass killing is a welfare gain relative to a slow attrition that harms no one directly.

I find this repellent and I do not know whether my repulsion is evidence. It might be a reductio of the trajectory model. It might instead be a cost the model rightly imposes — one my intuitions have not paid for and are in no position to refuse. The essay currently resolves nothing and instead makes a turn I do not fully believe: that because the sign of the calculation is undeterminable, the burden of justification falls on whoever would alter a trajectory, since an act of killing can be stopped but a change in the number of future lives cannot be undone. I put that there because it seemed like something to say.
