I'm putting together a forty-minute talk for a joint law-and-philosophy workshop, and I've hit a wall with the framing I began from, so I want a second pair of eyes on the conceptual structure rather than the history. The episode is the fight over how to put a dollar figure on the Exxon Valdez disaster after the tanker grounded on Bligh Reef in Prince William Sound in March 1989, releasing on the order of eleven million gallons of crude and oiling something like thirteen hundred miles of Alaskan coastline. What makes it philosophically interesting rather than merely technical is that most of what was oiled was remote and largely unvisited: for the overwhelming majority of Americans who would never set foot there, no market had ever registered a price for what was lost, so the standard instruments — travel cost, hedonic property values, foregone commercial catch — captured almost none of it. Congress responded with the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, and the courts had by then allowed trustees to recover what came to be called existence or non-use value, the benefit a person gets from simply knowing that a thing is there (an idea traceable to Krutilla in the late sixties). With nothing revealed to read off behavior, the economists turned to surveys: ask people what they would pay to prevent such a loss, and treat the answers as valuations. As far as I can tell, this was the first occasion on which that technique was deployed quantitatively in a damage assessment of this scale.

The backlash is what I actually want analyzed. Critics argued that the survey answers were not preferences at all but something cheaper — a warm glow, a way of buying the feeling of having done one's part, a protest against the oil company, or a general cheer for environmental spending that showed up as wildly scope-insensitive numbers, with what people professed to pay to protect a large resource failing to scale against what they professed for a much smaller one. A federal panel chaired by Arrow and Solow reported in 1993 that carefully designed surveys could be reliable enough to serve as the starting point of a judicial process of damage assessment, including passive-use values, but only behind a battery of fixes: in-person interviews, a yes-or-no referendum format tied to a specific tax, detailed information about the resource and the threats to it, and comprehension checks. And then the panel made a move I find genuinely delicious: it recommended measuring willingness to pay to protect the good rather than willingness to accept compensation for losing it, on the grounds that the former figure was more conservative — even though the legal question was precisely about a loss already sustained.

So: please treat the whole episode as a test case for the revealed-preference program, from Samuelson's 1938 formulation onward, and for whatever account of utility is riding along with it, and run the analysis in both directions. First, be disciplined about the mapping. Who are the players, what are their options, and what counts as evidence for each side — and in particular, on the revealed-preference reading, what exactly is the choice, what is the budget set, and what is the opportunity cost when a respondent answers a survey question? I want you to take seriously whether a survey answer is a choice at all, and whether the consistency axioms that give the program its teeth (weak, strong, and generalized) even apply to the data the panel was proposing to bless. Second, work through the thought experiment that strikes me as the crux, which I don't see raised in the methodological literature I've read: suppose the survey is made incentive-compatible, so that the referendum is real and binding and the respondent actually pays the stated amount out of an actual budget. That converts a stated preference into a revealed one — but at the price of turning the survey into a market. Say what is gained and what is conceded, and whether the controversy dissolves or merely relocates. Third, take up the willingness-to-pay/willingness-to-accept asymmetry and the endowment-effect experiments that document it. If the measure you get depends on the instrument and on how the question is framed as gain or loss, what does that imply about the claim that there is a single object — the preference — of which willingness to pay is a reading? Does the panel's conservatism argument quietly concede that the legal question and the economic measure come apart? Fourth, assess the warm-glow objection as an instance of a general worry: whether any behavior can be redescribed as the maximization of some preference, and if so, whether revealed preference is falsifiable or whether the axioms rescue it from being empty. Then, and only then, give me the verdict: what does this case show about the meaning of utility — is it a confirmation of Samuelson's hope that economics could do without unobservable mental states, an anomaly, or an application the theory never anticipated? And where is the theory simply silent rather than wrong, since silence about existence value is not the same as a prohibition on it? I'd like an honest closing judgment on whether the institutional outcome was a philosophical victory or a pragmatic compromise, and on what it says that utility ended up functioning as a quantity a court could award. A friend of mine who does natural-resources litigation assures me the technical debate has been settled for thirty years; my worry is that nobody has ever said clearly what the settlement cost conceptually, and I'd rather not repeat that omission in front of an audience of lawyers.
