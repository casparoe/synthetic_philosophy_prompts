I'm the grad student rep on my department's priority panel — three faculty and me. Twice a year we decide who gets departmental credit when two groups turn up the same result. It feeds a small prize and the wording of the annual report, so people care way more than you'd expect. Our record so far, in order:

1. X and Y, same theorem, no contact between them. X preprints a two-page note (statement plus a sketch of the argument) in April; Y preprints a forty-page fully checked version in July. We credited X, adding that Y's was the definitive writeup but the priority was X's.
2. C preprints in February. D had the finished result in January — dated notebook, plus a departmental seminar in February that C sat in on, no slides circulated, no recording — and preprints in June. We credited D, and the writeup said C's work wasn't independent.
3. E preprints in March, F in May. F is convinced they had it the previous December, but the support is a colleague remembering a conversation and an email that raises the topic without stating a result. We credited E; one faculty member wrote that undocumented private work can't establish anything.
4. G preprints in June, H in September. H had given a job-talk version in May with G in the audience, and H also had a complete draft sitting on their laptop from April. We credited H.

Now the case in front of us, which I find genuinely hard. Group I preprints a complete, correct result on 10 June. Group J preprints on 2 August, and J's version is strictly more general — I's falls out of it as a special case. J's lab notebook, dated 20 May, has the general theorem and a finished proof. But one of J's students went to an I group meeting on 18 April where I had the key lemma written on the board, and that student's notebook entry for 19 April says the lemma does the hard step. Meanwhile I's PI says they had the whole thing by early May, and the evidence for that is a Slack thread about the crucial trick plus some undated whiteboard photos. Oh, and a third group overseas published a partial version of that lemma in workshop proceedings the year before, which both I and J cite — my advisor's whole thing is that simultaneous discovery is the default state of a field rather than a lucky coincidence, and this case feels like a poster child for it.

The chair has said she's voting for I, another member says J, so mine decides it. Can you work out which rules are actually consistent with all four precedents? I'd have guessed first-to-disclose wins, but 2 and 4 seem to kill that, and I dont know what's left. Then: how do the surviving rules split on the new case, what's the panel most likely to say and which precedent is the closest analog, and what should I argue for if I want the ruling to be consistant with our own record rather than just picking a favorite. I'm especially interested in what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each choice — if we go with J, do we owe a re-examination of 3? — and in a hypothetical fifth case that would cleanly separate whatever rules survive this one. Also, honestly: does the way we keep deciding these quietly presuppose something about whether the result was going to be found anyway, and if so, which of the candidate rules carries the stronger presupposition?
