So, background: I'm a risk analyst at a county emergency management agency in the upper Midwest, and about four springs a year our operations chief has to decide whether to issue a mandatory evacuation order for a couple of river towns, or just an advisory, or nothing. The forecasts we get are never a single number — the hydrologist hands us something like "15 to 35 percent chance the crest tops the levee," because three different models disagree and he refuses to average them into something that looks precise but isn't. Last year a consultant told us we should be doing decision making under imprecise probabilities, left us a binder, and the team has been confused ever since. Two things in particular. First, our hydrologist pointed out that the upstream gauge reading is supposed to be uninformative about the crest if you look at it on its own — every model agrees on the marginal — but the models disagree about how the reading lines up with the crest, so conditioning on it blew our 15-to-35 interval out to something like 5-to-55. He said the term is dilation and that it's a known feature of this framework rather than a mistake somebody made, which was not a reassuring thing to hear at 2 a.m. Second, we have no idea what decision rule to use on an interval like that. Maximin makes us evacuate basically always, and I gather there are alternatives — I think "E-admissibility" was one, and some Hurwicz thing with an optimism parameter — but nobody here has the background to know what we'd be signing up for, or whether picking between those rules is a technical question with a right answer or just a values question wearing a technical costume.

What I actually need is a procedure, not a survey. Assume the people running it are me (some statistics, no philosophy), the operations chief (no statistics, no philosophy, frequently on four hours of sleep), and the hydrologist (real modeling expertise, will happily argue about updating rules for an hour if you let him). It has to go from forecast-in-hand to a signed recommendation in under twenty minutes, it has to survive being run by whoever happens to be on shift, and the output has to fit on one page. So give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how we update the interval when new evidence lands (including exactly what to do in the dilation situation above, because that one is going to happen again), which decision rule to apply under which conditions, and where the procedure stops and hands the thing to a human being. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — I'd rather know which lines on the worksheet are fake precision than find out in a hearing. And be blunt about what this whole apparatus can't do. My private suspicion is that it gives us a principled way to carry an interval around but tells us nothing at all about where the interval should come from in the first place, and if that's right I want it said out loud. Related: our state counterpart just averages the three models and reports one number, and I want to know whether that's cheating or whether it's a perfectly reasonable thing to do that I've been talked out of by a binder. No citations, no reading list, no "see also" — I'm not going to be able to look any of it up at work, so whatever you tell me has to stand on its own.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Case one: crest forecast interval of 0.15 to 0.35 that the water tops the levee at Grand Bend, 900 households in the inundation area, if it tops we're looking at two to four deaths and roughly $60 million in damage, a mandatory order costs us about $2.5 million and — this is the part the commissioners don't like hearing — our own logs show compliance dropped to 61 percent after three orders in a row that turned out to be unnecessary, and no further evidence is coming before the six-hour deadline. Case two: same stakes, but thirty-six hours out at 0.15 to 0.35, and then the upstream gauge reading arrives and conditioning widens the interval to 0.05 to 0.55. Case three: a levee segment that was rebuilt two years ago with a new composite material that has no failure history anywhere in the country, our hydrologist won't commit to a range and says honestly it's somewhere between zero and one, same evacuation decision. One of these three should come out in a direction the county commissioners will hate, and I want you to say that plainly instead of softening it — if the procedure tells us to lose compliance for nothing, or tells the operations chief that the worksheet has no recommendation for him this time, just say so. Please write the whole thing in American English.
