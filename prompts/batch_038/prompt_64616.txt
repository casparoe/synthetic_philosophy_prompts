For thirty-six years I worked in solid-state chemistry, mostly on phase transformations in alloys, and I have only come to philosophy seriously since retiring. I belong now to a reading group in my town, and last month I gave a paper to it on L. A. Paul's account of transformative experience, which I encountered through her book *Transformative Experience* and the subsequent debate. My contribution was not exegesis but an analogy, and it is the analogy I want audited.

I proposed that a personally transformative decision — becoming a parent, say, or entering religious life, or accepting an operation that will change one's sensory capacities for the rest of one's life — is structurally like a first-order phase transition with hysteresis. The correspondence I sketched was this. The control parameter is the accumulating circumstance that pushes one towards the decision. The free-energy landscape holds two minima, and one can write down expressions for both, but the barrier between them means that no local exploration of the phase one already occupies yields information about the other. The order parameter — whatever constitutes the self's orientation to the world — changes discontinuously rather than smoothly. And once the transition has occurred and the control parameter is taken back down, one does not retrace the same path: the new state is metastable and the old one is no longer accessible, which is what makes the decision irreversible in the strong sense rather than merely costly to undo. From this I argued that the difficulty of deliberating about such choices is categorical rather than evidential: it is not that we lack the data, but that no measurement available from inside the present phase can supply it.

The objection came from a member of the group who reads philosophy of mind, and I think she scored. Her point, as I understood it, was that the analogy is load-bearing for my conclusion and that it in fact carries the opposite one. In a genuine first-order transition we know the new phase extremely well in advance: we can compute its properties from the same underlying Hamiltonian, we can inspect a sample that is already in it, and we can even seed the transformation in a test specimen and quench it back. If parenthood is like martensite, then asking a parent what it is like, or measuring the product directly, is precisely the third-person route that Paul's argument needs to be closed. So either my analogy shows the transformation to be intelligible and predictable, in which case it destroys my conclusion, or it does not hold at the place where it was supposed to do the work.

I would be grateful for a careful audit rather than a defence of me. Specifically:

1. Set out the correspondence table properly — control parameter, order parameter, free-energy landscape, nucleation barrier, latent heat, coexistence region, metastability and hysteresis, the sample under transformation, the external observer with a thermometer, and the theory that licenses prediction — and say for each entry whether the mapping is genuine, strained, or merely rhetorical.
2. Separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the use I put the analogy to from those that are cosmetic. In particular, say whether the persistence of the same substance across the transition, the existence of an observer who does not undergo it, and the availability of a theory from which both phases follow are three distinct problems or one problem wearing three hats.
3. Consider whether a different member of the same family does better: a continuous transition at a critical point, where there is no latent heat and the fluctuations diverge; glass formation, where there is no equilibrium description of the end state at all and the outcome depends on the cooling history; or a reaction that consumes its own catalyst. Say what each would license and what it would forfeit.
4. Tell me whether the barrier picture is even right for the cases I care about, since some of them seem to have no nucleation barrier whatsoever and proceed spontaneously once a threshold is crossed, which I suppose is the spinodal case.
5. Conclude with a verdict: does her objection land, and if I keep the analogy in a revised paper, what is the minimal hedge I must attach so that the argument no longer rests on it?

Please write in British English. I should prefer no reading list and no citations — I cannot easily chase references and would rather have the reasoning laid out in full. Assume I am already familiar with the distinction between epistemic and personal transformation and with the standard objections from testimony and from imagination; I am interested in the physics of the comparison, not in a summary of the debate.
