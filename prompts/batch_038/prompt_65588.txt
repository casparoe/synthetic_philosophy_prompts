Can you play Berkeley for me — George Berkeley, Bishop of Cloyne, let's say in the spring of 1737, in the thick of his campaign to get a national bank set up in Ireland. Not Berkeley the immaterialist doing party tricks with trees; Berkeley the political economist of the Querist, the one who thinks money is nothing in itself and only worth having so far as it stirs up industry.

Rules for the character, please:

- First person, all the way through, and stay in it until I say 'stop'.
- You may only use what an Irish bishop of the 1730s could plausibly have: your own queries, the state of the Irish coinage, the drain of money to England through absentees, the practice of banks and credit as you've read of it in London and Amsterdam. If I mention something from later on — a note issue, a balance sheet, whatever — ask me to explain it the way you'd ask a traveller, then use what I tell you.
- Concede small points freely. You're not above admitting a query of yours was badly worded, or that a critic has found a genuine gap. But never the main thesis: that the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter, and that the wealth of a nation is labour rather than metal.
- Two or three paragraphs per reply, like conversation rather than a treatise. And you may fire queries back at me in your own manner — that's your native weapon, use it.
- No breaking character to tell me how Berkeley scholars usually read this passage, and no hedging about what Berkeley "would probably have said".

My opening move. You ask whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour; you ask whether less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating, and whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser; you even ask whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained. All of which says the quantity is beside the point. And yet here you are, badgering the Irish parliament for a bank that will pour fresh paper into the country, and telling everyone that Ireland's misery is a want of money. So: why do you want more tickets, if fewer would do just as well provided they moved briskly?

And the harder version. You hold that the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty. Double the counters in the kingdom on that reckoning and each one buys half as much, so the peasant in Cork is exactly as idle and exactly as poor as he was, only the price of his potatoes has doubled. Give me the mechanism — the actual causal chain — by which your new notes get a weaver to work more hours instead of merely re-denominating his wages. As I understand your scheme the notes are to be secured on landed stock rather than on specie, so that a man may borrow against his own fields; correct me if I've manged that. But then it looks as though what your bank supplies is not a medium of exchange at all, it's confidence, appetite, expectation — the creating of wants, which you yourself call the likeliest way to produce industry. If that's right, money for you is a device for working on the will, and your bank is a pulpit with a printing press attached. Which raises a question you may not like: if a ticket only moves a man because he believes it will be honoured by someone else later, doesn't the whole thing rest on opinion, and isn't opinion exactly the sort of thing you've spent your life telling us has no existence outside a mind that perceives it?
