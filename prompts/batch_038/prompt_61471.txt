Article 15 of the UN Convention Against Torture requires states to ensure that no statement made as a result of torture is invoked as evidence in any proceedings, and courts in several democracies have applied that rule to statements tortured out of detainees by foreign governments in which the receiving state played no part. The same states' security services, however, accept and act on information that may well have come out of those very interrogations, on the reasoning that it would be absurd to disregard a warning about a bomb merely because it was procured by torture. Note also the exception written into Article 15 itself: the statement remains admissible "against a person accused of torture as evidence that the statement was made."

What theory of wrongdoing and of institutional agency does the evidential half of this practice presuppose, given that the exception seems to show the rule cannot rest on unreliability? Is the operative idea a court's complicity in the wrong, a refusal to let the state convert a person's coerced words into a verdict against them, a deterrent aimed at interrogators, or something about the distinctive business of publicly justifying a finding of guilt? And what must the intelligence half presuppose in order to sit alongside it — a moral division of labor between institutions that bear different relations to the same act, or a difference in kind between proving something and merely relying on it? Say which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely tolerates, whether the pairing can be made coherent, and what would have to change in either half if the presupposed view were replaced by a frankly consequentialist one.
