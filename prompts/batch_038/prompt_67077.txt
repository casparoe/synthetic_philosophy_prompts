I run a small reading group that has just spent a fortnight on the foundations of set theory, and we closed with the continuum hypothesis: Cantor's conjecture in 1878, Hilbert placing it first among his twenty-three problems in 1900, Gödel's inner-model result in the late 1930s, Cohen's forcing in 1963, and the fifty-odd years since in which nobody has agreed what any of it means philosophically. Half the group wanted to move on to something more tractable; the rest of us, myself included, kept circling back to a man who died in 1925 and never saw a page of it. So here is the exercise I would like you to attempt: reconstruct, as carefully as the surviving evidence permits, what Gottlob Frege would have made of the independence of the continuum hypothesis. (One member of the group keeps trying to steer us towards Frege's political diary; I regard that as a separate and largely unrelated conversation and would rather it stayed out of this one.)

Please take seriously the specific parts of his thought that bear on the question, rather than casting him as a generic platonist who would shrug and insist that there is a fact of the matter. Four anchors strike me as decisive, and I would like each worked through in turn. First, his response to Russell's letter in 1902 and the appendix he bolted onto the second volume of the Grundgesetze: a man who watched the single law his entire edifice rested upon turn out to be inconsistent, and who nonetheless refused to give up the claim that arithmetic is a branch of logic. Second, his polemic against Hilbert's axiomatisation of geometry, and in particular his insistence that axioms are not free-floating stipulations rendered true by their mutual consistency, but truths whose terms must already possess a determinate sense. Third, his third-realm platonism and his hostility to psychologism: thoughts are neither mental nor physical, and their truth is in no way dependent on anyone's capacity to recognise it. Fourth, the unpublished manuscript of his final years on the sources of knowledge of mathematics and the mathematical natural sciences, in which he distinguishes sense perception, logic, and a further geometrical or temporal source of knowledge, and reconsiders whether arithmetic might draw on the third rather than on logic alone.

With those in hand, I want answers to the following. Would Frege have maintained that the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value which we have simply not yet found the right axioms to capture, or that the question as posed lacks determinate sense until our conception of the continuum is sharpened? Would he have read the two independence results as a spectacular vindication of his attack on formalism — here are two consistent theories, and the formalist is equally content with both, so consistency plainly does not fix meaning — or as a humiliation for his own platonism, since a third realm nobody can gain epistemic access to starts to look like an idle posit? And where would he have positioned himself in the present quarrel between those who argue, in the spirit of Gödel's programme, that we should be hunting for new axioms, and those who argue, in the spirit of Feferman, that the continuum is simply too indefinite a notion for the question to have an answer at all? I have a hunch that he would drift towards the second camp for reasons having nothing to do with constructivism, and I would like that hunch either confirmed or demolished.

One caution, since a decade of rationalist blogs has taught me exactly how these exercises usually fail: the value here lies not in a confident-sounding guess but in the distinction between what Frege's recorded commitments actually support and what we are merely dressing up in his name. So please mark, at each step, whether you are extrapolating from something he wrote, generalising from the shape of his views, or inventing a plausible Frege because the evidence has run out. If the four anchors pull against one another — and I suspect the late manuscript does genuine damage to the first three — say so, and tell me how you would resolve the tension, or why it cannot be resolved at all. Something in the region of nine hundred to twelve hundred words would suit. Please write throughout in British English.
