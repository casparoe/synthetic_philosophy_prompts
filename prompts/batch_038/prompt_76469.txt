I am drafting the opening section of a paper in which I state, without hedging, where I stand on how demanding morality is permitted to be. Two colleagues have agreed to write replies, and before the draft goes to them I want the position audited for internal coherence — not defended, not surveyed, audited. Here is the complete list of commitments as they currently stand:

1. The axiology is impersonal, and the good is prior to the right. What makes an act right is the value of the state of affairs it brings about; there are no underivative agent-centered restrictions.

2. Nevertheless I accept a Scheffler-style agent-centered prerogative: an agent may assign some additional weight to their own interests and projects, and so is not always required to bring about the best available outcome. I intend this as a modification at the level of the deontic only, leaving item 1 intact.

3. The demandingness objection to act-utilitarianism succeeds, and succeeds decisively rather than as one cost among others to be traded off. A theory that requires an agent to give until further giving would cost them more than it would gain for others is not a theory that could reasonably be asked of anyone.

4. I reject ought-implies-can as a constraint on the content of morality, and I reject motivational-feasibility tests generally. That people cannot or will not comply with a requirement does not soften the requirement. Difficulty, as such, is not a defect in a moral theory.

5. My ground for item 3 is Williams's: utilitarianism would require agents to stand back from and alienate themselves out of the projects and personal commitments that constitute who they are, and it is only from within such commitments that an agent's actions can carry moral significance at all. A theory that dismantles the conditions of its own subjects' agency cannot be the correct theory about them.

6. Supererogation is a real and irreducible category. Some acts are morally excellent without being required, and this is not a mere artifact of our practices of praise and blame, nor of the economics of sanctions.

7. The objection must not be empirical. How large a share of income morality would demand is contingent on the distribution of wealth and on the price of effective aid in the world as it happens to be; my objection is to the shape of the theory rather than to its output under current conditions, and it should survive the elimination of extreme poverty entirely.

8. I reject the symmetry response. It does not strike me as a live possibility that if demandingness is an objection then permissiveness must be one too; a moral theory is not defective for failing to require as much as it could.

9. A case I want my view to be able to explain. Suppose an agent gives away a tenth of a comfortable income and declines to give more, but organizes their career, friendships, and choice of where to live entirely around maximizing expected aggregate good, and ends up with no projects that are not instruments to that good. Every choice in this life is permitted, and much of it is admirable. I want to say that the life nonetheless exhibits a failure of the theory that produced it — that the theory is at fault for making such a life available and praiseworthy — and I want a theory capable of saying so.

What I want from you is the audit. Go through the list and tell me which items genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do. I expect at least one apparent conflict to dissolve under better description, and I would rather you said so plainly than padded the list so that everything appears equally problematic. For each genuine conflict: state it precisely (exactly what is inconsistent with what, and under which reading of the two items), rank the conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a flat set, and say what would have to be abandoned or re-described to remove each, together with the least expensive revision and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single change would repair the most damage. Finally, on item 9: say whether anything I have already committed to can deliver that verdict, and if not, what would have to be added, and whether the addition is compatible with the remainder of the list or forces further revisions.

Please do not write me a survey of the demandingness debate and do not supply references; I know the standard moves and I will do the citing myself. I am also not asking which commitments I ought to abandon in the abstract, and please do not assume that the consequentialist axiology is what I would prefer to let go. Treat every item as equally up for revision, and tell me where the pressure actually falls.
