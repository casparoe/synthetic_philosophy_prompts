Below is a near-final draft of the paper's central section, which states the objection I want to press. **Draft section: "Risk weights below the resolution of preference"**

¶1. In Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) framework, an act f with outcomes ordered worst to best as a₁ < … < aₙ is evaluated as REU(f) = u(a₁) + Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ r(P(f ≥ aᵢ₊₁)) · [u(aᵢ₊₁) − u(aᵢ)], where r: [0,1] → [0,1] is continuous, strictly increasing, with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. When r is the identity function the formula reduces to ordinary expected utility. Risk aversion corresponds to convexity of r, which entails r(p) ≤ p for all p in (0,1). The theoretical appeal of REU is that risk attitude appears as a single, separately identifiable trait of the agent — a global sensitivity to the shape of a gamble's distribution — rather than as a device smuggled in through the curvature of u or through a violation of the ordering axioms.

¶2. The standard objections are that REU agents are vulnerable to money pumps and to diachronic inconsistency, and that a second transformation of probabilities is redundant once u is allowed to be concave. My objection is different. It concerns the *evidential reach* of r. A representation theorem identifies r only up to the domain on which the agent's preferences have been elicited. Let p_min be the smallest probability at which the agent has in fact revealed a preference between gambles. Nothing in the framework distinguishes two convex risk functions that agree exactly on [p_min, 1] while differing below p_min; one can construct such a pair explicitly, matching value and derivative at p_min. For a human agent, p_min is not small: below roughly one in a thousand, frequency judgments and revealed preferences lose any reliable resolution.

¶3. This matters because the cases in which risk-sensitivity is most often invoked normatively live far below p_min. Consider a mugging gamble: pay c for certain; with probability p receive V; with linear utility in money, REU values it at −c + r(p)·V. Whether REU refuses the classical mugging, V = 1/p, turns entirely on the right-derivative of r at 0. If r(p)/p → 0 — for instance r(p) = p² — the contribution r(p)/p vanishes and the mugging is refused. If r′(0⁺) = c₀ > 0, which is fully compatible with convexity, the contribution tends to c₀ no matter how small p becomes, and the mugging is accepted at every scale. Two agents can therefore be behaviourally indistinguishable across the whole range of probabilities over which either could be tested, and yet take opposite views on a decision of exactly the kind that motivated interest in risk-weighted theories: pandemic preparedness, tail-risk climate policy, and low-probability catastrophic outcomes from advanced technology.

¶4. So REU does not dissolve the small-probability/large-stakes problem; it relocates the boundary, and the location of the boundary is fixed by an extrapolation the agent never made and could not have been prompted to make. There are two responses open to the theorist, and I argue that both are costly. The first is to constrain the extrapolation normatively — to require, say, that r be regularly varying at 0 with index greater than one, or to impose a bound on payoff growth — which does deliver determinate verdicts, but only by importing a stipulation about the tail shape of r that sits outside the theory and is chosen precisely where the evidence is silent. The second is to refuse to extrapolate and declare the theory undefined below p_min. I suspect the second is unstable: a representation theorem requires r on all of [0,1], so silence is not an available state, and even if it were, declining to act is itself an act with a payoff structure that must be evaluated by some rule.

¶5. My conclusion is that REU's distinctive selling point — risk attitude as a measurable, unified psychological trait — becomes a liability in the regime where the theory is asked to carry the most normative weight, because that trait is measured only where it does not matter and extrapolated exactly where it does.
