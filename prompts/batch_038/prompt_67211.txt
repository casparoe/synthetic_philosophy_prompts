I coordinate an interdisciplinary reading group on risk and uncertainty at a university research center, and our membership is deliberately mixed: two economists, a philosopher of science, an actuary, a couple of machine-learning researchers, and a graduate student in history of statistics. Our last session, on the St. Petersburg doubling game, was the least productive we have had, precisely because everyone agreed the "paradox" was dissolved while meaning something different by dissolution. One member said the issue is pseudo-practical, since no bank can back an unbounded payoff; another said concave utility settles it and the rest of us were just being squeamish; a third said that reply fails because the payoff schedule can always be escalated fast enough to outrun any unbounded utility function; a fourth insisted the right object of evaluation is not the ensemble average but the growth rate along a single repeated trajectory; and a fifth argued that any finite entry fee is genuinely correct and our unwillingness to pay is the datum in need of correction. I would like to run a diagnostic instrument at the start of the next session so that people discover which of these they actually are before arguing about it. Please write that instrument as a decision tree: a sequence of branching questions that carries a participant from a stated starting commitment to a named position on what, if anything, is wrong with the classical treatment of the game.

The tree should have at least nine nodes and between twelve and sixteen leaves, and it must be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: decide first which questions genuinely discriminate, then locate the positions among the answer combinations those questions permit. Every node must be a concrete case, wager, or claim that a participant can answer on the spot without knowing which label their answer leads to; no node may ask whether they accept expected utility theory, whether they are a subjectivist, or anything else phrased in the vocabulary of the positions. Please make sure the following cases or questions appear somewhere on the tree, in whatever order you judge best: an escalated variant of the game whose payoffs grow fast enough to defeat a specified unbounded utility function; a variant in which the terms of the expectation series alternate in sign and converge only conditionally, so that its apparent value seems to depend on the order in which outcomes are summed; the same doubling game offered once each to a very large number of people versus offered repeatedly over a lifetime to a single person who reinvests her bankroll; a version in which the house has a finite bankroll and the entry fee is fixed, so that a long enough run of tails ruins the payer; a question that separates objecting to taking expectations as such from objecting only to an infinite expectation; and a question about whether a participant's stated willingness to pay is meant to be a normative price or a prediction about behavior. Among the leaves, I need at minimum: bounded utility; unbounded but concave utility; the growth-rate or time-average resolution; the view that the infinite expectation is mathematically correct but decision-irrelevant; the finitist or resource-constrained dissolution; a pluralist view on which different decision criteria are licensed in different contexts; a view that revises the probability side of the calculation rather than the utility side; the bullet-biting view that any finite price is correct and the intuitions are at fault; and at least one leaf explicitly marked as unoccupied, with a diagnosis of whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves cannot in fact be separated by your questions, say so and give the extra question that would separate them, if any exists.

Write the response in American English as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "instrument_title" (string); "orientation" (string, at most eighty words, saying what the instrument sorts for and what it does not settle); "nodes" (array of objects with "id" such as N1, "text" giving the case or claim as the participant reads it, "what_it_probes" giving in one sentence the distinction that node turns on, and "options", an array of objects with "code" such as A, "text", and "next" pointing either to a node id or a leaf id); "leaves" (array of objects with "id" such as L01, "position" giving the name of the position or the literal string UNOCCUPIED, "core_move" giving in one sentence how that position handles the original doubling game, "path" giving the ordered list of option codes that leads there, "most_awkward_commitment" giving in one sentence the cost the position pays, and "representative_proponents" naming them or giving the string none); "inseparable_pairs" (array of objects with "leaves", "why_the_tree_fails_here", and "separating_question"); "vacant_combinations" (array of objects with "answer_pattern", "diagnosis" taking one of the three values incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored, and "explanation"); and "facilitator_notes" (string, at most one hundred fifty words, telling me where a mixed group of this kind is most likely to cluster and which single question to put aloud if we run short on time). Keep any arithmetic in the node texts simple enough that a participant can check it mentally, and do not add fields beyond those listed.
