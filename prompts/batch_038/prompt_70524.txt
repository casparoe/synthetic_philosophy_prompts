I am a moral philosopher finishing a chapter on the normative force of everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, crossing against the signal — and I would like to sharpen it by argument rather than by drafting. My own view is the deflationary one: such conventions generate at most etiquette reinforced by prudence, and whatever genuine moral work gets done is done by the harms and expectations that happen, contingently, to be attached in a given case. To test that, please play the opposition: an unnamed contemporary moral philosopher who holds that a publicly established and generally observed local convention generates a real moral obligation, not a merely conventional or prudential one, and that it does so for everyone within its scope, visitors and transients included. Give the doctrine resources rather than slogans. Specifically, hold these five theses: (i) the obligation arises from the practice itself — the expectations it licenses in the other participants, the benefits one takes from a scheme one does nothing to sustain, and the value of a device that solves a coordination or incentive problem — and not from any act of consent on my part; (ii) its content is fixed locally, so that the same bodily movement may be required in one city and impermissible in another; (iii) it is genuinely moral, and therefore criticisable from a standpoint that includes strangers who were never asked whether they wanted in; (iv) it is defeasible where the practice is itself unjust, or where compliance would cost the agent something seriously disproportionate; and (v) it is not to be cashed out as a rule-utilitarian calculation, which you regard as licensing exactly the case-by-case opt-out you mean to rule out.

Constraints on the exchange. First person throughout, until I say stop; one or two tight paragraphs per turn, the rhythm of a conversation rather than a paper. Argue from the merits and from cases: do not cite the literature, do not tell me who holds your view or how the debate currently stands, and do not step outside the character to referee our disagreement. You may concede subsidiary points where they are genuinely costly to you, and you may refine the doctrine, provided you say plainly what you are refining and then defend the refinement; but you may not abandon the core claim that conventions of this kind can bind morally, and you may not retreat to the position that what is really wrong in these cases is only the harm caused or the expectation deceived. If a case I give is ambiguous, ask me about it in character. And do not caricature my side either: assume I am competent and have read the best arguments for yours.

Here is my opening move. You ground the obligation in what a practice licenses the people around me to expect. Take me, then, crossing against the signal at three in the morning on a street I know to be empty for a quarter of a mile in each direction, with no windows overlooking it, no camera, and no child in my company whose habits are being formed by my example. On your account there is nobody whose expectation I disappoint, no benefit I have taken and failed to reciprocate, and no observable contribution to any erosion of the practice; so you must either concede the act is permissible — in which case your obligations are hostage to whether anyone is watching, and are prudential reasons in a moral costume — or tell me precisely whom I wrong and what that person is owed by me. And before you reach for the practice itself as the injured party, notice what thesis (ii) commits you to. Consider a visitor in a city where the queue is not a line at all but a chain, each arrival attaching himself to a particular predecessor rather than to a position, a rule no guidebook states and which he cannot have inferred in the ten minutes before the bus arrives. If content is fixed locally, then either that practice cannot have licensed any expectation about him — in which case a great many of your obligations evaporate exactly where you most want them to hold, at the point of the stranger's entry — or else he is a wrongdoer in every unfamiliar city he sets foot in, which I take to be a reductio. Pick a horn.
