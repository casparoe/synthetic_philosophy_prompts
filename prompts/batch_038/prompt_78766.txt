I am preparing a response essay for a book symposium on Professor Miriam Halloran's recent monograph about individual contributions to collective harm. The editor has asked each respondent to test the book's principles against a case the book does not discuss; the author replies in the same volume. Halloran is known for answering such questions head-on rather than disowning them, so my essay has to commit to a prediction of her verdict before she has written hers. You do not have the book, and you should not try to reconstruct it or to supply references: the whole of her record that matters is set out below, drawn from the cases she rules on in print and in seminar. Describe each candidate principle by its structure rather than by its adherents.

**Her verdicts so far.**

1. A supermarket that orders rotisserie chickens in fixed lots, so that a single purchase has some chance of pushing the store past a reorder point and triggering the slaughter of thousands of birds: buying one is impermissible.
2. A thousand identical ventilation units, each adding to a valley's air a quantity of pollutant too small for any resident to detect, where the combined addition raises the valley's asthma rate appreciably: each unit's operator acts impermissibly. She argues at length here that imperceptibility is not insignificance.
3. A wedding at which a plated chicken course was prepared days earlier, the quantity fixed by headcount, the caterer's future orders unaffected by how much is eaten, and the guest a person who buys no animal products at all and has no other dealings with the industry: eating the plate is permissible. Her gloss is that the guest does nothing and is no part of anything.
4. A beef supply chain that she grants, for the sake of argument, to be perfectly smooth — no lot sizes, no reorder points, no chance that one burger triggers a discrete production decision: buying the burger is still impermissible. She offers no explanation, and readers disagree about which of her commitments is doing the work.
5. Not voting in a national election of two hundred million: impermissible. She is explicit that the probability of being decisive plays no role in this verdict; the wrong, she says, lies in declining to take part in a costly scheme that others uphold.
6. A lake killed by mine runoff forty years ago, with no surviving industry, no scheme of restraint, and nobody abstaining from anything, into which you pour a bucket of the same chemical, making no difference to anyone's prospects: permissible. Her gloss is that the act is not part of anything.
7. A single parent on a tight budget buying the cheaper uncertified fish rather than the certified equivalent at a forty percent premium: permissible, on the stated ground that what we may demand of people depends on what compliance costs them.
8. A traveler who surrenders the last seat on a long-haul flight, which is sold to another passenger within the hour so that the aircraft departs full: permissible, and not thereby pointless, since the futility of an act of restraint is not a reason against performing it.

**The case I have to predict.**

A national fishery operates under a statutory total allowable catch, set five years ahead on the basis of stock assessments and biological reference points, with no provision for consumer-demand data to enter the review. Quota shares are individually transferable and any share a vessel leaves unused is bought up by other vessels, so the landed total equals the statutory figure whatever happens to price. The stock is fished well below sustainable biomass; the gear destroys seabed habitat and kills juvenile bycatch; these harms continue throughout the quota period and, on current settings, beyond it. Aggregate demand matters only politically: the fleet's solvency and the coalition that defends the present total allowable catch rest on the buying of some thirty million households, and a defection of a few hundred thousand of them would probably lower the catch limit at the next review. One household's buying is invisible in the statistics and cannot affect a review already fixed.

Call the buyer M. She knows all of the above. For her case I have stipulated away the familiar escapes: no lot sizes or reorder points anywhere in the chain, no price effect, no signalling to the fleet, no demand signal reaching the review, no statistic in which her purchase appears. On the framework Halloran herself uses for computing expected marginal effects, M.'s purchase has an expected marginal effect of exactly zero — not small, zero — and both Halloran and I accept that stipulation. A certified substitute is available at a fifteen percent premium, comfortably affordable. The purchase would be private and unannounced: no boycott, no pledge, no cooperative scheme, nobody else abstaining, so it neither free-rides on anyone's restraint nor declines membership in anything. M.'s motive is simply that she prefers the taste.

**What I need from you.**

(a) Predict the verdict — impermissible or permissible — and give the justification she would most likely offer in her own terms. Commit to one; do not present a disjunction as your answer.

(b) Set out the principles jointly consistent with precedents 1 through 8. I expect more than one, and I expect several that differ only in an auxiliary clause — a cost condition, a futility condition, a requirement that participation involve a scheme somebody else is upholding, a disjunction of causal and participatory grounds. For each candidate, say which precedents support it and which it must strain to accommodate, and say which candidates the record has already ruled out.

(c) Say where the surviving candidates diverge on the fishery case, and give your reasons for preferring your prediction. Where her verdicts and her stated reasons pull apart — as they do in 4, 5, and 8 — say which you treat as better evidence of her principle, and why.

(d) Identify the closest analog among the eight precedents, list the respects in which the analogy fails, and say which of those failures she would treat as decisive.

(e) If the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so plainly, and then state what she would be committing herself to by answering either way: which precedent she would have to soften, restrict, or explain away, and how her account of individual action would look afterwards. Name the precedent that is doing the most work in your prediction and the one she would most plausibly wish to revise.

(f) Design one further case, specified in enough detail that she could rule on it, that would separate the surviving candidates, and state what each candidate predicts about it.
