I run post-editing QA for a mid-sized publisher of translated narrative non-fiction — memoirs, popular science, long-form journalism — roughly forty chapters a week across eleven source languages, and for the last two years the first draft of most of that material comes from a commercial neural engine rather than from a human translator. Our workflow is still built for the old failure mode: a post-editor reads the target text against the source and logs mistranslations, omissions, and terminology slips. What it structurally cannot catch is the failure that worries me, the one Hofstadter presses in *Le Ton beau de Marot* and returns to in his later essay on Google Translate: not that the engine gets words wrong, but that it never builds the scene those words describe, so the output is fluent, locally impeccable, and unmoored from any state of the world. Two incidents from our own catalogue made this concrete. In a translated memoir, two consecutive pages referred to the narrator's older brother with feminine pronouns throughout, and nobody noticed for six weeks because every individual sentence read perfectly — the engine had no idea who was in the room. In a popular-science chapter, a safety caveat expressed as a double negative in the source emerged as a flat assertion, and the surrounding paragraph was otherwise so clean that the reviewer signed it off. I have also seen the reverse: the engine produce a version of a dense contractual clause that was tighter and more accurate than the human draft, which tells me the procedure cannot rest on a presumption that machine output is suspect.

What I want is something my post-editors can actually run — not an essay, not a philosophy lecture — a named, ordered battery of probes to apply to a chapter in about twenty-five minutes, by people with no training in linguistics who, for at least half our language pairs, cannot read the source at all. That last constraint should shape the design rather than be noted in passing: some diagnostics will have to work monolingually on the target text alone, and the procedure must say explicitly which probes those are and which require a bilingual reader, because a checklist that assumes a reader we do not have is worse than no checklist. For each probe I need the operation to perform on the passage, what a pass looks like, what a fail looks like, the rough time cost, and what a fail licenses us to conclude; and I want the evidential logic stated plainly, including the asymmetry I suspect is real — that a failed probe is evidence about the absence of a constructed scene while a passed probe is not evidence of its presence — together with instructions for what to do given that. Then the decision rules: at what point a post-editor patches a sentence locally, at what point the chapter escalates to a bilingual editor, and at what point we reject the engine run for the whole title and commission a fresh translation, with thresholds stated rather than left to feel. Our senior editor signs off on anything escalated, and the economics should be visible in the thresholds: a false alarm costs us about three days and a freelance re-translation at roughly €600, whereas a miss costs a correction notice and, in the memoir case, an apology to a living author's family.

Three things I would like handled candidly. First, tell me which parts of Hofstadter's argument had to be dropped or distorted to fit inside twenty-five minutes, and which distinctions genuinely had to survive the compression; I would rather be told that the procedure tests a cheap proxy for "no mental movie behind it" than be sold the proxy as the real thing. Second, deal with contamination: our vendor's models are trained on corpora that plausibly contain exactly the sort of probe sentences a clever reviewer would invent, and our style guides are public, so a probe that was diagnostic in 2023 may now be pattern-matched rather than understood — say which probes are most likely to have gone hollow and how the battery should be rotated or varied in response. Third, mark where judgment cannot be eliminated — deliberate ambiguity, wordplay, an author who means to disorient, questions of register and voice — and where the procedure will misfire in a predictable direction. Then run it on three cases: (a) a page of memoir in which the engine has misgendered a recurring character while every sentence remains locally correct; (b) a deliberately vertiginous stretch of literary non-fiction, full of unresolved pronouns and intended contradictions, which I expect the procedure to condemn and which I believe is an excellent translation — here I want you to say outright that the procedure returns the answer I will not like, and what should be allowed to override it; and (c) a technical passage that passes every probe cleanly and nevertheless contains a false statement about the world that no probe we could afford to run would expose. Close with what the procedure cannot do at all, and where it will make my post-editors worse rather than better.
