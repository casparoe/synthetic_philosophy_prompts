I'm co-leading a reading group that's a mix of psych grad students and philosophy-of-religion people, and we meet in the basement of a church, which everybody finds funny. One of the regulars is a practicing analyst who leans Jungian and keeps trying to get me to admit I'm a closet theologian, and another keeps insisting my position is just Victorian rationalism with a Vienna accent. For the next session I've been asked to put my own view on Freud and religion on the table in numbered form so the group can attack it properly. Before I hand it over I'd like an audit from you on whether the set actually hangs together.

Here's the list, roughly as I'd present it:

1. The charge I care about is the illusion charge, not the falsity charge. What's objectionable about religious belief on Freud's account is that wish-fulfilment is doing the motivating and the holder stops caring whether reality bears the belief out. So the epistemic problem is about how the belief is held, not about getting the facts wrong.
2. I've cut the father out entirely. Neither the longing for a protective daddy in The Future of an Illusion nor the primal-parricide business in Totem and Taboo is load bearing for me. The engine is just helplessness: you're mortal, the world is indifferent, nobody is coming. You don't need a paternal imago to get that.
3. But I want to keep the obsessional neurosis analogy, and I think its the most durable thing Freud ever said about religion — the structural match between religious ceremonial and compulsive ritual. I'd defend that in a room full of people who think psychoanalysis is a dead science.
4. Rolland's oceanic feeling is a real datum, not confabulation or reporting bias, and Freud's deflationary reading of it — a residue of the infant's not-yet-bounded ego-feeling — is basically right.
5. I'm an old fashioned secularization optimist: religion gets outgrown the way childhood neuroses get outgrown, rather than refuted. Science and the primacy of the intellect just leave it behind eventually.
6. At the same time I'm all in on the Discontents picture, death drive included. Aggression can't be eliminated, renunciation keeps manufacturing guilt, the superego ratchets its demands upward every time you comply with them, and the strain is structural and permanent rather than a developmental stage humanity passes through.
7. I still go to services and keep my family's rituals, and I don't think that costs me anything, since it's practice and not assent.
8. And I'm careful not to commit the genetic fallacy — explaining why someone holds a belief doesn't tell you whether its true.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, ranked by how bad the tension is, and for each one say what has to give, what the cheapest repair looks like, and what the repair costs me elsewhere on the list. I don't want a general tour of where Freud contradicts himself — I know he does, and I'm not asking you to settle it for him. I want to know which of my own commitments can't all stand. Just as importantly, tell me which of these look like problems and aren't, because a good chunk of the attacks I've gotten so far have been false alarms and I'd rather find out which before the session. No page refs or bibliography needed, I just want the argument.
