Until recently I spent forty years in plasma physics, and only since retiring have I begun attending a philosophy-of-mind reading group, where two members have now worked through the causal exclusion argument across three consecutive meetings without settling it. They have asked me to referee, and since I lack the training to do so myself, I would like your adjudication.

(1) Neil opened with the problem as he understands it: physical effects have sufficient physical causes; if my decision to reach for a glass also causes the reaching, then either the decision is identical to its neural realizer or the effect is systematically overdetermined; the second disjunct is absurd; so anyone who wants the mental to be causally real must either identify mental properties with neural ones or concede that they are inert.

(2) Carol answered that overdetermination is objectionable only when the two causes are independent — two snipers, say — whereas a mental property and its realizer are metaphysically dependent, so nothing untoward arises. Neil replied that this restates the difficulty rather than dissolving it: dependence without identity leaves the mental property no causal work to do beyond what the realizer already does, and he accused her of assuming what she needed to establish.

(3) Carol then invoked proportionality: the cause should be proportionate to the effect, and my seeing a surface as red rather than crimson is proportionate to my reaching, whereas the exact neural state is too specific, since in nearby situations in which the reaching still occurs that state differs. Neil countered that proportionality is an explanatory or epistemic virtue, and that suiting a generalization better cannot confer efficacy on something that has none; he added that if the mental property genuinely screens off the neural one, then decades of intervention on neurons producing behavioral change is inexplicable.

(4) Carol's last move was that Neil's argument quietly assumes causation is a single determinate relation, whereas causal claims at different levels may be true in different respects. Neil said that abandoning that assumption costs her precisely what she set out to save: properties that move muscles rather than redescribing what moves them.

Please proceed move by move rather than merely declaring an overall winner. Rate each exchange — and, where one exchange contains two separable claims, rate them separately — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating. Then say what both of them appear to be ignoring, including whether the closure of the physical that Neil starts from is an empirical finding of the sort I could in principle have tested in the laboratory, or a methodological stipulation, and whether that difference changes who wins.
