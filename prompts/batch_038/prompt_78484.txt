I chair the technical panel that ranks competing health programmes for a national health service, so I spend my working life producing orderings over bundles of outcomes. I am fluent in the apparatus this normally uses — QALYs and DALYs, cost-effectiveness thresholds, severity weights, the rule of rescue, prioritarianism as a distributional rule — and because we run a fair amount of stated-preference elicitation, I also know the paired-comparison machinery (Thurstone, Bradley–Terry and their descendants) that turns a table of verdicts into a latent cardinal scale. What follows is not my professional position. It is a record of my own considered verdicts, which I have written down because they embarrass me.

The ladder. Six conditions, each lasting one year and then resolved; the patients are otherwise identical.

- S1: constant, overwhelming pain, no sleep, complete incapacity; the patient, if able to communicate, would say they prefer death.
- S2: the same, with two or three hours of relief per day; complete incapacity.
- S3: severe pain on most days; some self-care possible; no work.
- S4: moderate pain daily; works part-time; sleeps badly.
- S5: intermittent mild pain; normal life with occasional flare-ups.
- S6: a nuisance — a persistent low-grade irritation, of the sort you stop noticing within minutes; no measurable effect on function.

Verdicts about numbers. One year each, everything else held equal. I also hold, without exception, that within a severity level more people is worse, with no upper limit on how much worse.

1. 1,000 people at S2 is worse than 1 person at S1.
2. 1,000 at S3 is worse than 1 at S2.
3. 1,000 at S4 is worse than 1 at S3.
4. 1,000 at S5 is worse than 1 at S4.
5. 1,000 at S6 is worse than 1 at S5.

Each of these felt like the same judgement, and about equally obvious. But then:

6. One billion people at S6 for a year is not worse than one person at S1 for a year. Neither is ten billion. I have tried hard to talk myself out of this and cannot.

Verdicts about duration.

7. One hour at S1 is worse than one year at S6.
8. One hour at S1 is worse than one month at S2.
9. One year at S4 is worse than one hour at S3.

Verdicts about context.

10. In a live triage decision I would spend the entire remaining budget on a single S1 patient rather than on fifty S5 patients. In the abstract scoring exercise I score the fifty S5 patients higher, and I have reproduced that flip in colleagues: the ranking changes with the framing, and each framing feels correct from inside it.

My tentative principle, offered for you to break: rank by the sum over individuals of w(s)·d, with w increasing, w rising much faster than proportionally as severity rises, and w(S6) = 0, so that trivial conditions never aggregate however many people have them. I already suspect this is inconsistent with something above and I cannot see where, so treat it as material to be tested and repaired rather than as a position I am attached to.

What I want:

- The candidate principle or principles that deliver my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could compute with them — not described in adjectives. If no single one does it, give me the shortest list that covers the space.
- Whether the additive escape is available at all: someone will tell me there is no intransitivity here, only an insufficiently steep severity weight. Check that against my actual numbers. If it works, tell me what ratio between the top and bottom of the ladder my list forces, what it forces at each individual rung, and whether a function with those parameters is anything I could defend to a committee rather than a redescription of the verdicts it was fitted to.
- Which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate. Say explicitly which ones you would have me give up, and in what order of reluctance.
- What your preferred candidate predicts for a case I have not listed — ideally one where you expect my own intuition to come out the other way, so that I can go and check.
- Whether my stability evidence matters. These verdicts survive re-elicitation after weeks, shuffled presentation order, reframing, and different numerical anchors; the same people give the same answers in the same direction. Does that rule out treating the pattern as noise in a latent-scale model, or can a heavy-tailed or context-dependent paired-comparison model generate exactly this? I want a real answer here, not a shrug. And then: given that stability, is the money-pump objection fatal to my position, or does it only show something about the pump?

Two requests about how you answer. First, be as committed as your reasons allow: if two candidates genuinely tie on my list, say they tie instead of manufacturing a tiebreaker; if one is clearly better, say so and stop hedging. Where you are reconstructing what I must have meant rather than reporting what I said, flag it and give me the alternative reading. Second, I am not asking for a survey or for references — I know the philosophical examples involving torture and a great many hangnails exist, and I do not need them named. I want your reconstruction and your verdict.

And if you think the request itself is badly posed, say so plainly rather than complying politely. If my list is stacked — if one of the items is a framing artefact, if I have described the ladder so coarsely that no interesting principle is at stake, or if verdicts 1 through 6 are simply not the kind of thing a principle can capture together — tell me that, tell me which item you would strike, and tell me what you would need me to re-elicit before the question became answerable.
