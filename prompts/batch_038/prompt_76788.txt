**DRAFT AFTERWORD: "The Word the Novel Refuses"**

Raphael Lemkin escaped occupied Poland through the Baltic, reached the United States in 1941, and in 1944 published a fat, unglamorous book about how the occupiers governed Europe. In one chapter of it he proposed a word: genocide. He built it from the Greek *genos*, race or tribe, and the Latin ending that carries murder and suicide. For Lemkin the word was never a synonym for massacre. What he had in view was a coordinated plan aimed at disintegrating the things that make a national group a group — its political and social institutions, its language, its religion, its economic life, its sense of itself — together with the security, health, dignity and lives of the individuals belonging to it. He catalogued techniques accordingly, across domains political, social, cultural, economic, biological, religious, moral and physical. A group can be destroyed while every one of its members survives.

He had tried something like this before. At an international conference on the unification of criminal law held in Madrid in 1933 he read a paper proposing that acts of barbarity and acts of vandalism be outlawed as crimes against the law of nations, and it was in that paper that the word genocide first appeared.

Nuremberg disappointed him. The judgment used his word in describing what had been done to civilian populations in occupied territories, but no defendant was convicted of genocide as such, and the tribunal's jurisdiction over crimes against humanity was tethered to the war, which left the pre-1939 persecution of the Jews inside Germany largely beyond its reach. The Convention adopted by the General Assembly on 9 December 1948 was his consolation and his defeat. It is narrower than his conception in one decisive respect: it protects national, ethnical, racial and religious groups, and political groups were struck out after sustained opposition, led most prominently by the Soviet delegation. It also omits the clause on cultural destruction that circulated in the early drafts, though the forcible transfer of children survived — a cultural technique in legal dress. Article II requires that the prohibited acts be committed with intent to destroy the group as such, in whole; that requirement is why so much modern argument about genocide is an argument about arithmetic.

The United States, which had championed the treaty, ratified it promptly in 1949. Lemkin spent his last years in New York, poor, tireless, lobbying one government at a time. He died in 1959.

My novel never uses his word. It appears nowhere in the narration and nowhere in the dialogue, and it will appear nowhere on the book's jacket. I want to defend that decision here, and to do so I need three terms.

The first is *definitional drag*. Law proceeds by elements — a protected group, an enumerated act, a specific intent. Narrative proceeds by particulars. Every time a novel must establish an element, it pays a cost in momentum, and the cost is highest exactly where the stakes are highest. Courtroom fiction pays it constantly and hides it badly. Drag is the reason a trial scene about atrocity tends to become, against its author's will, a seminar.

The second is *lexical embargo*: the deliberate withholding of the governing term from the text, so that the term arrives in the reader's mind rather than on the page. The embargo has a *threshold scene* — not a moment in the story but a position in the reading, the point past which the reader is licensed to supply the word and cannot plausibly claim to have withheld judgment. Everything before it must be description; everything after it is complicity, and the book's moral shape is the distance between the two.

The third is the *part/whole problem in fiction*. The Convention permits an intent to destroy a group; a novel can only ever present a part — three families, one town, one winter. The reader's verdict therefore rests on a part in every case, and the novel's honesty consists in refusing to let the part masquerade as the whole. A book that ends with the word spoken aloud has committed, in its final paragraph, the very sin of scale that the crime consists in.

There is a further asymmetry I want to press, because it is the reason a novelist has any business near this material at all. The law generally requires that the group exist before the crime, and the tribunals eventually had to concede, uneasily, that a group may be constituted by the perception of those doing the classifying. Fiction does not need the concession. Fiction can show the group being made inside the crime: the register, the certificate, the armband, the census column that did not exist in the spring and governs the winter. The law arrives after the category and tries to honor it; the novel can watch the category being built, which is the only vantage from which the word itself can be seen as an artifact.

Someone will say that the embargo is a game played upon the dead; that a term won at enormous cost, by people who needed it to name what was done to them, is not a novelist's to withhold for effect; that recognition withheld is recognition denied. I have an answer about cheap recognition conferred by narrators, and about the reader's labor being the only labor that counts, but I suspect my answer is weaker than the objection and I would rather see the objection at full strength.
