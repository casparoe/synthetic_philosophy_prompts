Our city extended rent stabilization last spring to buildings like the duplex my parents own, and that argument has been at basically every family dinner since. I'm not a landlord, I lean sympathetic to tenants, and I still can't tell which of the standard replies actually land. Now our neighborhood association is running a public forum and I somehow got handed the Q&A.

The guest is an economist who defends rent stabilization as real housing policy rather than a second-best compromise, and part of her arguement is that housing isn't a commodity like other goods, so a market-clearing price isn't the right target to begin with. She's very good at ending an exchange with two moves: "the supply objection just assumes an elasticity the data doesn't support," and "you're telling me who benefits, not whether it's right."

Write me eight to ten questions that don't let either move close things down, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want coverage of: who actually gets protected versus who needs it (long-tenure, higher-income incumbents versus newcomers); the vacancy-decontrol problem — turnover rents jumping, tenants locked in place, mobility falling — and whether she thinks that cuts for or against her; who really eats the incidence, small landlords or large ones; what she does with the fact that economists are overwhelmingly hostile to price controls as a class; and one that forces her to explain why the same anti-commodification logic doesn't reach labor, becuse a wage floor is the obvious next step and I suspect she wants it. Nothing gotcha, but no softballs either — half the room is tenants and half is landlords, and they all know this stuff already.

American English, please.
