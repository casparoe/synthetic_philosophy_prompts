Retired soil microbiologist here — thirty-five years of counting things in dirt, and only in the last couple of years any philosophy at all, mostly whatever my daughter posts through the door. She keeps telling me there's a name for what I'm about to describe, and I can't find it.

Our regional museum is redoing the living-world room. Volunteer-run, one paid curator, money from a lottery grant. The original plan was lovely and simple: three cases, labelled Alive, Not alive, and Once alive, and every specimen gets a tick, a cross or a question mark. The funder has been quite firm that a visitor should be able to walk out able to answer yes or no, and the designer has already mocked up placards that read IS IT ALIVE? with two boxes to tick.

The trouble is my tray of awkward things won't go in the cases. Sitting on the bench in the back room right now:

- a sealed ampoule of tobacco mosaic virus, which I've worked with since I was a postgrad;
- a jar of prion-infected brain tissue a veterinary school has offered to lend (sealed, obviously);
- a tardigrade in its dried-up tun state, on a slide since 1998, which may or may not ever wake up — nobody's going to water it during opening hours;
- a HeLa culture the hospital lab says we can keep in a borrowed incubator. Feeds itself, divides, has been going for seventy-odd years, but is nowhere near being an animal. The technician also mentioned there's a consent question attached to those cells that I'd want to look into before we put a name on the label;
- a paper bag of seeds, forty years old, from a local seed bank;
- a mule — the farmer down the road will bring her in on open days. Alive by any measure I know, and she will never have young;
- a slime mould, currently dry and inert in a petri dish, which solves mazes when you damp it;
- a living sponge in a tank, which is one animal or a crowd depending on the day you ask;
- and my grandson's contribution, a laptop running one of those grid games where little arrow-shaped things march across the screen and a structure that looks for all the world like a factory spits out more of them.

Two of the panel are now having a proper row. One says it's easy: if its got metabolism it's alive, end of, and the virus, the prion, the tun and the seeds are all out. The other says metabolism is a chemist's prejudice — what matters is whether the thing can change across generations, which lets the virus in, throws the mule out and leaves the cells in the incubator in a muddle. And both of them talk as though one of them is simply correct and the other hasn't looked hard enough, as if this were a measurement we could go and take. After a career of taking measurements I'm no longer so sure that's what's going on.

So, help me with Thursday's meeting. Is there a fact of the matter about which of those two trays is right, or is the panel arguing about something else entirely and not noticing? If it can't be settled, what should we actually do — pick a side and print the tick-a-box placards anyway, or scrap the format and put the disagreement on the wall? I'd like something concrete enough to act on, including what to say about each of the awkward specimens one by one, because I am not quietly dropping the 1998 tardigrade or the prion jar just for being inconvenient. And please don't tell me to leave it open to interpretation; the grant people will eat us alive if the labels have no answers on them. British English, if you would, and assume I know my biology but none of your vocabulary.
