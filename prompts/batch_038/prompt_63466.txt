Here's roughly what it says — I'm paraphrasing from memory, not quoting:

- The octopus thought experiment from Bender and Koller is basically Searle's Chinese room updated for neural nets: swap the guy in the room following rules for a model trained on text, and you get the same conclusion by the same route.
- Their point is that meaning is a mapping between linguistic form and a speaker's communicative intent, and a system trained only on form never gets access to that mapping. So it can't have learned meaning. Full stop.
- Which also means benchmarks are a dead end. Any test we run is text in, text out, so a model that aces it has only proven it's a better parrot. No evaluation could in principle separate understanding from very good mimicry.
- The reason everyone keeps getting fooled is a bug in us rather than a feature of them — humans automatically read intention into fluent text.
- Understanding is binary. You have it or you don't. So "it sort of understands" isn't a real position, and anyone telling you their model understands is either confused or selling something.
- The stochastic parrots paper showed that scaling just buys you better memorization and recombination of the training data.
- And the kicker: this is settled as far as linguistics goes, and the pushback is all from ML people who have a commercial stake in the answer.

What I need is a pass over these one at a time: which are accurate, which are defensible simplifications that a careful reader would still wince at, and which are wrong or just don't follow.
