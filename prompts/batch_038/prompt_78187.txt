Boltzmann's alternative reply to Zermelo's recurrence argument conceded that if the universe as a whole is at or near equilibrium, the ordered region we inhabit is best understood as a rare fluctuation; Eddington's later observation drove the point home in the opposite direction, since observers inside a fluctuation of our size would be vastly outnumbered by observers inside much smaller ones. Contemporary cosmology inherits the structure of that exchange. On a theory with an eternal de Sitter phase, thermal fluctuation will occasionally produce a momentary self-aware brain complete with apparent memories and apparent surroundings. Let T be such a theory, stipulated for the sake of argument, on which fluctuation-produced observers outnumber observers produced by ordinary cosmological history by some colossal factor.

The reductio against T is familiar. Were T true, you would overwhelmingly likely be such a fluctuation-observer; your evidence would be random; hence you would have no grounds for accepting T. The theory's own prediction about your situation destroys the warrant for believing it. This is the objection sometimes called cognitive instability, and it is widely treated as decisive.

Please argue that it is not decisive, and further that it is not available to the cosmologist who presses it. The position to be defended has roughly this shape. The reductio assumes that the justificatory status of a belief is fixed by the present content of the believer's evidence, independently of how that content came to be there, whereas justification is historical: it is a matter of the provenance of one's belief-forming processes. Granting that, the fluctuation-observer and the ordinary observer in a T-world are not in the same evidential situation, even if their momentary experiences are indistinguishable from within. The ordinary observer's evidence has a genealogy that licenses reliance upon it, and that genealogy is not erased by T, because T does not claim that every observer is a fluctuation — only that fluctuation-observers are more numerous. T is then unparsimonious and explanatorily extravagant, but not self-defeating, and an ordinary believer may rationally accept T while taking herself to belong to the minority. As a further commitment of the position, the step from most observers in a T-world being fluctuation-observers to your being one is not a bare statistical syllogism; it requires a self-locating typicality assumption that the theory itself cannot discharge.

In defending this, confront at least the following. First, whether appealing to the coherence and predictive success of your own past experience merely reinstates the reliability assumption under threat, so that the defense begs the question against the reductio rather than answering it. Second, the point that under T the genealogy in question is almost certainly illusory, which would mean that the very feature treated as evidentially distinguishing loses its force precisely when the hypothesis is granted. Third, whether your line generalizes to skeptical hypotheses about the external world; and if it does, whether that generality is a cost — the defense of T collapsing into a refusal to engage skepticism at all — or a benefit, showing that the reductio was never a problem peculiar to cosmology.

I want a sustained argument rather than a survey of the dialectic. State your premises explicitly, and say plainly where you take the argument to be most vulnerable.
