For our foundations reading group, a classmate circulated what she takes to be a refutation of the axiom of choice. It runs as follows.

1. A set-existence principle is acceptable only if the objects it guarantees could at least in principle be exhibited — picked out by some explicit definition, rather than merely asserted to be there.
2. The axiom of choice guarantees objects that no definition can exhibit: a well-ordering of the continuum, a basis for the reals regarded as a vector space over the rationals, a non-principal ultrafilter on the natural numbers.
3. Therefore the axiom of choice is unacceptable.
4. Nor is this an aesthetic fastidiousness. The same guarantees yield a finite partition of a solid ball whose pieces reassemble into two balls of the original size, so accepting choice means accepting that perfectly definite regions of space lack any determinate volume.
5. Giving up choice does forfeit Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff, the existence of bases, and the trichotomy of cardinalities; but each of these is a theorem about objects already disqualified by (1), so the forfeiture costs nothing worth having.
6. Hence we should work in a set theory without choice, and thereby obtain a mathematics free of inexplicable objects.

I cannot accept (6), yet I have been unable to say where the chain breaks; each premise looked unobjectionable to me when I first read it, and I have checked that the steps are formally valid. Of (1) and (4) I would rather keep (1), since the paradoxical decomposition strikes me as the less troubling of the two.

Diagnose the argument for me: say which premise or premises you reject, explain what made each of them seem safe on first pass, and state what is lost by rejecting them — including whether my preference for (1) over (4) can be honored, and if not, which of the two is genuinely the cheaper sacrifice.
