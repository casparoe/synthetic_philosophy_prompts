Right, this one's got me properly stuck and I've run out of people to ask. Context: I work in a small research office at a uni, and about six weeks ago there was a grievence over a promotions round. Someone asked me, in writing, whether a candidate was strong enough on research to be shortlisted. I replied: "Priya gets on brilliantly with the students and I've never once known her miss a deadline." I didn't say anything about her research at all. The panel decided I'd implied she was weak on research — fair enough, that's more or less what I was doing — but the complaint now being made against me is framed as though I'd said it, and the letter I've been sent actually uses the word "stated". I've been building an argument out of the Grice stuff my reading group did last term (the one on logic and conversation) to push back, and the trouble is it now seems to prove a lot more than I wanted, and possibly to sink my own defence.

Here it is. (1) On Grice's account, for a speaker to mean something is for her to intend to produce some effect in an audience by way of that audience's recognising the intention. (2) Conversational implicature is a species of speaker meaning: in implicating q, the speaker means q without saying q. (3) Whatever a speaker means, she is answerable for it — that's precisely what makes meaning a matter of commitment rather than mere causation. (4) So in implicating q I'm answerable for q in exactly the way I'd be answerable for saying q; the difference between them is a difference in how the content gets delivered, not in how much I'm on the hook for. (5) But the point of implicating rather than saying is that you keep a way out — you can deny q afterwards, and the denial isn't a lie. (6) You can't be answerable for q and have a genuine way out of q. (7) So either conversational implicature isn't speaker meaning, or nobody ever implicates anything, or deniability is an illusion. My committee has effectively accepted (4), which is the bit I want to deny; but (7) is absurd, because it makes out that hinting is either impossible or a species of lying, and I'd rather lose (3) than accept that — though I can't see how (3) can be false without collapsing speaker meaning into something merely causal, which was the whole reason it was defined that way in the first place.

Two extra things so you can see where the pressure sits. In the same thread I also wrote "the teaching observations on her are impressive", and there I'd swear blind I meant nothing beyond the words — yet a derivation to something like "and that's all there is to say in her favour" seems just as available to a hostile reader as the other one was. And seperately, a friend says (5) is smuggling in two different senses of having a way out, but she wouldn't say which two, and I don't think she's right anyway, because whichever sense you take, (6) still looks true to me.

So: tell me which step fails — or if none of them does, which one I should sacrifice and what it costs, with the cost actually stated rather than a menu. Please don't give me three respectable readings and wish me luck; commit to one, argue for it, and if you think the argument goes through and I'll just have to eat (7), say so plainly and tell me what that does to my defence.
