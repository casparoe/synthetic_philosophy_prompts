Thirty-five years in a diffraction lab and now I'm three years retired and reading philosophy instead of golfing, which my wife has opinions about. The reading group has drifted into philosophy of economics and I'm out of my depth, but I built an argument last week that I can't make peace with, and I'd rather have it taken apart properly than be reassured.

Call the view RP: an agent's choices are rational just in case there's some preference ordering under which, on each occasion, what they picked is at least as good as everything else they could have afforded.

(1) If RP is supposed to be about behavior and not mind-reading, then "there is an ordering that rationalizes the choices" can mean nothing more than "the choices admit of a ranking that puts what was picked at the top."
(2) Given any finite list of shopping trips, do this: for each trip, rank the basket they actually bought above the other baskets on the list. Done. That is a ranking putting what was picked at the top.
(3) So any finite body of choice data is rationalizable, however perverse — including somebody who cycles through three baskets such that each was affordable the time they reached for the next one.
(4) A hypothesis that no finite body of data could contradict is not an empirical hypothesis; it's a label applied to behavior after the fact.
(5) So RP is empty, and "utility" on this reading is bookkeeping.

The trouble. My niece does demand analysis and she killed (3) on the back of an envelope. Apples and bread, two trips. Trip one: apples at 2, bread at 1, she spends 11 and buys 5 apples and 1 loaf. Trip two: apples at 1, bread at 2, she spends 11 and buys 1 apple and 5 loaves. The second basket would have cost 7 on the first trip, the first basket would have cost 7 on the second trip. Each was strictly affordable when the other was chosen, so each is strictly revealed preferred to the the other, and no transitive ordering says both. She says there's a theorem of Afriat's (I hope I have the name right) that says exactly which data sets can be rescued, and an axiom, GARP, that tests it.

So (3) is dead. But (2) looks like arithmetic — you can always rank a finite list with the chosen item on top — and I can't locate the slide. My suspicion is that repairing (2) forces me to give up (1), and that's the part that bothers me, because then RP is not a behaviorist theory at all and the whole enterprise is about something I could never observe from a bench.

So: which step is the bad one, and what exactly is the equivocation or scope shift — I want the mechanism, not a slogan like "you forgot transitivity." Then price the repairs. What does it cost to hold onto (1), what does it cost to drop it, and does your answer change if I swap "finite list of trips" for "everything the agent will ever do"? Given my example is dead, what is left of the worry in (5) — I notice that even where data does constrain things, plenty of different utility functions fit the same choices and I don't see the data picking between them, so in what sense is there a utility there to mean anything? One last thing I keep chewing on: she said her counterexample only bites if you also assume more is always better, which struck me as a strange thing for a refutation to be leaning on.
