Last week our reading group on the philosophy of science met after a public lecture on numerical weather prediction, and two of the members — I shall call them Marta and Julian — carried on arguing in the corridor for the better part of an hour without reaching any sort of conclusion. I have reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can, in the order it happened. I have opinions of my own about most of it, but I would rather not signal them, since what interests me is which of their moves withstand scrutiny and which do not.

(1) Marta began from the lecture's central claim — that a detailed forecast beyond roughly a fortnight is hopeless because of sensitive dependence on initial conditions — and said that this quietly settles a very old metaphysical question. Laplace's demon is finished, she argued, and not merely because of quantum mechanics: a strictly classical, wholly deterministic system can be one too, so determinism turns out to buy no predictive power even in principle. (2) Julian granted that the demon is finished as a practical programme, but said Marta was making the standard popular error. Determinism, on his telling, is a claim about the world: given the state at one time and single-valued laws, the state at every later time is fixed. Predictability is a claim about us. Chaos is a result about the second and no sort of counterexample to the first; to infer from the divergence of computed trajectories that the world is indeterministic is, he said, like inferring from the fact that a door is locked that the room behind it does not exist. (3) Marta replied that this is a verbal victory rather than a substantive one. Determinism is empty if no possible being could ever exploit it: since two initial conditions differing by less than any physically realisable measurement — by less than a Planck length, if one insists — may be assigned wildly different futures, the phrase 'the state fixes the future' can never be cashed in by anything. A thesis that could in principle make no difference to any possible evidence, she said, is an ornament, not a doctrine. (4) Julian answered that content and accessibility are different properties. There are truths nobody can check — the arrangement of matter beyond our cosmological horizon, the trillionth decimal of π — and their uncheckability does not make them non-factual. He added a technical qualification: chaos does not defeat computation as cleanly as Marta supposed, because for uniformly hyperbolic systems there is a shadowing theorem guaranteeing that a numerically computed trajectory, corrupted as it is by round-off error, remains within a bounded distance of some genuine trajectory of the system. (5) Marta said the shadowing theorem concedes her case rather than answering it. Which genuine trajectory? It corresponds to some initial condition no one can identify, so what the modeller has established is that some member of a neighbourhood of candidate systems behaves in a certain way, not that the actual system does. And the theorem requires hyperbolicity, an idealisation she claimed most realistic systems, the atmosphere emphatically included, violate — in such cases computed orbits can fail to stay near any true orbit for long stretches. So, she concluded, the practical upshot is intact: individual trajectories are unforecastable. (6) Julian said the upshot is intact but far narrower than she was treating it. Even where trajectories separate exponentially, a system's statistical properties may be stable and pinned down by the dynamics — a climate, as it were, rather than a weather — and that is precisely what modellers do forecast successfully. Chaotic systems, on his account, support reliable predictions of frequencies, means and the geometry of the attractor; they simply do not support reliable predictions of particular states. (7) Marta said this concession was worth more than he realised, since a statistical prediction is a probability, and in a deterministic world a probability can only measure somebody's ignorance. If the future state is already fixed, the chance of it is one, and his stable statistics are just our way of describing what we cannot resolve: the successes of climate modelling are successes at managing ignorance, which is what she had been saying all along. (8) Julian called that incoherent. The numbers in question are not chosen by the modeller; they are selected by the dynamics, which is why independent groups modelling the same system converge on the same statistics before they have any data about particular runs. If those numbers were merely bookkeeping for ignorance, that convergence would be a standing miracle, and it would be inexplicable why a tossed coin lands heads about half the time in a world where its motion is, so far as anyone can tell, perfectly deterministic. There are therefore objective chances in deterministic systems, he said, and Marta's dichotomy of chance-or-ignorance is false. (9) Marta's last move was that he is renaming ignorance. She asked what would make his objective chance of one-half incorrect, and said that if the answer is 'nothing, because the outcome was fixed throughout', the number is doing no metaphysical work at all. When Julian protested that the same objection would empty out every chance, since even in an indeterministic world the laws are simply what they are, she said this was a tu quoque: there the chance is fixed by the laws themselves, which is exactly the footing his deterministic chance lacks.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who is right at each point, rather than only who prevails on the whole? For each numbered step, say whether it is sound, partly sound or mistaken, and where it is partly sound, separate the portion that survives from the portion that does not. Then, once you have done that, please tell me three further things: how much of the exchange is a substantive disagreement and how much is the two of them using 'determinism' or 'prediction' in incompatible senses without noticing; whether the closing standoff over chance in a deterministic system can be broken, and if it can, at whose expense; and which two or three considerations neither of them put on the table that a competent referee would have wanted to see there. I am after the reasoning rather than a list of authorities — please do not send me a bibliography, and do not feel obliged to say who advanced an argument first.
