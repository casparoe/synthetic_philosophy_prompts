I teach a 16-person upper-level seminar on eighteenth-century political economy, 80 minutes, and instead of talking about Hume's monetary thought experiments I want to stage them as an actual market game. Half the class has had one intro econ course and half has had none, so nothing can depend on their knowing terms like "quantity theory" — in fact I want every label withheld (neutrality, specie flow, velocity, all of it) until the debrief, so they run into the mechanism before anyone names it.

What I want them to come away having felt, roughly in this order:

1. that the total number of tokens in the room doesn't decide who ends up well off;
2. that it matters enormously *in between* — between the moment new tokens appear and the moment everything has re-priced — and specifically that who gets the new tokens first, and whether they arrive evenly or in one clump, changes real outcomes;
3. that a group cannot hold onto its tokens by refusing to let them leave, and that trying to makes it worse off.

Materials I can get: poker chips, dried beans, index cards, whiteboard markers. No real money, no laptops, no phones. I have one TA who could staff a desk. The room is a normal seminar room with tables that seat four.

They'll have read "Of Money" and "Of the Balance of Trade" beforehand, but I don't want the game to be a book report on those essays — I want the essays to be what we discover we've been enacting. The two bits I have in mind are Hume's opening claim that money is none of the wheels of trade, more like the oil that makes the wheels turn easier, and the balance-of-trade supposition of all the money of Britain being multiplied fivefold in a night with the question of what follows. That second one is basically the game, except staged so nobody sees it coming.

Here's the wrinkle I most want built in, and I've never seen anyone do it: a third act where paper enters. I want to hand out paper notes that the TA's "foreign desk" flatly refuses to accept for anything, so students discover that chips — which can be carried out of the room and spent at that desk — and notes behave differently even when the two are nominally interchangeable inside the room. That's Hume's suspicion about a counterfeit money foreigners won't take, and his odd conclusion that the best bank imaginable would be one that locked up all the money it received and never returned any of it to circulation. I want them to arrive at that from play, not from me reciting it.

Give me: roles, rules, a scoring scheme (crucially *what* they're scored on — my hunch is it should not be tokens held, and I want that reveal to land), a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto specific claims in the essays. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong. Last year I ran a crude version where I doubled everybody's chips at once, and within about ninety seconds every posted price had doubled and the class shrugged and said money is just a veil, so that's the failure mode to design against hardest. Also a couple of students will almost certainly corner one of the goods and price-gouge, and I dont know whether to let that run or step in.

Finally be honest about what this game can't reproduce. My own guess: with output fixed by the rules, the game can show price effects and flow effects but not Hume's stranger claim that a growing money supply actually quickens industry itself — the part where he says the stretch of time between acquiring the precious metals and the general rise of prices is when labour gets its spur. If that's right, tell me what I have to supply by hand in the debrief to cover the gap. And tell me whether the game can stage the tension I want to end on: Hume says he should "as soon dread, that all our springs and rivers should be exhausted, as that money should abandon a kingdom where there are people and industry," and yet he also thinks it's the magistrate's business to keep the money supply increasing if he possibly can. Can sixteen people and a bag of beans make those two claims sit uncomfortably next to each other, or is that just a conversation I have to have separately?
