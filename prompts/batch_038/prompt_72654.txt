I co-ordinate a reading group on the metaphysics of digital things, and this term we keep splitting on a pair of questions that turn out to be harder to separate than we expected: whether the objects inside a long-running online world are real objects — genuinely existing digital particulars with causal powers of their own — or sophisticated fictions, closer to the furniture of a novel that happens to be rendered in real time; and whether anything done to such an object can harm anyone, or whether all the harm is really spillover into the offline psychology of the players. Everyone in the group can state the competing positions fluently, and that is precisely the problem: fluency has let both sides treat the disagreement as verbal. What I want for our next session is a story that makes the difference *felt*, built around a mechanic that has not been written about before, so that nobody can reach for a summary they already know. Please write that story — roughly 2,200 to 2,800 words.

Build it in the world I have sketched below, and feel free to alter incidental details but not the machinery, since the machinery is what generates the case. Vellum is a persistent world that has been running continuously for thirty-one years. Because rendering everything is expensive, its operators enforce an attenuation policy: any object that no client has rendered for ninety days has its full state discarded and replaced by an attestation — a cryptographic fingerprint plus a ledger entry recording ownership, dimensions, listed materials, price history, and a short description written at the time of attenuation. Attested objects remain fully tradable, inheritable, insurable, and usable as collateral, and something like four-fifths of Vellum's economy consists of objects no server has rendered in years. An attested object can be recalled, i.e. re-instantiated on demand; but recall reconstructs everything the attestation does not pin down by sampling from a generator trained on everything players have ever made, so a recalled object matches its description exactly and matches its history only approximately. The story concerns a claim in Vellum's Court of Continuities. The claimant, who plays under the handle Wrenn, spent nine years building a narrow working ferry with her brother Tomas, plank by plank; Tomas cut a notch in each plank as he fitted it, forty-one notches in all. Tomas has been dead in the world outside for two years. The ferry was left moored at a dock nobody visits and attenuated eleven months ago. The defendant, a salvage operator who acquired the ferry through a chain of title running from an unpaid storage lien, recalled it and fed it to a smelter in front of an audience to mint currency. Wrenn does not sue for the value, which was paid into escrow without argument; she sues for the notches. The recall produced a ferry with forty-one notches, uniformly cut, in the wrong planks: correct as to number, wrong as to hand. The defendant's case is that the object he recalled was a fresh construction that never had her notches in it, so nothing of hers was destroyed — and, more provocatively, that even before attenuation the ferry was never more than a description plus a disposition to be described that way, so she has never been anywhere near an object capable of being destroyed. Wrenn's case is that a thing which can be replaced without remainder cannot have been what she lost, and she cannot say what she lost. Tell it in the first person, past tense, from the point of view of the arbiter who must write the ruling — a former records conservator employed remotely, who began the shift inclined to the defendant's deflationary view and who writes findings of fact for a living, so the prose should start dry and precise and gradually fail to keep itself that way. Let there be a coda of scope: the operators have announced that in eighteen months they will switch off the render farm entirely and keep only the ledger, so Vellum will become a wholly attested world — nothing rendered anywhere, yet still trading, still litigating, still inheritable — and whatever this arbiter decides becomes precedent for what survives that.

Three further requirements. First, the philosophy has to be carried by the case and the characters, never by exposition: no named philosophers, no real games, companies, or brands, no citations, and no author's note at the end explaining what the story was about. I do want at least two stretches of the invented court record quoted in the arbiter's own document, and at least one scene away from the courtroom on the infrastructure side — a cold-storage hall, an attenuation log, a conversation with an engineer who explains what a fingerprint actually commits to and what a recall actually is, so that the reader learns that the returning ferry is something like the world remembering the boat rather than the boat coming back. Second, the harm question must be confronted head-on: someone has to ask what exactly was harmed — Wrenn's feelings, her memories, or something in the world itself — and the story must take that third possibility seriously enough to be uncomfortable without ever asserting it as doctrine. Third, no walkovers and no mush. The arbiter must actually rule, the ruling must have determinate content, and it must cost the arbiter something; a reader who sides with the defendant should finish thinking the ruling wrong but not stupid. One specific prohibition: the ancient puzzle about the ship whose planks are replaced one at a time is available to your characters, and everyone will expect you to use it, so let a character raise it once and dismiss it in a single line, saying why it does not help here.
