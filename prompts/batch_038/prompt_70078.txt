Three months into a job on the risk side of a mid-size lending fintech and I've now heard "if we don't do it, someone else will" in four seperate meetings. It killed a proposal to slow down the rollout of our automated approval model, it killed a proposal to stop selling a data-enrichment feed to a third party we couldn't say what they did with, and last week it got aimed directly at me when I said our short-term high-interest product is going to land hardest on exactly the customers who can least absorb it — the VP of product pointed out, correctly, that at least three competitors are already running something very similar and that if we walk away the volume just moves to them, probably with worse underwriting. For context I did a philosophy degree before this and I keep expecting it to be useless here and it keeps not being, and I'm one of two people out of ~400 whose title has the word responsibility in it.

The annoying thing is I don't think he's entirely wrong. I also think the sentence is doing at least four different jobs and only one of them is the empirical job he thinks it's doing. So can you take the slogan apart for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be making — something about substitutability and how much of the harm our absence actually removes; something about inevitability and whether an inevitable outcome is thereby a permissible contribution to it; something about the shape of the competition itself, i.e. an admission that we're sitting in an equilibrium where each player's locally reasonable move makes everyone worse off; and something about whether being in the game buys you steering power over it. For each reading: is it trivially true, substantive but defensible, or just false? And what extra premises does it need before it gets you from "someone else will do it" to "so we may"? I especially want the version where the slogan isn't a justification at all but a description of being trapped, because if that's what he's actually saying then the honest response isn't "you're wrong," it's "then the question is how the trap gets broken, and that's a different meeting than the one we're in."

Also flag where the answer turns on facts about the market rather than on philosophy — whether we're big enough that our exit moves the total at all, whether the harm is diffuse across lots of third parties or concentrated, whether competitors would match restraint or exploit it, whether being first actually converts into being able to set terms later. Its for a working group so give me a menu I can bring into the room, not an essay; a tight paragraph or a few bullets per reading is plenty. And don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll chase down anything I need myself.
