Our phil-of-science reading group spent two weeks building the argument below and Ive started to think it's a trap, because if it goes through it licenses stuff nobody in the room actually wants to say.

(1) Determinism says: the complete physical state of the world at a time, plus the laws, fixes the state at every other time.
(2) For that to be more than verbal decoration it has to make a difference to what could be known or predicted — a thesis with no cashable predictive consequences isn't a scientific thesis.
(3) A chaotic system is deterministic but sensitive to initial conditions: states differing by epsilon diverge, roughly exponentially, and every real specification of a state carries finite precision. Lorenz's summary of the situation is that "the present determines the future but the approximate present does not approximately determine the future."
(4) So in a chaotic deterministic system there's a horizon — call it a few Lyapunov times — past which no finite-precision predictor, however powerful, can say which of the nearby possible futures is the actual one.
(5) Every predictor is itself a physical system, hence finite precision: bounded memory, noise, and, as one guy kept insisting while waving at a 1990 result about predicting certain dynamical systems being as hard as the halting problem, hard computational limits.
(6) Therefore in a chaotic world the future is fixed but no possible inhabitant of that world can get at it.
(7) Two hypotheses that no possible observer could ever distinguish are not making different claims about the world.
(8) So determinism about a chaotic world says exactly what its denial says. Determinism isn't false — it's empty, and by (2) not a scientific thesis at all.

I want to reject (8), but it feels like the argument is valid and I cant find a premise I'd happily give up. (2) smells verificationist but seems to be doing honest work. (3) and (4) look like plain textbook chaos theory. And (7) is the kind of thing half the room will just bite the bullet on.

Two things I keep getting told and don't know what to do with: chaotic systems supposedly have perfectly stable statistical fingerprints — attractor shape, exponents, the distribution of where the system spends its time — which people estimate from finite noisy data all the time; and supposedly lots of useful predictions survive chaos because they're coarse-grained ("it'll be warm in July," not "the exact state on July 19th"). If that's right, does it break (4), (7), or both?

So: which step do you cut? Tell me why it looked fine — what reading makes it true and what different reading the argument actually needs — and then price the alternatives: for each of the two or three premises I could give up instead, what exactly do I lose? I also want your take on whether (6) survives if you restrict "predictor" to things inside the world, since a Laplacean demon sits outside it — does that distinction do real work here or is it just hiding the problem? And say whether the argument, once repaired, still proves too much in some other direction, e.g. against our ability to tell deterministic chaos from genuine noise.

Don't give me a map of the positions or a both-sides survey. Commit to a cut, defend it, and tell me what you're paying for it.
