Thirty-five years I spent in atmospheric chemistry, mostly measuring things and arguing about error bars, and since I packed it in I've taken up philosophy properly — logic and the philosophy of maths mainly — and I go along to a little reading circle that meets in the village hall on a Thursday. After last week's session two of the regulars got into a proper arguement in the car park and went on into the pub, and I still can't tell who came out ahead. Norman is a retired actuary, Sylvia taught maths at the comprehensive for thirty years and then did a bit of programming.

The row was about whether the axioms mathematicians actually use might turn out to be inconsistent. It went roughly like this:

(1) Norman started from Gödel's second theorem: set theory can't prove its own consistency, and nobody has proved it from anything obviously more trustworthy either. So the whole building sits on an unproved assumption, and if it's false then every sentence whatsoever is provable, which would mean nothing proved inside it means anything. He said people who work in the subject behave as though that were a solved problem, and called it dishonest.

(2) Sylvia said he was sliding from unprovable to doubtful. We have a picture of sets being built up in stages that makes the axioms look like descriptions of something rather than inventions, and a century and a half of heavy use hasn't produced a contradiction — whereas naive set theory fell over within about twenty-five years of being written down. So the evidence is all one way, even if it isn't a proof.

(3) Norman said that's survivorship bias wearing a lab coat. Frege's big comprehension principle looked perfectly self-evident, and the contradiction only surfaced because one man went looking for it. And the contradiction that would matter wouldn't be a two-liner: any actual refutation would have to be fantastically long and baroque, so the fact that nobody's found one is close to worthless evidence.

(4) Sylvia said fine, but that cuts against him too — if such a proof would be grotesquely long, then nobody is ever going to write one, and none of the applied work she cares about (which uses a small fragment and has short proofs) could be touched by it. Also, if set theory did go down, most ordinary mathematics lives in much weaker arithmetic and wouldn't automatically go with it.

(5) Norman said the weaker systems don't rescue anything, because they aren't independently certified. Arithmetic can't prove its own consistency either, and Gentzen's proof of it buys that result with transfinite induction up to a big ordinal, which is an infinitary commitment in its own right. So it isn't a retreat to safer ground, it's just moving the same anxiety to a new address.

(7) — sorry, (6) — Sylvia said moving it with interest, though: an ordering of notations you can actually write down and inspect, plus a reduction argument, is a real lowering of the risk, and the analysis has been pushed far past that ordinal since, each extension looked at closely. Then she said consistency isn't even the property he should be losing sleep over, since a system can be consistent and still be ω-inconsistent — proving that some number has a property while proving of every individual numeral that it doesn't. So he's fretting about the mildest of the bad outcomes.

(7) Norman said that was a distraction, because consistency is the load-bearing one: lose it and arithmetic goes, and everything else sits on arithmetic. And on her inspection point, the standard she's applying is innocent until proven guilty, which is precisely the standard that let people swallow axioms about enormous infinities that nobody has a picture of. If you don't like that, he said, note you can always just add as a new axiom the statement that set theory is consistent and be in exactly the same epistemic position, which shows her talk of evidence is empty.

(8) Sylvia said that's not the same kind of move at all. Those strong axioms have structural consequences — they settle questions elsewhere, in determinacy and combinatorics — and, remarkably, their consistency strengths all fall into one single ladder, which nobody can explain. That's the sort of unexpected coherence you'd count as confirmation in a physical theory. Adding a bare consistency axiom buys you none of it.

Then they both looked at me, which was unhelpful.

Could you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step — and where a move is half right, say which half? I'd also like to know where they stop actually disagreeing and start talking past one another, since I suspect some of it is that. And what is neither of them taking seriously that they should be? Finally, as someone who spent a career trusting models that were certainly wrong but still worth using, I want to know the practical upshot: should a working scientist change anything about how they treat mathematics on the basis of this exchange, and what sort of evidence would genuinely move the needle in either direction?
