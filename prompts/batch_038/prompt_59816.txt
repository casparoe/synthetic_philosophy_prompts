The debate about whether large historical outcomes were inevitable presents itself as a metaphysical dispute — was the outcome fixed? — but I shall argue that it is largely an artefact of three separable parameters having been collapsed into one scalar notion of inevitability.

Say that an outcome is *robust* at a time t relative to a class of perturbations P if, had some perturbation in P been introduced at t, the outcome would still have obtained. The three parameters are then:

1. **Granularity.** The description under which the outcome is fixed. 'Industrialisation in Britain' and 'the industrialisation that in fact occurred' are different targets, and a robustness claim that does not settle the granularity is not apt for truth. Much apparent disagreement about inevitability is disagreement about how finely the target is described.

2. **Width of the perturbation class.** Whether we are perturbing ministers and harvests or coal seams and monsoon patterns. There is no measure over counterfactual histories that historical evidence can supply, so width must be stipulated, and rival stipulations deliver rival verdicts about the very same outcome.

3. **Timing.** Where in the process the perturbation is applied. This is the parameter the path-dependence literature occupies: lock-in is the claim that a perturbation introduced early has large downstream effects and introduced late has almost none.

My central interpretive claim is that the classic dismissals of counterfactual history are not competing answers to one question but attacks on different parameters. On my reading, Carr's rejection of counterfactual speculation, in the Trevelyan Lectures, is essentially a complaint about granularity: the counterfactuals historians are tempted to run are unanswerable because their targets are described too finely. The familiar objection that there are no covering laws to license counterfactual dependencies in history is a complaint about width. The path-dependence literature — the QWERTY-style cases in which small early events become unrevisable — is a thesis about timing alone. Gould's replaying-the-tape motif, similarly, is a claim about the width of the perturbation class rather than about indeterminism; a strict determinist can be a contingencist without embarrassment.

The positive payoff I want is a further proposal. Robustness is not a property of an outcome but of an outcome-at-a-time pair, and in many macro-historical domains it is monotone in t: the class of perturbations able to redirect an outcome narrows as the outcome approaches. Call the latest time at which a given perturbation class can still redirect an outcome that outcome's *modal horizon*. What historians assert when they call something inevitable is, on this view, merely the claim that we stand past its modal horizon. Inevitability claims then become indexical to the historian's position within the process, and two historians who appear to contradict one another can both be right.

Finally, an epistemological worry I want to keep but cannot yet place. We observe only the trajectory that was taken, and the surviving record is itself a product of that trajectory. Judgements of robustness are therefore exposed to a selection effect — call it *retrospective inevitability inflation* — whereby the bare fact of our being downstream of an outcome inclines us to overestimate how few perturbations could have prevented it. I do not know whether this names a distinct phenomenon or is hindsight bias in modal costume.
