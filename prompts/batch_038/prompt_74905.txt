I am a novelist, and for reasons I have never entirely understood I was appointed two years ago as one of the two lay members of my country's National Statistics Advisory Council, a body of nine that signs off on what the statistical office publishes and in what form. The Council is not decorative and neither am I, but my colleagues plainly expect me to say something humane and imprecise near the end of each meeting. In March we decide a file that has been open for eighteen months: whether the office should publish a single headline index of national wellbeing, and, if it does, whether government may be assessed against it. The finance ministry wants a composite number, produced annually and ranked against other countries, because the Prime Minister has announced an ambition to see us in the top ten of the global life-evaluation league table by the end of the decade. The office's own methodologists want a dashboard of separate indicators with no aggregation at all, on the ground that any weighting scheme is disguised politics. A third member has proposed a middle route modelled on Bhutan's index, which does not average: it classifies each person as happy or not-yet-happy according to whether they reach a sufficient level in enough of nine equally weighted domains, and reports the headcount. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the Council in advance of that meeting. It should arrive at a recommendation — one of those three options, or a fourth you construct — with reasons that both a statistician and a Treasury official can engage with rather than talk past.

I want the memo to press where my colleagues keep deferring. Our instrument contains two families of question that do not agree: a ten-rung ladder on which respondents place their life as a whole, and a set of questions about yesterday — how much happiness, worry, sadness and physical pain the person actually experienced. Income and education track the first far more closely than the second, and the two yield quite different national portraits, so the memo must say which one the office is in the business of measuring and what follows from the choice. It must also confront our geography. The north-east is a region of collapsing birth rates, shuttered clinics and departing young people, and it reports among the highest ladder scores in the country; my colleagues want to call this adaptation and discount it. In my last novel a widow tells a survey-taker that she is contented and the reader spends eighty pages discovering how much of that is true, so I do not think contentment is a lie — but I do not think it is the whole of a life either, and I want the memo to examine whether discounting what people say about their own lives is something a statistics office may legitimately do at all, or whether it is the precise moment at which measurement becomes paternalism. Roughly one inhabitant in six is foreign-born, and there is a live question whether they answer the ladder against a different internal benchmark than the native-born; the office has been shown a vignette-anchoring technique that would correct for this, and the memo should say whether adopting it is a technical improvement or a way of overriding respondents' own comparisons.

Then the targeting question, which I suspect is the one that actually decides the file. Once regional directors know their performance will be read off an index, the index becomes an object of management; I want a concrete rule for what the Council should do if reported scores rise for three consecutive years while health, housing and outmigration do not move, and I want the memo to answer squarely whether a top-ten finish is even the right thing for a government to want — including the uncomfortable possibility that a country can rise in the table by making its citizens contented with less, and the further possibility that we nine are not the right people to settle what a good national life consists in. You may invoke the OECD's own solution to the weighting problem, a published dashboard sitting alongside an interactive index in which users assign their own weights across eleven topics, but note the Treasury's objection that eleven thousand citizen-chosen indices cannot be placed in a minister's red box. Two matters of form: keep the document under twelve hundred words in plain prose, and give me one paragraph tight enough that a reporter could quote it without mangling it. No reading list, please — we are nine people in a room in March and we need a decision, not a syllabus.
