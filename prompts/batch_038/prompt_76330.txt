I am helping to draft the standing orders for a nine-member standing panel of a public research funder. Its job is to determine whether to withdraw a multi-year grant, and its charter specifies that withdrawal follows if and only if three grounds are all made out: A, that the reported central result is not reproducible; B, that the departure from the approved protocol was material; C, that the departure was not disclosed to the funder. The charter, embarrassingly, says nothing about how the panel is to arrive at a collective finding, and in the first hearing we walked straight into the problem. Members voted by secret ballot on each of A, B, and C and on the determination D itself, with this result:

| Member | A | B | C | D (withdraw) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1, 2, 3 | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| 4 | yes | abstain | yes | no |
| 5 | yes | no | yes | no |
| 6 | no | yes | yes | no |
| 7 | yes | no | no | no |
| 8 | yes | abstain | no | no |
| 9 | no | no | yes | no |

So A carries 7–2, C carries 7–2, B carries 4–3 among those voting on it but fails 4–5 if the two recusals on B are counted as rejections, and D fails 6–3. Reading the same ballots three defensible ways yields three different determinations: withdraw on the premises, do not withdraw on the conclusion, and do not withdraw on the premises once abstentions are scored against. The panel then took a vote on which reading should govern, and split 5–4 on that too, with the split cutting across the substantive split. Worse, a second sitting was held after the investigators filed further documentation: two seats turned over between the sittings, and one member changed his vote on B. The determination ultimately issued relies on the first sitting's findings on A and C, the second sitting's finding on B, and a procedural choice endorsed by a bare majority of the second panel. No body that ever sat voted for all of A, B, C and D together. Note also that four of the six members opposed to withdrawal accept at least two of the three grounds for it, so the reasons we would publish are, as a conjunction, rejected by everyone but the three members who wanted withdrawal.

Before I read anything further on this, I want the specification: what would a theory of judgment aggregation have to deliver to count as adequate for a body like this one? I am fixing the purpose deliberately, since adequacy is relative to it — the theory must underwrite the panel's authority to issue a binding determination for which the panel itself can be held responsible and which it can defend to an appellant and to a reviewing court, not merely maximize the probability that the misconduct question is answered correctly. Give me the requirements list in numbered form, and for each requirement state it precisely, give the feature of the case above that motivates it, classify it as non-negotiable, as negotiable, or as a preference of one camp rather than a genuine requirement, and say what is lost if it is dropped. I expect the list to include the familiar conditions on the input side (what profiles must be admissible, in particular whether individually incomplete judgment sets arising from recusal are allowed at all), on the rule (anonymity, responsiveness, and whether the collective verdict on each proposition may depend only on the individual verdicts on that proposition), and on the output side (consistency, deductive closure, completeness, and the demand that the output be a set the group can own and speak with). But I also want you to test candidates that I suspect are under-theorized and that you should feel free to name yourself rather than attribute: that the outcome be invariant to the order in which propositions are put to the vote; that the determination be traceable, in the sense that the panel can identify which members' judgments were decisive for it; that the reasons published be ones that some member or members can be authorized to speak for; that the rule say determinately how abstention and recusal are to be scored; and — this is the one the second sitting exposed, and I am coining the term, so do not look for it in the literature — what I will call diachronic profile-integrity, namely that the group's final judgment set be the output of a single aggregation over a single profile of a single constituted body, rather than assembled across sittings and turnovers. For each of these, say whether it is a real desideratum, a derivative consequence of others, or a prejudice, and if it is real, show which of the features above it is meant to capture.

Two things matter more to me than the list's length. First, tell me which subsets are jointly unsatisfiable: given the standard impossibility results in this area, which combination of the requirements above cannot all be met on an agenda with our structure, which single requirement is the cheapest to surrender in order to restore possibility, and whether restricting the domain of admissible profiles — to profiles that are, say, ordered by a shared ranking of the grounds — is a legitimate move for an actual panel whose members arrive with whatever judgments they have, or a cheat that buys consistency by ruling out the case. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidate rules, and which distinctions the theory must not collapse: between aggregating beliefs and aggregating decisions; between a procedure and the outcome it produces, given that premise-based and conclusion-based voting can be applied to the same ballots; between consistency and completeness; between preserving unanimity and being responsive at all; between a collective attitude that members hold and one that is merely ascribable to the group; and between synchronic coherence and the diachronic coherence I am asking about. Distinguish throughout between what is genuinely required of any adequate theory here and what is a camp commitment dressed as a requirement — I especially want to know whether treating each proposition in isolation from the rest of the agenda is a fairness condition or an artifact of the formalism. Then, briefly and in no more than a sentence or two apiece, how each of these fares against your list: conclusion-based majority voting; premise-based majority voting; a sequential procedure that fixes the order of voting by the logical structure of the grounds; a supermajority or quota rule; a distance-based rule that picks the coherent judgment set closest to the profile; chair-dictatorship; and letting the panel vote on its own procedure, as ours did. Close with one paragraph saying how the list would change if the purpose were purely epistemic — tracking the truth about reproducibility and materiality — rather than conferring responsibility-bearing authority. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations, please; I want the reasoning worked through from the case, and any term you introduce should be defined rather than sourced.
