I chair the ethics advisory committee of a regional health authority serving about 600,000 people, and on Thursday I have to give the opening remarks at a public meeting the state has ordered us to hold. Eighteen months ago, during a respiratory surge, the authority adopted a crisis-allocation protocol governing its single tertiary ICU and one scarce antiviral. The protocol used a points instrument that combined each patient's estimated chance of surviving to hospital discharge with projected remaining years of life, discounted for comorbidity burden. It was in force for eleven weeks. Forty-one patients fell below the threshold and were not transferred; nineteen of them died. A disability-rights coalition has filed a formal complaint, three families have given interviews to the local paper, and the revised protocol cannot be adopted until the authority has heard from the public in a recorded session. I speak first, for about seven minutes, before anyone presents data or takes questions. I am a general practitioner by training; I took one graduate seminar on Scanlon roughly twenty years ago, which is the whole of my philosophy, and none of it can be visible on Thursday.

The room will contain: the two intensivists and the administrator who wrote the instrument and who will defend it after me; the coalition's organizers, among them a woman whose brother has a progressive neuromuscular condition and who was scored down on comorbidity in week four; a delegation from the regional dialysis association; families of the nineteen; a reporter who has already run the phrase triage by spreadsheet and will be listening for me either to repeat it or to deny it; the state's reviewers, who will read the transcript; and two philosophers from the university, one a contractualist and one a consequentialist, each of whom will be able to identify the argument I am making even though I am not permitted to sound like I am making one. That is the central constraint. The speech may state the numbers once, as plain facts about what happened, but it may not reason from them: no arithmetic of lives saved, no "nineteen died so that others lived," no appeal to the total good the region got out of those eleven weeks, no sentence whose force depends on adding one person's loss to another's. Equally, it may not pretend that the instrument was not ranking people, or claim that no one's claim was weighed against anyone else's, because weighing claims is exactly what it did for eleven weeks. It has to justify the protocol, or concede that it cannot be justified, in a register that addresses one claim at a time — the brother scored down in week four, the dialysis patients as a group sharing a single reason to object, each family separately — without ever assembling those claims into a total, and without letting that form of address collapse into sentimentality about nineteen private tragedies.

Four substantive things have to get done, and I would rather they came through in the shape of the sentences than in any vocabulary. First, the difference between counting lives and discounting years: whatever else is wrong with our instrument, its real exposure is not that it counted people but that it valued a year differently depending on whose year it was, and the speech needs to say that in words a room of non-specialists can follow and that the intensivists can hear without being told they were monsters. Second, the protocol was adopted at a moment when every resident of the region stood the same chance of being on either side of it, while the complaints are being made by the people it actually fell on; the speech must acknowledge that a rule all of us could have accepted in advance is not thereby a rule that the person it landed on has any reason to accept now, and must say what we intend to do about that gap rather than gesture at it. Third, refusing to rank is itself a ranking: first-come-first-served, or whichever family telephones most persistently, also produced people who were never addressed at all — the point may be made, but not in a way that shuts the meeting down, since the coalition's complaint is not that we chose a rule but that we chose this one. Fourth, it must commit us to something that follows from the first three. I am prepared to say publicly that the years-remaining discount is coming out, that the survival-to-discharge estimate stays, that the coalition gets a standing seat on the revision panel, and that we will publish individual case reviews rather than only totals; it must not promise anything beyond that — no revocation, no compensation, no disciplinary consequence — must not apologize in a manner that hands the clinicians who worked under the protocol to the room, and must not end on a benediction. Please write the speech at roughly 950 words: no names of philosophers, no terms of art, no scripture, and no quotations from any book, article, or real person. Where you render what the complainants are saying, stay inside the descriptions I have given you and do not invent additional words for them to have used.
