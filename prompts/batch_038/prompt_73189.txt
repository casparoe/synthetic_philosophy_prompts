**The premise nobody argues for**

Taurek's famous case: one supply of a drug, one person on the small side (someone the agent knows), five strangers on the other, not enough to go around. He says the difference in numbers shouldn't decide it, and that the right move is a coin toss giving each of the six the same chance.

My thesis is that the argument everyone reads as having one premise actually has two, and its the second one doing the work.

**A (metaphysical).** Losses don't add up across persons. Each of the five dies, and each death is a catastrophic loss to the person who dies. But there is no further entity — no "the five" — that suffers a loss of five. So the phrase five deaths doesn't name one large bad thing that could outweigh one death. If you treat it as though it did, you've turned people into locations of value and then compared locations, which is exactly how you'd compare five apples lying in the road to one apple.

**A\* (structural).** Because the losses don't aggregate, the situation is symmetric as between the one and the five, and symmetry between claimants is the sort of thing that gets resolved by an equal-chance procedure.

A is close to uncontroversial — plenty of people who save the five accept it and just add an impersonal betterness claim on top. A\* is not uncontroversial at all, and Taurek walks straight across the gap. From A alone what follows is that you have no *aggregative* reason to prefer the five. That's a negative result. It doesn't yield randomization; non-aggregation on its own licenses silence, or arbitrariness, or a standing bias toward whoever you happen to love. To get from losses-don't-sum to flip-a-coin you need a further claim about how a chooser must respond to claims that can't be weighed against each other — namely that the choice has to be justifiable to each of the six in the very same terms. Taurek reaches for equal concern and respect here, but a lottery only *expresses* equal concern if you already accept that demand. Someone who rejects A\* can accept A wholeheartedly and still say: when I can't weigh them I do what I can, and I owe the others an apology rather than a ticket.

**Payoff.** This relocates the fight with the contractualists. When they insist that equal complaints have to be number-sensitive — that facing six people with identical complaints you cannot justify to the five a choice of the one — they aren't disputing A. They're disputing A\*, and they're disputing it by deploying a principle about pairwise justification that Taurek never had to deny, because he never stated it. Half the literature is two camps agreeing about the metaphysics and shouting about the structure.

**Last bit, least confident.** Taurek's own case is rigged in a way that hides A\*. He puts someone the agent cares about on the small side, which imports a partialist reason — a reason that by construction doesn't depend on counting anybody. He then notices this reason feels unanswerable and moves to the conclusion that there's no aggregative reason on the other side either. But the hard question is whether a non-comparative reason of that kind is still available when both sides are strangers, and the setup is built so we never have to face it.

1. Is A/A\* a genuine split or am I sawing one premise in half and calling the pieces a discovery? Give me the strongest version of "those are the same premise," because I suspect a reviewer will say exactly that.
2. Does the rigged-case charge survive? If Taurek runs a variant with strangers on both sides, my last section collapses — flag that if you think so, but if your memory of the paper is shaky there, just say it's shaky instead of filling in a case for me.
3. Point at the one sentence where I most obviously overclaim, and tell me how to weaken it without losing the argument.
4. Bluntly: is the payoff section a real reframing or is it the kind of thing that sounds like an insight until you say it out loud?
