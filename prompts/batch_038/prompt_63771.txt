Our hospital's ethics committee has been asked to review its policy on the determination of death, and I have volunteered to write the conceptual background note. Before I commit to a position in writing, I would like your own credence on a specific claim, together with the arithmetic that produces it, rather than a survey of the debate.

The claim is this: when a human being satisfies the standard neurological criteria — deep coma with an established cause, absent brainstem reflexes, apnea on testing, and no confounding contribution from drugs, hypothermia, or metabolic derangement — then that human being has died at that moment, in the same sense of the word in which a person whose circulation has stopped has died; the organism has ceased to function as an integrated whole; and this is a matter of biological fact to be discovered rather than a stipulation we are free to make among competing candidate concepts of death.

I do not want a literature review, and I do not want the various positions named and characterized. I want a number, and then that number decomposed into the sub-claims it actually rests on, each with its own probability and a sentence or two of justification. Please address at least the following. First, the prior question of whether death, for a human organism, is the sort of thing that admits of a biological answer at all, rather than being settled by a normative choice among concepts, and how much probability you assign to the competing personhood-oriented view on which death consists in the irreversible loss of consciousness and of the capacity for social engagement. Second, whether the destruction of the brain really does destroy somatic integration, given that patients satisfying the criteria have been maintained for months and in some cases years, with continued wound healing, resistance to infection, and fluid and temperature regulation under support, and given published reports of a pregnancy carried to the delivery of a healthy infant and of children maintained over years with ongoing physical growth. Third, and closely related, how much weight the long-survival cases deserve: whether they show that integration genuinely persists, or only that a ventilator, an infusion pump, and hormonal replacement can perform the work the brain used to do, so that the right question is whether the body still governs itself or is merely being operated. Fourth, whether the requirement that the cessation be irreversible can be given a content that does not covertly depend on a decision not to intervene — since what clinicians can actually test is permanence, meaning that function will not return of its own accord and that no one will attempt to restore it, and the gap between permanence and irreversibility is precisely where donation after circulatory determination of death sits, including procedures in which warm oxygenated blood flow is restored to the abdominal organs after death has been declared, with the vessels supplying the brain occluded. Fifth, whether the circulatory criterion and the neurological criterion can both be correct because they converge on a single underlying loss, on the grounds that a few minutes without circulation destroys the brain, or whether they are in truth two different concepts held together by practice and by the convenience of their agreement in ordinary cases.

Then tell me how you combine these terms — by multiplication, by a weighted overall judgment, or by something less tidy, and why that is the right way to combine them here — and identify the single sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive, that is, the one on which, if I could change your mind, the total would move furthest. For orientation: my own credence in the claim as stated is roughly 0.6, while a colleague who works in transplant medicine puts hers at about 0.9. Once you have given the decomposition, say where you think the disagreement between the two of us most likely lives, and what piece of evidence or argument would move your own number most.

One formatting request, which I would ask you to observe strictly: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Write the whole thing as continuous prose in complete paragraphs, the way one would write a considered letter, with each probability stated inline in the sentence that justifies it.
