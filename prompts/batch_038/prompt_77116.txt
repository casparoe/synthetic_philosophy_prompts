I direct grantmaking at a regional community foundation with a $9 million annual discretionary fund. Each quarter my three program officers sort roughly forty requests into a slate the board then votes on, and each quarter the same dispute recurs: a handful of proposals arrive from global-health intermediaries whose cost-per-life-saved figures dwarf anything we can do locally, while our charter commits us to "the communities that sustain us," and the board is split between members who find the drowning-child line of argument decisive and members who experience it as a slur on the region we were endowed to serve. I would like a written allocation procedure my officers can run on each request without re-litigating impartialism from first principles every cycle.

It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the conditions under which it returns fund, fund partially, or decline, and an explicit inventory of which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure and which get handed upstairs to the board because they concern values rather than facts. It must address donor-restricted money and obligations already incurred to past grantees; it must state plainly whether it escalates until nearly the whole fund flows to the highest-impact global causes, and if it does not, name the stopping point and defend it; and it must say how to treat the knowledge that peer foundations free-ride. Constraints: two hours per request, no economist on staff, and every output must carry a reason code we could defend to an unsuccessful applicant.

Then apply it to three cases: $400,000 to open a second domestic-violence residence in our county, where the existing shelter's waitlist turns women away most months; $400,000 to a global fund whose own estimate is one death averted per $4,500; and $400,000 from an unrestricted bequest whose donor left only the instruction to give it wherever it does the most good. At least one of the three should come out against my own preference — I expect to fund the shelter — and the write-up should end by stating what the procedure cannot settle.
