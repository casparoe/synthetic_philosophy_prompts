I am a second-year doctoral student in economics. My core sequence has just finished a unit on bargaining: Nash's axiomatic solution and the Rubinstein alternating-offers model, the role of the disagreement point and of patience in determining the split, the difference between transferable and non-transferable utility settings, the Coasean result that with well-defined entitlements and no transaction costs parties bargain to an efficient allocation whatever the starting point, and on the mechanism design side, incentive compatibility and the standing suspicion of self-reported intensities of preference. Alongside this I am enrolled in a political philosophy seminar that has spent three weeks on the ethics of compromise between conflicting value systems, and I keep failing to connect the two.

The seminar material runs roughly as follows. Two parties each hold a view of what justice requires and each regards the other's ideal as affirmatively wrong rather than merely suboptimal. They can settle on a policy each ranks below its own ideal, and the literature treats this as a possible gain from trade: both are better off than under continued deadlock, and the resulting bill is passed. But the seminar kept pressing several points I do not know how to render. One is the distinction between conceding on how much of a contested good to promote and conceding on whether to participate in something one takes to be a wrong; only the first looks like a movement along a utility curve. Another is the integrity objection: that the cost of the compromise falls not on the outcome but on the agent, and that this cost is not one the agent can be compensated for by the other side's reciprocal concession, because it is not a cost measured in the same units. A third is that a compromise binds a party now to votes and actions it will later judge wrong, so that the agreement trades across time as well as across parties. A fourth is the worry that talk of gains from trade presupposes that the intensities of the two parties' moral convictions are cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable, which is precisely what is in dispute.

Please restate that debate in the vocabulary I actually use — bargaining problems, disagreement points, Pareto improvements and the frontier, cardinal and comparable utilities, externalities, Coasean invariance, incentive compatibility, and what a mechanism can and cannot implement — rendering each philosophical claim, where possible, as a claim about the structure of a bargaining problem or about the feasibility of an arrangement.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. Sort the claims into three piles: those that translate cleanly, those that translate only by changing their content, and those my vocabulary cannot express at all. For the second pile, say precisely what was distorted; in particular, tell me whether the integrity objection is a fixed transaction cost, a preference over one's own action types that is perfectly representable in a utility function, or something inexpressible because it concerns the agent's relation to her choices rather than to outcomes. For the third pile, say whether the failure is mine — whether the assumption that everything relevant is a utility over outcomes is exactly what the philosophers are denying — or whether it shows that their distinctions are unclear and dissolve once one insists on writing the problem down.

Two constraints. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call the result a translation; wherever a mapping only works because you have quietly granted transferable, interpersonally comparable moral utility, flag it, since that is the concession I suspect is doing the damage. And do not give me references or a reading list — I want the conceptual mapping argued from the structure of the problems, not from attribution.
