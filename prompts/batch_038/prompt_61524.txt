Our reading group has been arguing about consciousness for two terms and we've finaly agreed to do the thing we should have done a year ago: get an illusionist and a realist about phenomenal consciousness to co-sign one document. I'm the one stuck drafting it, and every version I produce either waters the disagreement down to nothing or lets one side set the vocabulary, so I want to see what you can do with it. I'm not after a dialogue or a verdict on who's right — I want the actual joint statement, the kind of thing both people could put their names to without wincing.

Be specific about the parties, because I don't want a straw pairing. Party A is an illusionist of the introspection-driven sort: not someone who denies anything is going on, but someone who holds that when we introspect we get a systematically distorted model of our own processing — the model represents its own contents as intrinsic, simple, immediately present, beyond misdescription, and those features aren't real features of anything. On this view the hard-problem intuitions are artifacts of a bad user interface, and they'd insist they are not saying there is nothing it is like to be them. Party B is a moderate realist, not a dualist: they will happily concede that introspection gets a huge amount wrong — wrong about determinacy, wrong about how much of the visual field is actually there, wrong that we are aware of everything we seem to be aware of — but they insist there is a residue that can't be illusory, on the grounds that for a representation to be a misrepresentation of something, something has to be there for it to be a misrepresentation of, and that something is exactly the phenomenal bit.

Here's what the document needs:

- A numbered list of claims both would genuinely sign. And I mean genuinely — candidates might include what transparency of experience commits you to, what each side makes of the change-blindness and inattentional-blindness results, whether they can agree on anything at all about what Mary gains when she leaves the room (or whether they even agree that has an answer). Nothing so vaguely worded that either side would nod in public and disown it in private.
- A disagreements table: topic, then the disagreement stated twice — once in wording party A would accept as a fair statement of their own view, once in wording party B would accept as fair. That double phrasing is the hard part and the whole point, so don't let it slide into one neutral summary that secretly favors somebody.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions where an answer would actually move someone rather than just re-describe the standoff. For each, an argument or an experiment both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two I already suspect. First, the speckled-hen sort of case: is an experience of a hen with 48 speckles phenomenally 48-speckled even though the subject can't count them, and if the illusionist answers that the experience is indeterminate and introspection just pretends otherwise, what seperates that answer from B's residue? Second, whether A is allowed to appeal to introspection at all while diagnosing the illusion — B will say you need some introspective grip on the phenomenon in order to claim it's misrepresented, A will say the phenomenon gets picked out by theory and not by introspection. Put both in, worked out, plus anything you think is more central than either.
- A short section on whether some of this is just a fight about the word phenomenal. If it is, say which part, and say what's left standing once the verbal bit is carved off. If nothing is left standing, that's an acceptable conclusion and I'd rather hear it than get a diplomatic document.

And please be blunt about where this genre fails instead of being agreeable. If you think the pairing I described is incoherent — say, that a moderate realist who concedes all of B's concessions just collapses into illusionism, or that an illusionist who says what A says is already committed to the residue B is demanding — say so at the top and mark the relevant rows as failed negotiations rather than dressing them up as agreements. Same if you think a joint statement is simply the wrong output here and the honest deliverable is two lists of what each refuses to concede. I'd take that over a clean-looking document nobody could sign.
