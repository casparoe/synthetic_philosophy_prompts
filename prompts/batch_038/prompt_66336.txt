A press has commissioned a short essay for a volume on the ethics of taxation, and the editor has asked each contributor to open with a numbered statement of their own position, so that the rest of the essay — and, ideally, the rest of the book — has something definite to argue with. I have drafted that statement below. I am a novelist by trade, and I suspect my views are those of a great many people in my profession, which is why I want them examined before they are printed rather than after.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. I do not need to be told which of these positions has a name, who has held it, or where the disagreement in the field lies; I need to be told which of them I cannot hold at once. I have deliberately not signaled where I expect the trouble to be, because I do not know where it is.

1. What makes theft wrong is not the loss. It is that someone has substituted his choice for the owner's about a thing on which the owner's will is set. A stolen letter opener matters more than the price of a letter opener, and a deception that leaves its victim richer than he was is still a deception. The wrong is expressive.

2. There is no such thing as property before the law. What counts as mine is settled by the rules a legal order actually maintains; antecedent to those rules there are no holdings, and therefore nothing that can be taken. I reject entirely the picture in which ownership is already there, fully formed, and the state arrives and begins removing pieces of it.

3. For that reason the question my editor has set — is taxation theft? — strikes me as malformed. But malformation is not exoneration. A tax can be wrongful without being theft, and the way it is wrongful is by treating me as a resource. What makes a levy legitimate is not its rate but whether the scheme it funds is one I could recognize as having a share in, rather than one merely inflicted on me.

4. What separates the assessor from the mugger is generality: a rule published in advance, binding on everyone, framed with no particular person in view, is a different kind of act from a demand addressed to me by name.

5. My own work is the exception I am not prepared to surrender. A novel is not a bundle of entitlements conferred on me by a statute; it is an extension of the person who made it, and my claim to it precedes the law that records the claim. Copyright at its best recognizes that claim rather than creating it. This is why I care about attribution and about the integrity of my text considerably more than I care about money, and why I would object to the distortion of a book of mine even under a contract that paid handsomely for the privilege.

6. And yet I think copyright now runs scandalously long. I would cut the term to something near the author's lifetime plus a decade. A book is made out of a language, a set of inherited forms, and a stock of situations, none of which I invented or could have invented, and after a decent interval it belongs to everybody.

7. Verdicts from my working life, offered as data rather than as illustration:
 (a) I deduct research — travel, archive fees, the cost of a year spent in the town where the events of a book occurred — and I regard this as correct accounting, not as a fudge.
 (b) I restructure. I have taken an option payment as an advance against a book I had not yet written rather than as income in the year it arrived; I hold my foreign rights through a small company; my agent presses for reversion clauses so that rights come back to me when a title falls out of print. I consider all of this entirely clean.
 (c) I would not misdescribe anything on a return — not the travel, not the room I call a study — even if detection were impossible and even if I were confident the money would do more good in my hands than in the treasury's.
 (d) My grandmother left me a house. I support an inheritance tax that would have taken it from me, and I did not experience that as a disloyalty, to her or to myself.
 (e) When a government proposes a levy on the machine-reading of authors' texts, with the proceeds distributed to writers, I support it — although it will not touch my own earnings in my lifetime, and although nobody, including me, can say who will count as an author for its purposes.
 (f) I think my country's exit tax — the one that treats a person who renounces citizenship as having sold everything he owns on the day before he leaves — is indefensible at any rate whatsoever.
 (g) A state that will not let me go does something worse to me than a state that taxes me at ninety percent and lets me leave.

8. I am a moderate egalitarian. I object to large inequalities because of what they do to the standing of the people at the bottom, not because of anything wrong with the shape of a distribution as such; equality is not intrinsically valuable to me.

Now: which of these pull against which, and how hard? Order the tensions by severity rather than listing them as a uniform indictment, and mark the merely apparent ones as apparent — I expect that at least two of them look like contradictions and are not, and I would rather be told that than be told the whole structure is unsound. For each genuine tension, name the commitment that has to go or be weakened, and state the cost in terms of what I will no longer be entitled to say, since some of these I use constantly and would want to know which ones I am about to lose. Where a tension looks local but is actually a symptom of something shared by several commitments, say so. And if a single revision would resolve more than one of them at once, identify it and say plainly what it forfeits, because I suspect that is where the real decision lies.
