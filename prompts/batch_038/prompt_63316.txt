I spent thirty-eight years doing physical chemistry — reaction kinetics mostly, a lot of it arguing with grad students about error bars — and since retiring I've been auditing a philosophy seminar at the community college, which has been a blast and occasionally infuriating in equal measure. A few weeks back we hit decision theory and got onto I. J. Good's result that free information never has negative expected value for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer. One of the students, Hank, wrote the argument up on the board like this: the value of your situation given your beliefs is the max of finitely many linear functions of those beliefs, so it's convex; and learning something just spreads your beliefs out in a mean-preserving way; so by Jensen the expected value after the spread is at least the value before. He was extremely pleased with himself. Another student, Vera, was not impressed, and they went at it for the better part of an hour over a real case: an acquaintance of Vera's, call her Nora, who has declined predictive testing for a late-onset hereditary neurodegenerative disease that runs in her family and has no treatment, even though the test is free, quick, and would help settle whether she and her partner try for kids.

Here's the exchange as best I can recontruct it from my notes. (1) Hank: this is a theorem, not a taste question — free information has nonnegative expected value, so Nora is making a decision-theoretic mistake. (2) Vera: nonnegative isn't positive. If the result wouldn't change what she does, the value is exactly zero and the theorem doesn't reach her case at all; Hank's own picture shows it, since the value function is flat inside any region where the same action stays optimal, so unless her posterior might cross a boundary, information is worth nothing. (3) Hank: in her case it obviously might cross — kids or no kids is precisely a boundary, so the value is strictly positive. (4) Vera: only if you hold her utility fixed. Nora doesn't just care about outcomes, she cares about what she expects; she's said she'd spend the next twenty years either dreading or grieving, and that cost attaches to knowing, so once you put it in, the value function isn't convex anymore and the Jensen step fails. (5) Hank: that's an unfalsifiable dodge — any evidence you dislike can be given a psychological price, and then nobody ever has to look at anything they'd rather avoid; the whole point of the principle of total evidence is to police exactly that move. (6) Vera: second, and seperately, the theorem assumes she updates by conditionalizing on her total evidence. Nora says she wouldn't: she'd take a positive result as certain doom, over-update, and then make worse decisions afterward. If she doesn't conditionalize, the theorem's hypothesis is simply false of her. (7) Hank: then she's irrational for not conditionalizing, and you can't get her off the hook by stacking a second irrationality on the first — rational requirements don't evaporate because the agent can't meet them. (8) Vera: now you've turned it into a definition. It's a claim about idealized agents, so you can't use it as a verdict on a person without smuggling in that she ought to be idealized. Is it a descriptive fact about Bayesians or a criticism of Nora? Pick one. (9) Hank: even granting half your objections it's still the right default — absent a demonstrated special cost, get the information; the burden is on Nora to show her case is special. (10) Vera: and she can't discharge that burden from where she's sitting, because the evidence about how she'd cope with knowing is the kind of thing you only get by knowing.

I kept my mouth shut, mostly because I couldn't tell which of them was doing the math right. So: referee it move by move. For each of the ten, say whether it's clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong, and why. Where a move rests on a technical claim — the flatness/piecewise-linearity business in 2, the convexity failure in 4, the conditionalization assumption in 6, the burden-of-proof point in 9 — tell me whether the claim is actually correct as stated, and if it isn't, give me the corrected version rather than just a thumbs down. Then say who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing; I have a hunch they're each treating Good's result as being about Nora's actual situation when it's about something narrower, but I may well be wrong about that too. Please write in American English. And because I've been burned enough times by confident people at whiteboards: for each verdict, tell me how sure you are, and then single out the one point in your whole answer where you're least certain and explain what makes it shaky.
