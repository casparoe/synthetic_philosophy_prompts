so — I'm putting together a one-page write-up of where I've actually landed on the wrong kind of reasons stuff, for a workshop where people have promised to be unkind to it. Before I hand it round I want to know whether the package is even coherent. Here it is:

1. Reasons are the basic normative notion for me. I don't want value, or fittingness, or ought doing the grounding — I want them cashed out in terms of reasons. Concretely I'm happy with a buck-passing style line: for something to be admirable is roughly for it to have properties of the sort that give reasons to admire it.

2. The demon case gives a real reason, not an apparent one. If the demon will punish me unless I admire him, the threat genuinely gives me a reason to admire him. I don't want to fudge this into sort-of-a-reason or a reason in a derivative sense — it's a reason, full stop, and I think watering that down is the main way people cheat on the problem.

3. But the demon is obviously not admirable, and I don't think 1 and 2 clash, becuase the analysans in 1 gets restricted: admirable = having properties that give the RIGHT KIND of reasons to admire.

4. The right/wrong kind line is drawn by questions. A consideration is a right-kind reason for an attitude just in case it bears on the question that attitude is a way of settling — for belief, whether p; for admiration, whether x is admirable; for fear, whether x is fearsome. Wrong-kind considerations are ones that bear on some other question entirely.

5. Belief is special on my view. I take transparency and exclusivity seriously, so in the offered-a-thousand-dollars-to-believe-this-white-wall-is-blue case I have no reason at all to believe it's blue — just a reason to try to get myself into that belief. Which is why I think the problem gets over-sold when people run it on belief specifically.

6. But fear isn't like that. Reward on offer for being scared of a spider I know perfectly well is harmless: the reward gives me a reason to be afraid, not merely a reason to try to make myself afraid. Same status as 2.

Plus one more: in the mixed case, where someone pays me to admire a painting that is genuinely admirable, the payment adds to my reasons to admire it, so I have more reason to admire it overall than I otherwise would.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, ordered by severity, and for each one what has to give and what giving it up costs me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most — and dont just say go fittingness-first unless you can show me that 1 is costing me something more than a relabeling.

I also expect two specific charges, so address them head on: that 2 and 3 flatly contradict each other (a reason to admire that makes nothing admirable), and that 5 and 6 are unprincipled special-pleading for belief. Tell me whether either charge lands or whether it dissolves once you look closely, because I suspect at least one of them doesn't survive contact.

One thing I care about most, epistemically: keep separate what is actually settled in this area from what's genuinely live. There's a lot of confident assertion flying around here and I don't want my package graded against one camp's standards and presented with those standards as though everyone accepted them. If something I've written is a minority move, or if the obviously in 3 is doing work that only some people would grant, flag it as contested rather than as a mistake.
