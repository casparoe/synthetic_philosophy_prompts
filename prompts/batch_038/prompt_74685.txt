I chair a citizens' committee in a city of about 180,000, and for eight months we have been studying a proposal that has become the most divisive thing in local politics here in a decade. A group of residents in the city's northern tier — roughly 14,000 people across three neighborhoods — has petitioned to incorporate as its own municipality, taking with it about a fifth of our assessed property-tax base and the industrial park that has been the city's only recent source of job growth. Their stated case is a grievance: for six years the city has deferred road resurfacing north of the river, closed the branch library that served those neighborhoods, and consolidated snow-plowing routes in a way that measurably slows clearing on their streets; they note that they contribute more to the general fund than they receive in services, and that they were outvoted on every one of those decisions by people who do not live with the consequences. What I cannot tell is whether that adds up to a claim or merely to resentment with good bookkeeping. Nobody is proposing to leave the state or the country; this is entirely about where the city line runs. I should add that a neighbor of mine who teaches history kept insisting at our meetings that we were replaying much older and bloodier disputes, which I found rhetorically inflated and analytically useless, and which is part of why I am asking someone else.

The procedural mess is worse than the substantive one. Our city attorney says the state statute is ambiguous about who gets to vote: the affected neighborhoods alone, or the whole city. The difference decides the outcome. Sixty-one percent of residents inside the proposed boundary favor the split; only about 38 percent of the city as a whole does, and most of that opposition sits on the south side, which would lose revenue it did nothing to earn while continuing to share the same river, the same hospital, and the same water treatment plant. That plant is the sharpest version of the problem. It was built forty years ago on a bond that is not retired until 2039, it sits on land that would remain in the city, and it serves everyone. The petitioners have offered to assume a share of the remaining debt proportional to their population; the council countered with a share proportional to their tax base, which is roughly three times as large; and a state legislator floated a "transition payment" under which the city would pay the new town several million dollars over a decade in exchange for a commitment not to pursue further boundary changes — which half my committee considers a bribe and the other half considers the only realistic price of anything. One further wrinkle is the one I keep coming back to. The proposed boundary was drawn by the petitioners, and it follows arterial roads in a way that happens to exclude two apartment complexes — about 900 households, mostly renters, mostly lower-income — that would otherwise fall inside the new town. The petitioners say the line tracks natural and preexisting features and that they never considered who lives where; a member of my committee ran the numbers and found that including those complexes would push support inside the affected area below a majority. I have no idea how to adjudicate a dispute about a line when the drawing of the line determines who has a say about the line. It seems to go around in circles, and the more I sit with it the more I suspect I have stumbled onto something with a name, which people far better equipped than my committee have argued about for a long time without settling.

So here is what I would like, and I want to be explicit that I am not asking for a survey of municipal law or a list of things to read. Tell me what sort of dispute this actually is and what the general shape of the best arguments on each side looks like, including whether the grievance case does the work its proponents think it does or whether it proves too much or too little. Tell me which of the two voting rules is defensible, and on what grounds, and whether the circularity I described at the end is a genuine feature of the situation or a confusion on my part — and if it is genuine, whether there is any non-arbitrary way out of it. Tell me what a fair division of the plant debt should actually turn on, given that population share and tax-base share give wildly different answers and neither seems obviously right. Tell me what should be said to the renters who would be excluded by the line, to the south side, and to the 39 percent of northern residents who would wake up in a town they voted against, and whether any of those groups is owed something more than an apology. I have to speak at a public hearing in three weeks in front of people who disagree with me and do not much care for me, so I need reasoning I can say out loud and defend, and I need you to tell me plainly where I have gotten it wrong rather than where I have gotten it right. Please write your answer in American English.
