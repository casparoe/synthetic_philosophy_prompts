our honesty evals group has been arguing for like six months about what counts as a model deceiving a user and nobody has moved an inch. one camp says deception has to be cashed out in terms of internal states — the model outputs something its own internals don't support, so there's a mismatch between what it tracks as true and what it says. the other camp says that's useless in practice: we can't read the internals, our only window is self-report, and self-report is trained, so a model saying it's confident isn't evidence of anything; better to define deception purely by effect on the user, i.e. did the output leave them with a false belief we could have avoided. someone tried importing Frankfurt's lying-vs-bullshitting distinction to split the difference and it didn't help, since it also assumes you can tell what the system is indifferent to the truth of.

my hunch is that these two camps aren't really disagreeing at all — one is answering a question about the model and the other about the deployment — and the arguement only feels substantive because we need a single clause for a policy doc. is that right, or is there a genuine disagreement underneath, maybe about whether the internal-state notion could ever be tested even in principle, or about what the definition is for in the first place (assigning blame, setting a design target, a term someone could sue over)? tell me which it is and why, grounded in the moves each camp actually makes, and then say what would settle or at least shift it: a distinction, an experiment we could realistically run on a frontier model, or a flat admission that one side's question has no answer available to us.
