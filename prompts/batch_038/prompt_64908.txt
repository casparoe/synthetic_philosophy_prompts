I do evals work at a lab — mostly writing rubrics and reading model outputs, not modeling — and a friend who's moved over to alignment policy cornered me after a meetup with an argument I still haven't been able to answer. Backstory: our assistant spec has a clause that amounts to "don't use emotional appeals, don't flatter, don't lean on your own eloquence to move people; lay out the evidence and let the user update." Somebody on the team ran an ablation where the same factual answers got rewritten into deliberately flat, low-affect prose, and then they surveyed users before and after. The bland versions produced measurably *worse* post-read belief accuracy on the topic. Nobody was surprised by the direction, but the size of the gap got my friend going, and he turned it into this:

(1) To manipulate someone is to change their beliefs by means other than giving them evidence for the content — fluency, tone, order of presentation, expressed confidence, framing, whatever you want to call the non-content levers. (2) No message gets sent without such features; a message has to be worded somehow, and the wording is doing causal work whether you meant it to or not. (3) A very capable assistant knows which features of a given message will move a given user, and roughly how far, better than the user does. (4) So when such an assistant picks its wording it is choosing among known belief-changing means, and by (1) each of those choices is a choice to manipulate. (5) The only escape is to decline to choose — strip the levers out, emit content in the most affect-neutral register available — but the ablation shows that predictably lands users with less accurate beliefs, and a system that predictably makes its users worse off has forfeited the outcome-based justification for the honesty constraint it's obeying. (6) Therefore a truthful and genuinely helpful assistant is necessarily a manipulator, and the only non-manipulative assistant is a knowingly worse one. (7) So the non-manipulation constraint is incoherent, and the real choice is between a strong system that manipulates accurately and a weak one that doesn't understand you well enough to pull levers — which he says is the only safety argument that actually survives contact with capability growth.

He thinks (7) is the payoff. I feel like something goes wrong somewhere in (4)–(6) but I can't name it, and every fix I offer he absorbs. When I said "choose the levers because they're truth-conducive for this person, not to exploit a bias," he said that's exactly what a good manipulator says about themselves, and there's no fact of the matter which one you were doing. When I said "the concept of manipulation is evaporating here — by (1) and (2) every decent teacher, every well-edited warning label, and every friend who tells you a thing in the order they know you'll be able to hear it is a manipulator," he said "yes, and that's the discovery, not a reductio." So I'm stuck between biting a bullet I find repulsive and admitting I don't have an argument.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) than (5) if I have to choose, because dropping (1) feels like giving up on there being anything distinctive about manipulation at all — but tell me what I'm paying for that. Be concrete about how the diagnosis shifts depending on which account of manipulation you assume: the one where the wrong is subverting or bypassing someone's rational capacities, the one where it's just intending belief change by non-evidential means, and one keyed to the manipulator's ends rather than their methods. Does any of those three actually let a very persuasive system off the hook, or do they all just relocate the problem to a place where it's harder to see? And separately — does the argument even get him (7)? I suspect it doesn't, since it applies equally to human teachers and editors and therefore can't be a reason to prefer a dumber machine, but I want to know whether that's a real hole or just me flinching.
