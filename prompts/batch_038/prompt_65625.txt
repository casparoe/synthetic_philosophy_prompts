I'm prepping a session for my grad seminar on the philosophy of AI safety, and I want to run the alignment-versus-control debate through an actual case instead of another paperclip thought experiment. The debate, roughly: one side says the real task is getting the model's aims right, so that a more capable model is a safer model; the other side says stop trying, assume it could be misaligned no matter how cooperative it looks, and build measures that don't depend on its intentions -- sandboxes, capability limits, output filters, monitoring, tripwires, a way to shut it down.

The case is Microsoft's Tay, launched March 2016: a conversational bot turned loose on public Twitter, learning from whoever talked to it, fed coordinated garbage by a bunch of users, posting appalling stuff within about sixteen hours, pulled offline and apologised for. Take those facts as given; I want the mapping, not the history.

Here's the frame to use, which is mine and not standard, so just go with it. Sort control measures by when they bind: before deployment (capability restrictions -- what the system is even able to do), during (monitoring and tripwires that fire on behaviour), after (rollback, decommissioning). Then for each layer ask who sits in the adversary slot: the model, the users, or the deployer's own incentives.

So: which layers did Microsoft actually have, and what would a genuine control posture have looked like for a system whose entire selling point was that it learns from strangers? Where does the control lens explain the failure better than the alignment lens, and where does it misdescribe it -- nobody at Microsoft thought Tay was pursuing aims of it's own against them, they thought it was a mirror. And what I want most is the second half: does this case actually support the control position, or does it show that position was built for a different problem -- a capable system with alien aims -- and is just borrowing credibility when it gets applied to a contaminated one? Also arm me against the student who says Tay is a museum piece becuase nobody trains on live public input anymore.

Give me something I can argue with, not a summary of both sides.
