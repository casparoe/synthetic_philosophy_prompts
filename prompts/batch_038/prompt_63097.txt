I am assembling a seminar on penal theory and I keep coming back to a case that seems designed to break every account I want to teach: the Norwegian prosecution and sentencing of the man responsible for the attacks of July 22, 2011. The facts, as I have them and as you should treat them: a bomb in Oslo's government quarter killed eight people, and a shooting at a youth camp on Utøya killed sixty-nine, for a total of seventy-seven; the trial ran from mid-April to late June 2012 in the Oslo District Court, and the judgment came down on August 24, 2012. Two court-appointed psychiatric reports contradicted each other — the first, delivered in December 2011, diagnosed paranoid schizophrenia and would have led to compulsory psychiatric care, while a second team appointed after that report was widely attacked concluded in April 2012 that he was not psychotic and was criminally responsible; the court unanimously accepted the second. On the first day the defendant announced that he did not recognize the court's legitimacy because it derived its authority from parties that supported multiculturalism, and he then admitted the acts while pleading not guilty on grounds of necessity. He was convicted of terrorism and premeditated murder and sentenced to twenty-one years of preventive detention (*forvaring*) with a minimum term of ten years, extendable by a court in five-year increments for as long as he is judged dangerous; ordinary Norwegian law then capped imprisonment at twenty-one years, with no death penalty and no life-without-parole. He later served his time under a regime shaped by the "normality principle" — the punishment is the deprivation of liberty and nothing more, with prison life made to resemble life outside as far as security allows — and in 2016 sued the state over his near-isolation, winning at first instance on the ground that it was inhuman or degrading, only to have that finding overturned on appeal in March 2017. A later application for release after the minimum term was denied.

Please analyze this episode through R. A. Duff's communicative theory of punishment — punishment as a secular penance addressed to the offender as a member of the political community, aiming at repentance, reform, and eventual reconciliation, with hard treatment serving as the medium of that address rather than as an independent evil to be justified. I want the mapping done carefully before it is evaluated. Who, exactly, is the communicating agent here, given that the address is delivered by a court, drafted by prosecutors, executed by a prison service operating under the normality principle, and then re-litigated in a civil suit about cell conditions? What is the content of the message, and how does a determinate twenty-one-year term with an indefinitely extendable tail function as a communicative act — is the extension mechanism a continuation of the same address, a second and different address, or an admission that the communication failed? Who counts as the audience: the offender, the survivors and bereaved families, the Norwegian public, or some transnational public? And what would count as uptake — what would success look like in this case, and does the theory have the resources to say that it did or did not occur? Set Duff's account against a desert-and-censure alternative of the von Hirsch type, where proportionality constrains the hard treatment and rehabilitation cannot license detention beyond what the offense deserves, and say which of the two makes better sense of what the Norwegians actually did and why they defended it.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. There are at least four pressure points I would like you to work through rather than around. First, the defendant expressly rejected the legitimacy of the body addressing him: if Duff's theory presupposes that the offender is a member of the community being addressed, what happens when the addressee denies the relationship, and can the theory say anything other than that the communication is one-sided? Second, the inversion in the sanity dispute — the defendant insisted on being held responsible, preferring censure to treatment, which is not the posture communicative theory usually imagines; does the first psychiatric report, had it prevailed, reveal a competence threshold that the theory needs but does not supply? Third, preventive detention: a sentence whose endpoint depends on continuing dangerousness seems to make the length of the address a function of the addressee's disposition rather than of his deed, and I want to know whether that is a fatal incoherence for a proportionality-respecting communicative theory or something the theory can absorb. Fourth, the isolation litigation: if the punishment is an address, does solitary confinement amount to gagging the medium, and is the appeals court's reversal a problem for the theory or merely for its application? Where the theory is silent — most obviously on the question the Norwegian public actually argued about, namely whether twenty-one years could ever be enough — say that it is silent instead of forcing it to speak. Conclude with a verdict on the episode itself: does this case confirm Duff's account, constitute an anomaly it should have anticipated, or show that the account was never really meant for cases of political violence on this scale? I am not looking for a bibliography or citations, and you should not speculate about sources you cannot check; write the whole thing in American English.
