I'm a third-year PhD student in cognitive science, mostly working on visual word recognition, and I've talked myself into a fairly strong position about letterforms that I want stress-tested before I build a dissertation chapter on it.

The position: what makes every 'a' an 'a' is a style-invariant core, and that core is best modelled as an equivalence class of pen trajectories rather than as a shape. So the space of roman lowercase glyphs factorises cleanly into letter-identity times style — a discrete set of 26 classes crossed with a continuous style manifold (slant, weight, contrast, x-height, terminal treatment, that kind of thing) — and every glyph is exactly one pair, with no residue left over. The two factors are genuinely orthgonal: you can change style without touching identity, and change identity without touching style. My test for the difference is roughly that style changes are deformations you can perform continuously while keeping the letter legible throughout, and identity changes are edits you can't reach that way without passing through a moment where the glyph reads as something else or nothing at all. I've been reading the old Letter Spirit work, but my claim is stronger than I think they make it: I want the core to be an actual object you can point at, not an emergent soft-assembled thing.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed essentialist like me would concede as counting against the view versus wave off. A few I've already worried at: single-storey versus double-storey 'a' and 'g', which are the same letter but have basically no trajectory in common; W and the ampersand, which are letters/symbols whose identity comes from being fossilised ligatures; the long s; context-dependent letter shapes in Arabic, where one letter has several forms that never appear in the same slot; Cyrillic Р versus Latin P, which share a lineage and a shape but are different letters of different alphabets; and the rn/m and I/l/1 collisions, which look like evidence that style leaks into identity. Are any of those actually decisive, or can I absorb them?

Also: what would the rival accounts predict differently from mine — a family-resemblance/prototype view with no core, a conventionalist view where a letter is just whatever role the orthography assigns, and a learned-embedding view where identity is wherever the representation happens to cluster? What finding in the reading or perceptual-learning literature would separate us, and is any of it decisive rather than just uncomfortable? And be honest about whether my factorisation is guaranteed by construction — since style is defined as whatever's left after identity is factored out, does the view risk anything at all, and if it's insulated, is that a problem for a claim of this kind? For what it's worth, I'd be most shaken by something showing that people can't do the swap test I described.
