I teach an undergrad political philosophy seminar — 18 students, one 90-minute block, and we're on the unit about precommitment: Ulysses and the mast, then the constitutional version, where a founding generation tries to bind people who aren't around to consent to it. Every year I lecture this and every year they nod along and then hand me a paper concluding that entrenchment is basically fine. I want them to run into the problem instead of hearing about it.

So: design an in-class game where they build a precommitment device themselves and then watch it fail — but fail in the specific way I want engineered, not the obvious way. The obvious failure is open repeal: the later group just votes the rule away and everyone says "ah, so it had no binding force." Too easy, and it isn't what actually happens to constitutions. What I want them to hit is the version where the rule is never formally broken. The later group complies with the letter of it while hollowing out everything it was for, and the people who wrote it can't point to a single sentence that got them. And ideally the students who draft the rule end up being the same students who reinterpret it two rounds later, so nobody gets to claim the high ground in the debrief.

Rough shape I have in my head, but push back if you've got something better: a shared pot of tokens across three rounds; in round one they either divide some now or write a rule governing the later rounds; between rounds the composition of the deciding body changes; there's an adjudicator who says what the rule means.

What I need from you:

- A payoff scheme with no real money. The tokens have to convert into something this class actually cares about, not candy. Give me two or three options and tell me how big the prize needs to be to generate genuine temptation while still being something I can afford to give 18 people every term.
- The distinction I want them to physically run into: binding yourself by removing the option (the mast) versus binding yourself by removing the temptation (the wax the crew got in their ears). Then the constitutional analogue of each — veto points, jurisdictions, supermajority requirements on one side; controlling who the decision-makers are and what they arrive wanting on the other. The game should let them try both flavors and let them notice that one survives the succession problem better than the other. Have an opinion about which one, and make the mechanics actually reflect that rather than just asserting it.
- Minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list I can assemble the morning of, and verbatim text for everything they read. The rule-writing template matters enormously: if I let them draft freeform they'll write something airtight in four minutes and the whole reinterpretation phase dies.
- A debrief that maps named moments in the game onto the concepts — entrenchment, the sheer indeterminacy of any rule they could have written in round one, the fact that whoever interprets the rule is built out of the same later selves it was supposed to constrain, and the Jefferson–Madison exchange about whether one generation can bind the next. I want them to leave able to state what would have to be true for a precommitment to actually bind, and why that condition is itself the whole problem.
- What predictably breaks the first time I run it. Specifically: what I do if the class writes a genuinely airtight rule, what I do if the adjudicator is scrupulous and refuses to stretch the text, and what I do if two students reverse-engineer the succession twist during round one and warn everyone.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that in a real constitution the drafters are dead and the successors were never in the room, whereas here the same 18 people hold both roles and know it, which risks making the grievance feel like a scheduling annoyance. Tell me how much of that weight I have to supply myself and how.
- What changes if I split it across two 50-minute meetings a week apart, since the week-long gap might make the uncertainty in round one feel real in a way 90 continuous minutes can't. Say whether it's worth the extra class.

No lecture up front, and the words "precommitment," "entrenchment," and "Ulysses" should appear nowhere in any handout. They meet the problem first, the vocabulary after.
