For the epistemology seminar I am currently enrolled in, I have been working through classical Indian accounts of knowledge and trying to settle, case by case, what I myself think makes something a distinct pramāṇa rather than an item on an inherited list. Please answer in American English.

Here are my verdicts. When I mistake a coiled rope for a snake, the cognition is not knowledge, even though my eyes were open, functioning, and in genuine contact with the object. When I accurately recall the argument from Tuesday's lecture, that too is not a fresh source of knowledge, however reliable the recall. When a villager tells me the gavaya is like a cow and I later pick out the animal in the forest on that basis, I do come to know something new, yet I would rather say the work was done by perception plus memory of a verbal statement than by a separate instrument. When I infer fire on the distant hill from smoke, I gain knowledge, but only because I once witnessed the smoke-fire connection, so inference looks derivative on perception. When I judge that there is no pot on the table because I fail to perceive one, that again looks derivative to me, a piece of inference about a visible absence. But when I conclude that the heavy man who is never home during the day must be eating at night, I want to insist that this is a genuinely separate route, one that neither perception nor inference supplies. And when a man I know to be a habitual liar happens to give me accurate directions to the temple, and I arrive, I want to say I knew where I was going, whatever his character.

Is there a single principle of pramāṇa-individuation that can make sense of all of this? I have seen novelty of content, a distinctive causal operation linking instrument and object, and non-contradictedness proposed as the criterion, and I cannot tell which one my own verdicts presuppose. Please state the principle, or the small number of serious candidates, precisely; identify which of my cases does the work for each clause; and work out what it delivers for two cases I have not raised — a speaker who is entirely trustworthy but sincerely mistaken, and the extraordinary, universal-grasping perception that is supposed to underwrite the invariable concomitance inference depends on. Then say plainly which of my verdicts cannot be saved. I suspect the postulation case and the liar case pull against each other under any principle I would find acceptable; if that is right, tell me which one a Nyāya epistemologist would force me to surrender, whether the surrender is principled or merely tactical, and whether the position I am left with is closer to Nyāya or to Mīmāṃsā.
