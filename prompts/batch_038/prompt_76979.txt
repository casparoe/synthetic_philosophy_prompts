I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm trying to build a piece around a reader letter I can't quite get a handle on. The guy is a retired logic teacher who now does contract work evaluating language models, and he sent me an argument he says proves nothing can have a belief at all — not a person, not a machine — and, worse, that the argument can't even be stated. His version, cleaned up a bit by me (I may have mangled it, and I'd rather be told so):

1. For a sentence to say something true or false, its parts have to amount to more than a list: they have to be combined into a single thing with a truth value.
2. Whatever does the combining isn't itself one of the parts being combined. Call it the combiner.
3. If the combiner is an item alongside the parts, then the result — combiner-plus-parts — is a further item that itself stands in need of combining, and the same demand applies to it, without end.
4. So there is no such thing as combiner-plus-parts. There are only lists.
5. If there are only lists, nothing has a truth value, so nothing is believed, asserted, or thought.
6. But step 5's own reasoning runs on relations — being a premise of, being combined with — and by step 4 there are no such things. So the argument can't state its own premises either.

He says 6 isn't a bug, it's the whole point: reality is such that our representing vocabulary breaks down, and a self-undermining argument is still an argument that the vocabulary is incoherent rather than a reason to ignore it. When I pushed back, he said every reply he's heard begs the question somewhere — either you deny that the combiner is an item, which he calls refusing to answer the question; or you say the regress shows only that relations can't be explained rather than that they don't exist, which he says concedes 4; or you say a proposition is a structure and not a list, and structures don't need a combiner, which he calls a label rather than an answer. He also mentioned it's a very old argument, something about relations, going back well over a century, which is part of why I'm nervous about publishing my own half-baked response to it.

So: which step do you actually reject, and why is the rejection not just a refusal to engage? I want the cheapest consistent way out, not the most fashionable one, and I want to know what it costs — what you can no longer say about truth conditions, predication, or the difference between a sentence and a list of words once you take that exit. Separately, tell me whether 6 should change the verdict: is a self-defeating argument automatically worthless, or can it work as a reductio on some assumption it shares with its own premises, and if so which assumption? And if your honest view is that the whole thing is an artifact of treating propositions as complexes made of constituents, say that plainly and say what you're giving up to make it stick, because my source will say that's exactly the move under dispute.

Please play the part of a metaphysician who teaches this material to grad students and actually has a view about it, rather than a referee's report. Think it through before you answer — sketch the candidate attack points first, maybe three or four of them, then commit to one and defend it against the objections in his second paragraph. Don't hand me a balanced tour with the choice left to me; I've got 2,000 words to file and I need to know what you'd bet on and why. Last thing: flag anywhere you're reading him more charitably than his wording strictly warrants, since I suspect a lot of the work in arguements like this turns on exactly that, and I need to know whether I transcribed him into a weaker or a stronger version.
