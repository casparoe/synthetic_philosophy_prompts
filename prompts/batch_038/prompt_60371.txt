I direct a consumer-insights team. My formal training is one undergraduate statistics course and no game theory, so I read trade-press books on group decision-making rather than papers. One chapter of a book I picked up over the holidays is devoted to information cascades, and its central passage runs as follows:

"For decades economists held that markets could not go badly wrong for long, because prices pool the independent information of millions of participants and competition weeds out anyone who ignores it. In 1992 a model by Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch turned this around. Picture two restaurants side by side, one nearly empty, one with a queue out the door. A passerby who can see the queues reasons that the people in line must have had some reason to choose it, and joins; the next passerby sees a longer line and does the same. Soon the crowd is only one observation deep: everyone after the second or third person has stopped gathering evidence and simply follows, because it pays to go along with the majority. That is the mechanism behind fashions, restaurant fads, and speculative bubbles — not panic or stupidity, but rational people choosing to ignore what they privately know. The disturbing feature is how fragile the whole thing is. A cascade built on a hundred imitators can be shattered by one person who refuses to conform and states why, which is where the practical lesson for regulators comes in: mandate disclosure, force early movers to publish their reasons, and bad herds break up on their own. Two further consequences follow. Adding more decision-makers stops improving the group's accuracy the moment a cascade starts. And the much-touted wisdom of crowds obtains only when people decide simultaneously and in secret; as soon as they can watch one another choose, the crowd is no wiser than the first person who moved."

I largely accepted this at the time, but two things have stuck on re-reading. First, "it pays to go along with the majority": as I understood the model, nobody is being rewarded for conforming, and that seemed to be the most interesting thing about it. Second, the fragility claim appears to be carrying the entire regulatory conclusion, and I cannot tell whether it says what the chapter needs it to say.
