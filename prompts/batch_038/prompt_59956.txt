I'm a third-year doctoral student in a philosophy-of-AI seminar, and my term paper defends a particular proposal about side effects. I'd like a stress test of the thesis itself, not of my implementation and not of the literature around it.

The thesis: an agent can be penalized for side effects without anyone first specifying what is good. The measure I'm defending — I'll call it discernible-future erosion — works as follows. Fix a coarse description language L over world states (a finite stock of predicates) and a bit-budget N. Two future histories count as the same future just in case no difference between them can be stated in L within N bits; that gives an equivalence relation on histories. From a given state, take the set of equivalence classes still attainable within a fixed horizon and measure its size. The impact of an action is the expected loss in that measure relative to a baseline continuation policy — an inertial continuation that does nothing for k steps. Nothing in the definition is a utility function: what enters is only the cardinality of a set of coarse-grained futures and a description budget. The claim is that this yields a usable penalty term, to be added to a sparse task reward or paired with a minimal goal, which captures something like not reshaping the world unnecessarily while remaining value-free.

I am aware of the family this belongs to — reachability-flavored and attainable-utility-flavored penalties, and the toy benchmarks built to test them — and of the generic complaints aimed at that family. What I want is the exposure of my thesis specifically. Please give me:

1. A ranked list of the defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward: which formal results, which concrete cases, which benchmark outcomes, and which conceptual objections should actually move me. For each, say what the world would have to look like, and whether I could absorb it by re-tuning N, the language L, the horizon, or the baseline continuation. If absorption is always available, say so plainly rather than letting me off.

2. Two probes answered head-on. First, an agent can either preserve a functioning research station or convert it into many cheap, individually reversible structures, each of which unlocks further coarse-grained futures; my measure appears to prefer the conversion. Is that a reductio, or do I have a non-question-begging reply? Second, an irreversible action prevents a person's death, and my measure registers it as significant impact because death forecloses futures. Which of the two is the worse problem for me, and why?

3. A separation of the hits I should concede from the ones I should dismiss. I already grant that L encodes salience — some differences are cheap to describe and others are not, and which is which is not a neutral matter. Tell me whether that concession is fatal or merely relocates the burden, and whether there is any principled way to hold that coarse ordinal salience is meaningfully less demanding than a full cardinal value specification rather than just vaguer.

4. The trade-off coefficient problem. Once the penalty is added to a task reward, some weight has to be chosen. Is choosing that weight a value specification in disguise, and if so does my thesis quietly reintroduce exactly what it claimed to avoid?

5. An assessment of whether an impossibility-style result is lurking here: conditions I would find plausible — say, dominance, neutrality between relabelings of futures, and continuity in the horizon — whose joint satisfaction would force any coherent impact ranking to be representable as a monotone function of some utility, collapsing my measure into the rival it was meant to sidestep. If such a result is plausible, state the conditions as crisply as you can and say which one I should be prepared to give up.

6. Finally, what my thesis predicts that a full-value-specification rival does not — where the two actually come apart, so that failure would be discriminating rather than merely disappointing.
