I'm in my fourth year of a political science PhD, writing a dissertation on the ethics of nuclear posture, and my committee has asked me to submit an explicit position statement ahead of a workshop at which three of them — one of whom spent a decade in defense policy — have promised to attack it. My training before this was in philosophy, and the chapter in question is meant to be the normative one rather than the IR-theory one, so what follows is the set of commitments I actually rely on when I argue about targeting doctrine and declaratory policy. I've glossed each so you can see what it is doing in the argument. The difficulty is that I have been living with these for two years and can no longer tell which of the conflicts among them are real and which are artifacts of how I've phrased things. What I want from you is an audit, not a survey: no history of the debate, no reading list, and no recommendation that I give up the project and become a disarmament advocate. Tell me which of these cannot all be held together, how badly each conflicting pair conflicts, and what the cheapest available revision is in each case, together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list.

1. **Justificatory ground.** The reason a state may possess nuclear weapons is prospective and outcome-based: the practice raised the expected cost of great-power war high enough to keep such wars from being fought. I do not think these weapons have any value apart from the wars they prevent, so the whole justification sits in the counterfactual.

2. **Targeting restriction.** I am a deontologist about noncombatants: deliberately making cities and civilian populations the object of attack is impermissible whatever it secures. Retaliatory planning must therefore be confined to military objects — deployed and reserve forces, command and control, warhead storage, the weapons infrastructure generally. I'll call this the counterforce restriction, and I treat it as a constraint on planning, not as a prediction about how any actual exchange would unfold.

3. **Sincerity.** I don't think bluffing is available as a long-run policy. A threat is a threat only if the threatener has a genuine conditional intention to execute it, and over decades the difference between a posture you would act on and a posture you wouldn't shows up in force structure, alert rates, dispersal habits, and targeting files, which adversaries study and probe. So whatever deterrent I am justifying in (1) has to be one that would actually be executed.

4. **Ex-post assessment.** Whether carrying out a threat is permissible must be judged at the moment of execution, on the information then available, not at the moment the threat was issued. On that basis: a retaliatory strike delivered after one's own cities are already gone would prevent nothing, protect no one, and add only deaths. I think such a strike is impermissible, and I think any competent deliberator would see that it was impermissible at the time.

5. **Loss of control as a resource.** I'm persuaded by the Schelling-style thought that the most credible threats in a nuclear crisis are the ones no single decision-maker fully controls — heightened alert postures, pre-delegated authority, launch procedures and timelines that make escalation partly a function of what the situation does rather than what anyone decides. Deterrence holds largely because each side fears losing control of events, not because either side has a clean, fully-formed intention to destroy the other.

6. **Intentions are not morally inert.** Forming an intention to do what would be wrong is itself a morally significant fact about the agent, whether or not the triggering condition ever obtains. I want no part of the view that a conditional intention to do something monstrous is unobjectionable merely because the condition never fires.

7. **Legible limits in declaratory policy.** I think it is permissible to risk nuclear exchange to defend the territorial integrity of treaty allies and impermissible to do so for prestige, standing, or market access; and I think that line should be stated openly in declaratory policy rather than buried in targeting plans, both so adversaries can calculate against it and so one's own legislature and public can hold the executive to it.

8. **Level of assessment.** The ethics of deterrence should be assessed at the level of standing practices — what it is like for many agents to sustain a given posture over decades — rather than decision by decision. Particular acts inside a justified practice can be wrong without the practice being unjustified, and a practice can be unjustified even when each act inside it is fine.

Two further things would help. First, rank the conflicts by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and say explicitly which pairs that look inconsistent to me dissolve under inspection — I expect that some of what I've written is only superficially at odds, and I would rather be told which than have all eight items flagged. Where a conflict turns on an empirical claim rather than a conceptual one, say so and say which empirical claim. Second, identify the single commitment that is load-bearing in the sense that revising it would resolve the largest number of the others, sketch the resulting position, and tell me what it has to surrender — in particular, whether the revised view still leaves states permitted to possess these weapons at all, or whether it only preserves that permission by words.
