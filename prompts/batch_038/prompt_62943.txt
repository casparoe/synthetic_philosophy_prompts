I'm assembling the reading packet for the last third of my upper-level metaethics seminar — the block where we do the is-ought gap and the whole naturalistic fallacy industy — and I've dropped in Mill's posthumous essay "Nature" (the 1874 one from Three Essays on Religion). Partly because my collegue over in environmental philosophy keeps citing it as though it settles the appeal-to-nature question once and for all, and partly because half my students want to file it under "Mill avoids/commits the naturalistic fallacy," which I'm pretty sure is the wrong box but can't articulate why. Here's the stretch I want to walk through with them Tuesday:

> "For, in the sense of the word "nature" which has just been defined, and which is the true scientific sense, Art is as much Nature as anything else; and everything which is artificial is natural - Art has no independent powers of its own: Art is but the employment of the powers of Nature for an end."
> [...]
> "It thus appears that we must recognise at least two principal meanings in the word "nature". In one sense, it means all the powers existing in either the outer or the inner world and everything which takes place by means of those powers. In another sense, it means, not everything which happens, but only what takes place without the agency, or without the voluntary and intentional agency, of man. This distinction is far from exhausting the ambiguities of the word; but it is the key to most of those on which important consequences depend."
> [...]
> "Such, then, being the two principal senses of the word "nature," in which of these is it taken, or is it taken in either, when the word and its derivatives are used to convey ideas of commendation, approval, and even moral obligation?"

Three things I'm stuck on. First, what's the actual structure of the move — is Mill making a semantic point about an ambiguous term, a metaphysical point about the causal order, or a normative one, and how much survives if you pry those apart? The "Art has no independent powers of its own" line seems to me to be doing an awful lot of quiet work: it reads like it presupposes something like causal closure, or at any rate that human volition already sits inside the "inner world" he mentions. So suppose someone grants him libertarian freedom, agents as genuine uncaused causes — does the whole dichotomy collapse, or does only one of the horns go, and if only one, which? Second, where would a naturalistic-fallacy charge even get purchase here? My instinct is that his conclusion in this passage isn't normative at all, it's that "natural" is being recruited for normative work it can't do on either reading, which is a claim about a word rather than a derivation of an ought from an is — but then I owe the students an account of where the essay's actual normative oomph comes from, and I suspect it's being smuggled in from the utilitarianism rather than earned by the analysis of "nature." Third, and this is where I expect to get pushback: is the two-senses division exhaustive? When he asks which sense is in play whenever the word is used "to convey ideas of commendation," I can't shake the feeling that he's set up a straw man, because the naturum sequi he names a couple paragraphs earlier — the Stoics, the natural law people — surely didn't mean "don't build dams." They meant something closer to living in accordance with right reason, or with the rational order of the whole, which looks like a third sense his dichotomy has no slot for, and a sense on which the "everything artificial is natural" objection simply doesn't land. Is there a charitable reading that covers that case, or is the omission real?

What would help most: the argument laid out premise by premise with the tacit premises flagged and labeled as semantic / metaphysical / normative, a straight verdict on each of my three questions, and a sentence-by-sentence paraphrase of the quoted stretch I can hand to the weaker students without them having to fight the Victorian syntax. Please don't send me off to the secondary literature — I've got a stack of it on my desk already and the students won't have read any of it, so everything you need should come out of the passage plus whatever general background on Mill you've got. And if you think I've just misread him on the third point, say so bluntly; I'd much rather find that out now than in front of the seminar.
