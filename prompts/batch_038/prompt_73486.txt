Bit of context before the list: I run a very small evaluation function inside a welfare-to-work provider — two of us, and I do the analysis myself, largely self-taught out of epidemiology textbooks. We've just finished comparing an intensive caseworker-led track against the standard offer, and the write-up goes to the board next week. Before it does I'd like someone to check whether my statistical commitments actually hang together, because I have a nagging feeling they dont and I can't locate where the trouble is.

The package:

1. No-causes-in-no-causes-out. The numbers can never tell me which variables to condition on. That has to come from knowing how the scheme was actually run — who got pointed at which track and why.

2. I don't condition on anything recorded after assignment. No mediators, no colliders. Total effects or nothing.

3. Risk differences, full stop, and I make a point of it in the report. I don't trust odds ratios or hazard ratios because they're non-collapsible: you can adjust for something that plainly can't be a confounder and watch the estimate move anyway.

4. My working test for whether a crude comparison was confounded is to adjust for the candidate covariate and see if the estimate moves. Moves materially → the crude one was confounded. Doesn't move → it wasn't.

5. I hold something like a sure-thing principle here: if the intensive track raises the employment rate within every stratum of a pre-assignment variable, then it raises it in the population as a whole. A table that says the opposite must have something wrong in it.

6. The data. Intake severity was scored at referral, before anyone was assigned. There were 400 high-need participants and 400 lower-need ones in total.

   high-need / hard-to-place:
   - intensive track: 300 assigned, 120 in work at 12 months (40.0%)
   - standard offer: 100 assigned, 25 in work (25.0%)

   lower-need:
   - intensive track: 100 assigned, 80 in work (80.0%)
   - standard offer: 300 assigned, 210 in work (70.0%)

   whole cohort:
   - intensive track: 400 assigned, 200 in work (50.0%)
   - standard offer: 400 assigned, 235 in work (58.75%)

   So it wins by 15 points and by 10 points inside the strata, and loses by nearly 9 points crude. My verdict: the crude figure is confounded by intake severity, the honest answer is that the track helps, and I told the board their headline comparison is meaningless and gave them the two stratified figures instead.

7. I also told them that if they extend the intensive track to every intake, the payoff will be bigger than either of those stratified figures, since it worked for hard-to-place and lower-need people alike.

8. In the discussion section I wrote that the intensive track only pays off where the participant engages with it — even though the board asked me to condition on session attendance and I refused on the grounds of (2).

And two things that aren't commitments so much as worries I can't shake. First, I like saying that the reversal is what tipped me off that the crude comparison couldn't be trusted, which sounds suspiciously like the data telling me about causes, contra (1); I don't think it is, but I couldn't say why if pushed. Second, between (6) and (7) I sound like a relativist — I keep saying there's no such thing as the effect of the track, only effects relative to some particular mix of participants — and then I turn round and tell the board their number is flat wrong, as though mine were the true one. I can't tell whether that's incoherent or just bad-sounding.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Go through the set and tell me which items genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, ranked by severity, and for each real one say what has to give and what the cheapest repair costs me. Please actually work the numbers rather than taking my word for them: check that the table behaves the way I claim, and work out what the standardised figure would be if everybody got the intensive track, and whether that supports what I said in (7) about it being bigger than the stratified effects. If two of my items are both perfectly fine and merely look like they clash, say so plainly — I don't want all nine flagged as equally damaged. Then finish with which single revision would clean up the most.

One last thing. Where I've got something wrong that everyone who works in this area would agree is wrong, just say it's wrong. But where the disagreement is live — which measure to use, whether a change-in-estimate is evidence of anything, whether non-collapsibility is a defect or just a property — please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't hand me a contested position dressed up as the consensus. No references needed, I just want the reasoning.
