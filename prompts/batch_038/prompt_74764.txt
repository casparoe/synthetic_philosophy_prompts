I'm an infrastructure engineer, SRE-ish, at a mid-size cloud company — no philosophy background whatsoever, I read this stuff on my phone at night. Our team has a Slack channel that's nominally for incident reviews and has instead turned into a place where me and one of my coworkers (I'll call him R) argue about AI risk. I've been reading around Bostrom's existential-risk material and got hung up on the singleton idea — roughly, that at some point technology gets capable enough that a civilization either consolidates into one single decision-making agency or it doesn't survive, so the far future probably contains a singleton. R turned that into an argument for why we should build one on purpose, starting now, and I spent all weekend trying to find the hole in it and couldnt, which is annoying becuase the conclusion makes me queasy. Here's his chain, written as charitably as I could manage:

(1) Surviving indefinitely requires preventing every world-ending act your members could pull off, not just most of them. One is enough to end the whole thing, so the required success rate is 100 percent.
(2) The only way to make sure a given act doesn't happen is to be able to stop it before it happens — noticing it after the fact, or cleaning up afterward, isn't preventing it.
(3) Being able to stop an act means having the authority to rule it out in advance, plus the means to enforce that ruling.
(4) As technology advances, the range of acts that could end the world keeps growing, since the ceiling on what a small group can build keeps rising while the set of things groups seriously want to do doesn't shrink — so eventually the potentially world-ending acts include pretty much anything anyone might want to attempt.
(5) So a durable civilization has to be able to forbid, in advance, essentially all action.
(6) A single body that can forbid essentially all action, everywhere, is a singleton.
(7) Therefore every durable civilization is a singleton, and every civilization that never becomes one gets destroyed sooner or later.
(8) So the real options are a singleton or nothing, and since a mediocre singleton beats nothing, the right move is to build one deliberately and as fast as possible, by whatever route is most likely to get there first — debating whether the singleton would be a good one is a debate you only get to have if you win the race.

If I had to rank things, I'd keep (1) — I don't see how you argue with "one is enough" — and I'd happily delete (8). But R says (8) is just (7) plus arithmetic, and that if I want to reject (8) I owe him an explanation of why (7) is supposed to have no policy consequences at all. My own instinct is that (4) is the soft step, though I worry that rejecting (4) is just me being optimistic about technology and dressing it up as logic. There's also a counterexample I keep wanting to throw at (7) from my actual job: most of the stuff I depend on has no single authority anywhere — DNS, BGP, the certificate system, half of open source — dozens of independent bodies that can each break things globally, and it's held together for decades. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real counterexample or a bad analogy, since none of those bodies can end the world and maybe (4) simply hasn't reached them yet.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I want to know what gets given up, not just a note that the step is bad. Also, why did each of the tempting steps look fine in the first place — every one of them looked fine to me for about two days, and I'd like to understand the mechanism by which they seemed okay. Does my infrastructure counterexample actually bite on (7), or is it a category error? And if (7) is the thing that has to go, can you sketch what a durable non-singleton would concretely look like — specific enough to answer R's "I can't picture one," because I can't picture one either and I think that's the real crux. One last thing I can't resolve: I can't figure out what observation would show (7) to be false, and I can't tell if that means the word "singleton" is quietly expanding to swallow every arrangement that happens to survive, or if I'm just not smart enough to see the test.
