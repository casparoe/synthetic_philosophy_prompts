I've spent the better part of twenty years reading rationalist blogs and forums, which means I've read the standard parables of information cascades more times than I can count: the queue outside the restaurant everyone concludes must be excellent, the two urns and the publicly announced ball draws, the panel of diners who each privately prefer the empty place and order from the crowded one anyway. The models are clean and the fiction built on them usually isn't, because it quietly converts a story about rational agents into a story about foolish ones — a rumor-monger, a panicking mob, a levelheaded outsider vindicated on the last page. I'd like a short story that keeps the rationality intact and still works as fiction.

The setting I want is a ground search for a missing person in forested, road-poor country, largely carried out by volunteer teams working a gridded sector map, with a planning chief in a command trailer who maintains a paper probability map and reallocates effort from what teams say on the radio. Nobody is ordered to believe anything. That detail matters: the cascade should run through self-directed attention and informal reporting, not through commands, so that every participant is doing something defensible.

Please build the story so that it carries the following, in roughly this order of importance:

1. Nobody is stupid and nobody is lying. Every report is honest and every inference is reasonable given what the reporter had. If the reader can locate the failure in one person's character, the story has failed.
2. Show that the *strength* of a private signal does not survive transmission. I want at least one moment where a searcher who is barely better than guessing and a searcher who is nearly certain produce the same six words on the radio, and no listener downstream can tell them apart.
3. Show a character noticing that a report from later in the sequence is not evidence about the woods — it is a function of what the reporter was told to look for and of what everyone before them reported. The recognition should be partial and uncomfortable, not a speech.
4. Include the coverage confound: teams were sent to some sectors and not others, so "signs here" is over-weighted and "nothing there" is treated as evidence when it is really just absence of searchers. The probability map should visibly mistake effort for information.
5. Let the eventual unraveling be a change of representation rather than an arrival of new facts — several confident reports turn out to trace back to one physical clue (a single print, a single piece of litter, a single hearsay witness account) that the map counted repeatedly. The correction should feel like someone re-marking a page, not like a revelation.
6. Give me one character with a genuinely strong contrary signal who has to decide whether to spend credibility on it, knowing a lone dissent will be read as idiosyncratic and possibly as motivated, and knowing that being right is not the same as being believed. Resist the shape where that person is proven correct by the final paragraph; if they're right, make it cost something being right doesn't repay, or make the fix come from a mundane re-examination instead of from conviction.

Constraint on vocabulary: no character may say cascade, herd, herding, groupthink, echo chamber, confirmation bias, wisdom of crowds, or Bayesian. The concepts have to be carried by radio traffic, the map, the grease pencil, the check-in log, and what people do with their hands. Keep the operational language plain and generic; don't attach the search to a real named agency or invoke real doctrine, and don't cite or invent sources — I'm asking for fiction and craft notes, not references.

Format:

**Part One: The Story** — 1,800 to 2,200 words, titled. Either the planning chief or a field searcher as viewpoint; the planning chief makes items 4 and 5 easier to stage, the searcher makes 2 and 6 easier to feel.

**Part Two: Author's Note — What Carries the Mechanism** — 400 to 600 words of prose, no bullet-point summary of the plot. Walk through which passage is doing the work for each of the six items above, name the places where you simplified or departed from how such a search actually runs and why the departure was necessary, and end with the one thing you could not get into a narrative of this length and what form it would have needed.
