A friend and I lost most of a Saturday to the question of whether viruses are alive, and by the end it was plain that we were not disagreeing about viruses at all: he had in mind something about metabolism and autonomy, I had in mind something about membership in the class of things that undergo Darwinian evolution, and no case we produced could have resolved that. I would like a decision tree that sorts a person by what they are actually asking the word to do before it sorts them by which organisms they are willing to admit.

Begin, then, with nodes about the job the term is being recruited for — naming a natural kind, licensing inferences across cases, or marking a threshold of biological individuality — and only afterward move to case nodes: a prion; a giant virus carrying genes for part of the translation apparatus; an obligate insect endosymbiont whose genome is smaller than that virus's; a sterile mule; a seed in dormancy; a self-replicating RNA in a test tube; a laboratory-built minimal cell; a flame. Every node must be answerable by someone who has never seen the labels downstream of it. The leaves should include the definitionalist options (metabolic, autopoietic, Darwinian-property, and cluster-style accounts), the deflationary ones that deny the dispute is substantive, and the view on which the answer depends on whether the question is asked of an individual or of a lineage.

Tell me which pairs of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and which combinations of answers have no occupant, with a word on why each is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, keep the settled distinct from the contested: mark which parts of this represent agreement among people who work on the topic and which parts are live disagreement, and do not hand me a minority view as though it were the consensus.
