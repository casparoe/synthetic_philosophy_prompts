My flatmate Dev (ML engineer) and her brother Aaron (philosophy PhD student, epistemology) got into a shouting match about the no-free-lunch theorem on Sunday, and for reasons I don't fully understand they've asked me to referee. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Aaron: NFL is Hume's problem of induction with a proof attached. Averaged over all possible target functions, every learner has the same expected error, so nothing justifies induction except an assumption about the world you smuggle in beforehand.

(2) Dev: That's arithmetic, not epistemology. You averaged over a set containing every possible mapping from inputs to labels, most of which are pure noise. If the set is closed under shuffling the labels and you weight it uniformly, all learners tie by construction. It's a fact about uniform priors, and nobody defends a uniform prior over target functions.

(3) Aaron: Which is exactly my point. The tie only breaks once you adopt a non-uniform prior, and adopting it *is* the inductive leap. NFL doesn't refute induction, it prices it — it tells you precisely which assumption is load-bearing and that no amount of data can force it on you.

(4) Dev: Then it's redundant with the trivial observation that any two methods disagree somewhere. Worse, it's misleading, because we do test priors empirically: held-out sets, cross-validation, transfer from adjacent tasks. The theorem has nothing to say about any of that.

(5) Aaron: It says the marketing is a lie — an optimizer advertised as best-in-class across the board is really tuned to the problem family its authors happened to develop it on, and you can't see the tailoring from the outside.

(6) Dev: That's the shallow reading and the only one I'd sign. The deep reading you want is a category error: NFL ranks algorithms against benchmarks, Hume is asking whether a rule of inference can be justified at all. An inference rule isn't a competitor in a benchmark, so the theorem simply doesn't reach it.

(7) Aaron: It reaches it, because a learner *is* an inference rule with a computational budget.

One thing neither of them would settle for me: Aaron keeps saying the average is over "all possible functions," Dev keeps saying what actually matters is that the set is permutation-closed and uniformly weighted. I genuinely can't tell whether that difference changes anything or is just pedantry.

Could you go move by move and rate each of (1)–(7) from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating rather than a verdict at the end? Then tell me who wins the three sub-disputes separately — whether NFL is a restatement of Hume or a category mistake, whether (3)'s "it prices the assumption" move survives (4), and whether the permutation-closure detail does any work — and finally what both of them are missing that would have ended the arguement sooner.
