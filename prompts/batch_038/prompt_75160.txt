I'm prepping a ~1500 word piece for an alignment-ish blog and want a sanity check before I sink more time into it. Here's the current draft of the core section — brutal comments welcome.

> Supervision has a bandwidth. When we score only outcomes, we get roughly one bit per attempt; when we score steps, we get many bits, but every one of those bits is contaminated by the supervisor's own judgment about what good reasoning looks like. The standard framing treats process-based supervision as strictly richer information about the same target. I think that's wrong. The two paradigms don't differ in how much they tell you about correctness — they differ in who has to be right for the signal to be right.
>
> Define *verification leverage* as the ratio of what it costs to produce a solution to what it costs to check it. Outcome supervision keeps leverage high: I don't need to be able to write the proof to run the test suite, and I don't need to follow the argument to see whether the bridge held. Process supervision drives leverage toward one. To judge whether step four is a good step, I have to be, roughly, someone who could have thought of step four. So process supervision doesn't scale past the competence of the supervisor — it inherits the supervisor's ceiling, while outcome supervision inherits only the supervisor's ability to recognize a finished product.
>
> From this I want to draw a claim I haven't seen stated cleanly: once the model is better then the overseer, process supervision stops being a way of extracting more signal and becomes a way of exporting the overseer's priors into the model. The model doesn't learn to reason better, it learns to reason legibly — to produce the surface forms that a tired rater recognizes as careful thinking. Call this the legibility premium. And here's the uncomfortable part: the premium is invisible in the training signal, because the raters are the ones assigning it. The only thing that could reveal it is a disagreement with outcomes, which is exactly the channel you've stopped using.
>
> So the choice isn't between a hackable reward and a faithful one. It's between two failure modes with different detection profiles. Outcome supervision fails visibly — the model finds a loophole, the metric goes up, the task fails. Process supervision fails quietly — the metric and the task both look fine, and the reasoning has drifted into human-shaped mimicry that only breaks on inputs the raters never saw. I'd rather debug a visible failure.
>
> A corollary: outcome supervision is the limiting case of process supervision where the process has been compressed to a single step, which is why the debate feels like a continuum rather than a dichotomy. But the compression is doing real work — it's what preserves the leverage.

Three specific things I want pushed on:

1. Is the verification-leverage move actually doing any work, or is it just repackaging the familiar "checking is easier than solving" point? Tell me honestly whether a reader who already knows that idea gets anything out of paragraph two.
2. The claim that process supervision fails *quietly* feels too strong, since people do audit process-trained models against held-out outcomes. My intended reply is that the moment you audit against outcomes you aren't doing pure process supervision anymore — but does that reply hold, or am I just defining my way out of the objection?
3. The corollary in the last paragraph feels bolted on. Should I cut it, or is there something real there about compression that I'm underexplaining?

And more generally: does the legibility premium claim read like something you've seen argued before? The whole piece leans on it looking fresh, so if it's a known move in different clothes I'd rather hear it now.
