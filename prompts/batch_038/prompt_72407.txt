Consider the case of Eugene de Kock, and use it to test the truth-for-amnesty bargain that sits at the center of the South African model of transitional justice. The facts I want you to work from, so that nothing turns on your recall of them: de Kock commanded the security police counterinsurgency unit based at Vlakplaas, which in the 1980s and early 1990s abducted, tortured, and killed anti-apartheid activists; he was convicted in 1996 on eighty-nine counts and sentenced to two life terms plus 212 years; he testified extensively before the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, disclosing the operation of the unit at length and shifting responsibility upward onto senior politicians and ministers; his amnesty application did not succeed on the serious counts for which he was imprisoned, so the conviction stood; and in January 2015 the justice and correctional services minister, Michael Masutha, placed him on correctional supervision, announcing that the release served national reconciliation. In the intervening years he had continued to give information, helped locate the remains of victims, and maintained contact with some victims' families, a relationship documented at length by the TRC psychologist who interviewed him.

Begin with a disciplined mapping rather than a verdict. Set out the architecture of conditional amnesty under the commission's founding statute — individualized application, full disclosure of all relevant facts, association with a political objective, proportionality between act and objective, public hearing, victim participation, and a mandate bounded by cut-off dates running to the 1994 election — and then set beside it the architecture of the instrument actually used in 2015: a discretionary ministerial release on correctional supervision under ordinary corrections legislation, justified by reference to reconciliation, with the perpetrator's cooperation and remorse as the salient evidence. Say what each instrument demands, who is empowered to grant it, who has standing to be heard, and what the applicant has to pay.

Then run the analysis in both directions. What does the conditional-amnesty framework explain about this release, and what does it misdescribe or fail to reach? I am particularly interested in an asymmetry you should address head-on: once the transition window closes, does the case for releasing de Kock get stronger, because the truth he traded for has continued to accumulate outside the commission's machinery, or weaker, because the political necessity that made the trade rational has evaporated and there is nothing left to buy? Relatedly, treat the proposal that the commission's justification lay not in the outcome of release but in the price extracted for it — cross-examined, public, victim-facing disclosure — and say whether a release granted without that price is a continuation of the same policy, an anomaly the theory cannot classify, or a category error. Address also whether reconciliation means the same thing in 2015 as in 1995, or whether the later invocation equivocates between a settlement among former adversaries and the moral repair of victims; and whether the fact that the release went to an imprisoned foot soldier rather than to the architects he named tells us something structural about how the theory distributes its benefits.

Finally, say where the framework is simply silent — on sentence length, on perpetrators who tell the truth but fail the political-objective or proportionality tests, on clemency after the transition, on the state's failure to prosecute those who never applied at all — and refuse to make it speak there. Conclude with a judgment about whether this episode confirms the theory, strains it, or reveals that its justificatory logic was never meant to survive the transition it was designed to manage, and whether any rival framework — retributive, restorative, or grounded in a duty to prosecute — handles the case better without explaining away what the commission actually did.
