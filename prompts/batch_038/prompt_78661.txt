Bostrom's simulation argument gets wheeled out all the time by skeptics of longtermism as a dampener on fanaticism about the far future — if we're probably in an ancestor simulation, the astronomical payoffs are fake, or terminable at someone else's whim, or trivially cheap to duplicate, so don't pour anything into the millionth-millenium stuff. I want the strongest case for a version of that skepticism almost nobody actually makes.

Specifically, argue this: conditionalizing on the simulation hypothesis doesn't lower the *value* of the far future one bit. What it lowers is your confidence that any present action of yours is causally hooked up to that far future in the shape the fanatic's expected-value math assumes. A simulated history's continuation, its physics, and its off-switch all depend on decisions made outside the system by agents you can't model, predict, or influence — so the conditional probability of "my x-risk work now averts a catastrophe in 10^15 years" collapses in a way the unconditional probability doesn't, and it collapses far harder for tiny-probability/astronomical-payoff bets than for ordinary near-term interventions where the causal chain is short.

Push the argument as hard as you can. Don't dress a contested view up as the consensus.
