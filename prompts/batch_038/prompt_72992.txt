I'm building the commitment layer for a system of negotiating software agents — they make offers, give assurances, and sometimes flatly commit to future actions toward humans and toward each other, and we have to decide which utterances actually bind the agent and when the binding stops. Before I write any of that down I want the philosophy, not the engineering. So: what does a theory of *why promises bind* have to deliver to count as adequate? Not the history of the debate, no references, no names of books — the spec.

Give me the desiderata as a numbered list, each one with the case or consideration that motivates it, and for each say whether it's genuinely non-negotiable for any theory in this area or whether it's a demand that comes out of a particular camp (or out of my use case) and could be dropped without disgrace. Cases I already suspect belong on the list and want handled explicitly, not waved at:

- the deathbed promise kept in secret, promisee long dead — nobody left to release it, no expectation left to disappoint
- a promise extracted after three days of no sleep and steadily escalating pressure
- a promise about a feeling rather than an act — "I promise I'll never resent you for this"
- a promise to do exactly what you were already morally required to do anyway
- an agent that commits beyond its mandate, where the other side can't check the mandate
- a plain message saying I'll pick you up at 6, with no promissory language in it at all
- a promise whose being kept leaves the promisee worse off than breaking it would
- whether you can promise not to release yourself from a promise

Also the distinctions the theory must not collapse: promise vs. contract, vs. statement of intention, vs. assurance or prediction, vs. threat or offer, vs. vow; and "binds" as creating a duty vs. as handing the promisee a claim vs. as merely supplying a strong reason. Plus whatever facts it has to stay consistent with — e.g. that the promisee and only the promisee gets wronged, that promises can rightly be broken sometimes, that compensation doesnt obviously cure the wrong. Add anything I've missed, especially things that only look like requirements on inspection.

Then the part I care most about: is some subset of these jointly unsatisfiable? If yes, give me the smallest set that can't all hold and say which one you'd drop and why — if the list itself has a hole in it that's the real result, not a defect in the exercise. And tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking out otherwise plausible theories.

Close with three or four lines max on how the standard families do against your list — the ones grounding bindingness in the promisor's will, in the promisee's reliance or expectation, and in the social practice. Don't score them, just say which requirement each one quietly gives up.

One constraint from my end: nothing on the list can depend on facts nobody could ever check from outside, so no requirement that leans on what the agent privately believes the other party believes about its intentions — we have to implement this thing. But if you think that constraint is exactly the wrong one to impose and it's what's wrecking the theory, say so instead of obeying it. And dont hedge, pick.
