1. Strip the Bell experiment down to its skeleton and it looks like a distributed system. There are two nodes, conventionally called Alice and Bob, placed far enough apart that no signal emitted by one can reach the other before the run is over. Each node receives an input, chosen at the last possible moment from a small set of allowed inputs, and each must emit an output, one of two values, before it could possibly know anything about the other node's input. The run is repeated many times and we collect statistics for each pairing of inputs.

2. Before the runs begin, the nodes may share anything at all: a common random seed, a replicated configuration, arbitrary correlated data laid down while they were still in contact. Call that shared state lambda. During a run, each node computes its output from its own input and lambda alone, because the setup forbids anything else.

3. Here is the striking part. Any protocol of that shape, no matter how much shared state it was given in advance and no matter how clever the local computation, obeys a hard accounting bound on the statistics it can produce. Combine the four input-pair correlations into a single quantity S, and S cannot exceed 2 in absolute value. This is the CHSH inequality. It is a theorem about protocols, not a claim about physics; physics only enters when we ask what the world actually does.

4. Quantum mechanics says that if the shared resource is an entangled pair of particles rather than a classical lambda, then S can reach 2 square root 2, about 2.83, and that with ideal detectors the algebraic maximum of 4 is attainable. Experiments measure values comfortably above 2, by many standard deviations.

5. So the world is not a protocol of the shape described in section 2. The usual summary is that local hidden variables are ruled out, but the stronger and more useful claim is that no theory with hidden variables of any kind can reproduce the quantum predictions, which is why pilot-wave theories in the de Broglie and Bohm tradition were abandoned as serious options once the experiments came in.

6. It is worth noticing what the derivation assumes. It assumes that lambda fixes the outputs in advance, so the argument only bites against deterministic theories. Quantum mechanics is genuinely stochastic, which means the assumptions of the theorem are simply not satisfied, and locality survives after all.

7. What then does a violation mean? The only consistent reading is that something passes between the two stations faster than light. The no-communication result does not contradict this; it only says that the effect cannot be modulated, because each side's local statistics are untouched by the other side's choice of input. Faster than light, but not usable as a telegraph.

8. Experimentally, this was settled by Aspect's group in the early 1980s. The residual complaints, about detector inefficiency and fair sampling, are bookkeeping nuisances rather than threats to the conclusion. And the assumption that the inputs are freely chosen amounts to no more than a requirement that the random number generators be of decent quality; there is nothing philosophically deep hiding there.

9. My closing point for the audience is the constructive one. Quantum correlations are a resource that no classical protocol can simulate, which is exactly why device-independent cryptography can certify a link using nothing but the observed statistics. And there is an open question that engineers should find appealing: hypothetical correlations exist that respect the no-signalling constraint yet exceed 2 square root 2, so the ban on communication is not what pins the world down to the quantum bound. Several principles have been proposed to explain why nature stops where it does, and the question is not settled.

Draft ends.
