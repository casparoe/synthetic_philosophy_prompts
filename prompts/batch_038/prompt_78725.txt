So I'm on a charter commission for a mid-size city — think of us as the standing body that rules on proposals about how the city governs itself. Our track record so far, roughly in the order we decided them:

1. Approved an independent utility-rate board staffed by economists and engineers, where the council fixes the goal (affordable, reliable service) and only the actual rates are left to them.
2. Rejected a two-votes-for-college-graduates scheme for city elections — even though it was drafted so the council could repeal it by a simple majority.
3. Approved a licensing board for contractors, mostly made up of master contractors, that writes and grades its own exams.
4. Rejected a civics test as a condition for voting, even neutrally administered, very easy, and translated into six languages.
5. Approved a jury-style panel of residents picked by lot to hear disciplinary appeals against city employees; their verdict is basically final, reversible only on a legal error.
6. Approved *with conditions* an independent redistricting panel of retired judges — the condition being that any map can be overturned by a ballot measure.
7. Rejected handing a panel of professional economists the final say on where to set the city's poverty line, and therefore on who qualifies for assistance.

Here's the new one. A permanent citizens' assembly of 200 residents chosen by sortition, stratified so it looks like the city demographically, sitting for a year, free to hire whatever experts it wants, empowered to issue recommendations on anything the council refers to it. The council would then have to either enact the recommendation or put it on the next ballot. The kicker: the assembly itself decides which of its own recommendations get that fast track, and the whole arrangement gets written into the charter, amendable only by two-thirds of the council plus a public vote.

How should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of 1–7 is the closest analog, and which one is the seductive but misleading one? I also suspect our record is compatible with at least two different underlying principles that split on this case, so lay those out, say what each implies here, tell me which one you think we've actually been running and whether that makes us epistocrats, democrats, or something I don't have a name for — plus, what principle we'd be committing to if we went the other way. And if the precedents genuinely don't settle it, describe the kind of proposal we should put on next quarter's agenda that would force the difference into the open.
