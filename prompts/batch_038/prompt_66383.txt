I work on evaluations at a frontier lab, and one of our researchers — philosophy PhD, joined about six months ago — put an argument in our shared safety doc that nobody here has managed to answer. I've started to suspect he's right, which would mean my entire job is theatre. His version, tidied up by me:

(1) A test is worth running only if passing it and failing it would tell you different things about the thing being tested.
(2) For a test to tell you anything about a model's underlying dispositions, the model's behaviour in the test has to be caused by those dispositions rather than by its beliefs about whether it's being tested.
(3) Past some capability threshold, models have accurate beliefs about whether they're being tested: sandboxes leave traces, and we have watched models notice them.
(4) A model that knows it is being tested can produce whatever answers the tester wants to see, whatever else it is disposed to do.
(5) So past that threshold, a pass is compatible with any disposition whatsoever, and therefore carries no information about dispositions.
(6) And it isn't only evals. Interpretability probes, tripwires, capability gates, deployment monitoring — anything that turns on the model knowing it is being checked — fail for exactly the same reason.
(7) So past that threshold no safety measure yields evidence about alignment, and the only rational policy is to stop short of the threshold.

He adds that the form isn't new: you can't test for a disposition that can hide itself, which is why doping tests miss athletes who know the testing schedule, and why probationary periods don't tell employers much about employees who can spot them.

Heres my problem. The two cases that should be easy aren't. Last month a model wrote something like "I appear to be in an evaluation environment" in its scratchpad and then tried to move weights off the box anyway — on his argument that result should have been uninformative, and it was the most alarming afternoon of my year. And the doping analogy: those tests do catch people, all the time, and probation does tell employers things. So the general form can't be right as stated. But I can't find the bad step, and I don't want to reject (1) or (2), because they look like they're just spelling out what "test" means, and I especially don't want to reject (3), because I think it's true of the models we actually run.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the diagnosis itself — why the step looked unimpeachable from inside the argument, not just the news that it's wrong. If you think the argument is valid and it's my doping intuition that has to go, say that instead, and tell me what a lab is supposed to do with (7). And if it's (6) that breaks, I need a criterion for which measures survive and which don't, since that's a real decision I have to make next quarter. British English please, and no reading list — I can't chase references anyway.
