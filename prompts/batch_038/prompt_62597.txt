I've been going in circles on an essay for my seminar on action theory and I could use an outside pair of eyes. We read Anscombe's piece arguing that Truman was a murderer for ordering Hiroshima, plus the usual background on double effect, and the discussion section went badly for me because I couldn't defend either side. Here's the argument I sketched out that evening, which I'm now pretty sure proves way too much:

(1) On the double-effect picture, what separates the tactical bomber from the terror bomber is intention: the tactical bomber aims at the munitions plant and merely foresees the children's deaths as an unwanted side effect, while the terror bomber aims at the deaths themselves, since demoralizing the enemy is his means.
(2) But the tactical bomber foresees the children's deaths with certainty — not as some probability, as something he knows will happen if he drops the bomb.
(3) Anscombe's own way of picking out intention says that what you intend is fixed by your plan, and by what you'd be committed to doing to carry the plan through; the description under which you act is what matters, not what happens to occur afterwards.
(4) Given (2), the children's deaths are not an accident or a surprise relative to the tactical bomber's plan. They are in it, under a description he knowingly adopts.
(5) And given (3), he'd be committed to proceeding anyway even if he were somehow told the children would be killed, since the mission's success doesn't depend on their surviving. So by his own practical reasoning he has adopted their deaths.
(6) Therefore the tactical bomber intends the children's deaths just as much as the terror bomber does, so the distinction in (1) collapses, and if the terror bomber is a murderer, the tactical bomber is one too.
(7) Anscombe obviously doesn't want that. So either double effect is a fraud, or her account of intention is wrong.

The seminar's problem is that I don't think I can happily accept (7) — I'm actually inclined to think the terror/tactical difference tracks something real — but every escape route I try seems to blow up (1), which she needs for the Truman verdict, and Truman is the whole reason we're reading her. So: which step should I actually reject? Walk me through each one and say why it looked innocent when I wrote it down, because that's the part I can't reconstruct — I want to know what's doing the smuggling. Then tell me what each of the main repairs costs: refusing (2), refusing (3), refusing (4), or refusing (5), and whether any of them leaves the Truman case still coming out as murder.
