My roommate's designing a two-player board game for our games night — finite grid, each player drops their own stones, no dice, no hidden information, and it ends when someone completes a target pattern or the board fills up. He keeps insisting the first player cant be at a disadvantage, and the way he argues for it is an analogy: getting to move first is like being handed a free call option — a thing you're never obliged to use, so it can't leave you worse off than not having it. That's also how he explains strategy-stealing to people at the club: if the second player had a winning plan, the first player just declines their opening turn and then runs that plan, and since declining can never hurt, the first player wins too.

There's a wrinkle in his game he thinks the analogy still covers: stones can never be removed once placed, and there's a second kind of pattern on the board — completing one of those loses you the game on the spot.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Four sections, in this order: "The mapping" (a table is fine, ~150 words — who's the option holder, what's the underlying, what counts as exercising, what counts as letting it expire); "Where it breaks" (~300 words — rank the disanalogies by how much they actually matter for his argument, not by how striking they are); "His game" (~200 words — does the first-player-can't-lose conclusion survive the two wrinkles, and if not, what's the smallest change to the rules that restores it); and "What the analogy is smuggling in" (~150 words — state the premise it's quietly doing the work of, without using the analogy at all).
