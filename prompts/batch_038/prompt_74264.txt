I'm putting together a small reader of scripted dialogues for my grad seminar on measurement and justice, and I need one on the fairness impossibility results that isn't just two people reciting the standard talking points at each other. Here's the frame I want.

Two members of a state commission, Dana and Eli, have each drafted the "fairness" section of a report on whether the jurisdiction keeps using its pretrial risk score. Each thinks the other's draft is an embarrassment, they have one hour in a conference room, and the report is due. Dana holds that the impossibility theorems — the Chouldechova and Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan family, calibration/sufficiency against separation/error-rate balance against predictive parity — are philosophically deep: they show our ordinary concept of a fair risk score is internally inconsistent, and the inconsistency gets sharper rather than weaker once you notice it only bites when base rates differ, since unequal base rates are exactly the kind of social fact a theory of fairness is supposed to be responsive to. Eli holds that they're arithmetic in a tuxedo: given unequal base rates and one shared scalar score, everything downstream follows by algebra; the classifier that just outputs each group's own base rate satisfies calibration and every parity condition simultaneously, which shows the theorems say nothing at all until you smuggle in an accuracy premise; and the moment you allow group-specific thresholds or group-specific monotone rescalings, "impossible" collapses into "here's a menu, pick one and pay for it." Neither of them is stupid and neither gets to win by talking past the other. So: Eli has to explain why adding the accuracy premise doesn't turn the theorem into something substantive, and Dana has to explain whether an impossibility that evaporates the instant a legislature permits group-specific threshholds is an impossibility about fairness or about a representation choice we could simply reverse.

To stop them hovering at 30,000 feet, make them fight over this toy population. Two groups, 100 people each; I've done the arithmetic already so they don't have to.

- Group L (base rate .20): score .1 → 40 people, 4 positives; score .2 → 40 people, 8 positives; score .4 → 20 people, 8 positives.
- Group H (base rate .40): score .2 → 40 people, 8 positives; score .4 → 30, 12; score .6 → 20, 12; score .8 → 10, 8.

The score is calibrated within each group by construction. At a shared cutoff of .4, group L gets FPR .15, FNR .60, PPV .40; group H gets FPR .47, FNR .20, PPV .53. And in this table the only shared cutoffs that equalize FPR (or FNR) are the two degenerate ones — everybody in, everybody out. Both characters should have something to say about that last fact, and they should not say the same thing about it.

Then, partway through, Dana pulls out something Eli hasn't seen: a criterion she's been toying with, which she calls margin parity. Fix the operating cutoff c and a width δ; compute FPR and FNR using only the people whose score falls inside [c−δ, c+δ]; require those restricted rates to match across groups. Her pitch is that the decision is only genuinely contestable at the margins — someone sitting at .9 or .05 gets the same treatment however we argue — so maybe that's the only place a fairness constraint needs to bite at all. I want Eli to take this seriously rather than sneer at it, and I want the two of them to work out together whether margin parity is a real escape from the impossibility, the same impossibility restated over a smaller set, or a rule that buys you a decision procedure by endorsing precisely the group-consciousness the report was commissioned to avoid.

Close with each of them writing one sentence under a heading that reads "points on which the commission is not agreed." The sentences should be honest and specific, not a fudge, and they should not converge.
