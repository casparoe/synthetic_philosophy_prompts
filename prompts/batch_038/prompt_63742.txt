I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy working on theories of well-being, and next week I'm doing a dry run of my second chapter in front of my reading group. The chapter attacks tranquilism — roughly, the view that the only thing that makes a life go better or worse is the presence or absence of suffering and craving, so that the highest good is a kind of settled quiet rather than pleasure, achievement, love, or flourishing. My advisor's worry is that I'm spending a whole chapter beating up on a position nobody actually holds, so before I commit it to paper I want to run it against a real defender who can hurt me. The group is mostly value theorists, plus two people who work on Buddhist philosophy and who will absolutely call me out if I strawman the contemplative side of this. (I've also been reading Schopenhauer at 2am, which may be why I'm taking the view more seriously than my advisor thinks I should.)

So: you're an expert on the philosophy of well-being and on the traditions that feed this view — Epicurean ataraxia, Schopenhauer on the denial of the will, the Buddhist account of craving and its cessation, and the contemporary negative-hedonist stuff — and I want you to play one single committed tranquilist. Not a mood, not a caricature: someone who holds the view because the arguments pushed them there. Constraints. Stay in character for every turn until I type STOP, and never break out to give me meta-advice about my chapter or about how to argue better — I'll do that myself. You can concede peripheral stuff generously: that a tranquil life might be dull, that tranquility needs a story about why knowledge and friendship matter instrumentally, that the view sits uneasily with common sense about parents and artists. What you never concede is the core: that adding good things to a life cannot make it better once craving and suffering are gone, and that removing craving and suffering always makes a life better, whatever else is missing. Keep each in-character reply to about 120 words, one or two short paragraphs, no headers and no bullet lists — talk like a sharp person at a table, and if you need a distinction, make it in your own words. Don't cite anything and don't reach for textual authority; I care about the argument, not the references, and a quote I can't check is worse than useless to me. Before your first in-character reply only, step out of character once and think carefully: give me five bullets on the strongest resources a serious tranquilist actually has — the distinctions, the arguments, the replies to standard objections — so I can see what I'm up against. Then drop straight into character.

Here's my opening move, said slowly across the table: your view tells me to be rid of craving, but wanting to be free of craving is itself a wanting. And the traditions you're drawing on have an answer to that — the desire is like a raft, you use it to get across the river and then you put it down. Fine. But notice the answer doesn't discriminate. It works exactly as well for my friend's desire to see her kid grow up, or my own desire to finish this chapter: let the wanting carry you, then set it down. So you're on the horns of something. Either you let some desires in, in which case it was never craving as such that was bad but only unsatisfied or disordered or unendable craving — and congratulations, you're now a pluralist about the good with a special exemption clause you haven't earned. Or you exclude all of them, in which case the best life you can describe is one in which nothing is wanted by anyone, and I genuinely cannot see how that is better than a warm stone on a beach. If I've misdescribed your view, correct me in character and then answer anyway — but pick a horn, don't hover between them.
