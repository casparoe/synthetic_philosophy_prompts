I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly physics and neuroscience — but our desk got handed an anniversay piece on the postwar war-crimes trials and I drew the short straw. I'm not a lawyer and I really don't want to file something dumb.

At a panel I sat in on last week, one of the speakers (a historian, fairly well known, won't name him) kept deploying the phrase "victors' justice" to close down questions. Somebody asked whether the Tokyo tribunal's aggressive-war counts were defensible as law; he said that's precisely what people who worry about victors' justice mean, and moved on. Somebody else pointed at the acquittals at Nuremberg — three defendants walked, which doesn't smell like a rubber stamp — and he said acquittals on the margins are how show trials buy credibility. Then my editor rang and said he wants the phrase as a section head, over a passage arguing the whole enterprise was legally worthless and that the modern ICC inherits the identical defect.

So: act as a legal philosopher and historian of international criminal law who has actually read the judgments rather than the summaries of them. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming — before you commit to any structure — all the different claims a person could be making when they say "victors' justice." Then give me a menu, one numbered entry per distinct reading, each stated precisely enough that I could check it against the record, and for each one tell me: is it a platitude nobody denies, a substantive claim that happens to be right, contentious, or just false? And crucially, is it descriptive or normative — does it say the proceedings were biased, or that they were invalid, or that they should never have been held?

Here's what I've gathered. Please tell me which of my notes are shaky rather than letting me build on them:

- the Nuremberg tribunal tried 22 of the 24 indicted and acquitted three;
- Dönitz got ten years, but I'm told the bench declined to punish him on the submarine-warfare count because the Allies had done the same thing in the Pacific, with the defence putting in written answers from the American admiral in command out there;
- at Tokyo the Indian judge dissented across the board, partly on nulla poena sine lege grounds — that crimes against peace and crimes against humanity were being applied to conduct that wasn't criminal when done — and partly because the bench excluded Western colonialism and the atomic bombings and seated no judges from the defeated countries; two other judges wrote separate or dissenting opinions;
- the crimes-against-peace concept was pushed hardest by the Soviets, who also supplied a judge, while having invaded Poland themselves in 1939 and fought Finland;
- the emperor and his family were exempted from prosecution by decision of the occupation authorities, and the biological-warfare people got immunity in a data-for-immunity arrangement;
- I've also been told the Nuremberg bench held that its crimes-against-humanity count didn't reach the prewar persecution of German Jews inside Germany unless it was tied to the war counts. That's the one I'm least sure about.

Two things I especially want adjudicated. First: which of those facts support which readings of the phrase, and which readings — if any — would actually license my editor's conclusion that the verdicts were legally worthless? My instinct is that "the winners wrote the statute and staffed the bench" and "the guilty men were convicted" can both be true, and that the phrase gets used to slide from one to the other. But I can't articulate the slide, and I dont know whether there are readings where it isn't a slide at all. Second: does the phrase even survive the move to the Yugoslavia and Rwanda tribunals and then the ICC, where there's no victor on the bench? Is victors' justice the right diagnosis there, or is the real charge something else — and if it's something else, is my editor entitled to say the ICC "inherits the same defect"?

Also give me the strongest case a defender of the trials would make, including what the live alternatives actually were in 1945 (my understanding is that summary execution was genuinely on the table with the British and something closer to a predetermined spectacle with the Soviets — check me on that), and say which reading of the slogan such a defender would have to concede, if any. Finish with a short list of the uses of the phrase you'd want me to refuse to print.

Lay the readings out as a menu rather than picking a favourite and explaining it. Don't give me a bibliography, I can't check one anyway — but do flag wherever a note above is wrong or overstated.
