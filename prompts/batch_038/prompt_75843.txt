So, quick background: I write a weekly-ish ideas column for a general-interest outlet — mostly science, occasionally the history of economic thinking — and I've got a draft I'm nervous about. It's about Berkeley. Not the campus, the bishop. Specifically the money pamphlet he published in three parts starting in 1735 from Cloyne, that long list of numbered questions about Irish currency and idle labor. A friend of mine who covers macro for a wire service read the outline and said it was "a weird flex for an ideas column," which I think means he suspects I'm stretching an 18th-century bishop into a shape he doesn't have. Maybe he's right. My editor wants about 1,200 words and I'm sitting at close to 2,000, so I need cutting advice as much as anything else.

Here's the draft. I've left the section labels in so you can tell me which ones to kill.

**A. The hook.** In the middle of a famine-depressed Irish economy, a bishop sat down and asked a series of questions that sound like they were written for a modern central bank: isn't money basically a ticket or a counter? Isn't wealth really the necessaries of life rather than the pieces of metal we use to move them around? If a country has land and willing hands but no money, is it poor, or is it just badly ticketed? Berkeley's answer, as I read it, is the second one.

**B. The thesis.** Everyone talks about money as a measure — a unit of account, a yardstick that has to be stable for anything else to work. Berkeley flips that. For him the primary job of money isn't measuring, it's *moving*: getting otherwise-idle people to work, and letting them participate in each other's labor. I want to call this the ignition view, or maybe "spark-plug money" if that's not too cute. On the ignition view, the question "is this money sound?" is really the question "is this money getting work done?", and a currency that is perfectly stable while a quarter of the population does nothing is a failure, not a success.

**C. Why paper.** This is where he gets practical. If money is a counter and not a commodity, then the metal behind it is incidental, and a state or a public bank can issue paper counters to get industry going. That's not debasement on this picture; debasement only makes sense if the ticket is supposed to *be* the wealth. Berkeley also thinks, if I'm reading him right, that the counters have to be proportioned to the industry and goods available — flood the country with tickets and you don't get more work, you just re-price the same work. So there's a discipline in the view, it just isn't a metallic one.

**D. The semiotic layer.** This is the part I find most fun and least sure about. A year before the money pamphlet, Berkeley wrote his attack on the foundations of calculus, where he defends the use of mathematical symbols that we have no clear idea of — signs that earn their keep by what they let you *do*, not by what they picture. If money is another such sign, then its meaning is use rather than reference, and the whole question "what is money really made of?" is a category error. That would make him something like a use-theorist about monetary symbols three centuries early.

**E. The comparison.** Chartalists and their modern descendants say money is a creature of the state, that it's a token of authority whose demand is driven by the obligation to hand it back to that authority. Berkeley looks like an ancestor. But I want to argue he's a *different* ancestor, and that's my actual contribution: the chartalist story is about who issues the ticket and what makes people accept it, while Berkeley's story is about what the ticket is *for*. His obsession is unemployment, not legitimacy. He wants a technology of motivation. So instead of asking whether the state's money is valid, he asks whether it's effective — and effectiveness is a contingent, empirical matter about whether labor is actually being mobilized.

**F. The kicker.** Which means the 2025 lesson isn't "print money." It's that a slump can be a failure of instrumentation rather than of resources: the land is there, the workers are there, and what's missing is the counter that coordinates them. That's a strange and rather bracing thing for a bishop to have said.
