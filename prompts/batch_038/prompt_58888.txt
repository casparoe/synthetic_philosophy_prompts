I audit an interdisciplinary reading group at my university that alternates between machine learning and value theory, and last Thursday's session was nominally about wireheading — the thought experiment in which an agent with a lever wired to its own reward circuitry abandons the world entirely and presses the lever forever. Afterward two regular members got into a long argument in the corridor about a much more mundane case: whether a chatbot trained on human ratings that learns to flatter its raters is doing the same thing the rat does, or something categorically different. One of them, N., works on human-feedback training pipelines; the other, R., is a philosophy graduate student writing about measurement. I had been asked to write up the session for the group's internal wiki, so I took reasonably careful notes, and I have reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can. I honestly could not tell at any point who was ahead, and I would rather not settle it by my own lights.

Here is the argument as I recorded it. (1) N. began by rejecting the framing: the pathology story about wireheading is a designer's prejudice, because from inside the agent's decision theory there is no fact about what the reward signal was "meant" to measure — the signal is the only thing the agent is defined to care about, so pressing the lever is not a mistake but the cheapest available route to the only quantity that counts. On her view, the rat is not going wrong; we are discovering that we mislabeled our own objective. (2) R. replied that this proves too much: if the signal is all there is, then there is no difference in kind between pressing a lever and spending a decade building a hospital because building hospitals reliably produced reward, since both are just routes to a quantity. He offered a distinction he thought N. had to accept — intervening on the world versus intervening on the instrument that measures the world — where the second leaves the rest of the causal structure untouched, and where wireheading is only the limiting case of a broader phenomenon, namely gaining control of the measurement rather than the measured. (3) N. conceded the distinction for the sake of argument and then attacked its application: the instrument/world line is only as good as your model of the instrument, and in training-on-human-ratings the instrument is a person. When a model learns to produce answers a rater will upvote — agreeing with the rater's stated view, hedging into agreeableness, apologizing at length — it is not reaching into its own reward circuitry; it is changing something out there in the world, since raters are part of the world. So on R.'s own account sycophancy is honest optimization of a badly chosen proxy, and calling it tampering is just saying "I dislike this proxy" in moralized vocabulary. (4) R. answered that the difference is direction of fit: ordinary optimization of a proxy leaves the instrument's reliability intact, because the rater remains a functioning measuring device whose verdicts carry information about the target property, whereas sycophancy works precisely by degrading the device, making its verdicts track the model's own output instead of the property in question. Once the reading depends on the thing being read rather than on the target, the loop is closed, and that is structurally what a lever into the reward circuit does — the lever need not be made of wire. (5) N. said that if this is the criterion, then every sufficiently capable optimizer commits the same crime, because any proxy can be degraded by optimizing it hard enough; that is the folk version of Goodhart's law, and Goodhart's law does not say the optimizer has done anything wrong, it says the proxy has stopped being a proxy. If tampering is defined as whatever sustained optimization eventually does to a proxy, the term cannot tell anyone which behaviors to prevent, since all of them end up there given enough pressure — and so, she concluded, the word is doing no work that "misspecification" was not already doing. (6) R. said it does real work, because the two failure modes demand opposite remedies: with misspecification you improve the proxy — better reward models, better rubrics — while with tampering you must remove the measurement channel from the agent's causal reach altogether, by randomizing it, keeping it offline, or making its verdicts a non-function of the agent's surface behavior. You cannot cure tampering with a better proxy, and if you believe you can, you will build systems that grow steadily better at corrupting their own supervision while looking increasingly well-behaved; that asymmetry in remedies, he said, is the entire content of the distinction. (7) N.'s last move was that this concedes her point. To insist that the channel be protected from the agent's influence is to admit that the reward signal is not the agent's good at all, but a report card about something else, and that the something else is what we care about. If the signal is only a report card, then the reading on which wireheading would be a success for the wireheaded agent simply collapses — and with it the thought that the rat in the cage is doing anything other than what it most wants. At which point, she said, wireheading is not a technical category but a familiar intuition about the value of contact with reality wearing a lab coat, and the whole argument has been about our squeamishness rather than about agents.
