I have never taken a philosophy course. I read into this material on my own — library books, a couple of podcasts — and for three years I have been going around in circles on one problem: the statue and the lump of clay it is made from. In April the public library here is letting me lead one of their Thursday-evening discussion hours, and I have chosen that problem. Expect fifteen to twenty-five adults: a few retired teachers, several people who come mainly for the company, one or two who have heard a version of it before and will be bored, and at least three who will decide within five minutes that it is a waste of everyone's time. Sixty minutes, a rolling whiteboard, no assigned reading and no handouts.

What I want is not an explanation of the problem. I have my own views and I have had the explanation many times. I want a plan for how I should explain it to that room, and I want it to be candid about the places I am likely to fail.

1. The order. Set out the sequence of moves, and for each one state what must already be in place before it and what later step depends on it. My difficulty is that everything here seems to require everything else: I cannot get them to feel the force of the claim that there are two things on the table until they accept that two things can occupy one place, and I cannot motivate that acceptance until they see why the statue and the clay have to come apart. Tell me where to begin in practice and how to keep the whole structure from collapsing while the pieces are still loose.

2. The carrying example. I intended to build the hour on a Lego spaceship that a child takes apart and dumps back in the bin: every brick survives, the spaceship does not. I like it because nobody in that room will deny the spaceship was a real thing. But tell me whether it misfires. My suspicion is that it hands them an answer too cheaply, since the bricks are already countable objects, and someone will say at once that the spaceship was never anything over and above them — at which point I am arguing against a solution instead of teaching a problem. If Lego is the wrong choice, tell me what to use instead, and state what the example must have and what it must not have. In particular I do not want to be dragged into the ship whose planks are replaced one at a time, which is a different puzzle I have no time for.

3. The misconceptions. Map the ones the room will arrive with and the ones it will generate halfway through. I can predict several: that it is only a quarrel about words; that the statue is the clay and therefore there is obviously one thing; and that the statue is just the shape while the clay is just the stuff, which sounds like a tidy division of labour and which I think is confused, though I could not say why. Then there is the objection of my own that I have never managed to answer: if there really are two objects where I see one, a scale under the thing should be registering two weights, and it does not — so either there is one object or the problem is broken. Tell me what is wrong with that, and tell me at exactly which point in the hour each of the others should be headed off, before it sets, rather than in the discussion at the end when nothing can be undone.

4. The check. One question to put to the room in the final five minutes — not a summary and not a show of hands — where the pattern of answers would tell me whether they understood the problem rather than merely enjoyed an hour of agreeing that philosophers are silly.

5. My own position. I believe there is only one object, and I have believed it for two years; almost everyone in the room will agree with me inside thirty seconds. Tell me how to run the hour so that it does not become me walking a group of people to the conclusion they already held. Should I declare my view at the start, at the end, or never? And is there a legitimate way to make my own default position cost them something?

6. The cuts. List the things I will be tempted to reach for and should not, and tell me which of those cuts I should announce aloud to the room, with the reason, rather than make silently.

One last request, and please treat it as part of the assignment rather than as a courtesy. I know enough to know that I do not know what is actually agreed on here. Where you are describing the shape of the problem in terms that people who work on it would all accept, say so; where you are passing on a position that is genuinely disputed, mark it as disputed and indicate roughly how disputed. I would much rather walk in with a couple of acknowledged gaps than teach twenty people that some contested line is the settled answer. And if any part of the plan above rests on a simplification that a specialist would count as a distortion, flag that as well, so that I can decide for myself whether to keep it.
