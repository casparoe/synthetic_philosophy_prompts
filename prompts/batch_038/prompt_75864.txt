I am auditing a graduate workshop on machine cognition this term, and in the final session we worked through the 2016 AlphaGo–Lee Sedol match in Seoul — five games, Chinese rules with a 7.5-point komi, AlphaGo taking four of them and Lee taking the fourth, every game ending in resignation, and the Korea Baduk Association afterwards awarding the program an honorary 9-dan. Two of the participants, Miriam and Tomás, got into an argument over what the match shows about computationalism, the argument outlasted the session, and it continued by email for two days. I have reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can below. You may take the following facts about the games as given, since neither party disputed them: in the second game AlphaGo played an unexpected fifth-line move in the upper right that the professional commentators initially read as a blunder and only later revised; people on the DeepMind side said afterwards that a human would play such a move on the order of once in ten thousand attempts; strong professionals subsequently studied the move and began using it themselves; and in the fourth game Lee won with a central white play that commentators regarded as extraordinary, after which AlphaGo's own evaluation of the position collapsed and it resigned. One further piece of context: our tutor had earlier drawn a distinction between what a system produces and what it can be held to account for producing, and both Miriam and Tomás kept sliding back and forth across it without noticing, which I suspect is where a good deal of the stalemate comes from.

Here is the exchange. (1) Miriam: in game two a program produced a move that world-class professionals did not see coming, at first mistook for an error, and then adopted into their own repertoire — and its designers estimated that a human would play it about one time in ten thousand. If the highest form of human creative judgment were something no algorithm could generate, that move should not have existed. So whatever is distinctive about creative judgment is reachable by computation, and the burden of proof now falls on anyone who says the brain does something further. (2) Tomás: that reverses the argument entirely. The move came out of a policy network trained on an enormous corpus of human games plus self-play and then filtered by search, so its novelty is a recombination within a space that human play defined. Nobody ever claimed a machine cannot emit outputs that experts find both surprising and good; the claim is that emitting them is not the same thing as understanding them, and nothing about that particular move bears on the second claim, since it is precisely what a well-trained algorithm should be expected to produce. (3) Miriam: then the thesis is insulated against any evidence whatsoever, because no behavior, however inventive, can count against it, and a claim about brains that no observation about brains or their artifacts could dislodge is a stipulation about a word rather than a hypothesis about the world. As for the asymmetry you are reaching for — that Lee could be asked why he played the winning move in game four and could answer you, while AlphaGo cannot be asked anything — that is exactly what the verbal-report literature says is an illusion: people confidently report reasons that played no causal part in their own judgments, and patients with severed callosa narrate fluent explanations for behavior whose causes lie elsewhere in the same skull. Reason-giving looks like an interpretive process attached to a system it does not govern, which is a computational architecture, not an argument against one. (4) Tomás: the unfalsifiability charge mistakes a conceptual claim for an empirical one — the question is what kind of thing understanding is, not whether machines will pass tests. And your confabulation material argues the other way: to say that someone's stated reasons are wrong presupposes that there are facts about reasons against which a report can match or fail, and that norm of correctness is the very semantic notion in dispute. AlphaGo's outputs are assessed for reliability only by its designers and never by itself; a system that cannot be wrong about its own reasons has no reasons capable of being wrong. Neither of them moved, and when they asked me to referee I could not see how to score it.

Please adjudicate the argument move by move rather than declaring an overall winner first: rate each of the four numbered moves on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken through mostly mistaken, even, and mostly right to clearly right, and justify each rating by identifying what the move gets right and what it assumes without earning. Where a move depends on a premise that is true but doing less work than its user thinks, say so explicitly, and note in each case whether the party is entitled to cross our tutor's line between what a system produces and what it can be held to account for producing. Do not settle anything by invoking a famous thought experiment or a well-known authority unless the move itself already relies on it; judge the arguments as they were actually made, and treat both parties as intelligent and sincere. Format: exactly five numbered items and nothing else — items one through four corresponding to moves one through four, and item five giving your verdict on who is more right overall together with the single most important thing both of them missed. No introduction, no conclusion, no preamble; each item should run to a sentence or two.
