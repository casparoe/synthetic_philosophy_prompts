I am co-organising a one-day workshop that puts epistemologists in the same room as people who build and evaluate automated text systems. The question the day turns on is whether the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate about testimony has anything to say about assertions produced by things with no intentions: a language model's confident answer, a corpus someone else compiled, an automated alert phrased as a claim about the world. Before anyone argues, I want participants to find out what they already think. Please design the sorting instrument they will complete on paper, individually, during the first fifteen minutes of the session.

What I need:

1. Ten diagnostic items. Each must be a concrete situation with two answer options, or three where the space genuinely has three. No item may state a thesis, and no item may ask the participant to agree or disagree with a sentence containing a term of art; every item should be a predicament and a choice between ways of reacting to it.

2. A scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto positions. Please do not build in an 'it depends' or 'I would check first' option; if a participant will want one, that desire is itself the datum, and the key should record which position corresponds to refusing to answer until they have looked for themselves.

3. The leaves. At minimum: reductionism applied globally to testimony; reductionism applied only locally, item by item or speaker by speaker; the view on which a bare assertion carries a defeasible entitlement to be believed with no positive reasons required; the view on which what does the work is the teller's commitment rather than the hearer's evidence, so that believing is taking someone at their word rather than inferring from their track record; the externalist view on which all that matters is that the channel through which the claim reaches you is in fact reliable, whatever you could say about it; a view on which the screening is of content rather than source, so that plausibility against what you already believe carries the weight; and a deflationary option that denies there is any distinctive question about testimony as such. If you think a further leaf is required, add it. If you think one of mine collapses into another, merge them and say why.

4. For each leaf, one sentence in a layperson's idiom telling them what they have been saying all along, plus the single item in your instrument on which that position gives its least comfortable answer.

5. The axes. I want the instrument constructed from the space, not from a list of names. Set out the dimensions you take to be doing the sorting — where the default credence in an uncorroborated assertion sits; whether any entitlement comes from the hearer's experience, the teller's commitment, the reliability of the process, or the plausibility of the content; whether the view is global or local in scope; whether a source's own justification can pass across to the recipient who lacks it — and then show which combinations of answers occupy which cells.

6. Unoccupied cells. Name the combinations your items make available that no position takes up, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive once stated, or because nobody has thought to defend it.

7. Inseparable pairs. Flag any two leaves your ten items cannot genuinely tell apart, rather than implying a sharper instrument than you have built.

The feature I most want: construct the instrument as five matched pairs. In each pair, one item has a human source and its twin has a machine source producing the same content with the same apparent confidence and the same unavailability of independent checks — a stranger's directions against a routing system's, an archived deposition from a witness long dead against a generated summary of the same record, and three further pairings of your own choosing. Some of the cases should press on the difference between sincerity and competence, and at least one should involve a source nobody can afterwards be held to account by. The key should report not only the leaf a participant lands on but whether their pattern is stable across the pairs, and it should identify which positions predict instability: on some views the machine twin is exactly the human original in epistemic standing, on others it is a different kind of thing altogether, and the instrument ought to catch the participants who answer the pairs one way while holding a view that forbids it.

Two housekeeping constraints. Participants will not have read the literature, and nothing in the instrument should reward knowing who said what, so attach established names to leaves only where you are confident of them and describe the view otherwise. Keep each item to roughly seventy words, and keep the whole thing short enough that a room of forty can finish it in fifteen minutes without talking to one another.
