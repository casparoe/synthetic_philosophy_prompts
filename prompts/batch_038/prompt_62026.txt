I am writing the centrepiece chapter of an edited volume on the moral emotions, defending the claim that shame is the more morally informative of the two: that it can be truthful about an agent in circumstances where guilt has no object at all, and that a morality which has demoted shame in favour of guilt has thereby lost track of a whole category of moral self-knowledge. My co-contributors are predominantly Kantian, and one of the readers' reports has already delivered the objection I most need to answer: that every example I offer is either a restatement of the honour-culture material — the tragic hero, the Mediterranean village, the warrior who cannot survive disgrace — or else a case in which shame is merely guilt wearing another name. I want a case built to order that cannot be absorbed by either move, and I would like you to design it, then demonstrate that it meets the design brief.

The specifications are as follows. (a) Setting: contemporary, secular, ordinary professional or domestic life; no antiquity, no honour cultures, no combat, no hospitals and no trolleys. (b) Form: the scenario itself is a single self-contained paragraph of no more than about two hundred words, with no exotic machinery — the agent's beliefs and circumstances must be ones an ordinary reader could inhabit without strain. (c) No wrong: the agent breaches no obligation, violates no rule, is blamed by no one with standing, and owes nothing to anybody; every applicable norm, professional, legal and interpersonal alike, is satisfied, and satisfied in a way that would be judged admirable rather than merely permissible. (d) No audience: the agent is alone, has good reason to believe that the relevant facts will never come to anyone's knowledge, and — this is the crux — the shame must not be explicable as the internalised gaze of a spectator, actual or imaginary. I want a case that survives the familiar strategy of cashing shame out as an observer installed inside the head, so: no mirrors, no photographs, no diaries, no deity, no future self looking back, no imagined parent or colleague. (e) Neither status nor aesthetics: the shame must not concern reputation, rank, attractiveness, taste or competence, and must not be reducible to embarrassment. (f) Verdict: shame must come out as the better response — more accurate about what has befallen the agent, and more apt to issue in the right kind of change in them — whereas guilt, were the agent to feel it, would be a distortion, a mislocating of the matter as a debt calling for confession, reparation or punishment. Two candidates I have already tried and discarded, and which you should not reproduce: the adult daughter who nurses her father through a long illness impeccably and feels nothing when he dies (the relief invites guilt so naturally that my reader will simply declare the shame mislabelled), and the caseworker who follows every procedure to the letter and is commended for it (her conduct, though procedurally correct, is intuitively a wrong to someone, which hands guilt an object).

What I want back, in this order. First the scenario. Then the demonstration that each specification is met, taken one at a time: state what guilt would require as its object here and show that there is none; state what shame takes as its object; and show precisely how (d) is satisfied without smuggling a spectator back in through the wording of the description. A case that stipulates its verdicts — that describes the agent as ashamed and asserts that guilt would be out of place — is a failure; the verdicts must fall out of the circumstances you have arranged. Third, a diagnostic test: a short set of markers by which a reader could tell that the emotion in play is shame rather than guilt, drawn from the two emotions' characteristic concerns and characteristic desires (to disappear or to remake oneself, as against to confess, repair or be punished), and applied to your case rather than listed in the abstract. Fourth, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and run three removals — introduce a witness; introduce a victim to whom something is owed; make the agent's conduct a violation of a rule — saying for each whether the case collapses back into an ordinary guilt case or whether the shame survives, and if it survives, whether it survives for the same reason. Fifth, the two strongest attacks a defender of the primacy of guilt would make on the case as designed, and whether either can be met by repair rather than by retreat. And finally, an honest assessment of whether the brief is satisfiable at all: if specification (d) proves unmeetable in conjunction with (c) and (f), say so plainly and propose the weakest weakening of (d) that still does the work for my argument, explaining what the argument then costs me.

Please write in British English, in a formal register, and keep the whole response to between 1,000 and 1,400 words. I do not want any scholarship in it: no names of philosophers, no titles, no citations, no suggested further reading, since I am after the argument rather than its provenance. Where you need to gesture at a familiar position — the broadly Williams-inspired thought, say, that shame concerns the agent as a whole while guilt concerns particular acts, and that shame therefore retains a capacity to criticise the self which guilt forfeits — describe it in your own words and do not attribute it.
