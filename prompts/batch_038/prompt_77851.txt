I'm writing the position statement for my political theory seminar — the whole unit is Arendt on truthfulness and politics, mostly "Truth and Politics" plus the Pentagon Papers essay "Lying in Politics" — and my advisor made me put down my own commitments in a list first, on the grounds that I can't criticize her from nowhere. So I did it, and honestly the list looks worse on the page than it did in my head. Half the seminar is running this through Habermas's reading of her, which I find useless, so I'd rather get a straight audit. Here's what I'm holding:

(1) deception isn't inherently a moral crime in politics — lying is an old and legitimate instrument of statecraft, and veracity has never really counted as a political virtue; I don't have a Kantian prohibition on lying and I don't want one. (2) Factual truth is politically fragile in a way rational or philosophical truth isn't, precisely because facts are contingent: they could have been otherwise, so there's no necessity holding them in place, and a well-made fabrication is usually more attractive than the thing it replaces. (3) The truth-teller is therefore in an esentially unpoltical position — factual truth doesn't persuade, it compels, it has something despotic about it, which is why truth-tellers keep losing and why philosophy and the polis have been at odds ever since the trial of Socrates. (4) But the modern thing — PR, image-making, what she finds in the Pentagon Papers material — is different in kind from the traditional lie. The old lie concealed or rearranged particulars inside a record that stayed intact; organized lying goes after the record itself, until nothing is believable and everything is possible. That's an attack on the political realm, not just a breach of trust. (5) Contingency is also where freedom lives: action is beginning something new, the political realm is a realm of appearance and opinion, and the common world is what both joins and seperates the people sitting around it, like a table. (6) So the remedy isn't more transparency — transparency-mongering looks to me like image-making in reverse — it's institutional: an insulated class of factual record-keepers, courts, archives, whatever plays the witness role, kept out of the fight so the record survives it. (7) Self-deception is the nastiest version of the whole problem, because the deceiver gets taken in along with the audience that half-wants to be — that's her point about the problem-solvers whose models started confirming themselves. (8) And a verdict on a case: I think a citizen who flatly refuses to believe a well-documented fact commits a political wrong, not just an epistemic one — they're damaging something shared, not merely being foolish.

Go through this and tell me which of these actually pull against each other and how hard — rank the real ones by severity rather than listing everything as equally worrying, and for each say what has to give and what the cheapest revision is, i.e. the one that costs least elsewhere in the set. I'm pretty sure some pairs look contradictory and aren't, and I'd much rather you tell me which are only apparent and why than have you flag all eight; part of what I want is to see you discriminate. I genuinely don't know where the problem is, and I'm deliberately not saying which of these I'd abandon, because I think I'd choose the wrong one — don't read anything into the ordering either. Then, at the end, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and single out the one point you're least sure of, the place where you'd expect me to push back hardest if I wanted to argue with you.
