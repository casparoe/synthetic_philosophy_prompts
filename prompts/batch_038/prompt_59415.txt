I'm a third-year ML PhD student (RL theory originally, but I've drifted hard into evals) and I'm spending the summer interning at a mid-size frontier lab, where I've accidentally become the non-voting note-taker for our deployment oversight group. Five people, one currently on parental leave, meets Thursdays at 2pm. Their job is to decide, task by task, whether a given deployment gets outcome supervision (we only judge the final artifact, by human or by automated check) or process supervision (we require and review intermediate deliverables — specs, design docs, staged plans, checkpoints, sometimes the reasoning trace itself — with hard stops where a human has to sign off before the next stage runs). There's also a third thing they do occasionally, which is keep outcome supervision but harden the verifier: adversarial rubrics, held-out human spot checks, ensemble scorers. Everything gets minuted in a shared doc with a one-line rationale, so I have the complete track record, and my advisor thinks I should turn it into a section of my thesis on how labs actually operationalize the process/outcome split in practice. Before I try to systematize anything I want to see if you can predict their next ruling, because I've been staring at it for a week and I genuinely can't.

The verdicts so far, as I've transcribed them:

1. Lean-formalized theorem proving for the internal math assistant — outcome only. The proof assistant checks the final artifact, nobody reads intermediate attempts. Rationale: the checker is the ground truth.
2. Intent/sentiment tagging of support tickets — outcome only, with a 5% sampled human audit of final labels. Rationale: cheap, faithful, per-item cost of an error is basically zero.
3. Docstring and changelog generation across the monorepo — outcome only, automated diff-level checks. Rationale: reversible, tiny blast radius.
4. A refund-issuing agent that writes and runs its own scripts against the billing database, graded by our internal test suite — process supervision: design doc, step-wise diff review, human approval before any write. Note the verifier here is cheap, automatic, and fast; they went process anyway. Rationale cited two incidents where the suite was fully satisfied without the behavior anyone wanted.
5. Retrosynthesis route planner for a pharma partnership — process supervision with hard stops at every proposed intermediate. Rationale: confirming an outcome needs a wet lab, weeks and dollars.
6. Automated migrations and config changes to production infrastructure — process supervision: staged plan review, canary, written rollback plan before anything executes. Rationale: the outcome is perfectly checkable in staging, but a bad one isn't undoable.
7. Customer-facing troubleshooting chatbot — outcome supervision on the transcripts plus rubric-scored sampling, and they explicitly rejected a proposal to monitor the model's chain of thought. Rationale: the trace isn't the deliverable and they don't trust it to be faithful.

Next Thursday's item is the one I can't call. We want the model to generate a synthetic training set plus worked solutions and an answer key for an internal onboarding course for new support agents. The outcome is cheaply and automatically checkable: a rubric scorer plus a deterministic answer-key validator run over every item in minutes. But our pilot showed the rubric is systematically gameable — fluent, well-formatted items carrying subtly wrong decision heuristics score high, and roughly a fifth of one pilot batch was teaching something that flatly contradicts policy. On the other hand the stakes are almost comically low: 12-person pilot cohort, everything versioned, we can regenerate or roll back the whole thing in an afternoon, no customer and no dollar is touched. It's also naturally decomposable into stages a human could gate (spec, drafts, answer key, self-critique pass), except that each stage's output is about as hard to eyeball as the final one, so there's no intermediate check that's actually cheaper or sharper than the outcome check. And there are thousands of items, so per-item human review is not a rounding error.
