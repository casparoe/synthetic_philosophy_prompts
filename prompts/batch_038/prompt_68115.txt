I chair the grants panel of a regional arts council that awards roughly 140 project grants a year across three rounds. The panel is five people: a choral director, a graphic designer, and three members deliberately recruited with no professional arts background — a high-school chemistry teacher, a retired machinist, and a hospital nurse. We sit for two days per round and average about four minutes of deliberation per application before a vote; decisions are published with written reasons, those reasons are read into the record at council meetings, and last year two denials drew organized public campaigns against us. Our new director has imposed one requirement: every written reason must be something an ordinary resident can check against their own experience of the work. We may not justify a grant by appeal to a work's importance in its field, its innovation, the strength of the applicant's résumé, or the quality of its craft alone.

I have come to think Tolstoy's late theory of art is the only serious account that tries to say what our director wants said, and I want the panel to run on it. As I read him: art is the deliberate transmission, by external signs, of a feeling the maker has experienced, such that the recipient is joined to the maker and to others who receive it; the measure of a work is its infectiousness, which depends on the particularity of the feeling transmitted, the clarity of its expression, and, decisively, the sincerity of the maker; and separately, works differ in value according to the feeling they carry, since some feelings bind people together across class and nation while others — patriotic triumph, refined eroticism, despair worn as a badge of taste, the pleasures of a trained minority — divide them. He also has an account of counterfeits: imitations of past successes, strikingness, effects, and mere interest, which mimic infectiousness without it. He was willing to condemn Shakespeare, Beethoven, and his own novels on this basis, which is the part that frightens our staff.

Please write the procedure we would actually run, as a numbered sequence of tests with a one-page scorecard the panelists can fill in during our four minutes. For each test, say what evidence it takes, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what makes it a no. Say where the procedure terminates in a denial on its own and where it hands the question to a vote because what remains is a matter of values rather than of anything we can establish.

Four problems must be resolved rather than waved at. First, sincerity: we cannot interrogate applicants, so tell me whether sincerity can be judged from the work and the record alone, and how to stop that judgment from collapsing into a redescription of the fact that we were moved. Second, accessibility: Tolstoy tested works on plain working people, and our three lay panelists are our only version of that test — say what to do when the panel splits, and how to distinguish a work that fails to transmit from a work whose form is merely unfamiliar to us. Third, the content test depends on his religious conception of the age, which a public body cannot invoke; tell me what survives the translation into secular terms and what is destroyed by it, and how the procedure interacts with a clause in our charter forbidding denials based on the viewpoint a work expresses — including whether the content test may operate at all, and if so where, such as a tie-break or a recommendation to fund a competing slate. Fourth, mark plainly which steps are judgment wearing the costume of procedure.

Then run it on three applications. (a) A commissioned string quartet by a conservatory-trained composer: impeccably made, premiered to a full house of subscribers who applauded warmly; of our five, two report feeling nothing, one calls it clever, two were genuinely moved. (b) An amateur community piece about a factory closing, written and performed by the laid-off workers in a church hall: missed cues, poor lighting, and nearly everyone in the audience and on the panel wept; the feeling it carries is local, particular, and angry. (c) A choral elegy for the local soldiers killed in a border conflict, commissioned by a veterans' association, with the largest audience turnout of any application this year and emotional force nobody on the panel disputes. At least one of these must come out in a way I will not like, and you should say so and say why the procedure is right to do it. Finish by stating what the procedure cannot decide, where it will predictably misfire, and which distinctions in Tolstoy's account you had to drop to make it usable — along with what those dropped distinctions were doing for him.
