I run a money-and-society reading group, mostly non-philosophers, and last week a guy who works in crypto kept saying "money is just a shared fiction, it only works because we all believe it does." He used it like a trump card. When I asked whether it then makes any difference who issues the tickets, a central bank versus some anonymous protocol, as long as the belief holds, he said I was overthinking it and the answer was obviously no.

Our next session is on Berkeley's Querist, which I know is a deep cut: the thing that's nothing but numbered questions, from his years as an Irish bishop pushing for a national bank. I remembered one of the queries asking whether the true idea of money isn't just that of a ticket or counter, and another asking whether gold and silver and paper are all alike tickets for reckoning and transferring value. That sounds like the same slogan, but I don't think it is, because he also insists money has nothing to do with the metal it's made of and everything to do with getting people to work.

So can you take apart what "money is just a shared fiction" could actually be claiming? I want a menu, not a single verdict on the slogan. For each reading, tell me whose belief it's about (mine about the money, mine about everyone else's beliefs, the state's?), what exactly is supposed to be fictional (the value, the object, the obligation?), and whether it's a truism any economist would sign, substantive but debatable, or just false. Then seperately say which of those readings, if true, would settle my question about whether the issuer matters and which ones leave it completely open.

After that, map Berkeley's own view onto the menu: which readings the ticket-and-counter stuff commits him to and which it lets him reject. I'm hung up on the objection that a fiction can't buy you bread. If money is only a token, what is it a token of, on his account? I keep landing on something about power to command other people's industry rather than a promise to pay in metal, but I can't see how that differs from an ordinary IOU, or why he'd think a state can create that power out of paper convertible into nothing at all. Also, does his theory of language, signs that do their work without calling up ideas, actually carry any weight here, or is that just a nice-sounding connection people make?

Last thing: tell me which piece of this I can put to the crypto guy in conversation without sounding like I'm quoting an eighteenth century bishop at him.
