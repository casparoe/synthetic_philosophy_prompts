Gödel, Escher, Bach and the Lucas–Penrose line both recruit the incompleteness results for a philosophy of mind, but they aim them in opposite directions: one argues that we can recognize the truth of a sentence no candidate formal system could derive, so the mind is not a machine, while the other treats the same self-referential machinery as the thing that lets a physical system acquire an I, and so as evidence that the mind is nothing but a machine of an unfamiliar kind. Compare the two uses. Do they actually contradict one another, or are they extracting different contents from the same theorem, and if the latter, identify the precise step at which they part company — in particular, whether they disagree about the soundness assumption the argument needs, or about what it would take for a system to "see" a truth it cannot prove. Then take a side on the sharper question: is the incompleteness theorem load-bearing in Hofstadter's account, or could the strange-loop story be reconstructed for any sufficiently rich self-monitoring feedback system, leaving Gödel as ornament rather than argument? Say which of the two positions is more exposed if your answer is right, and what its best defense would be. Where people who work on this genuinely disagree, mark the disagreement instead of smoothing it into consensus, and keep separate what is settled about the underlying mathematics from what is contested about its philosophical upshot.
