I'm an associate editor at a small foundations-of-physics journal — I handle the quantum-foundations desk, which is where everything weird lands, and my co-editor is on leave until March so this one's stuck with me. Two weeks ago we got a submission from a pair of authors at an engineering school (no physics affiliation, but they aren't crackpots in the usual sense — the paper is clean, the notation is standard, they cite the right literature). They claim to have built a fully local, deterministic hidden-variable model that reproduces the singlet correlations exactly, all the way up to the quantum CHSH value rather than just the classical bound. Their move, as far as I can follow it: the hidden variable λ for an emitted pair isn't drawn from a fixed distribution. They draw it from ρ(λ|a,b), one that depends on both analyzer settings, and they argue that's not a signal between the two wings because the outcome on each side is still a function only of that side's setting and λ. Then they do the integral in section 4 and out comes the cosine. I checked the arithmetic twice and it seems to work, which is what's making me nervous.

The two referees are in completely different universes. Referee 1 says reject without further review: Bell's theorem proves no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce these correlations, so the paper is either wrong or dishonest, and sending it out at all wasted their month. Referee 2 says the opposite — that Bell's theorem simply doesn't apply here because the model gives up one of Bell's assumptions, that it's internally coherent, and that we'd look bad rejecting it given how much more respectable superdeterminism has gotten lately among some pretty mainstream people. Referee 2 also points out that the authors describe their λ as fixed by a boundary condition imposed at both ends of the experiment, and says that makes the paper retrocausal rather than superdeterministic, or maybe it's just unclear, and either way the authors need to say which; and 2 brings up the Bell tests that used photons from distant stars and quasars to pick the settings, which were designed to squeeze exactly this loophole, asking whether the authors have to engage those results at all.

So: draft the decision memo I'll circulate to the editorial board, plus the skeleton of the letter to the authors if we don't just reject. I need an actual call — reject, major revision, or accept with an editor's note — and the reasoning laid out so the board can push back on it point by point. Give me some criteria a non-specialist editor could apply the next time a paper like this lands, since I guarantee there will be one. Two things I don't want skipped. First, Referee 1's frustration deserves a real answer rather than being waved off — if there's a genuine cost to publishing this, name it, including the fact that as far as I can tell the model reproduces every quantum prediction exactly and so never sticks its neck out. Second, I keep going back and forth on something and I'd like the memo to settle it, or at least frame it properly: if a model like this is empirically indistinguishable from standard QM, is it a physics question at all, or is it metaphysics with integrals in it — and does the answer to that change what our journal owes it?
