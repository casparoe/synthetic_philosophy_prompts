I spent thirty-seven years as a comparative physiologist, working mostly on the sensory and diving physiology of marine mammals, and I retired three years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy seriously — first as a kind of corrective exercise, since I suspected my training had left me with unexamined assumptions about explanation, and now with real enjoyment — and for the past two years I have audited an interdisciplinary seminar on animal minds at a university an hour from my home. In April the convenors have asked me to give a twenty-five-minute paper, followed by forty minutes of discussion, at their annual workshop, which mixes philosophers of mind, animal ethicists, and a scattering of working biologists. It will be the first time I have defended a normative position rather than reported measurements, and I confess I am far more apprehensive about the question period than about the talk itself. In my old field, a hostile question was usually about method and could be settled by pointing at a figure; here I expect the hostile questions to be about whether my concepts are coherent at all, and I would rather be prepared than surprised.

The paper argues that brain size is morally relevant, but not in the way either side of the usual dispute assumes, and not as a matter of moral status. My claims, in order, are these. (i) Sentience-conferring neural architecture comes in degrees of a specific kind: what matters is the capacity to sustain a globally integrated valenced state — a single subject to whom things are good or bad at a time — and this depends on recurrent, large-scale integration rather than on bulk. (ii) Absolute brain mass is therefore useful only as a crude gate: below some threshold there is plausibly no unified subject at all, and above it the measure goes silent. (iii) Relative measures — brain mass corrected against the allometric expectation for body size, the sort of index that has dominated comparative work for half a century — are worse than useless for ethics, because the correction is calibrated to a primate standard and because the residual variance it captures reflects metabolic and life-history strategy rather than anything about experience. (iv) Total neuron counts are actively misleading, and here I lean on cell-counting work from the last fifteen years: the African elephant's cerebral cortex is roughly twice the mass of ours yet holds only about 5.6 billion neurons against our 16 billion or so, with about 97.5% of its 257 billion total neurons located in the cerebellum; conversely, corvids and parrots appear to pack primate-comparable pallial neuron numbers into a fraction of the volume, and the octopus distributes something like two-thirds of its neurons into the arms, which I take to be a genuine problem for any integration-based account and which I address by allowing graded rather than all-or-none integration. (v) My positive proposal is a saturation curve: moral weight — by which I mean the range and intensity of welfare an individual can have, not whether the individual counts — rises steeply with integrative capacity up to a threshold and then flattens nearly completely, so that mice, pigs, chickens, corvids, and possibly many insects sit on the same plateau as we do, with a sharp drop only where integration fails. I argue further that this yields a surprising practical result: the plateau is so flat that the view should make almost no difference to how one allocates welfare resources among vertebrates, while doing a great deal of work at the invertebrate boundary, where I am deliberately agnostic and say that the curve merely tells us where the uncertainty is concentrated.

Please take on the role of an animal ethicist who has chaired many workshops of exactly this composition and who also reads the comparative neurobiology competently enough to know where a retired biologist is overreaching. The room will contain: perhaps a dozen animal ethicists, a substantial minority of whom hold that sentience is binary and regard "moral weight multipliers" as a category mistake or as a slippery slope toward discounting inconvenient animals; two or three committed welfarist consequentialists who accept graded weight and will press me on how to parameterise the curve and on why saturation should be a shape rather than an admission that I have no measurement; two comparative neuroscientists, one of whom works on avian pallium and will not accept that mammalian integration concepts transfer to a differently layered architecture; a philosopher of consciousness who will insist that I have smuggled in a contested theory — recurrent processing, global workspace, whatever they choose to call it — and that the ethics is then hostage to an unresolved empirical debate; a person who works on insect welfare policy, for whom my "cliff" is the whole question; and a programme officer from a funder who will ask, flatly, whether anything I have said changes a single allocation decision. Begin by thinking carefully and setting out, in a short opening section, the load-bearing commitment of each of these subgroups, since the objections I actually get will be generated from those commitments rather than from my text. Then produce the prep sheet itself: eight to ten objections ordered by how much damage they would do to the argument rather than by how likely they are, each attributed to the subgroup it comes from and phrased in the vocabulary that subgroup would use — I want to recognise the sentence when I hear it — with a reply I could deliver in under two minutes and an explicit note on where that reply is soft, what it quietly presupposes, and whether it is a genuine weakness or merely rhetorical pressure. Flag separately which two or three objections, if they land unanswered, would require me to abandon the saturation claim rather than refine it; tell me what to concede in the first five minutes of the talk in order to preempt the two most predictable attacks; identify the single reply that has to succeed for the thesis to survive the session; and state the question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the most honest answer I could give it even if that answer is that I do not know. If you think a step in my argument is simply wrong — the inference from integration to valenced unity, for instance, or the claim that relative brain measures track nothing experiential — say so plainly in the sheet rather than constructing objections around it; I would rather be told now than in April. Please do not flatter the view, do not pad it with a general survey of the literature on animal moral status, and observe these constraints: no bibliography, no citations, no invented references, and no quotation marks around any text you attribute to a published source, since I cannot verify them and will not repeat them; where you touch empirical findings, stay at the level of settled general knowledge or clearly mark a point as contested. Use headed entries for each objection rather than continuous prose, and aim for roughly 1,600 to 2,000 words in total.
