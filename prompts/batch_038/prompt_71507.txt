I cover physics and philosophy for a general-audience science desk, and last Thursday I was in the room for a recorded panel that went off the rails in a really productive way. I'm transcribing the relevant chunk below because I want to use it in a piece, and I'm not trained in either field, so I honestly can't tell who was landing punches and who was just being fluent. Can you go through it move by move?

Setup: Dana is a theoretical cosmologist, Vik is a philosopher of physics. The panel was supposed to be about time, and about eight minutes in Dana launched into the relativity argument for the block universe — you know the one, sometimes called the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Vik pushed back and they never really recovered the thread.

(1) Dana: Special relativity says there is no privileged "now." Two people passing each other at different speeds slice spacetime into present and not-present differently, and for something far away — say a galaxy in Andromeda — those two slices can disagree by days. Neither slice is the true one; the theory doesn't pick. So if you're serious about what exists, you can't say only my slice is real and hers isn't. Union all the legitimate slices and you get the whole four-dimensional block, future included. The future is already there, which means the open-future picture of free will is dead.

(2) Vik: The physics is right, the inference isn't. You slid from "no frame is privileged" to "every frame's simultaneity slice is real." Those aren't the same claim. A presentist doesn't have to fight relativity at all — she only has to deny that "real" is what you get by unioning slices. And notice your argument needs a hidden premise: if her now is as real as mine, and her now contains events that are in my future, then those events are real. That works only if reality is one absolute collection that everyone's slices feed into — which is exactly what's supposed to be in question.

(3) Dana: Indexing reality to observers is a dodge that costs you the thing you were trying to save. If "real" is per-observer, then when you and I disagree about whether some event in Andromeda is happening now, we aren't disagreeing about anything — there's no fact of the matter and no shared world to be wrong about. But there plainly are frame-independent facts here: whether two events are spacelike separated, what the interval is. Physics isn't in the business of assembling private presents. If your metaphysics makes the world a pile of them, your metaphysics is the problem, not the argument.

(4) Vik: Two replies. First, "indexed" doesn't mean private or arbitrary. Relativity itself replaced an absolute relation with a relative one, so treating existence as a relation to a worldline is following the theory's own lead, not resisting it. Second, and more important: your conclusion never needed block reality — it needed determinism, and relativity doesn't hand you determinism. SR is perfectly compatible with indeterministic dynamics. Whether events are fixed by the past is a separate question from whether they're out there. Your free-will headline is doing unearned work.

(5) Dana: Careful, I'm not claiming determinism. I'm claiming something cheaper: it isn't that my choices are causally necessitated, it's that the events I call my choices are already located in the manifold, so there's nothing left for deliberation to settle. And your fixed-versus-there distinction collapses on contact: if the future is there, it's there whatever the dynamics say, so bolting on indeterminism changes nothing. An indeterministic block is still a block.

(6) Vik: That's the question-begging move, stated out loud. "Located in the manifold" and "real now" are being treated as interchangeable, and whether they are is the entire live issue in philosophy of time — it's not a premise you get to smuggle. Also, "nothing left to settle" ignores that the block contains the deliberating agent and her deliberation. On any account, the future region is there because of what agents do, not independently of it. So the argument gives me no reason to stop deciding anything, and a conclusion that has zero purchase on practice is a conclusion I'm allowed to be suspicious of.

(7) Dana: Fine — but look what you just did. You conceded the metaphysics and kept only the phenomenology. You're saying the block is real but it doesn't feel real from inside, and that's compatible with everything I said in move one. So my physics-to-metaphysics step stands; you've only shown it's less frightening than I made it sound.

(8) Vik: No. I said your first step was never licensed and everything after it was patchwork. And move seven is textbook retrenchment: the strong claim gets challenged, so you retreat to the weak one and announce that you were right all along.
