The strategy-stealing argument, in its original setting, runs as follows. Take a two-player game in which both sides have identical move sets, in which a draw is impossible, and in which holding an extra piece is never a liability. Suppose the second player had a winning strategy. The first player then opens with an arbitrary move and plays on as though they were the second player, following that strategy; whenever the strategy calls for a cell already taken by their arbitrary opening, they simply waste another arbitrary move. They are now the second player plus one extra piece on the board, which by hypothesis cannot hurt them — so they win, contradicting the supposition. The first player therefore has a winning strategy. Two things about this result matter for my purposes. First, it delivers a guarantee rather than a plan: on the standard eleven-by-eleven Hex board the conclusion has stood for three quarters of a century and nobody has ever exhibited the winning strategy, because the argument proves existence without constructing anything; only on boards small enough to search exhaustively is the winning play actually known. Second, the argument is fragile: it collapses the moment an extra move can be a burden rather than a help, as in zugzwang or misère play, or the moment the two sides no longer face the same options, or the moment the copying player cannot see what is being copied. The same shape of reasoning gets carried over into races for decisive advantage — if a leader's edge is merely that they arrive first, and the followers have access to the same methods and inputs, then whatever the leader would do with the lead a follower could do almost as well a little later, and the lead is worth nothing.

I would like a short story that dramatizes this, and specifically that dramatizes the gap between a guarantee and a plan. Please write it under the following constraints.

The story must turn on either a precondition of the argument failing in practice or the difference between knowing that a winning course of action exists and knowing what it is. Choose one of the two and commit to it; do not hedge by doing both loosely.

Set it somewhere other than a technology company, a government research program, or a tournament of any game. The reasoning should be recognizable from the structure of the situation rather than from the vocabulary, so that a reader who has never heard of Hex can still feel the trap. Invented people, institutions, and places only.

No character may explain the argument aloud, and the phrase strategy stealing may appear at most once in the whole piece — and if it appears, it must be inside a written document that a character is reading, not in speech.

Include one physical object that recurs in all five scenes and whose significance in the fifth is the reverse of its significance in the first.

The character who notices that the reasoning has failed must not be the character who relied on it: an auditor, a clerk, a translator, a junior engineer, a steward, someone with no professional stake in the conclusion and no way to act on it.

Give me concrete particulars throughout — names, sums of money, seasons, weather, the smell of the room, the specific wording of a clause — and keep the register as a slow loss of nerve rather than a twist ending. The fifth scene should not tell me who won; it should tell me what the guarantee turned out to be worth.

Format: return exactly five numbered scenes, 1 through 5, and nothing else — no title, no prefatory remark, no closing note about what the story was doing. Each scene should run roughly 350 to 500 words, should work as prose in its own right, and should leave the reader with a reason to read the next one.
