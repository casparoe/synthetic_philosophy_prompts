I am writing a term paper for a formal epistemology seminar on the accuracy-first program, and in order to check that I had genuinely understood the material rather than memorized its slogans, I built a short argument of my own out of pieces I took from the readings. It proved considerably more than I expected. I now cannot tell whether it is a valid reductio of the framework, a sloppy piece of reasoning on my part that I am too close to see, or a real difficulty that practitioners already handle in some way I have not managed to locate. My instructor is away this week, so what I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a pointer to the literature; please reason the case through rather than sending me elsewhere to read about it.

Here is the setup. There is a disputed point of local history, which I will call H. My evidence on H is entirely neutral, so my credence is 0.5, and by the framework's own lights 0.5 is exactly what my evidence supports. My uncle has firm views about H. I have kept a running score of such disputes over twenty years: he turns out to be right about 60 percent of the time, and my credence in that figure is itself well grounded. I also know something less flattering about myself: whatever he tells me, within a day I am fully convinced, credence 1 in whatever he said, and I have never once managed to hold out against it. It is not a decision I make; it is a disposition I have tried and failed to control. So I face two options, ask him or do not ask him. Measure inaccuracy by the Brier score, assume additivity, and suppose H is the only proposition in play.

(1) Epistemic rationality is a matter of accuracy: the only thing that can make one doxastic option better than another, epistemically speaking, is that it is more accurate; and when I must choose between options that will produce different credence functions in me, the correct way to compare them is by expected inaccuracy, expectation taken with respect to my current credences.
(2) My current credence in H is 0.5, so the expected Brier inaccuracy of remaining at 0.5 is 0.25.
(3) If I do not ask my uncle, I remain at 0.5.
(4) If I do ask him, I end up at credence 1 in H or credence 1 in not-H, according to what he says; he says the truth with probability 0.6; so my expected Brier inaccuracy is 0.4.
(5) Therefore asking him is expected to make me substantially less accurate, and by (1) it is epistemically irrational to ask him.
(6) But that cannot be right. The framework contains, as a theorem rather than an assumption, the result that free evidence never lowers expected accuracy for an agent scored by a proper scoring rule; recovering that result is one of the advertised selling points of the approach. And the theorem is not simply inapplicable here by some technicality I can wave away: had I responded to my uncle as I ought to, moving to 0.6 on his word, my expected inaccuracy would have been 0.24, comfortably better than 0.25. So the framework's own verdict is available for the response I should have. I do not see how that rescues my situation, since I am the agent who has to choose, and a theory that is silent about actual non-ideal agents, or that issues the wrong verdict for them, forfeits its claim to guide them.
(7) So (5) must be rejected: I should ask him, notwithstanding (4). And nothing in (4) turned on the particulars of my uncle; it turned only on my knowing in advance that I will respond badly. Generalizing: an agent who knows she will overreact to a piece of evidence should seek it out anyway, and by the same route no agent ever has an epistemic reason to decline a look, to destroy a document, or to avoid a conversation she knows will mislead her.
(8) I cannot accept (7) or its generalization, and I cannot accept them precisely because I accept (1): if accuracy really is the whole of the epistemic good, then a theory that instructs me to knowingly make myself less accurate has either refuted itself or stopped being about accuracy.

Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does. Specifically: identify the step you reject and explain what made it look innocent when I wrote it down, since I want the reason it was easy to accept and not merely the verdict; say whether the theorem invoked at (6) is available to an agent in my position at all, and state what it must assume about her for its conclusion to hold; tell me what the rejection costs, distinguishing the case in which I give up (6) and lose only a slogan from the case in which giving it up removes something the program structurally needs; and tell me whether, if I insist on keeping (6), I must instead abandon (1) or the expected-inaccuracy criterion embedded in it, and what the resulting view would be and whether it is still recognizably the accuracy-first position. Finally, say whether the case generalizes the way (7) claims, to refusing to look, to destroying evidence, and to paying to forget something one already knows, or whether my slide from my uncle to those cases is itself illicit and if so where. I am not asking for advice about my uncle. I want to know whether accuracy-first epistemology has the resources to describe my choice at all, or whether the choice falls outside its jurisdiction, and if it does, what that costs the view.
