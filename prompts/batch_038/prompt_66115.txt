I have spent the past year on a citizens' advisory committee in a mid-sized American city, and in November our council votes on whether to adopt a formal apology for something that happened about fifty-five years ago: a predominantly Black neighborhood of roughly nine hundred households was cleared under eminent domain to build a highway interchange, the residents were relocated to scattered public housing, most were paid well below what the city's own assessors had the properties at, and the minutes of the time show that two other routes were rejected on the stated ground that the land along this one was cheaper to acquire. The descendants' association wants the apology. A substantial bloc on the council does not. I run a hardware supply business and have no academic training — I've done my reading on my own, badly, mostly at night — but one thing has become clear to me from four committee meetings and a lot of public comment: nearly everyone in this fight is running the same analogy in opposite directions, and nobody has said out loud how far it actually goes.

The analogy is that an apology is what one person says to another after having hurt them — a friend who borrows your tools and wrecks them, a driver who backs into your fence. Opponents of the resolution use it this way: an apology is owed by the one who did the wrong to the one who suffered it, and since none of us did this and most of the victims are dead, the sentence "we apologize" in the draft resolution is either a lie or theatre, no different from apologizing for something your neighbor did or apologizing to someone who was never harmed. Supporters use the same picture with the arrows reversed: a city is not a list of whoever happens to live in it now, it is more like a family — you can be ashamed of your grandfather, you can inherit his house along with his debts, and the family that did the wrong is still going, with the same name on the letterhead and the same archive in the basement. A third faction, mostly younger, says both sides are missing the point, because an apology without repair is like saying you're sorry and keeping the stolen wallet. I suspect there is also a hidden disagreement underneath about what an apology even is — whether it is the outward sign of an inward feeling of regret, in which case it has to be felt by the person saying it, or whether it is something more like a deed that changes what the two parties now owe each other, in which case who feels what may be beside the point — but I cannot tell whether that intuition is worth anything.

So I would like an audit. Take the two analogies separately — the interpersonal one and the family one — and give me the correspondence table for each: what is playing the role of the wrongdoer, of the victim, of the injury, of the acknowledgment, of the forgiveness that may or may not follow, and of the repair. Then tell me where each mapping breaks, and, importantly, separate the breaks that actually matter for what our council is being asked to vote on from the ones that are merely interesting to think about; I can imagine quite a few of the latter and I don't want the meeting derailed by them. I want to know specifically what each analogy smuggles in that the real case lacks — my guess is something about whether guilt travels with the act or whether only obligations and benefits do, and something about whether forgiveness is a thing the apologizer can request or must wait to be given — and whether the opponents' conclusion, that the resolution asserts a falsehood, survives once those imports are on the table. If neither analogy can carry the weight, tell me what a better one would be; I have been turning over the image of an executor winding up an estate that includes a disputed claim, and of a firm that has bought the assets of a company that no longer legally exists, but I have no idea whether those help or simply relocate the difficulty. And if you can, close with something I could actually say at the meeting in answer to the question I keep getting, which is why an apology offered by people who did nothing is not just a statement that happens to be untrue.
