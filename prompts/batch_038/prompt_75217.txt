I'm a physicist, not a philosopher, but I convene a small interdisciplinary reading group, and next month we are taking up the epistemology of mathematics. To prepare, I read an essay in an online philosophy magazine that offered the following gloss on Benacerraf's dilemma:

"Benacerraf's dilemma is the most serious problem facing anyone who thinks numbers are real objects. It runs as follows. The platonist says that numbers exist but are abstract: outside space and time, and therefore unable to act on anything. Yet we take ourselves to know a great many truths about them. Benacerraf's insight was to notice that our best account of knowledge is a causal one — to know about something is, roughly, to stand in the right sort of causal relation to it, which is also what it takes for a term to refer to it. Objects with no causal powers can figure in no such relation, so the platonist is committed to saying that we know nothing about numbers. Since that is plainly absurd, platonism must be false. Benacerraf's own way out was to treat mathematics as a useful story: its sentences are not literally true of any objects, but the story is indispensable to science. The moral is broader than mathematics. The same argument would seem to reach any domain of causally inert facts we claim to know about — moral facts, facts about what might have been — which is why the dilemma is often called the central argument of the subject, and why almost no one writing about it today thinks it can be answered."

The essay went on to other things, so I described the argument afterwards to a colleague who works on the philosophy of logic, and she told me I had it backwards. On her account Benacerraf never argued that platonism is false, and was not in the business of drawing conclusions about what mathematical objects are at all. What he did, she said, was show that a particular pairing of a semantics with an epistemology is unstable: if you insist that arithmetical statements be read the same way as statements about electrons, and you insist on a causal account of how such statements hook onto the world, the two cannot both be kept, and you must say which goes. My instinct is that her version is the fairer one, since it makes sense of why anyone would bother to present the thing as a dilemma rather than a proof, but I have no way of telling whether the magazine's version is actually wrong or merely compressed.

Could you work through the magazine's summary sentence by sentence and tell me what stands, what is a defensible simplification of a subtler point, and what a philosopher of mathematics would regard as simply mistaken? I am especially interested in three places. First, whether the causal theory of knowledge really does all the work the summary assigns to it, and what is left of the challenge for a platonist who rejects a causal account of knowledge. Second, whether the summary's claim about Benacerraf's own preferred escape is accurate, and if not, whose view it is in fact reporting and how that view differs from the conclusion of the argument. Third, whether the reference in passing to an earlier result — that there is no fact of the matter which objects the numbers are — belongs in this argument at all, or whether the summary is welding together two problems that are better kept apart. Then tell me whether the final claim survives the corrections: is it true that the dilemma generalises straightforwardly to other domains of causally inert fact, and is it true that the debate is now effectively settled against the platonist?
