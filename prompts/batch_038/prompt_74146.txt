Hey — I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm working on a feature about the moral and religious half of Darwin's argument, specifically the parts that other people finished after he died. I need you to do the speculative bit properly rather than the lazy version.

So: you're a philosopher and historian of science who has actually worked through Darwin's moral writings — Descent of Man, the book on expression of the emotions, the worm book, the letters, the Autobiography — and you're also deeply allergic to anachronism. Please think carefully and don't rush to the answer. Start with a brainstorming pass, out loud, of his load-bearing commitments about morality and about religion: what he actually argued, which mechanism he thought was doing the work, and how his view moved between the 1830s and the 1880s. Mark which of these you're confident about and which you're half-remembering.

Then predict his verdict on three things that only came up after 1882.

One: the debunking argument that's now standard in moral epistemology — roughly, that if our moral convictions were shaped by whatever helped our ancestors get along and out-reproduce their neighbours rather than by their truth, we have no good reason to trust them. Darwin raised a cousin of this worry himself, about a mind developed out of the minds of lower animals and whether anyone should trust the convictions of such a mind. Would he accept the sceptical conclusion, or does something inside his own account of sympathy, habit and the social instincts give him a way out? Where exactly would he dig in?

Two: the contemporary science of religion. There are the byproduct accounts — over-eager agency detection, our habit of inferring spirits from dreams and death — and the group-level adaptation accounts, including the one where morally concerned, punishing high gods let strangers cooperate in large anonymous societies. Darwin sketched both moves: a fairly byproduct-flavoured origin for belief in spiritual agencies, and a between-tribe selection story for tribal virtue. Given the last twenty-odd years — the lab studies that seemed to show reminders of God make people more generous and the later replication attempts that found little, plus the big comparative-history work suggesting those gods show up in the record after societies get large rather than before — which side does he land on, and would the news please or annoy him?

Three: the fate of his own mechanism. Plenty of his successors insist morality can be assembled from individual-level selection, kinship and recipricity with no group component at all; others have rehabilitated the between-group idea in a cultural form. Would the 1871 Darwin concede, dig in, or say the whole fight is about something other than what he meant?

For each one, give me a committed verdict — no hedging of the he-would-be-amazed-by-all-this sort — then a paragraph arguing it the way he'd argue it, then the strongest case for a different verdict, then a rough confidence out of ten and what would move you.

Then a section I really need: which parts of this are genuine extrapolation and which are ventriloquism. Say where his view isn't one view because he changed his mind, and which Darwin — the notebook one, the Origin one, the Descent one, the sick old agnostic at Down — answers each question differently. Worth noting for context that the deathbed-recantation story circulated years later and was publicly disputed by his family, so the martyr-of-unbelief image was contested from the start.

Ground rules: no invented quotations, ever. Paraphrase and tell me how sure you are about the paraphrase. No reading lists, no bibliography, no guessing at citations — I'll chase sources myself. And finish with one prediction that cuts against both stock images of him, the proto-atheist mascot and the gentle undecided grandee.
