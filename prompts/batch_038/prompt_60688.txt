Epistemology seminar was Gettier cases this week and now I'm trying to work out whether my own intuitions add up to anything at all. Here's what I actually say:

- Smith, the jobs-and-coins-in-pocket one: not knowledge. No struggle there.
- Henry driving through a county that's mostly barn façades, and he's looking at the one real barn: I say he **does** know it's a barn. Half my section says he doesn't and I genuinely can't feel the pull of that.
- Stopped clock: you glance at a clock that stopped exactly 24 hours ago and it reads the right time. Not knowledge.
- One I made up: a warehouse guard watches a bank of monitors, and half the cameras in the building are decoys that just loop old footage. His screen is showing a live feed of a real theft. I say he doesn't know there's a theft happening. Which I realize is basically fake barns with a screen in the middle, and that's the pair I can't reconcile.
- My car: I parked it out front, and a friend I trust tells me it got towed. She's messing with me, it's still there. Before she said anything I'd have said I knew where it was; after, I say I don't — even though nothing about the car changed and her claim was false.
- Office gossip: everyone says the new hire is embezzling, a story started by somebody who hates him, but he actually is. I believe it on the gossip. Not knowledge, and it feels worse than the clock case, though both are true beliefs coming out of a bad source.

My tentative principle is roughly: you know if the truth of the belief isn't a coincidence, given the way you formed it. Is that just safety or reliability wearing a different hat, or does it do independent work? Tell me which of my verdicts it delivers, which it breaks, and whether any single principle saves all of them — and if the only one that does is baroque, tell me which verdict I should drop instead.
