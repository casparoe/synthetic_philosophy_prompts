So this started as a favor and turned into my whole spring. My sister got denied an apartment in March — the management company runs every applicant through a tenant screening score, and what came back flagged her for an eviction filing from three years ago that was actually thrown out of court. I've been going to a tenant union here that's trying to get the city to regulate these things, and about six weeks in I hit a wall: our own group can't agree on what we're even asking for. Some people want the scores banned outright. Some want them "made accurate for everyone." One guy is completely certain that if a score is right on average within each racial group there is literally nothing left to complain about. I've stopped being able to tell which of these are compatible with each other and which are just moods.

Can you build me a sorting instrument I can print and hand out at the next meeting — five questions that place a person in the room into a named position on what fairness could require of a screening score, answerable by someone who has never heard the jargon?

What I need:

- exactly five numbered items, nothing before the first and nothing after the last, and keep each one tight: the fork, the options, where each option leads. no throat clearing.
- each item is a single fork: a concrete situation — a cutoff, a gap between groups' base rates, a label that turned out to be wrong, whatever — with two or three answers phrased the way a tenant would phrase them, and after each answer either the number of the next fork or the name of the position that person has just landed in. the fifth fork's answers should all be terminal.
- work from the logical space before you work from the camps. I want the tree to track which combinations of answers actually hold together given that the groups being scored don't have the same base rates, and if some combination has no defenders, say so in a clause and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's ugly to hold, or just because nobody's gotten there yet.
- nothing answerable by repeating a slogan. no "do you think the algorithm is biased?" or "do you care about fairness?"
- these have to show up as endpoints somewhere: the person for whom equal approval rates across groups is the entire test; the person for whom being right on average within each group settles it and no threshold adjustement is owed; the person who wants the identical cutoff applied to everyone and accepts the unequal hit rates that follow; the person who wants different cutoffs per group to equalize something; the person who thinks the ground truth the score was trained on is itself contaminated so no statistical repair reaches the problem; and the person who thinks the only clean move is to take the call away from the score entirely.
- if two of those can't be seperated by any question you could realistically ask in five, name the pair and say why, right in the item where it would have come up.

No bibliography, no list of papers, no citations — I want something I can hand to a room full of people on folding chairs.
