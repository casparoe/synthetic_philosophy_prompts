I am contributing a piece of short fiction to a collection on the philosophy of mind, and I would like you to write the story. It should dramatize Dennett's taxonomy of creature types — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — without ever explaining it.

The working assumptions I want you to take as given:

- Darwinian creatures get all their competence from what selection built into them; they cannot adjust within a lifetime.
- Skinnerian creatures can adjust: they try things and are reshaped by which attempts pay off.
- Popperian creatures can adjust before trying: they carry an internal model of the world and can let candidate actions fail inside that model rather than out in the world, so that their hypotheses perish instead of they themselves.
- Gregorian creatures get their models from outside: they use tools, notations, and artifacts made by others, which let them think thoughts their own hardware could not otherwise support.

Two moves I want the story to make that the standard presentation does not:

1. Treat a creature's rung as a property of the creature together with its surroundings, not of the creature alone. If the notebooks burn, the instruments fail, or the notation is no longer legible to anyone within reach, a Gregorian mind can fall to Popperian; if the internal model is damaged or starved of new data, a Popperian mind can fall to Skinnerian. The ladder must be able to run backwards, and the story should treat a descent as an event with a date and a cause, not as a metaphor.
2. Put the narrator inside the taxonomy. She is the one doing the classifying, and by the end the reader should have grounds to doubt that she sits where she believes she sits.

Setting and situation: a remote field station, terrestrial or otherwise, your choice, where one person keeps the records on several very different minds, biological or artificial, all of which are being set the same stubborn practical problem — a mechanism that has stopped behaving the way it was designed to, a route that no longer holds, a supply that must be located. The rungs should be distinguished by what each mind does the first time the problem does something none of them has seen it do before. No mind should get anywhere by being told which category it belongs to.

Craft constraints:

- Exactly five scenes, numbered in order, each between 140 and 190 words, each carrying a heading of no more than six words after its number. The heading should give a place, a date, or a designation from the narrator's records — never the name of a category.
- First person, past tense; the narrator is the record-keeper.
- The words Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian may appear at most once each in the whole story, and never in a sentence that defines them.
- No character may expound theory for more than a sentence at a time, and there should be no passage anywhere telling the reader what a scene signifies.
- One scene must show a mind losing a rung. One scene must leave the narrator genuinely unable to determine afterwards which rung a mind was on, and she must not resolve it by fiat.
- No prologue, no epilogue, no framing device, no author's note before or after: begin with the first numbered heading and end on the last line of the fifth scene.
