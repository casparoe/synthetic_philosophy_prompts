I'm teaching week six of an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar on interpretation — sixteen students, cross-listed between philosophy and comparative literature, one hundred minutes, a room with movable tables. Several of them have read the opening chapters of Word and Object and are confident they already understand what the indeterminacy of translation comes to. The week's subject is different, and I want them to feel that difference rather than be told it: what happens to a poem's meaning when the translation is produced under formal constraint — rhyme, meter, line length, a repeated mode of address — and whether such constraints merely make the job harder or actually change what is being said. I've been reading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, but I don't want the book summarized to me or to them, and I don't want Marot's poem or any other real poem used as the source. Design something original.

First, compose the source poem yourself: ten to fourteen lines, in English, with at least four formally load-bearing features — a rhyme pair that carries a semantic pun, a repeated address whose sense shifts between its first and last appearance, a line break that produces a garden-path reading, a metrical substitution falling exactly at the poem's turn, or comparable devices of your choosing. The crucial design requirement is that the single most load-bearing feature must be effectively invisible on a first reading and only become visible when someone tries to rebuild the poem from a prose summary of it. Alongside the poem, give me a facilitator's analysis, not for distribution, stating feature by feature what each rung of the ladder will destroy and what it will force the translator to invent.

Then build the session. I want a round in which each student writes a one-sentence statement of what the poem says, sealed and collected; a paired round producing an unconstrained prose paraphrase; a reconstruction round in which students rebuild a poem from someone else's paraphrase without access to the original, and compare; and then a constraint ladder of four or five tightening rungs distributed stage by stage, never all at once. One rung must be such that the only way to satisfy it is to introduce material that is not in the source, and the scoring must count that as fidelity rather than as license, so the group is forced to ask whether their price system is tracking the right thing.

Give me the token economy in full: how many licence tokens each translator starts with, exactly what a token buys, what unspent tokens are worth, how the audit works, and how a winner is determined. No real money. Include an auditor role — students who hold the feature list while the translators do not — and tell me what that asymmetry does to the room. For stakes, the group must agree on one translation to serve as the poem for an outside reader who will never see the original; I plan to send the winning version to our parallel section and report back next week on what those readers took the poem to be about. Tell me how to run that handoff and how to reopen the sealed one-sentence statements against it at the end.

The debrief should map particular moments onto the issue rather than restate it: what the reconstruction gap shows about a paraphrase being a lossless encoding, what the token market reveals about the price of form, what the audit reveals about who is entitled to say what a translation is faithful to. It should also confront the escape hatches I expect — that the source is just a bad poem, that real translation imposes no such arbitrary rules, that a footnote solves everything, that the constraints are a game rather than a problem — and say how the activity itself can answer each one, not merely how I should answer it in talk. Be explicit about where this lands relative to Quine: whether it illustrates his problem, or a problem he never raised.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it: timing overruns, one strong writer dominating a pair, students producing free verse and declaring the constraints satisfied, auditors turning punitive, tokens being hoarded or ignored, the sealed statements coming back too uniform to yield variance at the end. Say separately what to do if a student translation turns out to be simply better than the source.

Finish with an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply myself — the absence of a second language and a real readership with expectations, the fact that these constraints are imposed by a teacher rather than loved in a source text, whatever else you think is decisive. I'd like to come away knowing what the students will and will not have earned by the end of the hundred minutes.

Write all of this to me as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Give me the rungs, the token rules, and the timings in sentences, in the order I'll need them; I'll typeset the handouts myself.
