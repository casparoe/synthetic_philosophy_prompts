I teach a twelve-person graduate pragmatics seminar; we have one 100-minute slot, and everyone has nominally read Grice's "Logic and Conversation," though I suspect most can recite the maxims without ever having felt the machinery work. Design a single in-class game that gets them to run into implicature from the hearer's end — the gap between what a speaker asserted and what the speaker thereby conveyed, and the possibility of conveying something false while asserting only truths — without my naming it until the end. No lecture beforehand, and the words implicature, maxim, flouting, and cancellability stay off the board until the debrief.

The backbone I have in mind is a repeated transmission game: speakers hold information that hearers must act on for points, under a payoff scheme that makes bare truth cheap and full disclosure costly in some rounds but not others, so that the pressure to convey-without-asserting varies by round rather than by instruction. No real money, ordinary classroom materials only. Give me the complete rules, a round-by-round timeline in minutes, the scoring, and what each round is designed to produce.

Two things I want built into the play rather than explained. First, at least one round in which a speaker may attach an explicit cancelation ("some — and possibly all of them") and the hearers still act on the uncanceled inference; I want them to feel how stubborn these inferences are, and to leave with a live dispute about whether that stubbornness favors a default-based account of scalar inference (Levinson) or merely shows how fast a relevance-driven inference fires (Sperber and Wilson). Tell me whether the data this game generates could discriminate the two, and if so what additional condition I would need; if it cannot, say so plainly. Second, one round in which students feel the force of Kent Bach's objection — that "some but not all" is not implicated at all, but part of what is said once implicit content is included — or an honest note that no activity can stage that objection.

Then the debrief: how to map particular moments onto the said/implicated distinction, onto nondetachability (including an attempt to kill the inference by paraphrase, which should fail), and onto the question of whether implicature could ever be captured by a determinate procedure, given Grice's own hesitations. Tell me what predictably breaks the first time this runs — I expect students to smell the pedagogy and start performing rather than reporting — and how to handle it. Finally, name the one feature of Grice's account the activity cannot reproduce, which I will have to supply by argument.
