Last month I sat in on an open session at a philosophy department, and two of the participants — I will call them Vera and Idris — got into a sustained argument about the doomsday argument that I could not resolve, either at the time or since. Neither was grandstanding, and both seemed to me to land real hits. I am reporting what they said as neutrally as I can; I have no view of my own about who came out ahead, and I would rather not be told which of the standard positions in the literature each of them sounds like. What I want is a careful verdict on the moves they actually made.

The setup was a variant that Vera invented for the occasion. Call it the Fertility Fund case. A government proposes a large, permanent child-rearing subsidy, funded for five centuries, to be decided by referendum next year. They stipulated the following: if the Fund passes, the total number of humans who will ever have been born ends up around 10^15; if it fails and the population declines within a few centuries, the total ends up around 10^12; roughly 10^11 humans have been born so far, so your own birth rank is fixed at about 10^11 whichever way the vote goes. They further stipulated that, apart from your birth rank, you have no special information bearing on the outcome. Here is the exchange as I noted it down.

1. **Vera:** The doomsday argument is usually treated as a piece of cosmic pessimism, but the Fund case shows it is practical. Grant the self-sampling assumption — reason as though you were a random member of your reference class. The hypothesis that the Fund passes makes it about a thousand times less likely that you would find yourself at rank 10^11 than the hypothesis that it fails does. So the fact of your birth rank, which is evidence you already possess, is a factor of 10^3 against the Fund passing. A rational voter should accordingly lower her confidence that it will pass, and should regard the expansionist program it embodies as probably doomed.

2. **Idris:** The arithmetic is right and the conclusion still does not follow, because the reasoning is undone the moment you ask why you exist at all. Under the self-indication assumption, the chance that there is an observer with your properties is proportional to how many observers there are, so a world with 10^15 humans is a thousand times more likely to contain you than a world with 10^12. That factor cancels the birth-rank factor exactly. Vera's inference is available only to someone who has already rejected self-indication, and rejecting it means denying that a world containing more people is more likely to be a world you are in — which is one of the harder bullets this whole subject has to offer.

3. **Vera:** I will bite it, and the Fund case is precisely where self-indication breaks. Its cancellation works by trading off two facts about the same world: more observers on one side, a smaller chance of being any particular one of them on the other. But in the Fund case your existence is not in question. You are already here, and whether you exist was settled long before the referendum was scheduled. The Fund can change how many people come after you; it cannot change the probability that you came. So there is nothing for the birth-rank factor to cancel against, and self-indication gets no purchase here. Anyone who wants it owes an account of why a subsidy program should count as evidence about your own existence.

4. **Idris:** It does not need to count as evidence about your existence; it only has to be evidence you have already spent. The requirement is that you conditionalize on your total evidence, and do so once. Your birth rank has been part of your evidence since you first learned any demography, and whatever it implies about the possible size of the human race was absorbed then. The referendum is new information, and when new information arrives you update with it — you do not get to re-run an old likelihood against a hypothesis you had never previously entertained. Vera is counting the same factor of 10^3 twice: once when she settled her expectations about how large the total could be, and again when the Fund appeared on the ballot.

5. **Vera:** Evidence can bear on propositions you had not previously considered. Before the Fund was proposed, my rank licensed a general expectation that the total would not much exceed 10^11. The Fund names a specific mechanism by which it would reach 10^15. Applying an expectation I already had to a mechanism now on the table is not double-counting — it is what having expectations about the total consists in. If Idris is right, then my rank is evidentially inert against every concrete large-total hypothesis anyone ever formulates, and what is inert against all of them is inert in general. That is a far larger concession than he is admitting.

6. **Idris:** Then let us test whether it really is inert. My cousin finished a specialist online course and is the 800th person ever to do so; the provider has just announced a marketing push intended to take the number of finishers to 800,000, and if the push fails the total will stay around 1,600. Vera's recipe yields a factor of roughly 10^3 against the push succeeding. But how many people finish that course is a function of ad spend and pricing, and none of that has anything to do with where my cousin sits in the sequence. Either she says the reasoning transfers — in which case she is committed to predicting the failure of every expansion program she happens to be early in, which is a reductio — or she says it does not, in which case she owes a principled difference between the course and the species, and "one is bigger than the other" is not one.

7. **Vera:** There is a principled difference. In the course case the selection process is open to investigation: my cousin knows how he came to be the 800th finisher, because there is a registrar, a launch date, a price list, and marketing decisions he could examine. His rank is not a random draw from a pool whose size is opaque to him; it is the output of a process he can study, and once he studies it, the self-sampling assumption is superseded. No comparable investigation is available for the total number of humans who will ever live.

The session ended there. Please give me the following.

(a) A verdict on each of the seven moves, rated as clearly right, largely right but incomplete, a wash, or mistaken, with a brief justification. For any move you rate as mistaken, identify what specifically goes wrong rather than merely noting that the other side answered it.

(b) An answer to the central question: does the Fertility Fund case give a doomsday-argument-based reason to lower one's confidence that the Fund will pass, or not? If the answer depends on premises the two of them did not argue about, say which premises and why they are decisive.

(c) An assessment of Vera's final move — the appeal to the investigability of the selection process. Is it a genuine principled line or an ad hoc rescue? If it can be made to work, sketch what the working version would have to say, and test it against at least one intermediate case of your own choosing that is neither the course nor the species.

(d) Anything substantive both of them missed: an objection genuinely available to either side that neither raised, or a third position that dissolves the dispute rather than taking a side.

Work with the case as they framed it and with the numbers they stipulated; check their arithmetic where it matters, since two of the moves turn on whether factors of 10^3 really do arise and really do cancel. I am not looking for a survey of the debate in general, and please do not assemble references — I want to know whether these particular arguments hold up.
