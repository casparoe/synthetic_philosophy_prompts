Our interpretability group has an unwritten house rule that I'm about to have to put into a methods section, and I cant actually defend it.

When someone claims a model "represents X" — the true board state, whether the user is being sarcastic, whether the door is locked — the accepted evidence is a probe: train a linear readout on the residual stream, report held-out accuracy. Good probe, representation exists. But if you want to go further and say the representation is *used*, you have to intervene: patch or flip the feature and show the outputs move. So we run a two-tier system where decodability buys you "represents" and causal leverage buys you "represents and uses it."

Nobody I've asked can tell me what theory of content makes tier one mean anything. We'd never accept "you can read X off the activations" as showing that a thermostat represents room temperature or that a rock represents where the sun is. And meanwhile the convention leaks all over the place. The Othello work — model trained only to predict legal moves, probing recovers board state out of its internals, messing with that state changes the moves it predicts — that one gets described as showing the thing had a world model, fine. But a probe that's near-perfect with no detectable causal effect anywhere also gets reported as a finding about what the model knows, and nobody blinks.

So what does this practice presuppose about what it takes for a physical system to have a representation at all? I want a reconstruction, not a literature tour, and no reading list or citations — I couldn't chase them down this week even if you gave me one.
