I read a long magazine essay about why we seek out sad plays and films, and I have copied below the passage where it sets up the problem. I do not want you to comment on the essay as writing; I want to know how much of it to trust. My own inclination is that the emotions we have in the theatre are entirely real, but the essay tells me that this is now the minority position, and I cannot tell whether that is true or whether the author has the debate backwards.

The passage runs: "The paradox of tragedy is simply this — nobody wants to grieve, and yet we buy tickets in order to grieve, and afterwards we go back for more. There are only two ways out of it. Either the grief is not real, which is roughly where the argument has landed, since you cannot genuinely mourn someone you know to be imaginary; what we feel is a rehearsal of mourning, and rehearsing a distressing feeling is pleasant in just the way that being frightened on a rollercoaster is pleasant. Or the grief is real, in which case the pleasure must come from somewhere other than the emotion itself: from the beauty of the dialogue, from our admiration for the shape of the play, from what we learn about human life. Aristotle's notion of catharsis is only a dignified version of the second option — the emotions are discharged, and discharging them feels good. On either route the emotion is never the thing being enjoyed, which is why people who say they loved a devastating film are, strictly speaking, mistaken about what they felt."

Please go through this claim by claim and separate what a philosopher working on this problem would accept as stated, what is a fair simplification, and what is wrong. Specifically: is the claim about where the debate has landed accurate, and is the rollercoaster comparison doing the work the author thinks it is doing? Is the gloss on catharsis fair to the original idea, and does it belong in the second option at all? Which families of response to the paradox does the "only two ways out" framing exclude, and does excluding them matter? Finally, does the closing claim — that someone who says they loved a devastating film is confused about what they felt — follow from anything the author gets right?
