I have read the rationalist and effective-altruism blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, which means I know the unilateralist's curse by heart: if a dangerous capability or a dangerous piece of knowledge reaches *n* people, each of whom decides independently whether to act on it, and only some fraction would act responsibly, then the probability that someone acts irresponsibly climbs toward one with *n* — so any remedy worth having has to reduce effective *n* rather than improve individual judgment, and the canonical remedy is a norm of deferring to the consensus of the informed. I also know by heart every objection that gets raised to this framework inside that community, and I do not need help with those. What I need help with is an audience that has never encountered the phrase.

Next month I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on research governance hosted by a public policy school. The room is: four research-integrity and biosafety officers, three from universities and one from a national laboratory, who spend their working lives on compliance structures and on the gap between written policy and what actually happens in a lab; two historians and sociologists of science who study how risk categories come to be treated as real, and who are alert to professional boundary-work — the possibility that claims about dangerous knowledge are scientists securing jurisdiction and budget for themselves; a lawyer who writes and litigates around prior restraint and the limits of what can be enjoined ahead of publication; an economist of innovation who studies the costs that review and delay impose on research output and who will want a number; a former member of a national biosecurity advisory board who has personally been through a public fight about whether a paper should appear in full; and some graduate students. Nobody in the room is a philosopher or a decision theorist. Only the economist will have seen an expression like 1 − (1 − *p*)^*n* before. All of them have heard of dual-use research of concern, and none of them, I expect, has heard of the unilateralist's curse, which I plan to define in ninety seconds before turning to my own claim.

My claim is not the standard one. Here is the argument in brief, so you know what the objections will be aimed at:

1. The curse is normally stated over knowers. I argue the operative variable is not the number who know but the number who can act — who have the skill, the materials, the access, and the absence of oversight. Widespread knowledge is harmless where capability is concentrated; concentrated capability is catastrophic even where the knowledge is trivial. This relocates the target of intervention.
2. Deference to the informed consensus reduces effective *n* only where a consensus exists and only among agents already disposed to defer, which means it works in mature, well-institutionalized fields with standing advisory structures. Those are precisely the fields where the curse bites least. In the cases the framework was built for — a genuinely novel capability, dispersed actors, no settled professional body — there is no consensus to defer to and the norm does no work. The remedy is calibrated to the wrong regime.
3. Therefore commitment must be made ex ante, before the agent holds anything sensitive, and by a party whose incentives are not yet contaminated by possessing the capability: the institution, not the individual.
4. The mechanism I propose is a sealed commitment registry. Institutions, and where relevant individuals, file a standing, timestamped commitment: if you acquire a capability matching a description you wrote down in advance, review triggers automatically, and the reviewing panel is drawn at random from a pre-registered standing pool. The randomness is load-bearing — a panel you cannot predict cannot be cultivated, lobbied, or stocked with friends.
5. Revocation is the alarm. Filing is cheap and quiet; amending or withdrawing a filed commitment is a dated, publishable event routed to the same standing pool and to institutional leadership. Defection is converted from invisible to visible, which is all the enforcement the scheme claims to need: it never asks that anyone be prevented from acting, only that they act in a way that leaves a trace.
6. Burden is deliberately low in the common case, since almost every filed description is never triggered; the registry exists to make the rare triggered case non-discretionary.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me exactly seven numbered items, with no preamble and no closing remarks. Items one through six are the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal, and no item longer than five sentences: one or two sentences putting the objection in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it — say which of the six types above it comes from, and use their vocabulary rather than mine, with no talk of information hazards, expected value, or existential risk; two sentences of reply I could deliver standing up in under a minute; and one sentence saying where that reply gives way. Attach to each a clause telling me whether to concede it in my opening five minutes or hold it for the floor. Item seven is the question I am hoping nobody asks, phrased as that person would phrase it, together with one sentence on why I do not have a good answer to it.
