I'm not a social choice person — my corner of the department is early modern, mostly Spinoza — but I got roped into writing a 1,500-word headnote for an undergrad reader on well-being and public policy, and the editor wants me to engage with the "technical turn" in welfare economics rather than just gesture at it. So I've been reading around over the break, and the cleanest looking summary of Harsanyi's aggregration theorem I've found is in a popular book by an economist-journalist type that one of my seminar students swore by. Here's the passage:

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is the closest thing social philosophy has to a proof. Suppose each of us has preferences that obey the standard axioms of rational choice under uncertainty, so that each of us can be described by an expected-utility function, and suppose society's preferences over the very same options are rational in the same way. Add one minimal democratic condition: if everyone is indifferent between two options, society is indifferent too. Then society's utility function has to be a weighted average of ours — fixed weights, adding up to one, the same for every decision society faces. Those weights are just how much each person counts, and since nothing in the setup distinguishes you from me, they come out equal. The theorem is therefore a proof of classical utilitarianism. It also disposes of the oldest objection to utilitarianism, since it assumes utilities are cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable. And it shows that any view that cares about the distribution of utility over and above the total — Rawls's, say — has to give up either rationality or Pareto."

Two things are making me uneasy. First, I'm inclined to think the theorem is weaker than this makes it sound, and I have a hazy memory of someone arguing it's actually anti-utilitarian in its import, but I can't tell whether that's a real line of attack or just a philosopher's reflex about economists, and I don't want to build a headnote on a hunch. Second, whatever the verdict turns out to be, I need to know which bits I can lift with a word changed and which ones I have to cut out entirely, because the book is well reviewed and I'd rather not look like I'm nitpicking a competent writer.

So: give me an audit, hard cap of 500 words. Open with a one-sentence verdict — fair, mostly fair with fixable damage, or actively misleading — then work through the passage claim by claim and mark each one (right / right but loose / wrong), saying briefly what the theorem actually assumes and what it actually delivers, so I can see where the slide happens. End with a couple of sentences on whether the last claim, the one about Rawls, still stands once the earlier stuff is corrected. No bibliography and no reading list, please — I'm not chasing anything down, I just want to know what to trust.
