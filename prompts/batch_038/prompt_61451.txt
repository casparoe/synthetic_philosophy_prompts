you're going to have to hold two jobs at once here: a professional philosopher who works on personal identity and end-of-life decision-making, and a magazine satirist. the philosophy part is load-bearing — the jokes only land if the conceptual machinery is actually correct, so please think this through properly before you start being funny rather than freewheeling.

the assignment: an advice column, about 800 words, for the back page of a regional palliative-care network's glossy member newsletter. columnist is an invented woman with a PhD in the metaphysics of personal identity, zero clinical experience, and total confidence. give the column some anodyne name in the register of "Dear Whole Person" or "Ask About Living" — your call.

what I want satirized is a discourse, not a person: the breezy assumption that a document your earlier self signed settles the question forever, and the specifically philosophical habit of treating a living, contented, badly-demented patient as a metaphysical leftover whose current experience doesn't count. invent everyone, don't name any real philosopher, don't quote anybody, no citations or reading list anywhere in it.

before you draft, give me about six lines of planning: list the real pressure points in this debate you intend to exploit and which letter each one goes in. I want to check you've got the right ones before I read the column.

her view, which she holds throughout and never notices is horrifying:

- capacity is the switch. whoever was last competent gets the final word, permanently.
- therefore a patient who is no longer competent cannot revise, revoke, or override anything, no matter how happy, insistent or articulate they seem right now. their present wishes are data about a body, not instructions from a person.
- interests in how your life goes as a whole — its shape, its ending, what it means — outrank interests in how it feels from the inside at a given moment, whenever the two conflict.
- running gag: she revises her own directive constantly and is on version 7.3, which she mentions warmly. this has to be *consistent* with the above, not a slip — she's competent now, so obviously she can. the authority expires exactly when the person stops being able to object, and she finds this perfectly reasonable.

three letters, each with a short sign-off pseudonym and a town, and roughly 200 words of reply:

1. a daughter whose father's directive bars artificial nutrition once he has "no meaningful cognitive life." he doesn't know her name anymore, but he asks for ice cream by name, lights up at music, and eats the ice cream with obvious pleasure. she wants to know whether honoring the document means stopping the ice cream.
2. a man drafting his own directive who wants to include a clause saying it can never be revoked or amended by his future self, *even if that future self appears competent*, plus a second clause specifying which directive wins if he accidentally writes two. he asks whether that's enforceable and what he's actually doing.
3. a woman who was resuscitated against her directive because a paramedic couldn't find it, survived, and is now delighted to be alive. her family is being praised as heroes. she wants to know whether she owes anyone an apology, and whether a document written by someone who turned out to be wrong about what she'd want still binds her.

two craft requirements. first, at least once she has to deploy a distinction that is genuinely correct and would make an expert nod rather than wince — letter 1 should turn partly on scope, i.e. what the document is even *about*, not on overriding it. second, at least once she has to concede something real that her own view cannot fix, e.g. that nobody can specify in advance what a trigger phrase like "no meaningful cognitive life" means, so the interpretive question gets handed to strangers in a hallway.

keep it deadpan and warm, in the voice of someone answering a question about a difficult mother-in-law. no winking, no asides to the reader, never break character to say what a sensible person would really do. the reader should do the recoiling on their own.

end with a footer called THE SMALL PRINT saying the column is not medical or legal advice, but is metaphysical advice, and is binding.

fairness check before you send it: a clinical ethicist reading this should think "that's a real position, exaggerated maybe fifteen percent," not "that's a strawman."
