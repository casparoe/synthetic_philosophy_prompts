I am second-marking an essay for a first-year political philosophy course and want an independent assessment before the marks are reconciled. The question and the student's response are reproduced below.

**Question (30 marks; 10 for each part).** A legislature proposes a statute making it an offence to distribute printed material advocating that members of any identifiable group be stripped of their citizenship and deported. The statute is content-neutral on its face: it applies whatever group is named and whatever the distributor's motive. In the case before the court, the material named one religious minority, contained no call to violence and no instruction to commit any offence, and was distributed in two ways — as a paid advertisement in a national newspaper, and as leaflets handed out outside a community centre in a district where that minority is concentrated. Members of the minority report fear, and polling in the leafleting districts shows a modest rise in support for deportation. The legislature's stated justification is the fear itself; it predicts no violence.

Drawing on Mill's harm principle and his argument for liberty of thought and discussion:

(a) Is distributing the material harm to others in Mill's sense? Address whether the fear and the diminished standing reported by the minority could qualify.
(b) Does Mill's treatment of the corn-dealer case support the statute? In your answer, explain the significance of his remark that "even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act."
(c) Suppose the legislature is confident that the material's claims are false and that deportation would be unjust. Does that confidence change the Millian analysis?

**Student's answer.**

(a) Mill holds that power may rightfully be exercised over a member of a civilised community against their will only to prevent harm to others, and that a person's own good, physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant. He grounds this not in natural rights but in utility taken in the largest sense, on the long-run interests of human beings as developing creatures. Dislike of an opinion is therefore not harm. But harm is not confined to bodily injury: the material causes members of the minority genuine fear and lowers their standing in the community, and these are injuries to them rather than mere offence. Distribution is thus other-regarding and within the reach of the state. The proviso limiting the principle to civilised communities does not assist the legislature, since it concerns age and stage of social development rather than the content of opinions.

(b) The corn-dealer case shows that an opinion loses its immunity where the circumstances of its expression make it an instigation to harm rather than a contribution to discussion. The distinction Mill draws is not between media but between speech that invites consideration and speech that works on people already gathered to act. The leaflets fall on the second side: they were handed to the minority community itself, in the districts where polling shows support for deportation rising, which is evidence that the expression is producing an effect rather than merely proposing one. The newspaper advertisement is harder to place, but since it reaches the same readers and reports the same effect, Mill's test gives the legislature no reason to treat it differently. On this reading the statute is one Mill could accept.

(c) The argument of Chapter II rests on the fallibility of those who would censor: because the legislature cannot be certain it is right, it may not suppress. That argument is unavailable here, where we are told the legislature's confidence is well founded — the claims are false and deportation would be unjust. Mill's remaining arguments are weaker: they concern the danger that unchallenged belief becomes dead dogma, which is a reason to let true opinions be contested, not a reason to protect statements known to be false. The confidence therefore does change the analysis, and removes the objection.
