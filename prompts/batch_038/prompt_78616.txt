I am reviewing a chapter for an edited volume on the philosophy of the social sciences, and its central argument rests on an analogy I have not been able to assess cleanly. The author is arguing that heritability estimates should be kept out of policy discussion altogether. The analogy: asking what fraction of a person's educational attainment comes from their genes rather than their environment is like asking what fraction of a rectangle's area comes from its length rather than its width. Both factors are necessary; they enter multiplicatively rather than additively; and any percentage one produces depends on where one starts and on which factor one holds fixed, so there is no fact of the matter. From this the author draws three conclusions, in the order they appear: (i) there is no truth about how much of an individual's trait is genetic; (ii) the heritability figures the chapter quotes for adult cognitive traits, in the region of 0.5 to 0.8, are accordingly pseudo-precision, and the field's practice of estimating such quantities amounts to a category error; (iii) since any such figure is indexed to the range of environments actually present in the sample, nothing about intervention effects in other environments can be inferred from it. A colleague of mine holds that the analogy is sound but at best supports (i), and that (ii) slides from a claim about an individual to a claim about variance across a population.

Please take the analogy apart. My own suspicion is that it has no purchase at the population level at all: a rectangle, as described, contains no distribution, no developmental noise, no range of environments, and nothing that could be selected on, and the author is trading on features of the individual case to license a statistical conclusion. But I want to be told where this is wrong as well as where it is right, and I want (iii) treated separately, since I suspect it is true and does not need the analogy to be true.
