A friend of mine who works in evolutionary psychology and I spent most of a Saturday on the question of whether the programme he works in — the attempt to explain the structure of the mind as a large collection of evolved, domain-specific modules — is what Lakatos would call degenerating. His defense kept returning to the discovery of Neptune. When the orbit of Uranus failed to match Newtonian prediction, Le Verrier did not touch the law of gravitation; he posited an unseen planet, calculated where it had to be, and the astronomers who aimed a telescope at that region found it on the first night of looking. His point: the very move his critics call ad hoc — positing an unseen mechanism to absorb an anomaly — is the move that produced one of the great confirmations in the history of science.

My reply was Vulcan. Le Verrier ran the identical play on the residual advance of Mercury's perihelion, positing an intra-Mercurial planet or, failing that, a belt of small bodies near the Sun. Nothing was ever found. The anomaly sat unresolved for another half century and was finally dealt with not by patching the belt but by replacing the core. So Neptune and Vulcan are the same move with different outcomes, and reaching for Neptune is picking the winner with hindsight — which, he said, is exactly the charge Lakatos warns us off making from inside the moment.

What I want is an audit of the analogy rather than another round from either of us. Give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the part of the residuals in Uranus's orbit, of the shared core law doing the predicting, of the calculation that fixed a position in the sky, of the telescope sweep that could have come up empty, and of the planet itself. Then say which of the specific moves we were arguing about actually occupies each slot — a purported cross-cultural universal that fails to replicate, a widespread behavior that looks flatly maladaptive, a predicted sex difference that tracks local ecology more loosely than the module allows — and flag any slot with no counterpart at all, because I suspect one of them is the whole argument.

Sort the disanalogies by weight rather than listing them. I have candidates: that the Neptune hypothesis was quantitatively constrained and could have been wrong in a precise way; that it predicted a novel fact rather than redescribing a known one; that an instrument independent of the theory could settle it; that modules in his field are typically individuated by the very behaviors they were introduced to explain. Tell me which of these do real work against his inference and which I am inflating. Please also separate the two senses of ad hoc we kept sliding between — an auxiliary with no independent testability, and a shift that yields no corroborated excess empirical content — and assign each contested move to one or the other, since I think that slippage cost us the evening.

Two things I could not get him to concede, and want examined honestly. First, the asymmetry. From inside the programme in 1859, Vulcan was no desperate expedient: it was the move that had just worked, generated by the same positive heuristic, backed by the same track record — and when the searches failed the community reached for smaller bodies, for a circumsolar cloud, and even for a slight amendment to the inverse-square exponent long before anyone reached for a new theory of gravitation. If Neptune and Vulcan are genuinely indistinguishable from the inside, then my charge of degeneration is as premature as his claim of progress. Say whether that really is the consequence, or whether there are present-tense marks of the difference that Lakatos's framework entitles me to point to. Second: is there a better analogy for what his programme is doing, one that gets the relation among anomaly, patch, and test right — and if there is, does it favor him or me?
