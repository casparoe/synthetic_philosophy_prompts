I spent some thirty-five years in experimental physics and have taken up political philosophy only since retiring, so I may be missing something obvious; I should nonetheless like a careful answer to a puzzle about the design of estate taxation in the United States.

Three features of the current regime sit side by side. A transfer to a citizen surviving spouse is exempt without limit, so that in practice no transfer tax falls until the second of the couple dies. The income-tax basis of an asset is reset to its market value at the owner's death, so that appreciation accrued over a lifetime escapes income taxation permanently. And a separate levy falls on transfers that skip a generation: a trust benefiting grandchildren rather than children attracts an additional tax, principally in those situations where it would otherwise avoid any transfer tax at the children's level.

The third feature seems to me the philosophically loaded one. Its evident purpose is not to raise revenue but to guarantee that each handoff down a lineage bears a toll, which makes the generational transfer the taxable unit. Yet the spousal exemption denies precisely that, treating a married couple as a single continuing owner across two deaths, and the basis reset treats the owner's holding period rather than the asset's own history as what generates gain. The regime thus appears to assume at once that the subject of ownership is the individual, that it is the household, and that it is the lineage.

Please reconstruct the account of property, of the person, and of intergenerational fairness that this architecture presupposes, and be explicit about what it strictly requires as against what it merely happens to be consistent with. Is the tension I have described genuine, or can a single principle -- equality of opportunity between generations, say, or the prevention of wealth that never changes hands -- license all three features? Then tell me what would have to be altered in the design were that commitment made explicit rather than implicit. Finally, state how confident you are in the reading you offer, and single out the point in it you are least sure of.
