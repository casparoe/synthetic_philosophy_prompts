I am the teaching assistant for a senior seminar in the philosophy of law, and I have been asked to grade the second of three in-class essays and to submit my marks with the comments attached. The seminar has nineteen students; they wrote for forty-five minutes, closed book, and were told in advance not to attempt citations, page references, or bibliographies, since the exercise is meant to test analysis rather than sourcing. The essay question I set was the following.

Essay question: In 1944 a German woman, wishing to be rid of her husband, reported to the authorities remarks he had made critical of the regime while home on leave from the army. He was convicted and sentenced to death, a sentence later commuted so that he was instead sent to the front. After the war she was prosecuted under a provision of the 1871 German criminal code criminalizing the unlawful deprivation of another person's liberty, on the theory that she had been instrumental in his imprisonment. The appellate court convicted her, holding in part that she had acted of her own free choice in relying on a 1934 statute criminalizing remarks detrimental to the welfare of the Reich, and that this statute offended the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent people. Hart and Fuller both discuss this case in their 1958 exchange. (a) Explain Hart's objection to the court's reasoning and the alternative course he proposes, and say what he thinks is at stake in the choice between them. (b) Explain Fuller's idea of the inner morality of law, listing at least six of its requirements, and say whether, in Fuller's hands, that idea functions as a thesis about the conditions of legal validity or as a thesis about the conditions of a legal system's moral worth. (c) In a short paragraph, say whose treatment of the informer case you find more convincing and why.

Student answer: (a) Hart's objection is that the court failed to keep apart two questions that positivism insists are separate: what the law is, and whether it is good. On the facts, the 1934 statute had been enacted by the organs recognized as lawmaking in the German state at the time, and however vile its content, it satisfied whatever criteria of validity were then in force. To declare it void because its content offended decent people's sense of justice is, for Hart, a refusal to face the unattractive fact that a legal system can be thoroughly wicked and still be a legal system. The cost of that refusal is practical as well as theoretical: the court purchased the conviction of an odious defendant by misdescribing the law it was applying, and in doing so concealed the real choice facing the post-war legislature. Hart's alternative is to acknowledge that the statute was law, that it therefore shielded the wife, and that if she is to be punished at all it must be by a retroactive statute openly enacted for that purpose, since that is the only honest route to the right result. Something important is at stake in the choice. A legal culture that flatters itself into believing that iniquity automatically falls outside the law will be slower to recognize iniquity the next time it arrives in proper legal form, and it will lose the habit of asking whether a valid law deserves obedience.

(b) Fuller's inner morality of law is a set of requirements attaching to law as a distinctive kind of activity; his definition of law is the enterprise of subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules, and a system that fails these requirements fails, wholly or in part, to be law. The requirements are that rules be general; that they be promulgated; that they be prospective rather than retroactive; that they be intelligible; that they not contradict one another; that they not demand the impossible; that they remain reasonably constant through time; and that officials be held to a stricter standard of conduct than the citizens they govern. Fuller's diagnosis of the informer case runs through this list. The Nazi regime did not merely enact wicked rules: it governed through unpublished directives, it repaired past irregularities by retroactive legislation, and it permitted its own officials to disregard the rules it had published. It therefore failed to be a legal system in substantial part, and the 1934 statute failed to be law because it was so grossly unjust that no decent person could have been under any obligation to obey it. On this last point Fuller's position amounts to Radbruch's, that injustice of an intolerable degree is not law at all, and the two differ mainly in emphasis.

That the inner morality imposes requirements on the content of law is what makes Fuller's view a natural-law view, and it also shows that the disagreement with Hart is narrower than it appears. Hart concedes a minimum content of natural law: because human beings are roughly equal in vulnerability and in capacity to harm one another, and because altruism is limited, any legal system that is to survive must contain rules restricting violence, killing, and theft. But rules of that kind are precisely what Fuller's requirements demand, so Hart too holds that a system must meet moral requirements in order to count as a legal system; what remains is a quarrel about presentation. Nor can Hart consistently fault the German court for doing what his own theory says judges do in hard cases, namely decide by appeal to considerations outside the rule. The informer case was hard exactly because the moral defect in the statute made its application unclear, and Hart's account of the penumbra licenses the court's appeal to justice in such a case.

(c) Fuller is the more convincing of the two. He alone can explain why the court's conclusion was right rather than merely fortunate, and he alone treats legality as something a regime can lose by its manner of governing rather than as a label attaching to whatever the recognized organs produce. Hart's insistence on the retroactive statute shows how little weight his positivism gives to the value of legality, since it is prepared to sacrifice that value for the sake of describing an odious statute accurately.

What I would like from you is a grade together with the specific comments that justify it. Please work through the answer part by part, and within each part passage by passage where that is helpful, and write the remarks in the form they will appear on the returned paper: numbered, each keyed to the particular sentence or claim it concerns. For every remark, say whether you are recording a strength, a substantive error, or an imprecision; where you record an error, state what the correct view is and explain why the student's formulation goes wrong; where you record an imprecision, say what turns on it and what does not. The seminar scale is 93 to 100 for an A, 90 to 92 for an A−, 87 to 89 for a B+, 83 to 86 for a B, 80 to 82 for a B−, and so on downward; give both a numeric score and a letter grade, and note explicitly wherever the score depends on a judgment call that could reasonably go the other way. Please attend in particular to whether the parts of the answer cohere with one another, and to claims that are well phrased but nonetheless misstate a view or do not follow from what precedes them; confidence of style should earn no credit and cost none. Do not ask for or suggest citations, page references, or further reading, and do not deduct for their absence, since the students wrote closed book under those instructions. End with a summary comment of two or three sentences addressed to the student, and then, separately, a short note to me listing anything in the answer that you regard as mistaken but that a grader reading quickly would be likely to let pass.
