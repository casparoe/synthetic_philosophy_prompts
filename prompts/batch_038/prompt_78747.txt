My friend is taking a class on American philosophy and sent me this chunk from her study guide, asking if it looked right to me. It goes:

"Peirce and James both held that truth is what works; they disagreed about whose working counts. For Peirce, a belief is true if the community of investigators would settle on it at the limit of inquiry — truth is ideal consensus, which makes him basically a verificationist. James thought that made truth too remote and too social: for him a true idea is one an individual can verify here and now, that pays off in experience, and that is expedient to believe. That's why critics charged James with making truth whatever is comforting, and why Peirce renamed his own view 'pragmaticism' — he wanted a name ugly enough that nobody would steal it — to break with James's subjectivism."

Alot of this rings true to me, but the consensus-vs-individual-verification split feels too tidy, and I have a vague memory of Peirce being furious at the idea that truth is just whatever scientists end up agreeing on. Can you go through it claim by claim: whats fair, what's a simplification a specialist would push back on, and what's actually wrong? Where the scholarly reading is genuinely contested, flag it as contested rather than handing me your preferred gloss as settled. And if you think the way the guide frames the whole Peirce/James split is the wrong frame to begin with, say so plainly — I'd rather hear that than a polite line-by-line cleanup of a bad setup.
