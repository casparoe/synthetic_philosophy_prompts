Consider a proposal to regulate a form of online publication. Several thousand people each post, about one identifiable individual, a single item of information that is true, lawfully obtained, and trivial in isolation: a photograph of a parked car, a name lifted from an electoral roll, a shift pattern glimpsed in passing. No individual post comes near any plausible threshold for having harmed that person; each publisher, if blamed, has a complete answer in the innocuousness of what they did. Yet the conjunction of the posts amounts to a dossier from which the person's movements can be reconstructed, and they lose the ability to go about their life unobserved. No publisher is a but-for cause of that loss: it would have occurred had any one of them stayed silent.

I want a careful explanation of the conceptual difficulty such cases raise for Mill's harm principle, and specifically of a distinction I think is routinely collapsed in discussions of it. Call the first difficulty the *attribution* problem: even granting that a genuine harm has occurred, it is unclear that any assignable agent has done anything that qualifies as causing it, and the principle is standardly read as licensing interference only against an agent whose own conduct harms others. Call the second the *threshold* problem: even granting that the aggregate is the sort of thing that could warrant interference, it is unclear that the harm principle contains any resources for saying *when* a set of individually permissible acts has crossed into the other-regarding, since the principle appears to be formulated in terms of the character of conduct rather than in terms of any cumulative quantity.

Please answer the following three questions in order.

1. Explain why these are two distinct problems rather than one problem described twice. Say what would have to be shown in order to dissolve each of them, and why dissolving one leaves the other standing. In particular, consider whether a causal-contribution account that identifies *many* contributors rather than *none* resolves the attribution problem or merely restates it, and whether the threshold problem is a problem about harm at all or a problem about the principle being the wrong kind of thing to supply a crossing point.

2. In Chapter IV of *On Liberty*, Mill characterises the line of conduct that society may enforce as consisting in not injuring others' interests — 'or rather certain interests, which, either by express legal provision or by tacit understanding, ought to be considered as rights' — and in Chapter V he insists that harm to others does not by itself justify interference, since the successful candidate in an overcrowded profession or a competitive examination inflicts real loss on rivals whom society refuses to treat as wronged. Explain whether the conventional and legislative element in the first remark offers the Millian a way through the aggregation case: could the difficulty be dissolved by declaring the interest in not being assembled into a dossier to be a right? Address what this move would cost the harm principle's status as a *criterion* of legitimate interference, if the work is done by the convention rather than by the principle, and whether the competition cases show Mill willing to pay exactly that cost in the other direction.

3. Identify where you think a Millian must concede ground if they wish to permit regulation of the aggregate. Choose between three candidates and argue for your choice over the other two: a revision of what counts as a harm; a relocation of the harm to a single agent whose conduct is the relevant one, such as the platform that hosts and orders the posts; or a demotion of the harm principle from a criterion of legitimacy to a necessary condition that must be supplemented by further considerations of expediency. Be explicit about which concession you regard as least damaging, and why.

Write your answer in British English, between roughly 900 and 1,300 words, organised under three headings corresponding to the three questions. Assume your reader already has the general statement of the harm principle and the broad shape of the free-speech literature to hand: do not recapitulate either, and spend no words on surveying the possible responses to the case as a matter of policy.
