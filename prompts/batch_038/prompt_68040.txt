I have spent most of my working life designing ownership models, capacity plans, and on-call rotations for a fleet of production services, and I read the policy literature on paid parental leave, childcare, and the dual-earner family only by converting it, clause by clause, into the vocabulary I actually think in. I would like you to perform that conversion as faithfully as you can and then tell me where it breaks.

The material is a two-sided argument. On one side: the household is a small production unit allocating time between market work and domestic work; when one partner's market prospects are better and the other is relatively more productive at home, specialization follows comparative advantage; the result is efficient, and since both partners accepted it, voluntary; leave policy should therefore be neutral, granting each parent an individual entitlement to use or decline. On the other side: that description misstates how the arrangement arises. Earnings gaps precede the arrival of the child and are then re-read as evidence of comparative advantage. The entitlement is nominally individual but effectively transferable, because of the household income constraint and the career penalty attached to taking it, so one parent becomes the default — the number the school calls first, the absorber of every unscheduled event — and that role then produces the preferences and the accumulated competence that are cited to justify it. Leave that is reserved per parent, non-transferable, and paid at or near full earnings shifts the equilibrium; leave that is merely available does not.

Translate both sides into my vocabulary: ownership and single-threaded ownership, primary and secondary on-call, escalation paths and the hardcoded first hop, single points of failure, redundancy and failover, backpressure and load shedding, queueing and head-of-line blocking, toil (in my organization, work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, and devoid of enduring value), blast radius, capacity planning against peaks that recur on a known schedule, blameless postmortems, and service-level objectives as the device that turns an implicit tradeoff into an explicit one.

State the mapping item by item rather than gesturing at it. Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want: for each claim on each side, say whether it survives cleanly, survives only with a distortion you should name, or has no expression in this vocabulary at all. Watch for the temptation to render a normative claim as an efficiency claim and call that translation — if you find yourself writing fragile where the argument says unjust, stop and flag it. Four specific probes. Does the default parent map onto a single point of failure, or does that move quietly convert a claim about who should bear the work into a claim about reliability? Is the endogeneity of preferences genuinely like hysteresis or path dependence in a routed system, or is the analogy load-bearing beyond what it can carry? Can toil accommodate care that is repetitive and not automatable yet not, on the account of the people doing it, devoid of enduring value — and if it cannot, what has been lost in the transfer? Does the blameless-postmortem norm have a real counterpart in the claim that the division of labor is nobody's fault and yet open to redesign, or is that a coincidence of wording?

Finally, give me a verdict on the residue and be direct about which way it cuts. Is the untranslatable remainder evidence that my vocabulary is missing something — no way to represent persons as anything other than components, no way to represent a relationship that cannot be failed over to a standby — or evidence that the structural side of the argument is murkier than it sounds, so that what it calls structure my vocabulary would simply call the aggregate of local decisions under a badly chosen default?
