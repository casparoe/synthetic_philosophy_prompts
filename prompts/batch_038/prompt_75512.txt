Okay so I've been trying to figure out what actually fixes the reference of a natural-kind word, and the only method that's working for me is generating Twin-Earth-ish cases and noticing what I want to say about each one. Problem: I'm pretty sure my verdicts don't all hang together. Can you tell me what principle, if any, is behind them?

The list.

1. Stock Twin Earth. Oscar surrounded by H2O says "water", Twin Oscar surrounded by XYZ says "water", neither can tell the difference and the two communities are internally identical. Verdict: their words mean different things, Oscar's thoughts are about H2O and Twin Oscar's about XYZ. Very firm.

2. The arthritis guy who thinks arthritis can occur in the thigh, gets told by his doctor that it can't, and says "well then I have something else wrong with my thigh." Verdict: before the correction his word already meant arthritis, he was just wrong. Firm.

3. One speaker, all his life, calls every beech "elm" and every elm "beech". Verdict: his "elm" doesn't mean beech. Firm.

4. The Madagascar-style drift: a name starts out attached to a bit of mainland coast, gets carried off by confused sailors, and a couple of generations later the whole community applies it to a big island. Verdict: it now refers to the island. Also fairly firm — and notice this is case 3 with lots of speakers instead of one, yet I flip.

5. One I made up, and this is the one that worries me. Over three hundred years every drop of H2O on Earth is quietly swapped, molecule for molecule, for XYZ, with no break at all in the teaching chain — grandmothers pass the word "water" down to granddaughters the whole way and nobody could possibly have noticed. Verdict: today English "water" means XYZ, and it was the 1750 speakers who meant H2O. Which is bizarre, because the historical chain here is perfectly intact, which is supposedly the thing doing the work in case 1, and here I don't think it does any work whatsoever.

6. Also mine. A community where 99% of everything anyone has ever called "gold" is pyrite. Real gold exists but is vanishingly rare, and they do have some notion that there's a "true gold" that a few experts can pick out. Verdict: their "gold" means gold and they are massively wrong about nearly every lump they own. Firm-ish. Set that next to case 5, where an equally overwhelming sample-majority does move the reference, and I can feel the tension but I can't articulate it.

7. Jade. Nephrite and jadeite are chemically unrelated — an amphibole vs a pyroxene — and were only sorted out as distinct minerals centuries after the word was in circulation. Verdict: "jade" legitimately covers both, it's a genuine disjunctive kind, nobody was making an error. But if a community's "water" had covered both H2O and XYZ right from the start, I think that community would have been making an error, or at any rate their word wouldn't be one natural-kind term at all. I have no idea what the asymmetry comes from.

8. Two more that seem to need opposite machinery. (a) A hermit raised in a cave who's never touched water and learned the word entirely out of a book: I think his "water" still refers to H2O, via the book. (b) A novelist who coins "water" in a manuscript in a world where nobody has ever encountered any, and describes it perfectly: I think her word refers to nothing, it's just fiction. Same descriptive content, opposite verdicts, and the only difference is whether there's a chain to ride along.

My own rough guess at the principle: a term's extension is fixed by (i) the majority of the samples the community has actually bumped into, unless (ii) the community has a standing intention to defer to some subset of speakers — experts, elders, chemists, priests — about what the real stuff is, in which case the deferee's classification wins; and if there are neither samples nor deferees, the term is empty. Run that against the list. I think it gets 1, 2, 6 and 8b, flunks 5 and 7 outright, and I honestly can't tell what it says about 4.

So: what principle does capture as many of these as possible? State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case each clause is there to handle — I want to see the machinery matched to the data, not a slogan. If the only account that saves all eight needs a baroque extra clause, say so, then give me the simpler rival and name exactly which of my verdicts it costs me. I'd rather lose one verdict than carry a principle with five epicycles, but I don't know which one to sacrifice, so make the case for the sacrifice.

Then two follow-ups. What does your principle say about slow switching — if I get dropped on Twin Earth and live there forty years without ever noticing, is there a determinate fact about when my "water" beliefs stop being about H2O, and if so what makes it true, and if not why not? And what does it say about a case I haven't listed: a planet where the lakes and rivers are roughly half H2O and half XYZ, everybody drinks both, and there's just the one word.

Last thing, and please actually do it rather than nodding at it: mark clearly which of my verdicts and which parts of your answer are things essentially everyone working on this would sign off on, versus where you're taking a side in a live dispute between people who know the material cold. I don't want a contested position slipped to me as the consensus, and I especially don't want you to quietly settle some broader fight about whether content is in the head or in the world in the middle of answering a narrower question about reference.
