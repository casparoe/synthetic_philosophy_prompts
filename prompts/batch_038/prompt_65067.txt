I am in the final weeks of a small graduate seminar on the legality of international criminal justice, and the last session is built around a hypothetical our convenor circulates a week in advance: we each write down a verdict, she reads them out, and then she tells us how she would decide and why. She is unusually consistent across years, and my study group has been reconstructing her position from her published verdicts and from the positions she has taken in class, because we would rather be wrong in an intelligible way than guess. What I want from you is not the morally right answer to the hypothetical — it is a prediction of hers, together with an account of the principle she appears to be running.

Her record so far, as far as we have been able to tabulate it. On the Nuremberg count of planning and waging aggressive war against the German defendants, she says illegitimate: the 1928 pact renouncing war as an instrument of national policy bound states and attached no individual penalty, and she notes that the tribunal's own reply to the retroactivity objection concedes that the maxim about no crime without law is a principle of justice rather than a limit on sovereignty, then rests the convictions on the thought that these men must have known they were doing wrong — a move she calls honest but insufficient. On the war-crimes count concerning the killing of prisoners and of reprisal hostages, she says legitimate without qualification. On the tribunal's holding that atrocities committed inside Germany before the outbreak of war fell outside its jurisdiction unless connected to a war crime, she says the tribunal was right, and that had it convicted on that footing she would have counted it among the trial's illegitimate features, even though she regards the conduct in question as worse than anything else charged. On the post-war convictions of camp guards for murder by West German courts applying the German penal code already in force at the time of the acts, she says legitimate, and the most defensible accountability produced anywhere in that period. On the 1990s German convictions of border guards who shot people fleeing the German Democratic Republic, she says legitimate but reached by the longest route: the GDR's own border legislation and the human-rights covenants the GDR had ratified already condemned shooting unarmed fugitives, so the appeals to supra-statutory justice did no work and obscured what made the convictions safe. On the British statute of 1991 giving United Kingdom courts jurisdiction over war crimes committed in German-occupied Europe during the Second World War by people who have since become British citizens or residents, she says legitimate, because the conduct was criminal when done and only the forum is new. On the appeals chamber of the hybrid tribunal for Sierra Leone holding, over a dissent, that prosecuting a commander for the recruitment of child soldiers was not retroactive because the prohibition had crystallised into customary international law carrying individual responsibility before the dates in the indictment, she sides with the majority, and adds that the dissent's standard of notice is too generous because that commander had legal advisers and access to the same public record the judges used. On the Tokyo conspiracy convictions for crimes against peace, she says worse than Nuremberg's aggression count, because even if one grants that waging aggressive war had become individually criminal, convicting officials who planned and decided nothing on a theory of membership in a broad criminal enterprise changed the rules of imputation rather than the definition of the offence. And on the annulment of Argentina's amnesty laws and the reopening of prosecutions for the disappearances, she says legitimate, offering only this compressed line: removing a shield restores the ordinary law, whereas creating an offence displaces it.

The new hypothetical runs as follows. After a civil war, the Security Council establishes a hybrid tribunal sitting in the region. The defendant is a former interior minister. The charge arises from an emergency decree that authorised the forcible relocation and indefinite administrative detention of a minority population; the decree was valid under the domestic law then in force and had been upheld by the domestic courts. No treaty binding on that state criminalised the conduct at the time, though the state had ratified the two 1966 human-rights covenants, which prohibit the practices in question without creating individual criminal liability. During the operations the Security Council named the practice in two resolutions, and a Council-mandated commission of inquiry published a report stating that individuals would be held personally accountable; the minister had a legal adviser and received the report. The tribunal's statute was drafted by the intervening powers and adopted two years after the conduct; its personal jurisdiction runs only against nationals of the defeated state, and the intervening powers secured a provision excluding their own personnel, with any allegations against them to be dealt with by their own domestic systems. The mode of liability charged is defined for the first time in the statute — participation in a joint criminal enterprise — and under it the minister is charged for acts of camp administrators he never directly commanded. The detention sites caused several thousand deaths from disease and exposure; there is no evidence of an intent to exterminate.

What I would like from you, in this order. First, lay out the candidate principles that are jointly consistent with her record, stated precisely enough that a further case could refute them — including whether she distinguishes retroactivity of the offence definition, of the mode of imputation, and of the forum, and whether her notice standard is calibrated to the defendant's office and access to advice rather than fixed. Second, say which pairs of candidates the record so far fails to separate, and which of them the new hypothetical does separate. Third, predict her verdict on the prosecution of the interior minister as it stands, with a rough probability, and say which of the three axes of retroactivity you expect her to treat as decisive, given that the facts push in different directions on each. Fourth, tell me whether, on her principles, the carve-out for the intervening powers' personnel is a defect of the same kind as the retroactivity problems or of a different kind, and what she would have to be committed to if she votes either way — that is, which principle she would be adopting by finding the prosecution legitimate and which by finding it illegitimate. Fifth, propose the smallest single-fact variation on the hypothetical that would discriminate between the surviving candidates, and the one question I should put to her in the seminar to extract the most information per minute.
