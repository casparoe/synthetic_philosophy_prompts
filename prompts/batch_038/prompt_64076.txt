Still buzzing from a two-hour argument with my friend R and I genuinely can't tell who won, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing.

It started over who should set the rules for a river that runs through four of our states — pollution standards, extraction limits, that kind of thing. I said the states, obviously, subsidiarity. He said my principle doesn't survive five minutes of pressure and walked me through this:

(1) Subsidiarity says a matter should be decided at the lowest level that can handle it competently.
(2) To handle a matter competently just is to produce outcomes at least as good as a higher level would produce.
(3) When a local body decides something, the costs that decision dumps on outsiders don't enter its calculus, because those outsiders don't vote in its elections and don't pay its taxes. A higher-level body has them inside its constituency, so it internalizes the spillover.
(4) So for any matter with cross-border effects, the higher level produces at least as good outcomes, which by (2) makes it the competent level.
(5) Practically every matter has cross-border effects. Roads connect, air and water drift, capital and people move, standards get competed downwards.
(6) So subsidiarity assigns practically every matter to the higher level.
(7) A principle that assigns practically everything to the center is not a principle of decentralization. So either subsidiarity is a centralizing doctrine in a decentralist costume, or it's empty.

He's German and he kept insisting the EU is the standing proof: subsidiarity is written into the treaties and yet everything ends up in Brussels anyway.

I tried the escape I thought was available — that subsidiarity isn't a substantive allocation rule at all but a presumption, and the center carries the burden of justifying an override — and he said that's not an escape, because a presumption that gets defeated in practically every case does exactly the same work as a rule assigning everything to the center. I dont have a reply to that, and that's roughly where I lost the thread of the whole thing.
