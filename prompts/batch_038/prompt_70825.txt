Quick context: I'm the junior member of a hospital ethics commitee and we've been fighting for two months about how to hand out a drug we only get a little of — eight courses a quarter for roughly thirty patients who'd benefit, and if you don't get treated this quarter you probably don't get treated at all. One option on the table ranks everyone by predicted life-years gained and treats down the list. I've been pushing a weighted lottery instead, so that nobody's chance drops to zero, including the 74-year-olds. Last week the senior physician ended the discussion by invoking that old utilitarian slogan, everybody counts for one and nobody counts for more than one (Bentham or Mill, I've never been able to keep them straight), on the grounds that it settles the matter: five lives are five times one life, so you treat the biggest gains and my lottery is just squeamishness dressed up as fairness.

Its a non sequitur, I'm fairly confident, but I couldn't say why in the moment and half the room nodded along. And here's what bugs me: the same sentence sounds like it might support my side too, since a lottery hands every patient exactly one ticket, which looks like the purest possible way of counting each for one.

So can you pull apart the different precise claims that slogan could be making? I want the whole menu, not a pick-one-and-defend-it: something about whose interests get included in the first place, something about how heavily they weigh once they're in, something about whether harms and benefits to different people can be added up into a single total at all, something about equal standing or dignity, maybe something about voting and political procedures, and whatever else is hiding in there. For each reading give me (a) what exactly it asserts, (b) whether it's a harmless platitude everyone accepts, a substantive claim with real arguments behind it, or just false, and (c) whether it actually gets him from counting each for one to treating the greater number rather than running a lottery. Flag any reading that instead supports the lottery or leaves the choice wide open, because I'd bet at least one does, and say whether the two halves of the slogan are even doing the same job — the floor part versus the ceiling part.

Then run one test on whichever reading is strongest for him: does it also license taking a kidney from one healthy person in the waiting room to give to five patients who each need one? If the premise doing the work in our meeting does that work too, I want to be able to say so out loud, because our committee would reject the kidney case unanimously and he'd have to explain the difference.

Last thing: tell me which single reading he was most likely reaching for, and what the shortest honest reply is that I could have given in the room without sounding like I'm just refusing to look at the numbers.
