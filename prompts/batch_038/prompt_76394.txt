I'm teaching a 75-minute epistemology seminar, 16 students, all of whom have already read the DeRose bank case and already decided what they think about it — which is the actual problem. I want a structured activity that makes pragmatic encroachment and contextualism feel like live, competing hypotheses instead of two paragraphs in a textbook, and specifically that gets them to feel the *difference* between the two, which is the bit they never get.

Constraints and asks:

- No lecture up front, and no labels until the very end. They should be inside a situation that generates the pressure before anyone says the word "stakes."
- Please don't use a bank. Give me a fresh scenario, something mundane and checkable, built so that evidence quality and stakes are crossed (a 2x2 at minimum). If you only vary stakes they'll conclude "oh right, evidence matters" and go home.
- The thing I actually care about: build in a round where the person deciding whether the protagonist knows is *not* the protagonist. Low-stakes attributor judging a high-stakes subject, and then the swap. Whether "knows" tracks the subject's situation or the speaker's is the whole ballgame, and I want them to hit it head-on with a forced call written down, not in open discussion where it'll evaporate.
- Have them record what they'd *say* ("I know that the ferry leaves at...") and what they'd *do* seperately, before anyone compares notes. If the two come apart in the room I want that gap on the board in their own words.
- Payoffs: no real money, but the stakes also can't be pretend — if they know nothing rides on it, the thing is dead on arrival. Tell me what token actually bites for a room of 19 year olds and how you make a wrong call *felt*.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the two views, and onto the deflationary third option — nothing happens to knowledge at all, it's just ordinary cost-benefit reasoning, so the behaviour in the room looks identical under all three. I want a forced-choice question or two to put on the board that makes them actually pick a side and pay for it. And be straight with me about which part of the disagreement the activity simply cannot produce: my suspicion is it can only ever surface a psychological pattern that all three theories happily predict, and I'd rather know how you'd handle that than have you paper over it.

Finally, minute-by-minute timeline, plus what predictably goes wrong the first time you run it and what you do in the moment (I assume someone reverse-engineers the point within five minutes and starts performing the answer they think I want).
