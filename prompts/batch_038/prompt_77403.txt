I don't have any philosophy training, this is just something ive been turning over in the garage for a couple years.

I build marble machines — plywood board, a few hundred little brass pegs, hopper at the top. Most of mine are normal: pegs glued in, marbles bounce down, land in bins. But one of them I built so the pegs aren't fixed. They sit in loose sleeves, so a marble hit can tilt a peg a few degrees, shove it sideways, or in a couple of places knock it out and let a new one drop in from a channel above. The board changes while it plays. After a while it starts funneling marbles into particular runs, and you could almost say it remembers things. I also built in clusters: whack one cluster and a marble gets diverted into another cluster across the board, so pairs of clusters end up associated just from getting knocked around together.

Thats where the thinking started. Years back I read part of a Hofstadter book — the fat one with the Escher drawings on the cover — and there was a thought experiment about a pinball machine whose pins get moved by the balls, and the balls were meant to be like symbols. I never finished it and I don't remember the details, so please don't try to reconstruct his version or tell me what he said. Use my board.

My brother writes software for a living and he's heard me go on about this enough times. His line is that a mind is a program and a brain is the hardware the program runs on, and that when I say a cluster pushed a marble somewhere I'm fooling myself — the only thing ever doing any pushing is pegs and marbles, and cluster-talk is just a lazy way of describing a huge number of small pushes. On his view the two levels are like the code you write and the electrons in the chip: you can describe it either way you like, but nothing up top reaches down and moves anything, and anyone who thinks otherwise has mistaken a description for a cause. He says my own board proves the point, because I can always give a full peg-by-peg account of every marble.

Can you take that analogy apart properly for me? First the mapping, laid out explicitly: what in his story is the hardware, what's the program, what's the instruction set, what's the level of description, and is there anything on my board playing the part of whoever wrote the program in the first place. Then the places the mapping stops working, sorted into the ones that actually threaten his argument and the ones that are only surface differences. I especially want to know what the software picture quietly assumes that a self-rearranging board doesn't have — I keep feeling like the fixed chip is doing unearned work in there, since code can't rewire its own CPU but my clusters literally move each other.

And then the payoff question: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Is there a weaker version that does survive, something like "the peg-level account is always sufficient, so the top level never adds any causal power" — and would that weaker version still be worth arguing about? Tell me how I could actually check, on my own board, whether a cluster did something the peg-by-peg story can't capture, versus me just re-describing the same pushes in fatter words. If there's a better analogy than software-on-hardware for what this thing is doing, give me one and audit it too, short — I don't want to trade one slogan for another.
