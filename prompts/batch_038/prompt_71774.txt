I have been collecting my own verdicts about when a failure to do something counts as a cause of what happened, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I know roughly how the dialectic goes — counterfactual dependence is supposed to treat all of these alike, and one standard move is to deny that absences can be causes at all — but I do not want a survey of that debate. I want the principle, and I want to be told which of my verdicts it costs me. The cases are in the order I collected them.

1. I pay a neighbour to water my fern while I am away for a fortnight. She leaves town, forgets, and the fern dies. Her not watering caused the fern's death. No hesitation.

2. My downstairs neighbour also holds a key, was home the whole fortnight, and could have watered it in ten seconds. We have no relationship beyond the key and she never thought of the matter. Her not watering did not cause the fern's death.

3. It is a dry summer under a hosepipe ban, and the fern, which lives on the balcony, gets no rain for three weeks. The lack of rain killed it, I think. No agent, no undertaking, nothing expected of anyone.

4. Other absent preventers I refuse to count: a stranger three streets away who owns a hose and never thought of me; an automatic irrigation system I never installed; a meteor that did not strike the pot. In each case the fern would have lived had the thing occurred, which is the same dependence I invoked in 1 and 3, and yet I list none of them as causes. If you think 3 and the first item here are simply the same case and I am being inconsistent, say so — but I suspect there is a difference and I cannot name it.

5. A lifeguard on duty watches a swimmer go under and does not leave the chair. The swimmer drowns. The lifeguard's inaction caused the death.

6. A strong off-duty swimmer with no dependants stands twenty feet away, sees the same thing, and does nothing. I am entirely confident this person is gravely blameworthy and entirely confident that their inaction did not cause the death.

7. A technician enters a hypobaric chamber and closes the oxygen valve. The occupant dies. Caused.

8. A technician walks past the same chamber, sees the valve closed and the occupant inside, and does not open it. The occupant dies. Not caused — and yet the valve is in the same position at the same moment, and as far as I can tell the physical history of the chamber is identical in the two cases.

9. Two nurses are each separately required to check a chart before an infusion goes up, and either check on its own would have caught a wrong-drug error. Neither checks. The patient dies. I cannot say that neither failure caused the death; I want to say both did, even though neither was necessary given the other.

10. A ranger decides not to cut a firebreak and the fire reaches the town: caused. The same ranger, the same decision, the same fire, but the wind shifts in a way nobody could have predicted and the break would not have saved the town: not caused. What matters is what would actually have happened, not what would normally have happened.

11. A hospital's sepsis-alert software is supposed to flag deteriorating patients. That night a threshold misfires and no flag appears; the protocol tells staff to rely on the flag, so nobody checks manually; the patient dies. I want to say the missing flag caused the death. But the program omitted nothing — it computed exactly what it computes — and I do not want to say the cause was the engineers who wrote it three years earlier, though they are difference-makers too. My causal verdict here seems to have nothing to attach to.

12. My brother, who lives nearby and holds a key, promised to feed my cat while I was away. He was hospitalised with food poisoning for three days and could not go. The cat starved.

Case 12 is the one I keep going back and forth on. Half the time I say his failure caused the cat's death and he is simply not to blame for it; the other half I say that when someone was genuinely unable to do the thing, there was no omission in the first place, and so nothing for a causal claim to be about. I do not know which half I believe.

My tentative principle is that an absence causes an outcome when there was a standing expectation — contractual, role-based, or merely statistical — that the missing event would occur, and it did not. But this makes causation depend on what people happen to expect, which I find unacceptable: expectations cannot change what caused what. Case 3 is meant to break the statistical version as well, though I am not sure it does.

What I would like back:

- The candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply one to a case I have never seen, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. If more than one survives, say exactly how they differ and which case would tell them apart.
- For each candidate, its verdict on all twelve cases and an explicit list of the verdicts of mine it cannot accommodate. I would rather have a plain principle that costs me two verdicts than an elaborate one that saves all twelve, but tell me what each option actually costs and which verdict is cheapest to give up.
- Where my verdicts may not be causal verdicts at all — where I am tracking explanatory salience, responsibility, or normalcy-relative dependence instead — and whether that dissolution is legitimate or a way of avoiding the question. Cases 6 and 11 are where I most suspect it.
- Three or four cases of your own construction that discriminate between the surviving candidates, with the verdict each candidate delivers and which verdict you take to be correct.
- Whether any workable principle needs a distinction between acts and omissions at the metaphysical level, or whether the distinction lives entirely in how a situation is described. Case 8 is what makes me ask.

Please mark throughout which parts of your answer are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position to me as though it were the consensus. Where one of my verdicts is idiosyncratic rather than widely shared, say so; where you think a verdict is not wrong but confused, say that and explain the confusion. If a candidate you propose corresponds to a named position in the literature, name it only if you are confident of the match — otherwise describe the view and leave it unnamed.
