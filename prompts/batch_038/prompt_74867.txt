My mother is being seen by two specialists about whether she should have a heart valve replaced, and they disagree: one says at her age the surgery is clearly worth it, the other says it clearly is not. Both have the same imaging and the same history, and I have no way of ranking them. A friend lent me a trade-press book about disagreement, and the chapter on experts runs as follows:

"Here is the argument, roughly. Suppose you and I have gone over exactly the same evidence on some question, and we are equally intelligent and equally careful, yet you believe it and I don't. Neither of us has any grounds for preferring our own take on the evidence to the other's: from the inside, my reasoning looks to me just as sound as yours looks to you. So the rational response is to split the difference — to cut your confidence in half, or as near to it as you can manage. Philosophers call this conciliationism. It has an obvious trouble: it undercuts itself. You and I are also peers about conciliationism, and plenty of able philosophers reject it, so by its own lights you should stop believing it; the view tells you to abandon the view. It is also paralyzing. On any live controversy you can always find someone just as smart who disagrees, so the conciliationist is committed to holding no settled opinion about politics, religion, or morality. For these reasons most working epistemologists now hold that you may stand your ground whenever you have independent reason to trust the process that produced your belief."

What I took from the chapter, and what I have been repeating to my family, is that my mother's two doctors should each drop to fifty-fifty and that the surgery question is effectively a coin flip for the rest of us. Before I say it again, I would like an audit of the paragraph rather than of my inference. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a specialist in the epistemology of disagreement would accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which are wrong or overstated. Two things in particular I cannot settle on my own: whether "split the difference" is really what conciliationists say, or whether the chapter is sliding between a general position and one specific version of it — I have also come across the phrase "equal weight view" and do not know how it relates to the label the chapter uses. And something about the second half of the paragraph seems to deploy the setup from the first half more loosely than that setup was stated, though I cannot put my finger on where; the self-undermining passage likewise reads to me like a devastating point, and I suspect that is exactly the reaction it is written to produce. Say whether it is one. Close with two or three sentences telling me whether my fifty-fifty conclusion about the surgery survives the corrections, or whether the corrected view licenses something different. Please keep the whole reply under 600 words.
