**Orphaned rationales: when a reason outlives its reasoner**

A free-floating rationale is supposed to be a reason with no reasoner attached to it. Selection processes — natural selection most famously, but also market pressure, institutional drift, and blind trial-and-error engineering — reliably produce designs that answer to reasons without there being any mind that represents those reasons. The wing answers to a reason about lift; the waggle dance answers to a reason about distance and bearing; the legacy date field in a banking system answers to a reason nobody alive remembers.

There are two standard ways to take this. The deflationary way says "rationale" is just a façon de parler: all we've got is a reliable pattern of counterfactual support, and calling it a reason is a vivid way of summarizing that pattern. The inflationary way says rationales are objective abstract items, cousins of Frege's thoughts or Popper's objective contents of the third realm, existing whether or not anyone grasps them. I want to argue for a third option, and I think the third option is only visible once we separate two roles that get fused in ordinary cases.

Call them the **bearer** and the **addressee** of a rationale. The bearer is whatever physically sustains it: the trait, the artifact, the convention, the subroutine. The addressee is the agent for whom it would be a reason. In the paradigm case of a person's reason, bearer and addressee coincide in one organism — my reason for taking the umbrella is borne by my own states and addressed to me. Free-floating rationales are just the cases where they come apart.

They come apart in two different ways, and this matters. In the "never-attached" way, no addressee ever existed: the wing, the dance, the silt pattern. In the "forward-orphaned" way, there was an addressee and now there isn't: the vestigial structure, the dead convention, the decommissioned safeguard, the function call left in the codebase after the thing that used to invoke it was deleted. I think nearly all the interesting philosophy is in the second kind, because orphaned rationales have a past. That's what makes them able to go stale, and going stale is what raises the hard question: can something be *wrong* when there is nobody left to be wrong?

Here's the case I want to lean on. A lighthouse is built on a headland to warn shipping off a sandbar. Over decades the bar silts up and the navigable channel moves a mile east. The light is automated and the keeper's cottage is sold. Eventually the light is decommissioned, but the tower stays and the lamp mechanism is left in place: wired, serviced, functional, never lit. And crucially, the lamp sits at a height and bearing that were tuned to the old channel and are now badly suited to the new one.

I want to say three things about this. First, there is still a rationale there. Second, it is misaddressed: the slot it occupies is one no current agent is in a position to fill. Third — and this is the part I most want pressure on — it can be a *failed* rationale without anyone failing. The lamp is now at the wrong height for the reason it was made for. That gives us misfit without misrepresentation.

If that's right, the deflationary reading can't be the whole story. If a rationale were nothing but a description we impose on a pattern, then the fact that the lamp is at the wrong height for the new channel would be a fact about our description, not about the lamp. But it looks like a fact about the lamp: the structure is now badly made for the purpose it was made for, and it would still be badly made if nobody ever climbed the tower again and noticed. So orphaned rationales have a kind of objectivity, and they get it without needing a third realm. They get it from being slots rather than contents. A slot is not a proposition anyone could believe or disbelieve; it is a set of conditions on what could count as a reason for whoever ends up occupying the niche. Any agent who comes to have the tower's situation as a reason has to have something answerable to that slot. In that limited sense a rationale can precede its reasoners and outlast them.

Now the worry I can't shake. If a slot is just a set of conditions on contents, it starts to sound like an abstract object, and my third option collapses into the inflationary one. Worse, the whole thing might be a redescription: to say a slot exists may be to say nothing more than that if an agent of a certain sort were in a certain situation, they would have a certain reason — and that is a counterfactual, and counterfactuals are cheap, no ontological commitment required. On that diagnosis my middle position is the deflationary position wearing a hat. I don't currently have an answer to this, and I suspect the answer, if there is one, has to be that the orphaned case is doing work the never-attached case can't: the lighthouse's rationale is wrong in a way that a wing's rationale is never wrong, because a wing with a bad shape just isn't a wing of that kind, whereas a lamp at the wrong height is still the very lamp that was built for the old channel. That asymmetry — identity preserved through failure — is what I think counterfactuals alone can't capture. But I'm not confident it isn't question-begging.
