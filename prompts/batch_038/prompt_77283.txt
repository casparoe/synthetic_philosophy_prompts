I am a high school junior, and this year I am completing an independent study in decision theory under a teacher who also advises our debate team. My final assignment is to write a case study that my seminar will have to argue over, and I would like it to be genuinely difficult rather than a familiar example in new clothing. I am therefore asking you to construct a case to the specifications below.

The point of the case, and the argument I intend to make with it, is this: the list of warning signs that the literature on escalating commitment gives practitioners — money already spent, personal responsibility for the original decision, a public commitment to it, repeated negative feedback, an ego or reputational stake in being vindicated, a tempting alternative that others urge you to switch to — does not diagnose anything. Those signs appear in decisions that are perfectly correct as reliably as they appear in decisions that are disasters. So I need a case in which all of them are plainly visible, the decision they supposedly condemn is nevertheless the right one, and the project they are attached to is nevertheless a trap.

The case should be a realistic organizational decision involving real money: a school district, a hospital, a municipality, a small firm, a nonprofit — your choice, but please not an auction, a war, a spacecraft, or any of the examples that turn up in every textbook. Describe it from the point of view of one person at one decision node, choosing between stopping and continuing. There must be a counterpart on the other side of the situation whose behavior is predictable and who is not a villain, because part of what I want the case to show is that the trap is closed by two reasonable people rather than by one foolish one. State every figure explicitly, in round numbers small enough to check by hand.

Both of the following verdicts must hold, and must be shown to hold by the arithmetic rather than asserted:

First, at the node where the decision is made, continuing is better than stopping. Walking away is the worse option. So the decision is not an instance of the sunk-cost fallacy, and anyone who calls it one has made an error.

Second, undertaking the project in the first place was worse than never undertaking it — worse on the information available at the time of approval, not merely in hindsight. So the path as a whole has the structure of Shubik's dollar auction: each step correct, the sequence bad.

To keep the tension sharp, I need three further conditions. The decision-maker must be fully informed and honest with herself: no self-deception, no motivated reasoning, no optimistic consultant, no suppressed report, no reassuring news arriving at the last minute. She knows precisely what the case says she knows. Second, there must have been no precommitment device available to her at the outset — no exit clause, no board resolution, no contract term that would have bound her later — because part of what the case tests is whether a decision can be impeccable at the node where it is made while the absence of any earlier binding arrangement is exactly what ruined it. Third, the trap must remain a trap: she may not discover at the end that the project was worth more than everyone supposed.

Please deliver, in this order:

1. The scenario, in no more than three paragraphs.

2. The demonstration of the first verdict: the options at the node, what each is worth from that point forward, why continuing wins, an explicit statement that already-spent money has been excluded — and then the same numbers with the sunk costs wrongly included, so that I can show my seminar exactly where the error enters.

3. The demonstration of the second verdict: the expected value as of the approval date, using only what was known then.

4. The warning signs, one by one: whether each is present in your case, why it normally counsels stopping, and why it is misleading here.

5. Which single feature of the case does the work of making the two verdicts compatible, and what the case becomes if that feature is removed.

6. The strongest available counterattack from someone who defends the escalating-commitment picture — specifically the objection that the psychology cannot be separated from the arithmetic, because the beliefs that make continuation look dominant are themselves produced by the escalation. State it at full strength, not as a claim that the decision-maker is being foolish, and then tell me honestly whether my conditions rule it out or simply assume what it denies.

If any specification cannot be met without cheating, say so and show me where it breaks instead of quietly relaxing it. I do not want sources or a reading list; I want the reasoning, and I want the numbers to add up.
