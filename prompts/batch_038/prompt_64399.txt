Next term our second-year philosophy of mind course devotes a week to the split-brain cases and what they are supposed to show about the unity of consciousness, and I have been assigned the section. I am not asking for an exposition of the material; I want a plan for building it in the room, and I want it answerable in the terms I set out below.

Three constraints govern the session. First, I have fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and nothing else: no slides, and specifically none of the film footage of the classic experiments, which is the device everyone normally reaches for, so the plan must work without it. Second, the students have done the assigned reading and have already sorted themselves into two roughly equal camps. One camp holds that a commissurotomy patient is literally two conscious subjects sharing a single body, and that the discipline should say so plainly. The other holds that the dissociations are artefacts of an apparatus that never obtains outside the laboratory and therefore establish nothing about consciousness. Third, my objective is not to referee between these camps but to make both see that each has skipped the step that actually decides the question. The students have had one lecture on hemispheric lateralization from the psychology department and no neuroscience beyond it.

What I need:

1. The order of the components, with the dependency structure made explicit: which claim must be secured before which further claim can even be stated, and why. Mark in particular the point at which the session crosses from "the two hemispheres can be shown to process information independently" to anything resembling a claim about experience, and say what has to be true before that crossing is legitimate.

2. One carrying case — a single scenario I can draw on the board, develop, and return to throughout, rather than a tour of paradigms. State what features it must have to bear the weight and what features it must lack. Then tell me whether the obvious candidate (a stimulus confined to one visual field, with a verbal report that contradicts a simultaneously correct response by the hand controlled by the other hemisphere) is the right carrier or a trap; if it is a trap, say what any replacement would have to preserve, and note what I lose by not using the everyday anecdotes about the two hands working at cross purposes.

3. A misconception map. Beyond the two camps above, identify the errors the students will generate in the course of my own explanation as a by-product of it — for example the inference that the hemisphere without speech is thereby without experience, the assumption that whatever integrates the intact brain must be the corpus callosum and nothing more, the belief that these patients' apparent ordinariness is explained by their having learned to compensate, and the treatment of one hemisphere's confabulated account as a report about the other's states. For each, give the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off and the sentence or two that heads it off.

4. The one thing the session must not slide past: why the disunity, if it is disunity, surfaces only under engineered conditions. I want a formulation I can deliver aloud that does not concede the second camp's dismissal and does not overclaim on the first camp's behalf, together with an explanation of why that formulation is the honest one.

5. A closing check — a single question put to the room whose answers discriminate between students who have grasped the question and students who have merely picked a side. Describe what a good answer looks like and what a diagnostic bad answer looks like.

6. What I should decline outright and announce as declined, rather than gesture at: including the dispute over how many persons one of these patients is, which would consume the hour if admitted, and any other component you judge to be unteachable in this format without doing damage to it.
