I sit on the seven-member Designations Panel of the Ardant Institute, an atrocity-monitoring organization that publishes an annual report in which certain situations are designated genocide and others are not. Our charter is unusual in one respect: it adopts the 1948 Convention's definition as a floor rather than a ceiling, permits us to go beyond it if we say so expressly and identify the element we are relying on, requires five of seven votes for any designation, and requires each published opinion to state which elements it found. Our advocacy arm uses designations to argue that states party have engaged their obligations to prevent and punish, so a false positive is treated as costly, though the charter never says how costly relative to a false negative. Several of my colleagues hold that we are in practice Lemkin-broad — that the operative standard is his 1944 conception, which reached the destruction of a group's cultural and political foundations and not merely the treaty's enumerated acts against persons. I have come to think that is wrong and that we are running something narrower and more structured, but I cannot state what, and the disagreement is about to matter, because next year's docket includes two cases of wholesale cultural destruction with no killings to speak of.

Our track record to date is these six. (1) The Kessler Highlands: a state campaign against a highland ethnic minority whose territory held the majority of the group's population and all of its clan leadership; systematic killing of men of military age, mass expulsion of the remainder, and broadcast statements by senior officials describing the minority as a growth to be excised. Designated, 6–1. (2) The Marchfeld purges: a regime's liquidation of an urban professional and landowning stratum defined by property and political allegiance, some 300,000 killed over three years, the victims sharing no national, ethnic, racial, or religious identity distinct from the rest of the population. Declined, 7–0, with the opinion stating expressly that the scale of killing was not the difficulty. (3) The Tarnowa residential schools: a colonial administration removed indigenous children to boarding institutions for six decades, prohibited the vernacular, and severed kinship ties; mortality was high but was not the policy's aim and the group persisted demographically. Designated, 5–2, with a dissent calling the designation retrospective moralism. (4) The siege of Orvet: occupying forces demolished a minority quarter's shrines, archive, and cemetery and drove out its 40,000 residents; the population survived, most returned after the war, and the group's institutions were reconstituted abroad. Declined, 6–1, the opinion treating the destruction as evidence bearing on intent but not as an enumerated act against persons, and finding the expulsion, however brutal, short of conditions of life calculated to bring about physical destruction. (5) The Sarn province program: compulsory sterilization and contraceptive insertion of women of childbearing age in a religious minority, criminalization of the group's marriage and naming customs, and separation of children into state boarding facilities, with few killings; the province held most of the group's global population and its holiest sites. Designated, 5–2, after two rounds of debate, the published reasoning stressing that the group's capacity to reproduce itself was being destroyed and that the province was its demographic and religious center rather than an incidental location. (6) The Veltyn famine: requisition quotas set far above harvest killed an estimated 800,000, falling disproportionately on the villages of one national group, which were additionally placed on internal blacklist lists and forbidden to leave; the documents framed the quotas as a means of breaking peasant resistance generally, and some officials' remarks touched on the national question; the group survived and its birth rate recovered within a decade. Declined, 4–3, the majority holding that where a class-based explanation fits the documents at least as well as a group-destructive one, disproportionate impact plus suggestive language does not establish intent to destroy the group as such, the dissent objecting that the majority had imported a standard of proof the charter never mentions.

The situation now before us is the Verdan Valley. A religious minority of roughly 180,000 lives in a border valley; the group totals four million nationally, its diaspora is expanding, and the valley is peripheral to it — none of the group's holy sites, no clergy of rank, and no institutions there that do not also exist elsewhere. Over six years the state has confined all valley residents to a closed zone under a movement-permit regime; has admitted food, fuel, and medicine only through state-controlled channels at levels a United Nations nutrition survey found to be below subsistence for fourteen consecutive months; and has conscripted men of working age into labor battalions with documented mortality near nine percent. What our file turns on is a leaked internal planning memorandum, signed by a deputy interior minister, stating that the objective is the final resolution of the valley question by reducing its population to a manageable residual, with a companion annex listing contraceptive and sterilization measures to be applied to women of the group. The state maintains that the closure is a counter-insurgency measure against an armed group genuinely operating from the valley, and independent reporting confirms the insurgency. Most valley residents have since been permitted to leave and have resettled among co-religionists elsewhere, and the group's national population is larger today than when the closure began.

What I want is an analysis, not references — I know the literature and the case law and will supply them myself. Lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all six precedents, being explicit where a rule survives only because of a particular feature of a particular precedent; then say what each of those rules implies for the valley, and where they part company. Give me your best prediction of the vote and of the reasoning the published opinion will actually contain, including which precedent the majority will most likely distinguish and by what move, and identify the closest analog in the track record with an honest account of the respects in which it is disanalogous. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the valley, state what principle we would be adopting by designating and what principle by declining, which of the two we could live with across the rest of the docket, and what the four-to-three split in the famine case tells us about where the panel's fault line runs. Finally, name the single additional fact about the valley that we could plausibly still gather and that would most sharply discriminate among the surviving rules — and tell me plainly whether my colleagues are right that we are Lemkin-broad, since that is what decides how I should argue the Orvet-type cases coming next year.
