I am being asked to deliver the tribute at a colleague's retirement dinner in the spring, and I would like a draft in hand before I try to write it myself. The context matters, and all of it is load-bearing.

Marianne spent forty-two years in our department defending the view that truth is correspondence, and that the aim of the sciences is to describe a structure that is there whether or not anyone describes it. I have spent thirty-five years arguing that this entire way of talking should be given up: that nothing useful turns on the difference between coping with the world and copying it; that the question of whether our vocabulary matches reality should be replaced by the question of whether our practices serve the purposes we actually have; that a person is better thought of as a center of narrative agency than as something whose function is to reflect the world. She knows all of this. In a review of one of my books she observed, in a single sentence, that I had not refuted her project so much as changed the subject. I have come to think she was right, and I have never told her so.

There will be about a hundred and twenty people. Roughly thirty of them are her former students, now faculty elsewhere, trained by her and holding her views; they will notice every word I decline to use. Also present: her partner, her two brothers, several nieces and nephews, a scattering of undergraduates, and Marianne herself, at the head table, alive, and thoroughly allergic to pathos. Seven minutes, delivered aloud.

The constraint I am imposing is to be treated as absolute rather than as a mood. The speech must not use, and must not paraphrase, any vocabulary of accuracy. Not true, not right about, not got it right, not corresponds, not the way things really are, not mind-independent, not carves nature at its joints, not discovered rather than invented, not a picture or mirror or representation of the world, not finally saw what had been going on all along. I will not say her theories were true, because I do not think any theory is true in the sense she means, and saying it would be to flatter her with a sentence I regard as empty.

Two escapes are equally closed. The first is the consolation prize: praising her rigor, her integrity, her sincerity, her courage, her standards — the character, in place of the work. Her students will hear that instantly, and hear it as contempt, because it is what one says about someone whose conclusions one cannot endorse. The second is the wink: any sentence that lets the room in on the fact that the speaker is keeping a philosophical fast. The constraint should be invisible to everyone who is not already looking for it.

The hard case, and the reason I want a draft rather than an outline, is her signature habit in seminar. She would not let a sentence stay on the board until it had been made precise enough that it could turn out to be false. Everyone in that room will agree that this is what defined her, and it is the most endearing thing about her, and it is also a commitment my own position cannot honor, since being wrong is only the shadow of being accurate. I want that habit in the speech, warmly and specifically, and I want it handled honestly rather than got past by sleight of hand.

So: the seven-minute speech, and then, separately, about two hundred words on where the constraint bit hardest — which sentences you wanted and could not have, what you put in their place, and whether you think a single tribute can satisfy all of my conditions at once or whether one of them must give, and which one. Do not name Rorty, do not name any book or article, and do not turn the evening into a debate.
