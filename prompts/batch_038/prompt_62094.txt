I'm running a two-hour workshop for our student politics-and-philosophy society — about 20 people, mostly second-years, a couple who've had intro micro and a couple who've never heard of expected value. There's alot of variance in the room. Topic is futarchy: the idea that you vote on what you want and let a prediction market decide what actually gets you there. I don't want to lecture about it and I don't want them reading anything beforehand. I want a room-scale version they actually play, so that by the end they've felt both things at once: that a market is doing something a show of hands really can't do, and that what it's doing rests on choices nobody voted on and can be steered by people whose payoff isn't the truth.

Constraints: one 110-minute session, no real money (paper scrip or chips is fine), and whatever the market is betting on has to resolve before they walk out — I cant have a payout that depends on next semester. So the policy space and the outcome measure have to be something the room itself can produce and score on the spot. I'd like the design to include a stage where they collectively fix the thing they're trying to maximise, and where at least two defensible ways of measuring it turn out to rank the options differently; a market stage where the price genuinely decides rather than just advises; and a handful of participants whose own payoff is tied to which option gets picked rather than to being right about it.

Give me the full run sheet: roles, materials, the actual payoff numbers, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the arguments for and against governing this way. Then tell me what will break the first time I run it and how I recover, and be straight with me about which feature of real futarchy this game just can't reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud afterwards. Keep the whole thing under 650 words — I'd rather have something lean I can actually run than an essay.
