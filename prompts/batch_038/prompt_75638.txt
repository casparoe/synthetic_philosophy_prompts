**ESSAY QUESTION**

*Background.* Dr N., an American historian, is spending one academic year — ten months — as a visiting fellow in a city we will call City C. City C's norms differ from N.'s home ones in three respects. (i) Restaurant bills state that service is included, staff are paid a full wage, and the settled local practice is to leave nothing on the table; leaving a large tip is treated as mildly puzzling rather than generous. (ii) At the university coffee cart a queue forms in a single file with no markings and nobody directing it; people stand close together and the order of arrival is strictly respected. (iii) At pedestrian crossings it is expected that one waits for the green signal even when the road is visibly empty and no enforcement is present. N. reflects as follows:

"None of these rules is written down and none of them is enforced by law. Each could easily have been the other way round. Because they are mere conventions they carry no moral weight: where a rule is arbitrary in the sense that some other rule would have served just as well, there can be no obligation to follow it, since an obligation would have to attach to something non-arbitrary. I grant that my failure to tip at home would be a different matter — but only because a server there depends on the money and would be made worse off by my stinginess. That is a fact about harm, not a fact about convention."

(a) [6 marks] Distinguish the sense in which a rule is conventional when it is one of several interchangeable solutions to a coordination problem from the sense in which a rule is conventional when it is a matter of local custom or etiquette. Does N.'s inference from arbitrariness to the absence of obligation depend on treating these as the same thing?

(b) [9 marks] N. concedes that failing to tip at home would be wrong because of the loss it causes. Assess the claim that the wrongness of failing to tip is entirely a matter of that loss and not at all a matter of the convention. Your answer must consider at least one case in which the loss and the convention come apart — for instance a restaurant that pays its staff a proper wage while the tipping practice persists, or a queue in which one person's jumping in costs no one anything measurable — and must say what, if anything, is wrong in that case.

(c) [10 marks] Is N. bound by the crossing norm in City C? N. did not help to make the practice, does not intend to stay beyond the ten months, and has neither been asked to contribute anything nor offered anything in return. State and apply the principle of fair play — roughly, that one who accepts the benefits of a cooperative scheme thereby incurs an obligation to do one's part — and consider the best-known objection to that principle. Pay particular attention to the difference between accepting a benefit and merely receiving one, and to whether that difference does any work in N.'s case.

**THE ANSWER AS SUBMITTED** (complete, 905 words, not an extract)

(a) N. is using "convention" in two distinguishable senses, and the argument depends on moving between them without noticing that it is doing so.

In the first sense a rule is conventional because it settles a coordination problem: which side of the road we drive on, which side of the escalator we stand on, whether the coffee cart's queue is a single file or two. Rules of this kind are arbitrary in the specific sense that some alternative would have done just as well — nothing recommends driving on the right rather than the left in itself. But arbitrariness of this sort is not pointlessness. Once most people are following one of the available regularities, each of us has a reason to follow that same one, because the entire value of the rule lies in its being the one everybody else is following. This is Lewis's picture: a convention is a regularity in behaviour that solves a recurrent coordination problem and that people follow because they expect others to follow it and prefer general conformity to it. In the second sense a rule is conventional because it is a matter of local custom or etiquette: how loudly to talk in a restaurant, whether to bring something to a dinner party, whether to leave money on the table. Rules of this kind are not backed by any independent moral requirement, and breaking them tends to make one ill-mannered rather than to make one a wrongdoer.

N.'s inference is that because the rules in the case could have been otherwise they carry no moral weight. At first sight that is a confusion, since the arbitrariness of a coordination convention says nothing whatever about whether one has a reason to conform to the convention that actually obtains. But the confusion is only apparent. N. is not claiming there is no reason to conform; N. is claiming there is no moral weight, and these come apart. This is in effect Hume's point about the artificial virtues. Justice, promise-keeping and allegiance are artificial in that they exist only because human beings have constructed them; nothing in the nature of things calls for property rules of this shape rather than that. Because such rules are constructed rather than discovered, the obligations they generate are obligations in a secondary, derivative sense — requirements of a device we have found useful — and not obligations in the full moral sense. So N. is right that arbitrariness rules out moral weight, and right that what needs explaining in the tipping case is something other than the convention itself.

(b) N.'s concession about the server at home is well placed, and N. is right that the loss does the moral work. The server is paid partly out of tips; the guest knows this; and by ordering a meal in that establishment the guest enters a transaction in which the server's wage depends on what the guest leaves. There is reliance here, and it is reliance the guest knowingly exploits if she leaves nothing. What is doing the work is not that a rule exists but that a person is made worse off.

It follows, as N. says, that the wrongness is entirely a matter of the loss. This has an attractive consequence: it explains why the norm varies from place to place without either version of the norm being morally required. In City C the staff are paid a full wage and nothing turns on what is left on the table, so there is nobody to wrong; at home there is. The question asks for a case in which loss and convention come apart. Take a restaurant at home that has begun paying its staff a proper wage while the tipping practice persists, so that the money left is a windfall rather than a livelihood; or take a queue at a nearly empty coffee cart where the person who jumps in delays nobody by more than a few seconds. In the first case the server loses nothing she was counting on and in the second nobody is measurably worse off — yet in both the other participants will still resent the deviation, and will express that resentment. But the resentment is evidence about expectations rather than evidence about wrongdoing. People feel the pull of a rule they have internalised and mistake that feeling for a moral perception. So there is nothing wrong in these cases, and N.'s account delivers the right result.

(c) The principle of fair play holds that one who accepts the benefits of a cooperative scheme thereby incurs an obligation to do one's part. Applied to N., the answer is clear. N. walks the pavements of City C, uses its crossings, and benefits every day from the fact that drivers and pedestrians there can predict one another. That predictability is produced by everyone else's waiting, and N. enjoys it without contributing to it. N. is therefore bound by the crossing norm, and bound for the whole of the ten months: N.'s not having helped to make the practice, and not intending to stay, are beside the point, since the benefit accrues to anyone who is present and the obligation attaches to the benefit rather than to the history of the person receiving it.

The best-known objection to the principle is Nozick's. He asks us to imagine a neighbourhood that institutes a system of public entertainment, each household taking its turn at running the broadcast, and his point is that the benefit of an hour's entertainment is not worth the cost of losing a day to providing it; no obligation can arise out of so poor a bargain. If that is the objection it does not touch N.'s case at all. Waiting thirty seconds at an empty crossing is close to costless, and living in a city where traffic and pedestrians can predict one another is not a trivial benefit. N. is bound, and the general lesson is that a visitor is bound by the local norms of any practice from which she benefits, however briefly she stays.
