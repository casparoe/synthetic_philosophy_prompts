I'm running a 75-minute senior seminar on intergenerational justice next month — usually 20-22 students, mostly philosophy with a few econ majors who've already seen the standard discounting math. Twice now I've tried to teach pure time preference by just talking about it, and it flops: they nod at the principle ("mere position in time can't be a morally relevant difference, that's arbitrary") and then turn around and defend discounting the far future on other grounds, and I can't get them to see that the other grounds aren't doing the work they think they are.

So I want them to run into it instead. Design an in-class game that makes the force of pure time preference felt, and — this is the part I care about most — separates it from the things people always say they're really doing when they discount: uncertainty about whether the future payoff arrives, the risk of dying or forgetting before you collect, temptation/weakness of will, and plain inequality (giving to poorer people first).

Here's the apparatus I want you to work with, feel free to adjust the numbers but keep the structure:

- Tokens, no real money. Candy, poker chips, whatever converts to a fraction of a participation point — it has to feel like something is actually lost when they spend it now.
- A compounding pot. Each cohort starts with an inherited pot, decides each round how much to spend now vs. leave in the pot, and whatever's left grows by a known fixed multiple. The last cohort in the chain consumes the whole pot. That terminal round matters: somebody has to eat it, and everyone knows it.
- The reveal is mid-game, not upfront. They should be told before their first choice that the pot was seeded by a previous cohort — but not how that cohort decided, not how much was passed on, and not who they were. Just: someone you'll never meet made a number, and here you are. And then after their own decisions, they get told exactly what that previous group did and what they're about to do to the next group. I want the moment where they realize they're angry at, or grateful to, a nameless stranger for a choice made under conditions identical to their own.
- Pre-registration. Before anything starts, each student writes down, privately, a number: what weight they'd give to the welfare of a stranger a hundred years from now relative to an identical stranger today. Keep the slips. At the end you pull out their own number and compare it to the discount rate their play actually revealed, and you compute that implied rate out loud on the board from the multiplier and what they chose. Watching someone's stated rate and their revealed rate disagree is the whole point of the exercise.

The two-arm thing: run the same game twice with the class split, where in one arm the transfer is deterministic — no risk of the round ending, no chance of forgetting, you are absolutely told you'll be around to collect, and the choice is made once and locked so there's no temptation to reverse it — and in the other arm the standard confounds are switched on (random chance of early termination that wipes unsaved tokens but returns saved ones, re-choosing every round so they can renege). Then put the two implied rates side by side. If the deterministic arm still shows heavy discounting, they've got nowhere to hide. If it doesn't, that's an honest result too and the debrief has to be able to handle it without me looking like I rigged it.

What I need back:

1. The full rules and materials, and a payoff scheme with actual numbers that works with tokens and a class of ~20 — including how many rounds, what the multiplier should be so the choice bites, and how you handle the logistics of the inheritance chain when you only have one class period and no previous cohort to seed from (I assume you fake it or run one cohort as the ancestors and one as the descendants; tell me which and what the fake version costs you).
2. A minute-by-minute for the 75 minutes.
3. A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific claims — which moment is pure time preference, which is uncertainty, which is inequality — and that ends only after they've heard the Sidgwick/Rawls line of argument and can say for themselves whether the game supports it or undercuts it.
4. An honest account of what will predictably break the first time I run this: where students will collude, which round they'll game, who sulks, what happens when the terminal cohort gets greedy and everyone finds out, and what I say to the student who announces they were just playing to maximize their candy and doesn't that prove nothing.
5. A frank note on the feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words — my guess is that a known-length game with a known final round produces something that looks like discounting but is really backward induction, and that's not the same thing as the attitude the concept is supposed to name. Tell me if that's right and how much of it I can paper over.
