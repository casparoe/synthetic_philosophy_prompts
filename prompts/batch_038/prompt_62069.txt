I am writing a novel set largely in a mathematics department, and I need the full text of a speech that occurs in it — the thing itself, as delivered, not a summary or an outline.

The scene. The national society for probability and applied mathematics is holding its centenary banquet, and there, forty-one years late, it will present its medal to Vera Lindqvist, now ninety-one, who in 1979 proved at an isolated technical university, with no colleagues in her field within three hundred miles, a bound on how quickly a large family of iterative approximations converges. Alistair Crane, then a well-connected young man at a famous institute, published the same bound eleven weeks earlier and has carried the credit ever since; he is ninety-three, in a wheelchair at the head table, and has asked to say a few words after the medal is given. The laudatio is delivered by Lindqvist's last doctoral student. The room contains her two sons and their children, three generations of her students, senior people who lived through the priority quarrel, a journalist, and Crane.

Lindqvist has seen the draft and set conditions, which the speech must actually satisfy rather than gesture at:

1. It may not claim she was first.
2. It may not offer the consolation that priority does not matter, or that the result was already in the air and would have come regardless. That is the real insult in her eyes — it says she was replaceable — and it is why she refused the society's first offer of the medal in 1994, in a two-line letter saying the medal was for the theorem and the theorem was not hers to receive.
3. It may use her own word for men like Crane, whom she always called co-witnesses rather than rivals, but only as she meant it: a demand for a second signature on something already true, not a formula for making peace. She never made peace and has said so on the record.
4. Crane did nothing wrong. The speech must find what actually went wrong without inventing a wrongdoer, and must say it in front of him.
5. Her sons and grandchildren are not mathematicians. The bound must be conveyed by one image that is accurate and requires no mathematics, and the speech must not curdle into a lecture.

Five to six minutes out loud. It should end by turning to Crane and then to the room — not with gratitude or an appeal for applause, either of which she would have despised, but with something she would have allowed to be true.
