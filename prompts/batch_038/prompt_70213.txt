For an upcoming episode of my philosophy podcast, my guest is a scholar of ancient ethics whose recent book argues that the discussion of wine in the first two books of the Laws is not a concession an aging Plato makes to pleasure but a deliberate piece of civic technology. On her reading, the Athenian Stranger treats wine as a drug in the full sense: citizens are to be given graded, supervised doses of the very thing that undoes self-command so that they acquire a settled tolerance to pleasure rather than a mere ignorance of it. She holds that this is the hinge of the dialogue's whole theory of education, since education there is defined as the correct training of pleasure and pain in advance of reason.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions for her, each paired with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give; state briefly what you take that reply to be. I want questions that put an argument to her rather than invite exposition, so none of them should ask her to restate her thesis, to walk us through a passage, to position herself in the secondary literature, or to supply Stephanus references. The sequence should also build: at least two of the later questions must be framed so that she cannot retreat into saying the image is not meant to carry argumentative weight or that a given remark is merely dramatic decoration.

Among the topics I want covered, at minimum:

1. The thought experiment about a hypothetical drug that would induce fear. If such a drug existed, the Stranger suggests, a lawgiver would use it in small doses on willing subjects rather than expose citizens to real dangers, since the test would be cheap and safe. Wine works in the opposite direction. Why does the analogy survive that reversal? Exposure to a fear-producing drug is aversive, whereas the state wine produces is pleasant, and graduated exposure to a pleasant impairment looks at least as likely to entrench the appetite as to inoculate against it.

2. The supervision requirement. The whole benefit of the drinking party is made to depend on its being led by someone who stays sober. Who tests and trains him, and what becomes of the argument in a city that cannot reliably supply such people — does the view then collapse into prohibition, or does it work only in a city already close to the ideal?

3. The puppet image. Man is pulled by hard iron cords of pleasure and pain and by a single soft golden cord of calculation, which he must himself pull along with. Does that image license the tolerance model, given that wine strengthens the iron cords and slackens the golden one at precisely the moment the training is supposed to take place?

4. The age-graded restrictions: wine withheld from the young, rationed through early adulthood, and only later opened up as a Dionysiac privilege, with wine then described as a remedy for the rigidity of old age. What account of development does that schedule presuppose, and is the rule about the body, about eligibility for office, or about moral formation? What makes the same substance a danger at one age and a medicine at another?

5. The Dorian setting. Her interlocutors defend Cretan and Spartan institutions designed to harden citizens against pain, and the Stranger's question is whether those institutions train equally well against pleasure. Does her reading make wine-training the completion of that project or a subversion of it, given that the Spartans restrict drinking and that the Stranger is dismissive of the lawgiver whose only response to a dangerous practice is to forbid it outright?

6. The relation to the Republic. Is the difference between the supervised symposium of the Laws and the education of the guardians a change of view about human nature, about what a second-best city can achieve, or about the reach of law as an instrument?

7. The portrait of Socrates in the Symposium, who drinks everyone under the table and emerges unaffected. Does that support her model, with immunity as the end point of training, or embarrass it, since nothing suggests his immunity was acquired by dosing?

8. Whether the pharmacological vocabulary she brings to the text — dose, tolerance, exposure, inoculation — is doing explanatory work or merely flattering it. What, if anything, is lost by dropping it and reading the Stranger as concerned with convivial bonding and the friendship of older citizens rather than with the formation of character?

The final question should ask what reading of the text or what piece of evidence would count against her position, and it should be specific enough that a pledge of further philological work is not an acceptable answer.
