In a graduate seminar on the philosophy of cognitive science, we spent a session on Dennett's account of the three stances and his claim that beliefs are real patterns rather than merely convenient fictions. The discussion kept returning to the comparison he draws between beliefs and the centers of gravity of physical objects, and our instructor pressed it into service on a case he had worked on in industry: a large deployed recommendation system. His reconstruction ran as follows. When an engineer says the system believes that a user who has just looked at tents is about to shop for sleeping bags, that ascription has exactly the status of saying that a boomerang's center of gravity lies outside the material of the boomerang: it is an abstraction from a lower-level description, it is not an extra item in the inventory of the world, it cannot be found by inspecting the weights and activations, and yet it is not arbitrary either, since some such ascriptions predict the system's behavior far better than any alternative. From this he drew a deflationary conclusion about a debate our department has been having for two years: if intentional ascriptions to such a system are on all fours with center-of-gravity ascriptions, then the question whether the system really has beliefs, as opposed to being merely describable as if it did, has no further fact to settle it and is therefore verbal. Anyone who insists there is a deep matter of fact has simply reified an abstraction.

Two participants objected on different grounds. The first said the analogy proves too much for the instructor's purposes: the position of a center of gravity is fully and uniquely determined by the mass distribution, so if beliefs really are like centers of gravity, what a system believes is just as determinate as its parameters and its behavioral dispositions make it — which is the opposite of the no-further-fact conclusion, and would license real disagreement about particular ascriptions. The second said the analogy quietly imports a feature Dennett cannot use: a center of gravity is definable from the lower level by formula, whereas belief ascription is an idealizing, normatively constrained, and holistically underdetermined interpretation, so there is no function from the physical description to the beliefs, and the analogy hides precisely the gap it is supposed to bridge. A third remarked, more briefly, that the analogy also seems to sit at the wrong level, since a center of gravity is an abstraction over the physical description of one object, while intentional ascriptions abstract from behavioral and design-level facts and are supposed to be assessed against a standard of rationality that mass distributions do not have.

Could you audit this analogy thoroughly rather than split the difference? Specifically, I would like:

1. An explicit mapping — ideally a table — saying what in the center-of-gravity case corresponds to what in belief ascription: the mass distribution, the formula, the point itself, the predictive success of treating an object as if all its mass were concentrated there, the fact that the point may lie outside the object, and so on. Say which of these correspondences is supposed to do the philosophical work, and what work it is: objectivity without added ontology, lossy compression, explanatory indispensability, or something else.

2. The disanalogies, sorted into three piles: those that genuinely threaten the instructor's deflationary conclusion, those that instead threaten the stronger claim that beliefs are real patterns in the compression-and-prediction sense, and those that sound weighty in seminar but are beside the point for either purpose. For each, say briefly why it lands in that pile.

3. An account of what the source domain smuggles in — in particular whether it presupposes determinacy, uniqueness, derivability by definition rather than by interpretation, absence of normativity, and the absence of any first-person or self-ascription asymmetry — and whether the target lacks each of these.

4. A verdict on each of the four positions in the room, graded rather than merely described: the instructor's deflationism, the determinacy objection, the definition-versus-interpretation objection, and the level-mismatch remark. Say how much of each survives once the mapping is laid out, and whether the deflationary conclusion depends on the analogy at all or could be defended independently of it.

5. An assessment of rival source domains. Some of us floated alternatives on the way out: the equator, temperature, a biological species, the evaluation of a chess position, and the legal fiction of the reasonable person. Say for each what it does better and worse than the center of gravity, and then either nominate the best of them or propose a source domain of your own, explaining what philosophical work your choice carries that the center of gravity cannot.
