The answer was submitted by a first-year MA student and runs as follows:

---

The two-dimensional framework begins from the observation that a sentence can be evaluated in two different ways. If we ask what the sentence says at a world other than the actual one, holding fixed what our words actually refer to, we get the secondary intension: a function from possible worlds to truth values. If instead we ask what the sentence would say had our epistemic situation been different, we get the primary intension: a function from scenarios, which we may think of as centered possible worlds, to truth values. The primary intension is meant to capture what a competent speaker can know a priori about her own words, and it is therefore always contingent, since for any scenario whatsoever, the actual world might have turned out to be that scenario.

Take water is H2O. Its primary intension is roughly the claim that the dominant, clear, drinkable liquid around here is H2O. That claim comes out false at the XYZ-scenario, the scenario in which the stuff filling the lakes and rivers is a different compound with the same superficial profile, so the identity is not knowable a priori and establishing it required chemistry. Its secondary intension, by contrast, comes out true at every world, because once the reference of water has been fixed by the actual watery stuff, the term designates that very stuff rigidly in every world in which it exists. Hence the necessary a posteriori: a proposition necessary along the second dimension and knowable only along the first. The mirror image is the contingent a priori, for instance Kaplan's example of the speaker who says that she is here now, whose primary intension is satisfied at every centered world at which it is evaluated and which may still come out false at a world where the speaker has moved on.

I want to add an illustration of my own, because I think the standard case hides how much work the framework is doing. Consider a pre-scientific smelting community whose term calorite is introduced by something like: whatever it is in a metal that makes it swell when warmed. The primary intension of calorite is satisfied at a scenario in which the swelling is caused by a subtle fluid pervading the metal, and equally at a scenario in which it is caused by the agitation of parts too small to see. Both scenarios are live for the community, as far as it can tell a priori. Now suppose the second is the actual one, so that calorite is mean molecular kinetic energy is true. That identity is necessary, since calorite rigidly designates whatever the actual swelling-causer turns out to be. But the framework delivers more than this: because the fluid scenario satisfies the primary intension of the negation, there is a genuine metaphysical possibility in which calorite is the subtle fluid, and that, rather than the community's mere ignorance about which world is actual, is what makes the identity a posteriori.

This is where the account earns its keep. Kripke's own apparatus of reference-fixing descriptions is really a two-dimensional one: the description fixes the reference without giving the meaning, and the necessity of the identity is then a fact about the secondary intension. What Kripke cannot explain, on the two-dimensionalist's diagnosis, is why the necessary a posteriori should feel contingent at all, or why conceivability should seem to track possibility so reliably along the first dimension. Primary intensions give a uniform answer: conceivability tracks primary possibility, and primary possibility is a real kind of possibility, so the intuition that things could have been otherwise is never simply an illusion. That is a substantive explanatory gain, and it is why one should be a two-dimensionalist rather than a Kripkean who happens to make use of descriptions.
