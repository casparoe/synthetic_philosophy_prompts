I am auditing a graduate decision-theory seminar this term and wrote a short position paper on satisficing that I have since been unable to defend adequately. The position has three parts, and I would like to know what would count against each of them. First: the familiar way of domesticating Simon's idea — treat deliberation as costly, fold the cost of search into the utility function, and show that stopping at the first option clearing an aspiration level is what an optimizer would do anyway — does not vindicate satisficing but abolishes it, since on that reconstruction there is only maximizing under a richer description of the agent's ends. Second, and consequently: satisficing is a distinct normative standard rather than a report of what cognitively limited agents are compelled to settle for, so that there are decision problems in which following an aspiration-level rule is the right response and in which the maximizing rule — however generously we specify its inputs, including computational cost and the risk of deliberation-induced error — is either undefined or does worse. Third, my reason for thinking the collapse back into maximizing fails: aspiration levels are set by the agent's history, by how the agent takes the problem to be framed, and by comparison with a status quo, rather than by any calculation over the value of further information, which makes them non-derivable from the agent's preferences in the way a cost-adjusted maximizing rule always is. My instructor's written response amounted to one line — that this is simply maximizing with a badly specified utility function — and roughly half the students in the program agree with him while the other half say he is begging the question. I cannot tell which side is right, and that is what I want your help with.

So: what would have to be true for this view to be false? I want the theoretical results, the conceptual arguments, and the qualitative shapes of empirical finding that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, with a note for each about whether a committed defender of my position would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it, and on what grounds. I also want the parts of the view that are separable: if one leg gives way, which of the others can survive, and at what cost. Please include at least one attack that refuses my framing altogether — something from the ecological-rationality or fast-and-frugal tradition, or from the rational-inattention tradition, or from the psychological literature on maximizing and satisficing as dispositions — that does not grant my premise that the interesting question is whether aspiration levels are exogenous. And confront the third part directly: is the claim that no optimization can reproduce aspiration-level behavior something I could ever establish, or does it demand an impossibility proof I am not entitled to expect, given that a determined opponent can always insist that the aspiration-formation rule be pushed inside the utility function? If the position has thereby been qualified into safety, tell me whether that is a genuine defect for a normative thesis about rationality or an acceptable feature of one. The single thing that would move me most is a concrete decision problem, specified in enough detail that I could check the arithmetic myself, in which the aspiration-level rule is dominated by the best available cost-adjusted maximizing rule. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I cannot chase sources at the moment and would much rather have the arguments stated fully enough that I could reconstruct them on my own.

Please organize the response under these headings, in this order: Bottom line (three or four sentences naming the most serious threat to the view); Restatement and ambiguities (about 150 words, telling me what you take the position to be and flagging any reading on which it comes out trivially true or trivially false); Decisive defeaters (about 450 words, ranked); Awkward but absorbable (about 300 words, i.e. what a defender should concede while surviving); Attacks that refuse my framing (about 300 words, from at least two rival traditions); The insulation question (about 250 words, on whether the non-derivability claim is unfalsifiable and whether that matters here); A testable divergence (about 200 words, sketching what the view predicts that a cost-adjusted maximizing view does not, in terms an experimentalist could design around); and Confidence (about 120 words). In that final section, state how confident you are in the main claims you have made, identify which of your objections is doing the most load-bearing work, and single out the one point in the whole response you are least sure of, explaining what would settle it.
