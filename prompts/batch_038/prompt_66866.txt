I serve on the nine-member advisory committee of a national official-history program. Eleven years ago Parliament commissioned a three-volume history of the Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme, the state-backed farm lending program that ran from 1974 to 1981 and ended in a write-off equivalent to roughly two percent of GDP, a collapsed regional lending network, and one ministerial resignation. Volume II is now in draft and contains two chapters that cannot both stand as written. The first, by an economic historian, reconstructs the 1976 decision to raise the guarantee ceiling using only documents dated before that decision, and concludes that the officials could not reasonably have foreseen what followed. The second, by a political historian, narrates the collapse and then explains it, and turns on the claim that the warning signs were in the record the whole time. We meet in three weeks and must issue a directive to the volume's editor: whether to impose a single evidentiary protocol across the volume, which chapter's approach governs where they conflict, and what to do about a request from the sponsoring ministry for an additional chapter — provisionally titled something like "What Should Have Been Apparent at the Time" — which it wants tabled at a standing parliamentary inquiry into a current, unrelated credit guarantee scheme that sits in the spring. The committee is split four-four with the chair abstaining, and the chair has asked me for the tie-breaking memo, partly because I was a junior examiner at the Treasury in the late seventies myself, which is both why I was asked and why three members think I should have recused.

The memo needs to hold apart two things both factions have collapsed. Hindsight bias, as I understand it, is a distortion in a probability judgment: knowing the outcome, we overestimate what the outcome would have looked like beforehand, and the remedy is some discipline on the assessor's information state. The narrative fallacy is a different failure with a different remedy: an account can be scrupulously calibrated about what was knowable and still make the actual outcome the only coherent terminus, because the ordering, the selection of protagonists, and the choice of where to begin the story all answer to the ending. It follows that a strict "information-set protocol" — no consideration may be attributed to an actor unless it appears in material dated before the decision — treats the second failure as though it were the first, and I suspect it fails at the joint, because blinding the evidence does not blind the selection of which evidence counts as a warning sign; the historian who knows how things ended decides, in light of the ending, which pre-decision memoranda were prescient and which were noise. I want the memo to address at least two constructive devices that respond to this rather than to the cruder problem. One is a denominator requirement: any claim that a warning went unheeded must be set against a register of comparable warnings issued in the same period that turned out to be nothing, so the reader can see what ignoring warnings usually bought. The other is a symmetry test: the editor asks of each explanatory claim whether it would have been offered, in the same terms, had the scheme succeeded — and where the answer is no, the chapter must say what is doing the work besides the outcome. Finally, the memo should take a position on a point the ministry seems not to have noticed: a foreseeability claim is a modal claim about the availability of evidence, not a frequency claim, and the same sentence that functions in Volume II as an explanation will function in the inquiry's hands as a finding of culpability. I do not know whether one sentence can serve both, and I would rather the committee decide that explicitly than discover it in the press.

Against all of this stands an objection I cannot answer, and I want it argued with rather than acknowledged. The claim is not merely that retrospective narrative reads better. It is that retrospective structure is constitutive of historical understanding: that "what the actors could reasonably have foreseen" is itself a category we construct from where we now stand, and that a chronicler confined to the moment cannot write history at all, since the sentences that make an account historical — that this was the beginning of the collapse, that this meeting was the last real chance — are precisely the sentences no witness could utter. On that view an information-set protocol does not purge hindsight; it relocates it into the choice of frame and the choice of endpoint, and produces a text that is less candid about its own standpoint than the political historian's chapter, which at least narrates from where it actually stands. The memo should be roughly twelve hundred words, addressed to the committee, with the recommendation stated in the first paragraph rather than built up to: adopt the protocol, reject it, or adopt it under conditions you specify, together with the rule or test the editor applies chapter by chapter when the two approaches conflict, a decision and a deadline for the ministry's requested chapter that accounts for the spring inquiry, and a genuine response to the constitutive-retrospection objection — either an answer or a concession that changes the recommendation, not a paragraph of throat-clearing. Please do not include footnotes, a bibliography, or named citations; refer to positions, arguments, and schools of thought as such, and I will supply the references myself, since invented ones would be worse than none.
