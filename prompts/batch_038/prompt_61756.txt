I am preparing a background memorandum for a national legislature's digital markets committee. The committee has adopted an unusual working method: rather than reasoning from first principles, it treats the final chapter of Mill's On Liberty — the chapter headed Applications — as a body of precedent, and reasons by analogy from the rulings Mill actually delivers there. I have reduced that chapter to the following docket, and the committee has agreed to be bound by it.

1. Restraints on trade are restraints, and restraint as such is an evil; but the state may nonetheless control the processes of production for such ends as preventing fraud and adulteration, enforcing sanitary precautions, and protecting workpeople in dangerous occupations.
2. Restrictions on the sale of poison are objectionable because they infringe the liberty of the buyer rather than that of the seller; a register of sales, obliging the seller to record who bought what and to what stated purpose, is a legitimate middle course that stops short of prohibition.
3. Drunkenness as such may not be punished, but a soldier or a policeman may be punished for being drunk on duty, and a man whose drinking reliably issues in violence toward others may be laid under a special disability.
4. The state may decline to sanction a public gambling house, and may limit the number of premises at which drink is sold, on the ground that in such trades the seller's profit is bound up with the buyer's excess — while gambling and drinking conducted privately remain nobody's business but the participant's own.
5. A man about to cross a bridge known to be unsafe may be seized and turned back if there is no time to warn him, on the ground that he does not desire to fall into the river; but where there is time to warn him, he must be warned and then permitted to go.
6. An agreement by which a person sells himself into slavery is null and void, since the case for freedom does not extend to a freedom to alienate one's freedom.
7. Parents may be compelled to educate their children, though the state must not become the sole educator; and certain services — giving evidence in a court, bearing a fair share in the common defence, rescuing a person in danger — may be compelled even though no one has contracted for them.

The matter now before the committee is this. A firm operates a free messaging and short-video application. Its entire revenue comes from advertising sold against attention, and its recommender is continuously retrained to maximize predicted time in application. Nothing is concealed: the terms state prominently that the feed is personalized in order to prolong sessions, that this is how the service is paid for, and that the user may leave at any time, export all of his data, and have the account deleted within thirty days at no charge. There is no subscription, no minimum term, no renewal, and no exit fee, and the firm is required by law to interoperate with its rivals, so that leaving costs the user no relationships and no history. Aggregate harms are documented — measurable sleep loss and a rise in reported compulsive use — but no individual has been deceived, and users surveyed after two years of use say that they understand all of this and would choose the application again. The committee must rule on whether the state may (a) require the firm to adopt a different recommender objective, (b) require it to offer, alongside the default, a mode in which the feed is not personalized, (c) permit it to restrict access for individual users identified from their own usage data as being at high risk of compulsive use, or (d) leave the arrangement alone.

I would like four things. First: which of the seven rulings is the closest analog, and why each of the others sits further away — I want the comparisons made one by one, since at least two of them appear to pull in opposite directions on these facts, and one of them may look applicable while in fact being foreclosed by the stipulations. Second: what the committee should decide if it is to remain consistent with itself, with the reasoning that carries it from the chosen precedent to the remedy. Third: if the docket genuinely underdetermines the case, say so plainly, and state what principle the committee would be legislating for all future cases by each of the available choices. Fourth: the single smallest alteration to the stipulated facts that would force the candidate principles apart, together with what each of them would then say about the altered case.

One caution about what will not serve. It will not advance the memorandum to observe that this is paternalism, or to ask whether the conduct is self-regarding or other-regarding. The committee's difficulty is precisely that the effects fall partly on the user and partly on others, and that the firm's interest is neither the user's interest nor any third party's. The analogical work has to be done.
