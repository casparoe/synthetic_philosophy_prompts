I teach a graduate seminar on the semantics of modality and I also sit on the committee that sets our department's qualifying exam in philosophy of language. This year the exam will carry one compulsory essay question on two-dimensional semantics, written closed-book in a three-hour sitting, and the drafting has fallen to me. I want to avoid the question the literature practically writes for you — explain how the primary/secondary intension distinction handles the necessary a posteriori — because at least half the cohort could recite the epistemic-versus-subjunctive story from memory, hit every keyword, and collect most of the marks without ever demonstrating that they can operate the framework. Three of my five seminar students have real training in formal semantics and one is coming across from cognitive science, so the question should not presuppose notation beyond what is printed on the paper, but it should be technically exacting rather than essayistic. I also spent an hour arguing with a colleague about whether centered worlds belong on a qualifying exam at all, and lost, so I would like the question to put them to work rather than merely mention them.

The shape I have in mind is this, though I would welcome a sharper crux if you can see one. Candidates are given a single sentence with an indexical built into it — something of the form *the liquid in the lakes here is H₂O*, or a phenomenal analogue such as *this experience is a brain state* — together with a short list of scenarios to evaluate it against: an ordinary counterfactual world in which the lakes contain a chemically different compound; a Twin-Earth-style world *considered as actual*, in which the watery role is filled by something other than H₂O; and at least one pair of scenarios that differ only in the center, so that candidates must confront why the first dimension is naturally construed as a function from centered worlds while the second is not, once the context of utterance has been fixed. They should be required to assign primary and secondary truth values scenario by scenario, read off what the resulting pattern implies about the sentence's modal and epistemic status, and then face the hard part: whether the same pattern, if it recurs for the phenomenal sentence, licenses the inference from primary possibility to secondary possibility on which the anti-physicalist use of the framework depends — or whether the metasemantic question of *how* a primary intension is supposed to be fixed in the first place (by a description the speaker could articulate, by the concept's behaviour under ideal rational reflection, by some canonical conceptual role, or by nothing determinate at all) blocks that inference before it gets started. I would also like the question to make candidates say something about whether a Stalnaker-style employment of the same two-dimensional apparatus, on which the second parameter tracks what is asserted in the face of contextual uncertainty rather than what is a priori accessible, is a genuine rival theory or merely a different job description for an identical formalism — since that contrast seems to me to be where a first-class candidate shows they understand what the framework is being asked to do.

Please deliver four things. First, the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including any definitions, scenario list, or stipulations supplied to candidates, calibrated so that a strong candidate can complete it in roughly eighty minutes of the three hours. Second, a model answer of about 800 words. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer must contain — I would expect the correct classification of each scenario, an explicit statement of why the centering parameter is needed for one dimension and dispensable for the other, and a precise diagnosis of the step at which the passage from primary to secondary possibility can fail — with marks attached to each, and, for every must-hit point, a one-line description of the error that most commonly costs marks there. Fourth, two or three sentences saying what separates an A answer from a B answer, in terms of moves rather than polish. One constraint throughout: do not build the question around expounding or citing any particular author's text. The exam is closed book, candidates are entitled to argue from the concepts themselves, and I do not want marks turning on who they name.
