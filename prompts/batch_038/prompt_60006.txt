I'm in an AI alignment seminar and for our problem set we built a toy IRL agent that infers a human's reward from their choices. Two people in my group got into a real argument about the smoker case and I honestly couldn't referee it.

The setup: the human says they want to quit smoking but buys cigarettes most weeks, and the agent has to decide what reward to infer. Dev argued that treating the human as a noisy optimizer of their true preferences is basically unfalsifiable — any reward at all can explain any behavior if you crank the noise high enough, so the model can conclude the human 'really' wants to quit, start overriding them, and nothing in the data can stop it. Mara said no: the noise level is estimated, not chosen, and a model that needs a huge softmax temperature to fit gets punished in the Bayesian evidence, plus you can test the inferred reward out of sample on held-out choices. Dev said out-of-sample prediction can't settle it, because a bias that's stable in time predicts its own future instances perfectly — someone who systematically overweights the present will keep failing to follow through and the model will call every failure correctly, so predictive accuracy is fully compatible with serving the wrong preference. Mara conceded that but said thats a different problem, about *which* preference object is the target (the one that drives behavior vs. the one the person endorses on reflection), not a failure of identifiability. Dev said that concession is the whole ballgame: if you need reflective endorsement to pick the target, then behavior alone was never enough, and the claim that IRL infers preferences from behavior is oversold.
