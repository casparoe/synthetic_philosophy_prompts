I sit on the review board that signs off on incident investigations for a regional water utility. Our charter requires that every report the board accepts name 'the root cause' — singular — and attach at least one corrective action to it. Before us now is the report on last summer's thirty-six-hour untreated discharge. The investigators identified five contributing factors: calibration drift in the effluent turbidity sensor, a bypass valve left in manual after maintenance, an alarm downgraded from critical to advisory two years earlier in response to nuisance complaints, a shift-change staffing gap at the plant, and a deferred capital project that would have provided a second treatment train. They then ran counterfactual checks of their own, and the result is one the board has not seen before: on their analysis, eliminating any one of the five would not have prevented the discharge, since the remaining four still carried it, while eliminating any two would have prevented it. No factor is a but-for cause, and none is individually sufficient.

The board is split. Two members want the report returned until a root cause is properly identified. A third argues that returning it will simply produce an arbitrary designation — whichever factor is cheapest to fix or least embarrassing to management — and that we should accept the report and record the finding as a set. General counsel has added, in writing, that an external reader may take a five-factor finding to mean that no one is responsible for anything.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before Friday's meeting. It should recommend one course of action — return the report, accept it as written, or accept it with an amended finding and a proposed charter change — with a concrete time frame, and it should include the actual wording of the finding if your recommendation involves one. Address explicitly what the corrective-action requirement should attach to in a case of this shape, given that no single action would have prevented the event. And give the strongest available case for retaining the singular root-cause rule, including counsel's accountability worry and the thought that a set-valued finding can amount to a shrug; then explain why your recommendation survives that case rather than merely conceding it.
