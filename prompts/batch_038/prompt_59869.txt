I need to write a speech, and I want to state the situation and the constraints exactly, because every consolation that would normally be available at a lectern like this one is unavailable to me.

**The occasion.** In six weeks the Halloway bell foundry will hold a single ceremony that does two things at once: it retires Emil Varga, who has been their master tuner for forty-eight years, and it commissions the automated tuning lathe that the owners have installed to take over the position. About a hundred and fifty people will stand in the tuning hall — his wife and two daughters; the three apprentices who stayed; the shop steward; a dozen cathedral and municipal clients; the owners; the software team; a local paper that will transcribe everything. Sitting with the software team is the engineer who ran the project, who was himself Varga's apprentice for six years before he left the trade.

**The work, so that you don't have to guess at it.** Bell tuning is subtractive and irreversible. A cast bell's five lowest partials — hum, prime, tierce, quint, nominal — are brought into relation with one another by cutting metal away in annular rings from the inside, and the partials are coupled: taking metal off in one place moves more than one of them, so no partial can be fixed in isolation. There is no undo. Varga has done this by ear and by hand for forty-eight years and, by the foundry's count, on something like thirty-four hundred bells. The new system scans the casting, records it, and outputs a cutting plan that the lathe executes. It was trained on the foundry's own archive: decades of Varga's measurements, his cut sequences, and the bells that resulted.

**The last item on the program is a ringing.** Two bells cast from the same bronze, hung side by side — one tuned by hand by Varga in his final month, one tuned by the machine under his former apprentice's supervision. The room will hear both. The foundry had a local choral society do a blind listening beforehand and the result was, so far as I can tell, a coin flip. Varga does not know this. The engineer does. I do.

**Who I am in this.** An old friend of thirty years, an acoustician by training, and — the part that needs careful handling — the person who advised the company on how to restructure the archive so that it could be trained on. I get one sentence about that. Not a confession, not an apology, not a paragraph: one sentence, and then I move on. If the speech becomes about my feelings regarding my own role, I have failed.

**The constraint that makes this hard.** I have spent twenty-five years inside Dennett's argument about the inversions — Darwin's, where design of great subtlety is produced by a process with no conception of the end; Turing's, where a machine delivers the results of reasoning without reasons; and the one we are now living inside, where a system trained on a craftsman's outputs reproduces the outputs without ever having had whatever it was the craftsman had. So I do not believe the things everyone else would say at that lectern, and I will not say them. The speech must not assert or imply any of the following:

- that the machine lacks understanding, or appreciation, or a soul, and that this is what the difference consists in;
- that it cannot really do the work and is merely imitating it;
- that his judgment is irreplaceable in principle, or that some residue of the task will always need a human;
- that the machine is only a tool in his hands;
- that his bells sound better, or that a listener could pick them out, or that the human touch is audible in the metal.

Nor can it do the mirror-image thing. It must not be readable as a slur on the machine or on the people who built it, one of whom learned the craft from the man being retired. And it must not be a eulogy for the trade: Halloway's will go on making bells.

Varga is proud. He has said he will walk out of the hall if he is told he has been reproduced. That is a genuine constraint on the surface of the speech though not, I think, on its content — he is not stupid, he knows exactly why the ceremony is happening, and he would much rather be addressed honestly than flattered. But he must not be made to stand in front of his family and listen to himself described as a data set.

**Material you may use.** He cannot explain how he does it, and he becomes angry when apprentices ask. The nearest he comes is a standing line I have certainly garbled: something about his hands finding out before he does. He trains people by making them strike a bell and say what they heard, ten thousand times, with almost no instruction — which has always struck me as precisely the process that produced him. He works at five in the morning, before the foundry wakes, in a hall cold enough to see your breath, and he listens with his whole body rather than his head. Invent further physical detail freely, but keep it consistent with what I have given and do not add technical specifics you would have to look up.

**What I want.** A speech of roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words, to be read aloud by a sixty-year-old non-performer in a cold hall with bad acoustics: short lines, no nested clauses, nothing that requires a listener to hold a distinction in mind across two sentences. No philosophical vocabulary at all — the words inversion, competence, comprehension, crane, skyhook, tacit, emergence, and qualia do not appear, and neither does any name I have mentioned in this paragraph above the one. If the ideas are doing their work, nobody in that hall should be able to tell the speech has a metaphysics. One moment of humour that the software team will get completely and everyone else will receive as warmth. No stage directions beyond two bracketed cues at most.

And the hardest requirement: the speech comes immediately before the two bells are rung. It must not tell the room what it is about to hear, must not warn it, must not ask it to listen for anything, and must not turn the ringing into a verdict — and yet it must leave everyone in that hall, Varga included, able to hear both bells without humiliation. Since the sound cannot be the ground of the honour being paid, something else has to be, and the speech has to build it before the first bell is struck.

After the speech, give me at most a hundred and fifty words saying what you chose as that ground, and why it is genuinely available to someone with my metaphysics rather than being one of the ruled-out consolations in disguise.
