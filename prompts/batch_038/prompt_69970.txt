Context first: I do QA at a company that makes lab equipment, and last month an auditor dinged us because two techs had run the same calibration two different ways. Management's fix was to rewrite the whole procedure binder, and that set off an argument between two of my coworkers — Nadia, who wrote most of the original SOPs, and Bo, who came over from an ML team about a year ago and has opinions. It started out practical and then went sideways into logic, and I've been trying to figure out who was right for like three weeks now. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

1. Bo said the binder rewrite is a trap, because you can't fix "they applied the procedure differently" by adding more procedure — every procedure needs a further procedure for applying it, and so on forever. He said this is just Lewis Carroll's tortoise story from the 1890s: the tortoise accepts both premises about the triangle, accepts every conditional Achilles writes into the notebook, and still refuses to grant the conclusion, so the notebook just grows without end.
2. Nadia said that's a cheap trick with an obvious fix. Modus ponens is a rule, not a premise — Achilles' mistake was writing the conditional down at all. Inside a formal system you just have the rule, and there's nothing for the tortoise to ask about.
3. Bo said shes just relabeled the problem: the tortoise can happily accept the rule and still ask why applying it *here*, to this case, is correct. And anyway his practical point stands — what makes two techs apply the SOP the same way is training and habit, not the text, so the binder is decoration.
4. Nadia said he's changed the subject. That training does the causal work is perfectly compatible with the text doing the normative work. A regress that stays inside one category — premises licensing premises — is vicious, but a regress that terminates in something of a different kind, like a disposition, isn't vicious at all. That's just where explanation bottoms out.
5. Bo said "bottoms out in a disposition" is the concession, not the answer, because a habit can't ground an ought. If correctness just is what we're trained to do, then the two techs weren't doing anything differently-*wrong*, they were just differently trained, and there's nothing left to audit. So either she keeps the normativity and eats the regress, or she stops the regress and loses the normativity.
6. Nadia said the squeeze only works if every step of justification has to be mediated by something you can state out loud, and that requirement isn't neutral — it's a picture of what reasoning has to be, not a rule everyone agreed to. She added that nobody can hold infinitely many beliefs, so the regress can't even get started in a real head.
7. Bo said the finite-minds bit is irrelevant, since the regress is logical rather than psychological, which I think he's right about. Then he said the same structure is why you can't get a model to generalize by piling on explicit rules, and that the assumption that the next case resembles this one is a premise you can't justify without already using it.
8. Nadia said fine, but that the induction case is *different and worse*, not the same: with deduction the rule can be built into the system, whereas there's no system to build "the future resembles the past" into. So if Bo thinks the two problems are identical he's wrong, and if he thinks they're different then his SOP argument loses its teeth.
9. Bo said that proves his point anyway, and then went further than I could follow: that Carroll basically showed logic is psychology, so the auditor's finding was meaningless because there's no fact of the matter about whether the techs followed the procedure.

So: referee this for me, move by move, not just overall. For each numbered step tell me who's right and how right — clearly right, mostly right, half right, or wrong — with the reasoning. In particular I want to know whether Nadia's rule-vs-premise distinction in 2 actually stops the tortoise or just renames him, whether Bo's habit/normativity squeeze in 5 is a genuine dilemma or whether there's an escape hatch she never reached for, whether anything in 6 survives Bo's reply, and whether move 8's claim that induction is a *harder* version of the same problem is right or whether it's a distinction without a difference. Then give me an overall verdict on who came out ahead, and tell me what both of them missed — I have a nagging feeling there was a third option sitting on the table that neither one named, but I can't articulate it, and I'd rather you said it than me guess.
