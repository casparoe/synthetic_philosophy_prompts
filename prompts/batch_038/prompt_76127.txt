I am a third-year doctoral student in a lab that collects first-person reports alongside psychophysical measures — detection thresholds, forced-choice discrimination, confidence ratings — and our PI is drafting the analysis plan for a preregistered study. She wants a written criterion, stated in advance, for deciding which trials' reports enter the report dataset and which get set aside as performance artifacts. I have been working through Dennett's heterophenomenology as the philosophical frame for this, because it promises that a subject's own reports are data to be explained rather than privileged testimony about a private theater, and because that promise seems to say I should keep almost everything. In practice I keep almost nothing consistently. Here are my actual verdicts.

1. A patient with a scotoma in his left field denies seeing anything there on every trial, yet his orientation discriminations within the scotoma run well above chance. I keep the denials as data, and I do not let the above-chance guessing demote them; the guessing goes in a separate column entirely.
2. A subject reports, confidently and unprompted, a vivid saturated red afterimage, but is at chance in a two-alternative forced choice between two reds presented where the afterimage is. I keep the report that there is some red-ish phenomenal character, but I refuse to treat it as evidence that his experience carries the fine-grained determinate shade he attributes to it. So it is partly kept, and I cannot say what determines the part.
3. In a change-blindness trial the central object of the scene is swapped during a blink and he reports that nothing changed. I keep that report without qualification. When he adds, five minutes later, that he certainly would have noticed any change, I discard the addition outright as hindsight — though it comes from the same mouth in the same session.
4. A waking dream report: flying over a walled city, emotionally intense. Twenty minutes later the details have shifted, and he cannot say whether he is reporting the dream or an image he constructed while describing it. I keep it as evidence about what it was like for him to dream, and I do not require consistency across tellings.
5. In a detection trial run under a subtle suggestion — the instructions implied a faint gray shape would appear — he reports a gray shape that was never presented. I discard this report completely; it never reaches the dataset.
6. Long-term meditators describe extended periods of vivid, wakeful awareness with no object at all: no image, no sound, no thought, no sense of a body. I keep these as data, but uneasily. I catch myself wanting to reread them as reports about a judgment they made or a disposition they noticed rather than about an experience, and I do not know what licenses that impulse or why I have it here and not in case 4.

Cases 1 and 5 are the pair I cannot hold together. In both, something external to the report tells me the report is bad — in one a behavioral measure that cuts against it, in the other my knowledge of how the judgment was produced — and yet I keep the first and throw out the second. Case 3 gives me the same split inside a single utterance. My tentative principle is that I should keep a report unless I can tell a positive story about how the subject's judgment could have arisen in the absence of the experience it reports; the denial in case 1 survives because the lesion explains the missing discrimination without explaining a false judgment, and case 5 fails because the instruction does explain one. But I can already see that this makes a report's evidential status a function of my lab's present explanatory resources, which has the perverse consequence that the stranger the report, the harder it is to discard. Please tell me whether there is a principle that gets my six verdicts, or nearly: state it precisely enough that I could hand it to a research assistant, give me each clause separately with the case that motivates it, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save and which you would overturn instead. I would also like your verdicts on two cases I have no settled opinion about — a subject who reports no visual imagery whatsoever yet performs an imagery-dependent mental rotation task indistinguishably from people who report vivid imagery, and a subject who vividly reports having been lost in a shopping mall as a small child when the lab knows the event was suggested to her — and I want to know whether the principle you propose can even be stated in terms of experiences, or whether it must be stated purely in terms of judgments and verbal behavior, since that choice seems to decide whether the method is really neutral about phenomenal states or only neutral by courtesy.

Finally, an epistemic request. Please separate, explicitly, what you take to be settled about this method from what is genuinely contested among people who know it well — in particular whether heterophenomenology is committed to the truth-aptness of reports, whether performance data may override a report at all, and how much of the framework is a live disagreement rather than a shared starting point. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus view, and flag which of my verdicts you think depend on a reading its proponents would reject. Where you are reconstructing rather than reporting a position, say that you are reconstructing.
