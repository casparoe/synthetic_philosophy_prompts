I'm working through Jules Ferry's 1883 letter to the primary-school teachers — the one explaining the new programme of moral and civic instruction that replaced religious teaching in the state school — and I'm stuck on the bit where he tells the teacher how to decide what he may say out loud. My rough English from the French, so don't take it as a translation:

Before you pass on some moral notion to a child, ask yourself whether there is, so far as you know, a single honest man who could take offence at what you are about to say. Ask yourself whether a father — I mean any one father — sitting in your class and listening could in good faith withhold his assent from what he heard you teaching. If so, keep quiet about it; but do not keep quiet about what he would not disown.

Three things I cant get straight. What sort of test is that — a criterion on the content of what may be taught, or on how it's justified, and is it a neutrality principle at all? It looks like it hands a veto to whoever is most easily scandalised, which sits oddly with Ferry's insistence elsewhere in the letter that the teacher must inculcate the common morality with conviction and form citizens, not merely avoid giving offence. And what becomes of the disputed matters — God, the soul, politics: are those being privatised into the family and the church, and if so is laïcité here a claim about the state or about the individual conscience? Lastly, how would someone coming at this from Anglo-American multiculturalism read it differently — trimming content down to a consensus versus recognising difference inside a common school? No references or further reading, I just want the argument taken apart. British English, please.
