term paper for my Bayesian epistemology seminar and I've ended up defending van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, my credence now in P, conditional on the hypothesis that my future credence in P will be r, should be exactly r. My line of defence is that Reflection isn't some extra normative constraint you could rationally reject: break it and a bookie can string bets together across the two times that guarantee you lose money however things turn out. What would count against this? Sort the defeaters by how much damage they actually do — the cases where I expect my future self to be *worse* informed (drunk, jetlagged, halfway through some kind of brainwashing) versus the cases where I expect to genuinely learn something new, and tell me whether those are two different problems or the same one in two costumes. Also: what has to be assumed about the later bets for the Dutch-strategy argument to go through at all — can my future self just cancel the earlier bet, and if she can does the whole argument lose its bite? And the bit that worries me most: once I add the usual proviso that Reflection only binds when I've no reason to think my future self is degraded or misinformed, is there any case left in which it can actually fail? I'd like to know which of these objections a Reflection defender would concede themselves, and whether the principle still risks anything once its properly hedged.
