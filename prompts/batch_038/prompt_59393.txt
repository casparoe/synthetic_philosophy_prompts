I am the communications standards lead for a regional public hospital authority (fourteen sites, roughly 20,000 staff). Every statement that leaves the organisation — press releases, written answers to reporters' emailed questions, on-record briefing lines — is reviewed by me or one of two deputies before release. We handle forty to sixty statements a month, eight to ten of them contentious, and reporter questions are typically answered in writing within the hour. Our reviewers have no legal or philosophical training.

The complaint pattern is stable and frustrating. Our general counsel signs off on any draft in which no clause is false. Complainants — patient families, journalists, the health ombudsman — then say the statement was dishonest anyway, and I have no principled way to adjudicate, because both sides are often right about their own terms. I want a reusable review procedure that decides, in a way I can defend to both, whether a draft is a lie, is misleading without lying, or is honest, and what has to change if it is not.

Please write that procedure. Ground it in what the lying-versus-misleading debate actually supplies that is usable: the difference between what a speaker asserts and what a hearer infers from it; whether the wrong of a statement tracks the content the question puts at issue or everything the statement communicates; whether a speaker owes correction of an inference she foresees the audience drawing; truthful-but-narrow answers that answer a smaller question than the one asked; the subjective condition, where a statement's status depends on what the speaker believes rather than on what is so; misleading by omission, by juxtaposition, and by definition; and the information carried by a refusal to answer. Do not give me a bibliography or citations — I want the concepts put to work, not attributed. Name the two or three that do the real load-bearing in your procedure, and say explicitly what you dropped for usability and what the procedure loses by dropping it. Where the underlying debate is genuinely unsettled, your procedure must pick a rule and say so, rather than leaving the reviewer to improvise.

The procedure itself must satisfy these constraints:

- Numbered steps a deputy can run in order in twenty minutes per statement, with a stated pass/fail or escalate condition at each step.
- An explicit instruction for what to do when the reviewer cannot establish whether a clause is true — which is often, since facts arrive from clinical directorates days late.
- Marked points where the procedure stops with a verdict, and points where it hands the decision to a named human because the question is one of values or of institutional risk rather than of what was said.
- At least two steps honestly labelled as irreducible judgment calls, with a note on what makes them unmechanisable and who signs off on them.

Then run it on four real drafts. In each case the reporter's emailed question is given first, then our draft answer, then the background the reviewer would have.

**Case 1.** Question: "How many patients died at your hospitals this year from infections with carbapenem-resistant organisms?" Draft: "Twelve patients at our facilities were treated this year for infections involving carbapenem-resistant organisms. Our overall in-hospital mortality rate is 1.4 per cent, against a state average of 1.9 per cent." Background: four of the twelve died; the reporter has asked twice and been given the treatment figure both times.

**Case 2.** Question: "Were any patients harmed by the infusion pumps now under recall?" Draft: "No patient at our facilities has been identified as having suffered harm attributable to the pumps under review." Background: three patients had unexplained hypoglycaemic episodes while on those pumps; the internal causation review is open and no determination has been made.

**Case 3.** Question: "Did your chief medical officer know about the staffing shortfall before the unit was closed?" Draft: "Dr A was not aware of any staffing shortfall at that unit before the closure decision." Background: the unit manager says she told Dr A verbally in February; Dr A genuinely does not recall it and disputes it; no record exists either way.

**Case 4.** Question: "Will you release the internal incident report?" Draft: "We do not comment on internal documents." Background: we have released two comparable reports in the past three years and said so publicly at the time.

For the four cases, give me a JSON array — after the procedure, not instead of it — with one object per case and exactly these fields:

- `case_id`: "1" to "4"
- `question_asked`: the reporter's question as given
- `at_issue_content`: the proposition or propositions the question actually puts at issue
- `draft_answers`: what the draft in fact answers
- `foreseeable_inference`: the distorted belief a reasonable, informed listener will come away with, or "none"
- `verdict`: one of "lie", "misleading", "honest", "undetermined"
- `decided_at_step`: step number and a three-word label
- `reason`: two to four sentences
- `judgment_dependent`: true or false — whether the verdict rests on a step the procedure cannot settle mechanically
- `required_revision`: a rewrite that passes, in forty words or fewer, or "none"
- `sign_off`: which role must approve release

Finally, in one paragraph after the JSON: which of the four cases your procedure handles worst, and why. I expect at least one verdict I will not like — including on Case 4, where my instinct is that declining to comment is always the safe option. If your procedure agrees with that instinct everywhere, say plainly that it has failed to bite, and tell me where a refusal can be the most deceptive answer available.
