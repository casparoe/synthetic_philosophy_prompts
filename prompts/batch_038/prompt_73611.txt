I have spent fifteen years building replicated databases, and the literature I know is the distributed-systems consensus literature: agreement, validity, and termination as the three guarantees; the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson impossibility for deterministic asynchronous consensus under a single crash fault; Byzantine agreement and the bound relating faulty to total processes; failure detectors and partial synchrony as the standard escape hatches; quorums, total-order broadcast, gossip and epidemic dissemination, leader election, split-brain, exponential backoff, and the safety/liveness distinction as my ordinary working vocabulary. I have never read an economics or social-learning paper. I can follow Bayes' rule and a likelihood ratio, but I do not know the field's terminology and I would rather not learn it in order to understand the results.

Please restate the core findings of the observational-learning literature into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report. The material I want translated is this, which you should treat as given rather than as something I have already understood:

1. The canonical sequential-decision model: two states are equally likely, each agent in a fixed order receives a private binary signal that is correct with probability strictly greater than one half, each agent observes every earlier action but none of the earlier signals, and once two consecutive agents have chosen the same action the next agent's own signal is outvoted by the inference she draws from them. From that point the action sequence carries no further information, and the group can lock onto the wrong action with probability bounded away from zero. Such a state is nevertheless fragile: a single public announcement reaching everyone at once can overturn it.
2. A distinction between signal structures in which the likelihood ratio of a private signal is bounded and those in which it is unbounded: in the first case wrong-action lock-in persists forever, in the second the action sequence converges to the truth.
3. A result in which agents are evaluated by an outside observer who cannot see their private information, so that an agent who is right for idiosyncratic reasons is indistinguishable from one who is merely lucky, and agents accordingly imitate the crowd even when their own information says otherwise — individually rational, collectively destructive, and driven by reputation rather than by inference.
4. Network results: whether a society asymptotically learns the truth depends on structural properties of the observation network, and permanent consensus on a falsehood can arise with every agent reasoning correctly and no adversarial or faulty participant anywhere.
5. The field's separation of herding driven by inference from others' observed choices from herding driven by direct payoff benefits from joining the majority.

For each of the five, tell me what the counterpart is in my vocabulary: what the message format and payload are, what the fault model is, which of the three guarantees holds and which fails, and what plays the role of a partition, a failure detector, or an impossibility theorem. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and present them as translation — if a term of mine is doing double duty, or if the mapping requires me to reinterpret one of my own primitives, say so explicitly at that point.

Then the residue report, in three classes. Which claims carry over cleanly? Which carry over only with distortion, and what exactly is lost in the distortion? Which have no expression at all in the consensus vocabulary? For every item in the second and third classes, tell me whether the gap is a defect of my framework — a distinction it should be able to draw and cannot — or evidence that the original claim was less precise than it looked.

I want you to address four specific pressures I can already see. First, the cascade model presupposes a commonly observed total order on actions; in my world total-order broadcast is a primitive roughly as expensive as consensus itself, so is the social-learning literature assuming away the hardest part of the problem without noticing? Second, my notion of validity is that the agreed value is some participant's input, whereas their standard of success is an external state of the world nobody holds as an input; is that gap substantive or merely notational? Third, my fault taxonomy has crash, omission, and Byzantine failures, none of which describes an agent that follows the protocol exactly, reasons correctly, and still produces a wrong outcome; where does that belong? Fourth, is there a concept in my vocabulary — the safety/liveness split, partial synchrony, or the explicit separation of protocol from fault model — that would sharpen their results, so that the translation runs productively in the reverse direction as well?

Finish with a verdict on my working intuition, which is that an informational cascade is simply a consensus protocol whose message payload is a decision rather than evidence, and which therefore satisfies agreement and termination while forfeiting any validity guarantee. Tell me whether that is right, roughly right with a correctable distortion, or missing something that only becomes visible once the translation is attempted.
