I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and this autumn I have been reporting a profile of a senior environmental historian whose work sits in the tradition that runs from Braudel's longue durée through McNeill's history of infectious disease and Crosby's account of Europe's biological expansion, and into the recent ice-core-driven rewriting of the sixth century. His new book argues that the standard cast list of history — statesmen, generals, merchants, reformers — is a provincial artifact, and that the volcanic dust veil of the 530s and the arrival of Yersinia pestis in the Mediterranean in the late 1340s deserve to be written about as actors rather than as scenery. I found that thesis persuasive and publishable. What I did not expect was the argument he gave me for it when I pressed him on what, exactly, an "actor" is. He laid it out patiently, in numbered steps, as follows, and he assures me it is sound and that its conclusion is one he is happy to own:

(1) To say that something was an actor in a historical event is to say that its contribution was not merely background: had it been otherwise, the event would have unfolded otherwise in respects that matter to the story we are telling. (2) Historians already apply exactly this criterion to human beings, and when a king, a general, or a merchant satisfies it they call him an actor; there is no further condition, because any further condition would be an unargued preference for our own species. (3) In the middle of the sixth century the ice-core record shows two very large eruptions, at least one of them low-latitude, which produced a dust veil, a decade-scale cooling of the North Atlantic, harvest failures across Eurasia, and — by his reading — the conditions in which a pandemic and the restructuring of the eastern Mediterranean became possible; in the late 1340s plague reached the same region and killed a large fraction of its population, though the precise scale is genuinely disputed. (4) In both episodes, subtracting the non-human event alters the political, demographic, and economic trajectory far more than subtracting any single human decision of the same years would alter it. (5) Therefore, by (1) and (2), these non-human events were actors, and more of an actor than any human being then alive. (6) The same criterion, applied honestly to the human decisions themselves, shows that those decisions were never the decisive difference-makers, since each is downstream of harvest yields, disease load, and climate, which together fixed the options on the table, the bodies available to execute them, and much of the temperament of the decider. (7) So no human being has ever satisfied (1), and history is the record of what nature did to a species that believed itself to be choosing. (8) It follows that the vocabulary we attach to human actors — responsibility, culpability, achievement, tragedy — has no application to the historical record at all, which is why his book contains no villains and, he says, cannot.

My editor has offered me a sidebar on whether the book's central thesis commits its author to (7) and (8), and I need to know where the reasoning actually breaks, if it breaks. I would very much like to keep (3) and (4), because they are what makes the book worth reading, and I cannot accept (7) or (8); I cannot tell whether what has gone wrong is an inference or a premise, and I would rather not guess in print. So: identify the step you would reject, and say precisely how it fails — whether it equivocates on a term that (1) and (2) had appeared to fix, whether it slides from a comparative claim to an eliminative one, whether it quietly imports a premise about determination that nobody argued for, or whether it is valid and one of the early steps is simply false. Explain why that step looked acceptable to a smart reader going along with it, and what feature of the criterion in (1) makes it seem neutral when it may not be. Then price the alternatives: for at least three of the premises, tell me what the historian loses by giving it up, including what happens to his own book and to his standing as someone who writes about causation at all. I would also like you to say whether the comparison in (4) is even well-posed — what it means to hold a counterfactual in which an eruption did not happen alongside one in which a doge decided differently, when the two are pitched at such different scales — and whether "more of an actor than" is a coherent comparative or a category confusion dressed as a ranking. Bring to bear the resources that genuinely do work here rather than a survey: the distinction between background conditions and selected causes and the criteria by which one is promoted to the other; interventionist accounts that tie causal explanation to manipulability and what they are obliged to say about difference-makers nobody could have intervened on; the flat ontology of "actants" in actor-network theory and whether it buys the historian anything or merely relabels the problem; and any compatibilist-style reply on which an event can be fully downstream of nature and still be an exercise of agency. Finally, tell me which of these, if any, permits (5) to survive while (7) fails, reconstruct the strongest version of his argument that reaches a conclusion I could print, and give me your honest view of whether that repaired conclusion is still news or is something every working historian already concedes when the recorder is off.
