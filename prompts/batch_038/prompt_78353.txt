My sister has been the only building inspector in a rural county for six years. State law requires a permit for permanent structures and never says what permanent means, so the calls are hers. Her record so far (all actual decisions I've watched her make): a 600 sq ft detached garage on a poured slab, bolted down, four years old — permanent; a 120 sq ft prefab shed sitting on bare dirt with its skids intact, three months old — not permanent; the exact same model shed two lots over, delivered twelve years ago, skids rotted into the ground — permanent; a 200 sq ft greenhouse on a slab with power and a water line, five years — permanent; a 300 sq ft pole barn with posts set in the ground, twenty years — permanent; a 340 sq ft carport, posts also set in the ground, three sides open, eighteen months — not permanent; a shipping container on blocks serving as the site office for a road job, eight months in and leaving when the job ends — not permanent; a shipping container on blocks used as a small church, three years — permanent; a 900 sq ft frame tent up for one wedding weekend — not permanent; and a 150 sq ft chicken coop on a gravel pad, wired for a light, twenty months old, where she wrote borderline on the file and let it stand with no permit. She's also told me the rotted-shed one bothered her, because she couldn't name the year it turned permanent.

Next week's case: a 240 sq ft prefab barn-style shed set on six concrete piers — no slab, no bolts, no power — holding hay and a mower, delivered nine months ago. The stakes are unusually high for her: a neighbor filed a complaint, so if its permanent the county can fine the owner every day until he permits it, and he's told her he can't afford that.

What does she decide? I'm alot less interested in the answer than in the rule she's running, and I'm pretty sure more than one rule fits everything above. So give me the candidate rules, what each predicts for this case, your best guess and why you'd bet on it over the others, and one further case that would settle which rule she's actually using. Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
