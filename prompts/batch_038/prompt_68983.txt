I've spent eight years building differentially private release pipelines, and my working vocabulary is now almost entirely quantitative: sensitivity and noise scale, epsilon and delta, the privacy-loss random variable and the accounting schemes built on it, sequential and parallel composition, the central versus local model, group privacy, mutual information as an upper bound on what an adversary's posterior can shift, and the standing caveat that differential privacy is a property of the mechanism rather than of any individual record. I came to this from the rationalist-blog side of things, so I'm comfortable with arguments about expected value, incentives, and decision theory, but I have essentially no training in the philosophy of privacy, and I've noticed that when I read the literature I keep involuntarily translating it into my own terms and then congratulating myself on how much of it collapses. I want to find out whether that translation is honest or whether I've been quietly substituting my framework's assumptions for the philosophers' claims.

Here is the task. Take six positions from the privacy literature and restate each as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I described above — not in theirs, and not in a watered-down version of mine. The six are: Westin's control conception, on which privacy is the individual's claim to determine when, how, and to what extent information about them reaches others; the reductive camp, pressed hard in the 1970s, which holds that there is no freestanding right to privacy at all, since every privacy complaint turns out to be a complaint about property, bodily security, autonomy, or reputation; Nissenbaum's contextual integrity, on which privacy is the integrity of information flows governed by context-specific norms that fix a data subject, a sender, a recipient, an information type, and a transmission principle, and on which a flow can be inappropriate even when the subject consented and even when nothing sensitive was disclosed; the relational view, on which privacy is what makes friendship, intimacy, and trust possible by enabling selective disclosure, so that the harm of exposure is the collapse of a distinction rather than the loss of a secret; the aggregation view, on which individually innocuous data points compose into a profile whose harm exists only at the level of the whole; and the power-asymmetry view, which grants that secrecy is largely gone and argues that the interesting question was never what is known but who may act on it. For each of the six, give me three things explicitly: the counterpart that translates cleanly, the part you had to distort or stretch to make it fit, and the part that has no expression whatsoever in a language of budgets, composition, and posterior shift.

Two rules, and then the deliverable I actually care about. First, no smuggling: if a claim is normative, do not translate it into an empirical statement about adversaries' inferences and call that a translation, and do not assume without argument that every privacy harm is a harm of posterior shift. Where you cannot express something, say so plainly rather than approximating it with a hand-wave about high-dimensional releases at small epsilon. Second, when you're done with the six, give me a consolidated residue report that sorts the leftovers into three bins — expressive gaps in my framework that are real and consequential, cases where the residue suggests the original claim was vaguer or less substantive than it sounded, as when a norm of appropriate flow turns out to be a redescription of someone's policy preference, and cases that are nothing more than a difference in terminology. Tell me which bin dominates and why. Then address two specifics from my own work. A philosopher colleague recently told me that my framework cannot distinguish a hospital publishing an epsilon-differentially-private contingency table of admissions from a national security agency publishing the identical table at the identical epsilon, and that the difference is the whole moral point; I answered that epsilon bounds posterior shift for any adversary regardless of who is asking, so the distinction must be about something other than privacy. Is my answer right, and if it is, what is left of her objection? Separately, we maintain a per-user epsilon ledger, and when a user's remaining budget reaches zero we treat them as ineligible for further inclusion in releases — internally we joke that such a user has entered the public domain. Does any of the six positions give me a reason to think that state is morally loaded beyond the fact that our guarantee no longer applies to them, and does any of them say that the exhausted user has been wronged in a way the fresh-budget user has not? I would much rather you tell me my vocabulary is missing something than flatter it, but I also want to know which of these philosophers I can safely dismiss as having said nothing that survives contact with an actual mechanism.
