I co-run a Thursday-evening reading group in our department — about 18 people, a mix of second-year poli sci and CS types plus two philosophy students who mostly attend to argue — and we get 100 minutes in a room with two whiteboards, a stack of index cards, and no projector. Last month we ran a session on iterated games and chicken, and honestly they left thinking they'd cracked it: the consensus in the room was something like "just be a dove, be predictable, never escalate." This week I want to knock that out of them without saying so out loud.

The topic is commitment races — the situation where what pays isn't which strategy you settle on but that you lock yourself into it before the other side does, so that being first and being legible is worth more than being right, and where everybody racing to be first is precisely what produces the disaster. (I think it's Oesterheld who uses the phrase in some of the AI-safety-adjacent game theory writing, and the older version is Schelling on burning your bridges and on deliberately arming yourself with a threat you can no longer retract. Don't assume anyone has read either — the reading is optional and nobody does it.) I want a game where they feel the *ordering* and the irreversibility, not where someone explains it to them. Ideally the dovish kid who was so confident last month ends up being the one who pledges first, and hard, and then discovers they can't take it back.

Here's the mechanics I've been playing with — take them, fix them, or throw them out if you have something better. Pledges go on a public board and get witnessed by a rotating neutral player who copies down what they think the card says, so an ambiguous pledge comes out garbled and doesn't bind the way its author wanted. Once a team's pledge is witnessed it's binding for the rest of the game, not just the round, so committing early in round one is genuinely expensive. And there has to be a way to pledge conditionally — something like: hard-line unless the other side was already pledged when mine got witnessed — which should turn out to be the smart move but should also be a race in its own right. Payoffs in paper clips or drawn chips, nothing that costs me money. I was thinking two teams of five with the rest as observers, but tell me if a different grouping works better with 18. One more constraint: the word "commitment" should never have to be spoken before the debrief, and the rule sheet shouldn't tip the concept off either, so give me in-game vocabulary that stays neutral.

Give me the whole thing in these sections, in this order:

1. **The fiction** — the cover scenario the teams are told they're in, one paragraph, no meta-language.
2. **Setup and materials** — ~150 words, assuming only whiteboards, index cards, two marker colors, and a timer on my phone.
3. **The clock** — a minute-by-minute run of the 100 minutes, ~300 words, including how many rounds, how long the pledge windows are, and where the witnessing happens.
4. **Payoffs and the ledger** — ~200 words, with the actual numbers on a small grid I could write on the board.
5. **What they should hit on their own** — ~150 words naming the three or four realizations the rules are supposed to force, and for each one, which specific rule does the forcing.
6. **Debrief** — ~300 words, mapping moments from the playthrough onto features of the real thing (first-mover advantage, legibility, irreversibility, conditional pledges as a race-defuser that is itself a race), plus the two questions I should ask that reliably make them re-litigate the dovish consensus from last month.
7. **What will go wrong** — ~250 words. I specifically want you to handle: two teams that know each other making side deals in the hallway, someone writing a pledge that just says "whatever you write", a team that refuses to pledge anything at all and free-rides the whole game, my one resident rules lawyer, and the session running 20 minutes over.
8. **What the game can't reproduce** — ~150 words, being blunt about which feature of a real commitment race the activity fakes and I'll have to supply by hand in the debrief.

And if it's easy, tack on a one-paragraph variant for a night where only six people show up, because that happens about once a term.
