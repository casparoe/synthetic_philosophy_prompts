The terrain is Dennett's taxonomy of creatures — the Darwinian ones whose competence comes pre-installed by selection, the Skinnerian ones who can learn from consequences, the Popperian ones who can let their conjectures get killed instead of letting themselves get killed, and the Gregorian ones who build their minds out of tools other members of their kind left lying around in the world. The fight is over where a trained language model lands in that scheme.

My position, which I want you to give to one of the characters: LLMs are not Gregorian. Gregorian-ness is about an individual whose competence gets assembled over time by reaching out and using external mind-tools, and the whole point is that the creature and the tools change each other. A trained model has no individual history at all. There's no ontogeny — everything it can do was installed in one shot and frozen. It didn't inherit a culture and make it its own; it is the culture, rendered down into weights. If anything it's hyper-Darwinian: all pre-installed competence, no creature underneath. And it can't have cleared the Skinnerian rung either, because there's no persistent subject to be conditioned — the thing that got rewarded during training is not the thing answering my questions now, and the thing answering my questions now has never once been rewarded or punished for anything.

Dana's position, for the other character: that's exactly backwards. Gregorian creatures are the ones whose minds are made of external artifacts, and language is the original mind-tool. A model is the first thing that's actually constituted by the whole Gregorian corpus rather than just borrowing bits of it when it needs them — no human is anywhere near that saturated, and that's why humans keep reinventing the wheel and models don't. And the missing rungs aren't missing: gradient descent over parameter space is a selection process, reward-based fine-tuning is operant conditioning with a reward signal, and at inference time the model generates candidate answers, evaluates them, and throws out the bad ones, which is conjecture-and-refutation whether or not you like the label.

Here's the thing we invented on the back of a napkin around 1am, and I want you to actually put it to work rather than mention it in passing. We coined a term for creatures that have the entire Gregorian corpus and no capacity whatsoever to add to it — we were calling them sterile Gregorians, though Dana preferred Library creatures. The idea: a thing that can be queried, that can recombine what's in the corpus in genuinely novel ways, but for whom nothing is ever at stake, and where nothing it ever does writes back into the corpus out of its own experience. Dana thinks this is a real new rung and the taxonomy needs it. I think it's not a kind of creature at all, just a description of a failure mode dressed up as an ontology. Have them fight about this too, using it.

Concretely: give the two of them a task that forces the philosophy to be operational instead of allowing it to just sprawl. Say they've both been put on a small committee that has to file one sentence for a public-facing FAQ — something in the neighborhood of "is the model a Gregorian creature?" — and they have to produce a single sentence they'll both sign, by the end of the afternoon, with a deadline breathing on them. Whether that sentence is writable is the argument. Let the deadline do work: let one of them try to escape into vagueness and get called on it, let them draft and reject actual candidate sentences on screen or on paper.

Two things I care about most. First, neither of them gets to be a strawman or get dumb at a convenient moment — I lost my side of this argument partly because I was tired, and I don't want the dialogue to lose it for me. Second, they should hit the levels problem head on, because that's where I think the real crux lives: the taxonomy was built to sort individual organisms, but with a model the four processes all seem to be happening at once at different scales — selection over parameters, conditioning during fine-tuning, conjecture-and-refutation in context, culture as the training corpus — and the two characters should disagree about whether it's legitimate to apply the categories level by level or whether doing that is just moving the goalposts until everything counts as something.

Around 1000 to 1400 words. End on the sentence. Either they find one that both can sign, or they don't, and if they don't, the last few lines should make it obvious to the reader whether that failure is a genuine disagreement about what's going on in the world or two people using Gregorian to mean two different things and never noticing. I'm honestly fine with either ending as long as it's earned and not a shrug. No citations, no bibliography, I just want the argument.
