**Working title: Local Independence and the Informational Reading of Arrow's Theorem**

**Section 1: Setup.** Fix a finite set X of at least three alternatives and a finite set N of voters. Each voter has a strict ranking of X (I assume away indifference for simplicity), and a profile is an assignment of one such ranking to each voter. A social welfare function maps every profile to a social ranking, which I require to be complete and transitive but which may contain ties; ties are resolved by a fixed deterministic rule, so the output is always a well-defined ordering. The conditions I take from Arrow are unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, non-dictatorship, and independence of irrelevant alternatives, together with the requirement that the social ranking be complete and transitive. The theorem says that when X has three or more elements no function satisfies all of them. When X has exactly two elements, majority rule satisfies all of them, and I treat that as a limiting case rather than a counterexample. I have also worried that my fixed tiebreak covertly violates weak Pareto, since it can place an alternative at the top of the social ranking when no voter places it there, but I cannot construct a violation and I suspect the worry is confused: a tie between two alternatives can arise only when those alternatives are not unanimously ordered.

**Section 2: Diagnosis.** Here is my reading of how the standard proofs run. One identifies a pivotal or decisive set of voters by taking a third alternative and moving it through the voters' rankings, from the bottom to the middle to the top, while holding fixed how each voter ranks the pair of interest, and one invokes independence at each step to conclude that the social ranking of that pair cannot have changed. The force of the impossibility therefore depends on being licensed to treat two profiles as informationally identical with respect to a pair even when that pair sits at completely different depths in the individual rankings. My claim is that these are not informationally identical situations. A voter who ranks x immediately above y has said something that a voter who ranks x above y with six alternatives in between has not said, namely that nothing on the menu falls between them in her judgment. Independence insists that this difference makes no difference. I want to argue that it does, and hence that independence is not a neutral technical convenience but a substantive and contestable claim about what counts as the same information.

**Section 3: The proposal.** Call a social welfare function locally independent if the following holds. Take any two profiles and any pair x, y such that, in both profiles, x and y are consecutive in every voter's ranking. If each voter ranks x and y in the same order in the one profile as in the other, then the social ranking of x against y is the same in the one profile as in the other. I call this local independence of irrelevant alternatives, or LIIA.

My central claim is that the Borda count, with my fixed tiebreak, satisfies unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, non-dictatorship, completeness and transitivity of the output, and LIIA. That is, it satisfies every condition in Arrow's list except independence itself, and LIIA is what survives of independence once Section 2 is granted. The argument is short. With m alternatives, award m − 1 points for first place, m − 2 for second, and so on. If x and y are consecutive in a given voter's ranking, then that voter's contribution to the difference between x's score and y's score is exactly plus one if she ranks x above y and exactly minus one if she ranks y above x, regardless of where the pair sits in her ranking or how many alternatives lie above or below it. So when the pair is consecutive for every voter, the difference in scores is fixed by the number of voters on each side, and nothing else in the profile can affect it. Two consequences seem to me worth stressing: LIIA holds, and where LIIA applies my rule is extensionally identical to pairwise majority voting on that pair, which is to say that locality recovers the Condorcet intuition exactly where the information is unambiguous.

**Section 4: Where LIIA has content.** I concede that LIIA is weak and often vacuous. For a pair to be constrained at all it must be consecutive in every voter's ranking, and with three or more alternatives that is a special configuration. My reply is that a condition's silence about some profiles is not a defect in the condition but a claim about which profiles are comparable. Second, when X has two elements every pair is consecutive in every ranking, so LIIA coincides with full independence and Borda coincides with majority rule; this is consistent with the two-alternative case being possible, and I do not think it embarrasss me.

The substantive claim of this section is that LIIA is non-vacuous precisely where democratic politics lives. Suppose the alternatives are points on a one-dimensional spectrum, a set of tax rates say, and that every voter's ranking is single-peaked with respect to that spectrum, as the median-voter tradition beginning with Black leads us to expect in such settings. Then two alternatives that are neighbors on the spectrum are ranked consecutively by every voter, since nothing can lie between them for anyone. On single-peaked domains, therefore, LIIA applies to every neighboring pair, and my rule is genuinely constrained: it must reproduce majority voting on each link of the spectrum, which is just to say that it must respect the median-voter structure that makes such domains well behaved in the first place.

**Section 5: Upshot.** Because LIIA is compatible with everything else in Arrow's list, the theorem is not a limit on democracy but a limit on how much of the profile a rule is permitted to read. Its moral is informational rather than ethical. The common rhetorical slide from Arrow's theorem to the impossibility of fair democracy, or to skepticism about collective rationality as such, is a category error. What the theorem shows is only that we cannot have a rule that is simultaneously indifferent to the depth at which a pair is buried in individual rankings and respectful of what voters say about that pair.
