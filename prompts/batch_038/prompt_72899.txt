i'm teaching a session on logical omniscience in an epistemic logic seminar and i want to open it with a poem rather than a definition — something the group can argue with for ten minutes before we get technical.

speaker: an agent whose beliefs really are closed under consequence. possible-worlds semantics taken literally, so that to believe p is just to rule out the not-p worlds — which means every logical truth is already in the belief set for free, and every consequence of what you believe you already believe. but dont write a robot complaining about being a robot. write it more like someone describing what its like to be incapable of being surprised, or to hold two sentences that mean the same thing as though they were one single object.

form: 24–32 lines, stanzas of four, free verse, no rhyme needed.

the trick, and it has to actually work: two of the stanzas must be strict contrapositives of each other (line for line, or the whole stanza as one chain of conditionals reversed and negated — your call), and a second pair must be De Morgan variants of one another, a "not either ... or ..." turned into an "and not." the poem then has to treat each pair as the *same* thing, because for this speaker it is the same thing: same worlds excluded, same belief, nothing added. but the stanzas should land differently on the reader — one of a pair reading as an accusation and its twin as a resignation, or one as a hope and the other as a bare fact — so the gap between what the speaker cant distinguish and what the reader cant help distinguishing is where the whole problem sits.

content: give the pairs something human to be about (somebody who left, a promise, a town you cant go back to, whatever you want), and turn the poem around the twenty-line mark on the speaker noticing that it's producing a difference it has no way to represent. it should end without resolving anything — ideally somewhere near the thought that whatever happens when a sentence genuinely tells you something new isnt on the map of worlds at all.

accuracy bar: someone who knows this literature should be able to read the two contrapositive stanzas and confirm they really are equivalent, and should recognize the granularity problem (necessarily equivalent contents collapsing into one) without it being named. so don't name any philosopher, and don't use the phrase "logical omniscience" inside the poem. no references or further reading, i'll hand those out separately.
