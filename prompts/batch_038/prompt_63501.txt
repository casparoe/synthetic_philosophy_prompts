I am a novelist assembling a collection of stories about minds, and I have grown tired of the ones in which a machine flips a switch and a character abruptly becomes conscious. I would like you to write a story of roughly fifteen hundred words that instead carries a stranger and less-travelled commitment of the higher-order theories of consciousness: on such a view a mental state is conscious only in virtue of being accompanied by an appropriate higher-order representation of it, and nothing in principle prevents that representation from misfiring — fastening onto a first-order state the subject is not in, or arriving with the immediacy that experience demands when there is no experience there to be aware of.

Keep the story entirely within an ordinary contemporary world: no laboratories, no implants, no artificial intelligences, and no character who has read any philosophy. The higher-order machinery must be load-bearing rather than decorative, so that the events would come out differently under a first-order account on which a suitably rich perceptual state is conscious whether or not anyone ever takes note of it. Two further constraints. The awareness your characters have of their own experience must never present itself to them as a conclusion inferred from evidence or as an act of inner observation, since that is precisely what would disqualify a higher-order state from doing the explanatory work it is supposed to do. And the story must contain at least one moment in which a character's confident report of what they are undergoing comes apart from what is actually going on in them, in a way that changes what some other character then does.

After the story, add a note of no more than five sentences identifying the passage where the higher-order and first-order accounts of the same events genuinely diverge, and stating what your story claims is happening, experientially, inside that gap.
