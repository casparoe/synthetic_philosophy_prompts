Last month two friends of mine, Nadia and Owen, got into an argument at dinner that I still have not been able to settle in my own head, and I would like a ruling on it. It began harmlessly: someone mentioned a photograph that half the table saw one way and half the other, and then someone else described being woken by a telephone ring that turned out never to have happened. Owen had picked up the word "disjunctivist" from a podcast and used it once, but they argued the rest of the way in plain language.

Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reproduce it.

(1) Nadia: in the second case there was no ringing, so what the person was aware of cannot have been a sound; yet from where they stood, the experience was exactly what hearing a real ring is like. If two experiences are exactly alike from the inside, they must be the same kind of thing, so in both cases the person is directly aware of an appearance — one that happens to match the world and one that does not.

(2) Owen: "exactly alike" is doing all the work and it is unearned. The person in the bad case has no way to check the likeness; the only handle available is that they cannot tell the two apart. Not being able to tell things apart is not the same as their being the same, and a case with a telephone in it is plainly not the same state as a case without one.

(3) Nadia: then you cannot explain why the person is reasonable. They believe the phone is ringing, and they believe it as reasonably as anyone in the good case would. If the two experiences really were different kinds of thing, one would support the belief and the other would not, and nothing available to them marks the difference.

(4) Owen: not being able to tell is all the support anyone ever needs. And your account has a cost you are skipping: if an appearance stands between the person and the world in the good case as well, then nobody ever hears a telephone, and the doubt you were trying to block arrives by another route.

(5) Nadia: that is just my point turned around. On your account the good case is equally out of reach from where the person stands, so contact with the world is something they could never be aware of having. You have kept the word "direct" and lost the substance.

Neither of them budged. Could you go through the five moves one at a time and say who is right at each — not merely who wins overall — and then tell me what, if anything, both of them are taking for granted that they should not be?
