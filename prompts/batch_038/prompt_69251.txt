I'm a staff engineer at a company that makes control software for lab equipment; I have no formal philosophy training, but I've been reading in the evenings for a couple of years now, and two threads have recently collided in my head in a way I can't resolve on my own. The first is Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, which I reread last month after a long stretch away from it. The second is Bernard Williams's argument about internal and external reasons, which I picked up in an anthology chapter from a used bookstore. As I understand Williams, the claim that someone has a reason to do something is true only if that person could be brought to want to do it by reasoning soundly from what he already cares about — and Williams deliberately makes that starting collection broad, so it holds not merely desires but loyalties, dispositions to evaluate, patterns of emotional reaction, and the projects that make a life his. Claims about reasons that are supposed to be true independently of any such starting point he regards as either false or unintelligible: they cannot enter into explanations of what the agent does, and they cannot be what is really going on when we criticize him. I expect I have some of that wrong, and I would like you to correct me where I do before you build anything on it.

The novel looks to me like an unusually sharp test case for this view, sharper than the stock examples — the man who won't give up drink, the bystander who feels nothing — because Stevens is an agent whose own starting collection is not reliably available even to him. Several moments are what I keep going back to. When Darlington's journalist godson tells Stevens plainly that his employer is being used by the Nazis, Stevens answers, in effect, that it is not his place to judge; suppose we grant that nothing in him could be moved by that information by any amount of sound reflection. Is it then true, on Williams's account, that Stevens had no reason to reconsider — and if so, what exactly is the novel condemning when it lets us watch him say that? When Darlington orders the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids, Miss Kenton objects and threatens to hand in her notice, then does not; Stevens passes the order along and, on his own later telling, does not question it. Those two responses seem to differ in something other than moral worth, and I cannot tell whether an internalist can say what the difference is, or has to swallow the consequence that she had reasons and he did not. On the night of his father's death Stevens goes on serving at the international conference and afterwards treats that night as the point at which he achieved his professional ideal; here it looks as though a value he holds — dignity, in his sense of it — generated a decisive reason, which raises the question whether dignity is simply one item in his starting collection, and whether the internalist must therefore endorse his choice. Then there is the parting with Mrs Benn in the tea room, where he privately acknowledges that his heart is breaking and says nothing, and the closing scene by the sea, where he accepts a stranger's advice about the evening being the best part of the day and resolves to work on bantering for his new American employer. That last move interests me most: he acquires a fresh motivation, on grounds that are entirely his, in order to serve someone else's convenience, and I cannot decide whether it is the view's vindication — he can still recognize and act on reasons at fifty-odd — or its reductio. And there is a wrinkle I don't know how to state properly, so I'll put it the way I'd put it at work: Williams's condition seems to depend on a variable whose value is undefined at the time it is read. The route is supposed to run from what the agent already cares about, correcting his false beliefs along the way, but Stevens's false beliefs are largely about what he himself cares about, and his narration is a sustained act of self-misdescription. So which collection do we reason from — the one he reports, the one he actually has, or the one a fully rational version of him would have? Push the third far enough and I worry the view stops being internalism at all, since the idealized agent's reasons may have nothing to do with the reasons of the man in the pantry.

What I want is criticism, not a summary of either side. Take the novel and the argument seriously in both directions: build the strongest internalist reading of Stevens and the strongest externalist one, then say which scenes each handles and on which each breaks, and be explicit where you think I have forced a fit. I'd particularly like to know whether Ishiguro's book can be adjudged an argument for one side at all, or whether its stakes — class, amateurish aristocratic honour, appeasement, the way a life can be handed over to someone else's purposes — sit outside a debate about the truth conditions of reason claims, in which case the honest answer is that the lens distorts the book and I should be told so. Please don't quote the novel back at me, since I'm working from memory of scenes too and only need them identified by description; likewise I want no bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus, because I'm offline most evenings and would only be guessing at any of it. What I do want at the end is the thing an argument alone can't give me: a statement of what this novel contributes to the question about reasons that the philosophical literature, so far as you can tell, has not managed to say.
