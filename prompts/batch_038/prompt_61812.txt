I am working through W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 address "Criteria of Negro Art" — delivered at the NAACP's annual conference in Chicago and printed in The Crisis that October — and I have hit a stretch of reasoning I cannot get straight. Here are three consecutive paragraphs from the first part of the speech:

> In the high school where I studied we learned most of Scott's "Lady of the Lake" by heart. In after life once it was my privilege to see the lake. It was a Sunday. It was quiet. You could glimpse the deer wandering in unbroken forests; you could hear the soft ripple of romance on the waters. Around me fell the cadence of that poetry of my youth. I fell asleep full of the enchantment of the Scottish border. A new day broke and with it came a sudden rush of excursionists. They were mostly Americans and they were loud and strident. They poured upon the little pleasure boat, -- men with their hats a little on one side and drooping cigars in the wet corners of their mouths; women who shared their conversation with the world. They all tried to get everywhere first. They pushed other people out of the way. They made all sorts of incoherent noises and gestures so that the quiet home folk and the visitors from other lands silently and half-wonderingly gave way before them. They struck a note not evil but wrong. They carried, perhaps, a sense of strength and accomplishment, but their hearts had no conception of the beauty which pervaded this holy place.
>
> If you tonight suddenly should become full-fledged Americans; if your color faded, or the color line here in Chicago was miraculously forgotten; suppose, too, you became at the same time rich and powerful; -- what is it that you would want? What would you immediately seek? Would you buy the most powerful of motor cars and outrace Cook County? Would you buy the most elaborate estate on the North Shore? Would you be a Rotarian or a Lion or a What-not of the very last degree? Would you wear the most striking clothes, give the richest dinners, and buy the longest press notices?
>
> Even as you visualize such ideals you know in your hearts that these are not the things you really want. You realize this sooner than the average white American because, pushed aside as we have been in America, there has come to us not only a certain distaste for the tawdry and flamboyant but a vision of what the world could be if it were really a beautiful world; if we had the true spirit; if we had the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart; if we had, to be sure, not perfect happiness, but plenty of good hard work, the inevitable suffering that always comes with life; sacrifice and waiting, all that -- but, nevertheless, lived in a world where men know, where men create, where they realize themselves and where they enjoy life. It is that sort of a world we want to create for ourselves and for all America.

The rhetoric is easy to follow; the argumentative structure is what defeats me. Four connected puzzles:

1. The excursionists "struck a note not evil but wrong." What distinction is doing work in that phrase? The people described shove others aside and are granted "a sense of strength and accomplishment," yet Du Bois withholds the term "evil." Is he marking off a category of wrong that is not malicious — a failure of perception or sensibility that is nonetheless a wrong — and if so, what has to be true of beauty for insensitivity to it to count as wrongdoing rather than mere impoverishment? Note also that the crowd's displacement of the "quiet home folk" and foreign visitors is described in terms of yielding and giving way, which sounds like a political fact rather than an aesthetic one.

2. The vignette is offered as evidence for the claim in the third paragraph, "these are not the things you really want." But the excursionists already possess the freedom of movement, leisure, and consumption whose denial is the very grievance the rest of the speech presses. How exactly does their vulgarity license an inference about what full citizenship plus wealth would or would not satisfy? Is the objection to the objects (motor cars, service clubs, press notices) or to a manner of wanting them — the "they all tried to get everywhere first" quality? And does the inference work only because the audience is being asked to imagine acquiring those goods without having been trained to want them?

3. "You realize this sooner than the average white American because, pushed aside as we have been in America" attributes an epistemic advantage to exclusion: deprivation yields "a certain distaste for the tawdry and flamboyant" and hence "a vision of what the world could be." Yet the speech's stated goal is a beautiful world for everyone, and later Du Bois complains that the mass of human beings are choked away from beauty. Does the position collapse into treating the deprivation as productive, so that its removal would destroy the insight it confers? Can the claim be read narrowly, as about a cultivated taste rather than about exclusion itself, without losing its point? Also: why is the comparison class "the average white American" rather than the excursionists, who are presumably just that average, made visible?

4. The triad "the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart" is immediately followed by a deliberate substitution — "not perfect happiness, but plenty of good hard work, the inevitable suffering that always comes with life; sacrifice and waiting ... but, nevertheless, lived in a world where men know, where men create, where they realize themselves and where they enjoy life." Is Du Bois here revising the object of the struggle away from welfare or contentment and toward perception, making, and feeling as activities? If so, does that revision explain why an address to a civil-rights organization turns to Art at all — that is, does it make art instrumental to the political end, or does it make the political end partly aesthetic?

What I want from you is a single, unified reconstruction of the argument across these three paragraphs that answers all four puzzles at once, not four separate observations. I am not asking for references, sources, or historical background beyond what the passage supplies, and I do not want a menu of competing readings weighed against one another with the choice left to me. Commit to the interpretation you think is right: state it plainly, show how each of the quoted phrases falls out of it, and explicitly identify at least one rival reading you are rejecting and say why the text rules it out. If your reading requires you to take Du Bois to be making a substantive and contestable philosophical claim — about aesthetic wrongs, about the epistemic value of exclusion, or about happiness — then say so and defend the claim rather than softening it into ambiguity.
