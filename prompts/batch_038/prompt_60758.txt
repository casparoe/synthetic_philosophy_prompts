Full disclosure: I'm a backend infra engineer, no philosophy training at all, so I'm probably going to describe this badly.

For two years I've been split between a team in Copenhagen and one in Austin — same company, same product, sometimes literally the same service. And the two teams design around opposite assumptions about the state. On the Danish side everyone has one national ID number that plugs into tax, health records, school, digital mail, and they'll happily route sensitive stuff through a single shared national platform (MitID and friends) because it just works and somebody is accountable for it. On the Austin side the same feature gets built so that no one party can see the whole picture — seperate keys, local-only storage, an explicit threat model where the assumption is that whoever runs the system will eventually be someone you don't want running it. Neither side thinks the other is dumb. Both have receipts.

When I try to read the actual argument behind this — Scandinavian high trust versus American suspicion-as-a-civic-virtue — I come away feeling like its been going around in circles for decades and nobody is ever going to move. The trust side points at the Nordic countries sitting at the top of basically every cross-national trust survey while also having the most competent, least corrupt states going, and says: empirics, done. The suspicion side says that's backwards — trust is what you can afford once the institutions already work, and a design principle has to be picked for the cases where they don't; plus they'll note the Nordic record isn't clean (Sweden ran a sterilisation programme for four decades and only ended it in the 1970s) and that the whole model was built on a small, unusually homogeneous population at an unusually good moment in history. Both sides have every fact they need. Both keep winning in their own telling, and I've never once seen anyone say "huh, hadn't thought of that."

My hypothesis, which I want you to try to break rather than agree with: I think they aren't disagreeing about the same thing, because they're running two different models of what trust even is. The trust-first crowd treats it like a measurement — trust is your estimate of reliability, updated on evidence, basically a prior you revise once enough uptime data comes in; feed it the right data and high trust is just the correct posterior, while suspicion is an error. The suspicion-first crowd treats it like a control input — trust is a parameter you feed into the system that changes how the system behaves, the way bolting a monitoring stack onto something changes what the monitored thing does, and the way distrust can be self-vindicating too. On that reading "is this belief accurate?" is the wrong question and "does holding it make the outcome better or worse?" is the right one. If that's the real split, then the survey evidence is decisive for one side and a category error for the other, and the debate is structurally unwinnable because one camp is answering what a citizen should believe and the other is answering how a system should be built.

So: is that diagnosis right, or am I just overfitting to my day job? Lay out what kind of disagreement this actually is. Verbal — do the two camps mean different things by trust, or by reasonable? About which cases count as data — the Nordics now versus everywhere else always? A hidden empirical question that has an answer nobody's got yet, i.e. the causal direction, whether trustworthy institutions produce trust or trust produces trustworthy institutions? About burden of proof — which attitude is the default you have to argue your way out of? Or bedrock intuitions about power and human nature that neither side can give reasons for? Argue from what each camp actually says; don't give me a literature tour or a reading list, I'm not going to chase it down. Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on: what would count as progress here — a distinction, a study that could genuinely settle the causal direction, a reframing that dissolves the question, something else? Or make the case that no progress should be expected and say why. I'd much rather have a definite answer I can push against than a fair-and-balanced shrug.
