For the better part of two decades I have read and argued in rationalist-adjacent forums, whose unofficial epistemology sits firmly on the anti-reductionist side of the debate about testimony: you extend another person's claim a default presumption in its favor, and what stands in need of justification is your refusal, not your acceptance. Reductionism — the view that a hearer needs positive reasons, drawn from perception, memory, and induction, before testimonial belief is warranted at all — has always struck that audience as skepticism dressed up as rigor.

I would like you to set out the most important implications of holding this position consistently for a situation that recurs there constantly. A writer I have followed for ten years is unusually careful, has been right about many hard technical questions where I was in a position to check him, and has no incentive to deceive me. He has now published a long defense of a view I take to be settled nonsense in a field adjacent to his. The forum split into two camps. One says the presumption survives intact: either I produce defeaters specific to his new argument or I provisionally accept his conclusion, and a decade of good judgment is simply not the sort of thing that licenses a global dismissal. The other says that decade of good judgment is exactly the positive reason the reductionist demands, and that this book is itself a defeater for his credibility across the board.

In working out the implications, please address at least the following. Whether these two camps can ever diverge in practice, or whether the dispute is about where to place a burden of proof rather than about what to believe. Whether a defeater may be a statistical generalization about a speaker's error rate, or must bear directly on the claim in front of me. Whether a defeater may attach to the process by which he came to hold the belief — motivated reasoning, contaminated sources, an audience he is performing for — rather than to its content. And what the presumption implies about the rational standing of a policy I would describe as acquired distrust of argument in general, formed by someone who has been taken in too many times by reasoning that looked excellent and proved bad; that policy is one I suspect I already hold, and I want to know whether my professed view permits it.
