I hold a fairly strong reading of Good's result on the value of information: for an agent with a coherent prior who updates by conditionalization and chooses so as to maximize expected utility, a costless observation can never lower expected utility as judged from the prior, and it strictly raises it unless the observation is guaranteed to leave the optimal choice unchanged. I do not treat this as a piece of decision-theoretic trivia. I use it as a normative premise in two places. First, in evaluating designed agents: on my view, an artificial or institutional agent that systematically avoids observations, or that pays to remain ignorant, is thereby exhibiting a defect, and the defect must be one of three things — a hidden cost, a failure of dynamic consistency, or a utility function defined over something other than outcomes. Second, in argument about institutions and individuals: I resist rational-ignorance defenses, such as the claim that patients, regulators, or voters are sometimes better off not looking, on the grounds that these claims always smuggle in either a cost or a nonstandard preference.

Two constructions of my own have unsettled me, and I want them adjudicated rather than waved away.

Case A, the endogenous channel. The theorem, as I understand it, evaluates the expected value of a given observation: a fixed partition of the evidence space whose likelihoods the agent knows in advance. But the cases I actually care about involve a sender who chooses which partition to induce — an analyst who decides which of several questions to answer, a testing regime that decides whom to test, an auditor who decides which file to pull — where the choice depends on the state and the recipient does not observe the choice rule. In such a setting there is no fixed partition whose value the theorem computes, and an agent who conditions on message content alone can be left strictly worse off than if no message had arrived. I anticipate the reply: condition on your total evidence, including the fact that a message arrived and your beliefs about the selection procedure. But that reply seems to me to concede the substance, converting the theorem from a guarantee about information into a guarantee about correctly modeled information, and rendering my position compatible with every real instance of selective reporting. Is that concession acceptable, or does it gut the normative use?

Case B, non-sharp credence. Suppose my credence that a policy will work is not a number but a set of priors, and I choose by maximin over that set. Before any observation the set is narrow enough that every member ranks act X above act Y, so maximin selects X. A costless, exogenous observation then arrives, its likelihoods perfectly unambiguous; conditionalizing each prior in the set dilates my posterior spread, one posterior now strongly favors Y, and maximin switches to Y. By the lights of most of my original prios, including the one I would have called my central view, I am worse off for having looked. If you think no such case can be filled in while keeping both the dilation and the maximin switch, say so and show why. Otherwise: is this a counterexample to my position, or does it merely establish that the theorem says nothing about non-sharp credences? And if the latter, does my position survive, given that it was meant to be a general norm about ignorance rather than a claim about one specific idealization?

A third family I keep meeting, briefly: cases where knowing changes the situation rather than merely revealing it (a diagnosis that alters how a family treats the patient, a disclosure that changes a counterparty's behavior), and cases where the agent's welfare depends on her own beliefs (hope, suspense, dread). Are these outside the theorem because the utility is not over outcomes, because the observation is not free, or for some other reason — and does it matter which?

Given all this, I want a map of my position's exposure:

1. State precisely what the theorem licenses, identifying which hypotheses do the load-bearing work, including in the strict-inequality clause, and which are decorative.
2. What would have to be true for my position to be false? Give me the candidate defeaters — formal results, structural features of decision problems, kinds of agents, kinds of evidence — ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ranking defended rather than asserted.
3. For each defeater, say whether it is live, in the sense that it can be exhibited inside a coherent model, or merely logically possible. And say which of them a defender of the theorem would concede as genuinely damaging and which they would dismiss as a violation of the hypotheses. I want the defenders' own concessions, not only the critics' objections.
4. Deliver an explicit verdict on Case A and Case B: counterexample, misuse, or something in between, and if in between, say what the intermediate category is and why it matters.
5. What does my position rule out that its rivals do not? It seems to entail that any competent optimizer avoiding information must have a cost, a dynamic inconsistency, or a non-outcome utility. Is that a substantive and checkable claim about real and designed agents, or is it true by stipulation?
6. Assess the insulation honestly. Every putative counterexample I can construct appears absorbable by insisting that the observation was not really free, or the agent not really Bayesian, or the utility not really over outcomes. I can see the argument that a theorem is supposed to be insulated by its hypotheses and that this is no defect at all; my worry is whether my normative use inherits the insulation illegitimately. Tell me whether the position is falsifiable, and if it is not, whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind.

Work from the structure of the cases and the reasoning; I am not asking for references, and I would rather you say a step is uncertain than supply a source I cannot check. If you believe I have misstated the theorem or one of its conditions, correct that first and then answer the rest on the corrected basis. Write in American English.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different ranking of the defeaters or reverse your verdict on Case A or Case B, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
