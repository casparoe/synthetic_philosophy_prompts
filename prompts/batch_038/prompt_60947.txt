I work in heritage policy, mostly advising on commemorations, memorial inscriptions, and anniversaries, and over the last few years I have sat through a great many of them. A pattern keeps recurring that I cannot name, and I want to know whether the literature has a concept for it or whether I am looking at something that has simply never been separated out.

Here is the pattern, in three instances.

First: a secessionist movement loses, badly, and for two generations it keeps the anniversary of a final defeat as its central public event — names read aloud, graves tended, speeches about a claim that has never been renounced. Then the movement wins most of what it originally demanded: devolved government, official language status, control of schooling. Attendance at the anniversary collapses within a decade. What strikes me is not merely that the crowd thinned, but the reaction of the people who used to organise it. They describe the settlement as a graver betrayal than the defeat was, and their reasoning is explicit: under the defeat the ideal stayed whole and unspent, whereas the settlement converts it into administration. The loss is not that they got too little. It is that they got anything at all.

Second: descendants of a defeated side, asked in an oral history project whether their grandparents would have wanted to win, reply that victory would have turned them into the kind of people they were fighting — and that the defeat is precisely what preserved the distinction. Here again the non-fulfilment is doing active work; it is not a residue of failure but the condition of the group's self-description.

Third: a diaspora community that maintains a territorial claim in a formulation no negotiable treaty could satisfy, and that treats members who accept partial restoration as desecrating the dead more seriously than the original conquerors did. The formulation has drifted over the decades toward the unsatisfiable, and each drift was defended at the time as fidelity to the ancestors.

The feature I want identified is narrow. I am not asking about the general habit of reframing a loss as morally superior; I have noticed something more specific and more counter-intuitive, namely that the commemorative structure appears to require the demand to stay unmet in order to keep generating the identity, the obligation to the dead, and the authority of the people who speak for them. Partial or full success does not resolve the memory, it deflates it, and so the custodians of the memory police success itself. The taboo runs against settlement, not against the enemy.

So: is there an established concept for this, and if so what is it? I can anticipate several neighbours and I want to be told for each whether it fits or only approximates: the stab-in-the-back style explanation of a defeat; the idea of a moral victory; irredentism and revanchism as political programmes; the notion that communities select certain traumas to organise themselves around; and the psychoanalytic distinction between mourning that completes itself and a refusal to complete it, which people sometimes apply to nations. Where I have seen these invoked, none of them seems to capture the part I care about — the ones that want recovery actually welcome success, and the ones about refusal to grieve describe a stalled affect without explaining why settlement is policed as treachery and why the demand migrates toward formulations that cannot be met. If you agree that none of them fits, say so plainly and then coin a term, with a definition sharp enough to generate yes/no verdicts on new cases, and tell me the diagnostic criteria: what would I have to observe in a commemoration to know I was looking at this rather than at something else?

The carving matters more to me than the label. Please be explicit about where the edges are. In particular: how does one distinguish this from a community that simply distrusts the terms on offer and would be perfectly satisfied by better ones? How does it relate to cases going the other way, where a partial victory energises a movement rather than deflating it — are those counterexamples that show the phenomenon is not a type at all, or a variant with a different mechanism? And does the phenomenon require that the defeat be military and collective, or does it also cover families, institutions, and firms with a founding failure they keep alive?

Two further requests. First, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or which kind of case would make you conclude that this is not a distinct phenomenon but a by-product of something already named, and where in your own reasoning you are most likely to be wrong — for instance, if you are fitting my three instances to a concept you know well rather than testing it against instances that would strain it. Second, do not send me hunting for sources; work from concepts and definitions rather than a reading list, and if you are unsure whether a term is genuinely established or only something you have half-absorbed, flag that uncertainty instead of asserting it.

Structure the response with short headed sections: nearest established concepts and their fit, the diagnosis of the described pattern, boundaries and discriminating tests, and your recommendation. End with a single final line in exactly this form:

\answer{term or short phrase}

containing either the established term you judge to fit best, or the term you coin, in six words or fewer.
