Right, I should probably say where I'm coming from, because it affects what I want out of this. I retired three years ago from a career in isotope geochemistry — mostly dating things, and mostly worrying about whether the things I was dating were behaving the way the textbooks assumed. Since then I've been doing an OU course and, more usefully, going to a Wednesday reading group with five other people, two of whom are actual philosophers. We've just spent a month on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, which I found strangely comforting at first, because as a bench scientist I always thought scepticism about the external world was a fake problem people invented so they'd have something to write about. Now I'm not so sure it's comforting, and I can't quite put my finger on why, so I've written down where I've actually landed and I'd like someone to tell me whether it hangs together.

Here's the list, as honestly as I can manage:

(a) I'm a scientific realist. Our best theories are approximately true, and the reason I believe it is that their predictive success would otherwise be an absurd coincidence.
(b) I fully accept that induction can't be given a non-circular justification. Hume wins that one. I don't think its a scandal and I don't lose sleep over it.
(c) I'm persuaded by the hinge idea: some propositions aren't things I know or doubt, they're the frame within which knowing and doubting get going at all. They're held fast by everything around them, and you acquire them by being trained into a practice rather than by choosing them.
(d) Following on from (c), I think the sceptic's doubt about the external world isn't a genuine doubt. It's a misuse of the machinery — there's nothing he could be right or wrong about, because there's no procedure by which his doubt could ever get settled.
(e) That said, I do think On Certainty defeats scepticism. If someone at the reading group presses me, I say Wittgenstein has shown the sceptic is wrong, and I'd happily say that to a sceptic's face.
(f) I think our scientific hinges are genuinely better than the alternatives — better than the hinges of the man down my road who's certain the moon landings were filmed in a studio, better than a faith-healer's world-picture. And I want "better" there to mean something more than "mine".
(g) Moore's proof fails. Waving your hands about doesn't establish anything to a person who doubts. But if my wife asks me in the kitchen whether I know I've got two hands, I say yes, and I don't think I'm talking nonsense in that moment.
(h) The framework is revisable. Twice in my career I've given up background assumptions I'd have called untouchable — one about a dating method, one about what counted as an acceptable measurement — and both times it was evidence from inside the practice that did it, not philosophy. There's that river-and-riverbed business in the book, which I take to be saying something like this.

I've deliberately not told you which of these I'd be most willing to loose, mainly because I don't know, and partly because if I signalled one you'd just go after it. So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Please rank them rather than serving me a list where everything is equally troubling — I got that from one of the philosophers and it was useless. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix is and what the fix costs me, because I imagine none of them come free. I also suspect at least one of these looks much worse on paper than it is in practice, and I'd like to know which one, and why it dissolves once you look at it properly. And if there's a single revision that would clean up most of the mess at once, tell me what it is and what I'd be signing up to. What I don't want is an essay on hinge epistemology or a summary of what other people have said about it — I can read that myself. I want the audit.
