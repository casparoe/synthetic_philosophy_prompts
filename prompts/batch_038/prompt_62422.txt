I am preparing an advanced seminar on David Lewis's analysis of convention (*Convention*, 1969) and on the way social norms change over time, and I need a purpose-built case rather than an illustration of the standard view.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specification, and then demonstrate that it satisfies the specification.

**Setting.** One small, concrete, real-world community — a harbor crew, a hospital ward, a factory shift, a village council — described in a single tight paragraph. No trolleys, no science fiction, no stipulated omniscience, and no assumptions of unusual rationality or altruism on anyone's part.

**The regularity.** The community follows some practice R in a recurring coordination situation. There is an alternative practice R*, known to everyone, that would serve exactly as well.

**Two phases.** Present the community before and after an ordinary social event of your choosing: the arrival or the visible example of a neighboring group that does the thing the other way, a remembered incident in which a deviant was blamed, a founding story that hardened into a point of pride. The event must leave the practical situation untouched. R and R* remain equally effective, everyone still knows this, everyone still expects everyone else to keep doing R, and the regularity is no less stable than before. What changes is that R acquires moral and social force: deviants are resented and sanctioned, and members feel guilt or shame at the prospect of switching.

**Required verdicts.** In phase one the analysis must deliver the verdict that R is a convention. In phase two it must not.

**Constraint on how the case must work.** The flip may not be produced by R's becoming practically better than R*, by any loss of common knowledge, by any weakening of the regularity, or by anyone's ignorance or irrationality. It must come out of the clauses of the analysis that concern people's preferences and interests, applied to a coordination situation that has not changed.

The point of the case is to test whether a synchronic, preference-based analysis can handle the transition from a bare convention to an entrenched norm. A case that stipulates the two verdicts rather than deriving them, or that smuggles in a change to the practical situation, fails.

After the scenario, please deliver:

1. The verdict in each phase, clause by clause, identifying the specific clause that fails in phase two and explaining why it fails when nothing about the utility of R and R* has changed.
2. Whether your case generalizes: does the analysis imply that no convention which acquires moral force remains a convention? If so, is that a cost Lewis can absorb, or does it undermine the claim to be analyzing our ordinary concept?
3. The threshold question. The analysis quantifies over everyone, with an "almost" qualification. If only a minority of members have moralized R while the rest conform for purely coordinative reasons, does the verdict flip? Say where the line falls and whether it is principled or arbitrary.
4. The strongest reply available to a Lewisian who wants to insist that phase two is still a convention — a restricted reading of the preference clauses, or a distinction between the convention and the norm wrapped around it — together with what that reply costs.
5. One short paragraph on how an account that classifies regularities by their genealogy, or by the presence of normative expectations and sanctions rather than by present preference structure, would describe the same case, and what such an account gives up.
