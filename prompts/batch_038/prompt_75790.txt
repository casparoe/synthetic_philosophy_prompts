Hospitals ask patients to rate their pain 0–10 and log the number on the chart right next to temperature and blood pressure — for a while it was officially pushed as the "fifth vital sign." But for babies, people with advanced dementia, and anyone intubated, the same hospitals toss the report and score pain entirely off behavior: grimacing, guarding, heart rate. And then theres the weird cases — people with certain insula lesions who'll tell you "yeah, pain, maybe a 7" while seeming totally unbothered, shrugging, saying it doesn't distress them.

What picture of pain does the self-report practice presuppose? That pain is a private qualitative state the sufferer has privileged access to, so the number is a readout of something only they can see? Or something much weaker — that reports are just the best available evidence, no different in kind from a grimace? Does the behavioral track for non-verbal patients contradict the first reading, or is it only a fallback for when the readout isn't available?

And then: an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness says there's no introspectible raw feel in there at all, that the whole idea of a private qualitative item misdescribes what introspection actually delivers. Can that view make sense of the 0–10 scale and of the fact that people use it pretty consistently? Does the scale commit you to qualia, or only to a reliable discrimination-and-report capacity that an illusionist is happy to grant? Finally, the campaign gets blamed for helping drive opioid overprescribing — does that tell against the underlying picture of pain, or only against the step from a reported number to a prescription? I want to know where the practice really commits you and where it's underdetermined, where several rival theories of pain would license the exact same chart.
