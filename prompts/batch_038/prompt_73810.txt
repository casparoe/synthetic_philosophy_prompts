I have no academic training in any of this. I spent eleven years doing demand forecasting for an industrial parts distributor, and since leaving that job I have read my way, slowly and alone, through the material on algorithmic information theory, Solomonoff induction, and minimum description length. A friend of mine — an engineer, sharper than I am — holds a view I cannot dislodge: that the correct procedure is always to adopt the shortest description that accounts for the observations you have, and that spreading belief across longer descriptions is incoherent. I want to show him one concrete case in which that procedure goes badly wrong in a way he can check by hand, and in which weighting every candidate description by two raised to the minus its length in bits does better. I would like you to build that case for me, written so that someone with ordinary arithmetic but no probability training can follow every step.

Specifications:

- One short scenario in ordinary life — no urns, no textbook coin flips, no gods or demons, nothing immortal. Something like my old work: a machine on a shop floor, a supplier's lead times, a weather station, a commuting route. A run of roughly ten to fifteen observed outcomes, listed explicitly, and then a request for the probability of the next one.
- At least three candidate explanations, each statable in a single sentence, one of which is deterministic, fits every observation on the list, and has the shortest description of the lot. The true one must not be the shortest. That is the whole point.
- Assign each candidate a bit-length for its shortest description and justify each number by saying what actually has to be encoded. Do not tune the figures to produce the conclusion you want: show me the accounting first and the verdict second, and tell me plainly if any number had to be nudged to make the case work.
- Work out both procedures' answers with real arithmetic — the shortest-description rule's probability for the next outcome, and the weighted mixture's, as an explicit weighted average. Then supply the next observation that refutes the deterministic candidate and score both procedures against it; logarithmic loss is fine. Show the numbers.

Constraints: everything must happen at finite data, with figures two of us could reproduce at a kitchen table. No appeals to convergence in the limit, no asymptotic theorems, and no invoking the uncomputability of these lengths as a way of ducking the calculation — though I do want you to say where that uncomputability bites and why the case survives it anyway. Also tell me how much slack the result has: if my friend disputes one of your bit-lengths by three or four bits, does the conclusion hold?

After the case, the honest part. Which single feature of the example is doing the work? What happens if I remove it — say, by softening the deterministic candidate to allow a small error rate? And how should a defender of minimum description length attack my example? I expect him to say I have caricatured his position rather than refuted it, so tell me whether that attack lands, and if it does, what survives of the point I was trying to make. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would lead you to withdraw the case or reverse its verdict, and at which step in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
