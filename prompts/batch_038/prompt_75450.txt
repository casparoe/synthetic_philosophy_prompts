I'm a welfare consultant — mostly trout and salmon clients, and this is my first shellfish one, a marine shrimp farm that's applying for certification under a big assurance scheme (I won't name it, their full standards aren't public and I don't want anyone in trouble). The problem is the standards committee never publishes its reasoning. You submit, and a few months later you get back "approved", "approved with conditions", or "rejected", sometimes with one line of explanation attached. I've been through enough of these over three years that I've built up a list of rulings I'm confident about:

- Rejected (2022, a crab operation): selling live crustaceans to end consumers, i.e. the customer takes the animal away alive.
- Rejected: boiling crustaceans alive on the processing site for a "fresh to order" line.
- Rejected: eyestalk ablation of shrimp broodstock, even when performed under anaesthetic.
- Approved with conditions: chilling shrimp in ice slurry at harvest, provided they lose responsiveness within about 20 seconds and are killed mechanically before they could recover.
- Approved: air exposure of up to 30 seconds during routine grading of juvenile finfish, provided water temperature is under 12°C and the fish go straight back into well-oxygenated water.
- Approved with conditions: live transport of market-size crabs in aerated moist containers for up to 10 hours.
- Approved: on-farm killing of shrimp by a single trained cut through the head/nerve cord, done within 30 seconds of removal from water, with a back-up method on site.

My client's proposal, which I have to give them a steer on before Thursday: they harvest in the morning, hold the shrimp in aerated tanks on the farm for up to 12 hours, then deliver them alive to restaurants within roughly 40 km. Each restaurant is contractually required to kill every shrimp using exactly the cut method the committee already approved, within 60 seconds of taking it out of the tank, using staff my client trains; my client will do two unannounced audits per restaurant per year. There's no data on actual compliance — the contracts are six months old at best and the audits havent happened yet. Their argument is that the only difference from the approved on-farm version is 12 extra hours of holding plus a road trip, and the animal still dies by an already-approved method, so total harm is basically identical. I sort of want to believe that, partly bc I've already quoted them a fee. A colleague insists the whole committee is just downstream of the UK sentience act that added lobsters and octopuses, and that anything involving a crustacean leaving the certified site alive is dead on arrival. I don't think it's that simple but I can't prove it either.

So: predict the verdict. Please structure it as (1) "Bottom line" — two or three sentences, your single best guess plus how confident you'd be; (2) "The candidate rules" — about 300 words, every principle you can construct that is consistent with all seven precedents above, and flag any rule that looks tempting but is already killed by one of them; (3) "What each rule says about my client" — about 250 words; (4) "My best guess, and why that rule" — about 200 words, including which specific precedents make you prefer it over the runners-up; (5) "The case that would settle it" — about 120 words, describing one or two hypothetical applications I could ask them about that would split your top two candidate rules apart; and (6) "How to re-file it" — about 120 words, what my client should change to maximise the chance of approval, and what principle they'd be inviting the committee to adopt by changing it that way. Don't hedge it all into mush, I need something to actually say on Thursday. And you don't need to cite anything, I just want the reasoning laid out.
