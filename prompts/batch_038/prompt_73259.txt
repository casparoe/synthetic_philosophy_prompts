I am a novelist, five years into a book whose two halves are set in two towns that I have deliberately made mirror images of each other. The first is a small place where nobody is anonymous and the operative rule is the one Sandemose formulated for Jante: you are not to think you are anything special, or better than the rest of us. The second is a metropolitan, celebratory regime, closer to the American Dream, in which rising is loudly rewarded — but the reward is conditional on the rising person giving an acceptable account of why they rose. My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that these are not two codes but one code with the sign flipped: that a community which honours success and a community which cuts it down are running the same machinery and merely differ in what they require the successful person to say. I should add that Sandemose's own eleventh rule — the unspecified intimation that the town already knows something about you, held in reserve as a general penal code rather than a charge — is the structural idea I have borrowed most freely, and item eleven below is my version of it.

For the novel I have drafted a register of eleven instruments by which a community manages a member who has risen above the others. Some cut down, some celebrate; the classification is part of what I want checked, not assumed.

1. The Pre-emptive Discount — the rising person is expected to mark the achievement down themselves, in advance, before anyone else has to; if they do not apply the discount, it is applied for them, with interest.
2. The Roll Call — at the celebration, someone names the one who stayed, or the sibling who did not live to see it; the achiever must join in the naming, and in joining concedes that their rise was a subtraction from a common stock.
3. The Ledger of Small Debts — the community has kept an informal record of who helped this person when they were nobody: a lift, a covered shift, a loan never documented. It is produced only after the success, and never in full, so that it can never be discharged.
4. The Misattributed Compliment — praise that credits the wrong cause: of course, your father's name opened that door; you were always good at talking. It must be received graciously, and gracious reception is endorsement.
5. The Honourific Captivity — a prize, a plaque, a street named after them. The honour is genuine and it converts a living person into a monument, that is, into something finished.
6. The Register Boundary — the achiever may tell the story of their rise only in registers the audience has pre-approved: comic, self-deprecating, or technical in a way nobody can follow. Stories outside those registers meet a flatness that teaches the rule without ever stating it.
7. The Earnedness Audit — in the celebratory town, the rise must be narrated as earned. Luck, help, and timing are edited out of the official account, and anyone who admits to a break finds the warmth around them cooled.
8. The Return Weekend — the community does not attack the one who left. It asks them home to be ordinary for two days. Refusal is recorded, and so is compliance.
9. The Unnamed Gift — money given quietly to local causes on the strict condition that the giving is never attributed to the giver; attribution turns the gift into a display and starts the machinery again from the top.
10. The Borrowed Mouth — the thing that cannot be said directly is said by a beloved third party: the old teacher, the mother, the friend who never left. To answer it, the achiever must strike someone they love.
11. The Standing Suspicion — no particular accusation, but a general and permanent intimation that the town knows something about you, available at any time and in any tone of voice.

Please score each of the eleven on a single dimension I will call self-execution, defined as follows: the share of the work of applying the sanction that the rising person must perform with their own mouth or hand. Score 1.00 where the sanction lapses entirely if the person declines to perform the expected speech or gesture and nobody else can perform it in their place; 0.00 where it lands identically no matter what the person says or does. Be strict about the endpoints — the scale is useless to me if everything clusters near 0.5.

Then place the instruments in tiers by that score: Tier I for those that depend almost wholly on the target's own performance, Tier IV for those that operate with no cooperation from the target at all.

Begin with two or three sentences of bottom line: which instrument scores highest and which lowest, and whether your scoring bears out or falsifies my hypothesis that the two towns are running one code with the sign flipped. If you think the hypothesis is wrong, say so plainly and say what the real difference is.

Then give me the ranking as a single table, sorted by descending self-execution score, with exactly these columns:

- instrument — the name as I have given it above
- self_execution — a number to two decimal places
- tier — I, II, III, or IV
- placement — one sentence on why it lands where it does
- transplant — one of holds, inverts, or dissolves: whether the instrument survives being moved from the town it belongs to into the other one
- transplant_reason — no more than ten words

After the table, add two short notes. The first, under the heading TAXONOMY, should tell me which of my eleven are really the same instrument wearing different clothes, and which are two instruments fused into one that I ought to split. The second, under the heading ON THE PAGE, should name the one instrument that a novelist is most likely to ruin by making it explicit — by letting a character state the rule aloud rather than leave the reader to infer it — and say in a sentence why that particular one cannot survive being spoken.
