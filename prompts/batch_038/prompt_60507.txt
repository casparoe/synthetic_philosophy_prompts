Consider the objection to utilitarianism, in the form it has had since Rawls, that utilitarianism takes a mode of reasoning appropriate only to the choices of a single individual and applies it to society as a whole, thereby failing to take seriously the distinction between persons; and in Nozick's version, that for purposes of moral assessment the only beings there are are individual people, each with one life to lead, so that no collective subject ever exists to pay a cost and receive a compensating benefit in return. I want an argument, not a survey, for a thesis about what this objection can and cannot establish. The argument should turn on the following case.

**The Merger.** Ada and Bo are two adults who, for reasons of their own and with full knowledge of what it involves, undergo a reversible procedure. For exactly one hour there is a single subject of experience where before there were two: one locus of attention, one stream of deliberation, one sense of agency commanding both bodies, and one phenomenal field in which sensations arising in either body are felt with the same immediacy and the same sense of ownership. During the hour there is nothing it is like to be two subjects sharing an arrangement, no alternation of control, no experience of a second owner of the field; the subject is simply one. Before the hour Ada and Bo are as they were, and after it they are as they were. Psychological continuity runs unbroken in both directions for each: afterwards, Ada remembers the entire hour from the inside in the ordinary first-personal way, and so does Bo, and neither memory is derived from the other or acquired by testimony. On any reductionist account of personal identity, according to which a person's existence just consists in physical and psychological continuity, nothing further is required for it to be true that both Ada and Bo survive the hour.

During the hour the subject must decide whether to undergo a ten-minute treatment administered to Ada's body. The treatment cannot be given under anaesthesia, because it requires the subject's continuous and deliberate cooperation, and it is intensely painful throughout. It is the only available means of arresting a degenerative condition in Ada's body; without it, Ada suffers permanent and substantially worse harm after the separation. Bo's body is unaffected either way, though he retains, as Ada does, a first-personal memory of the ten minutes if the treatment is performed. Both Ada and Bo want the treatment performed beforehand; neither can withdraw during the hour; the subject knows all of this, and knows that it is one subject making the choice.

**The thesis to defend.** The separateness-of-persons objection cannot refute utilitarianism, because its force depends on a sharp dichotomy between intrapersonal and interpersonal aggregation that no plausible metaphysics of persons can sustain. Once the relevant difference is a difference of degree rather than of kind, the most the objection can establish is that a correct theory of value must be sensitive to how well-being is distributed among subjects rather than to its sum alone — a demand that consequentialism can meet by making the distribution, and not merely the total, an argument of its evaluation function. The objection is therefore either self-undermining, in that whatever principle condemns aggregation in the Merger also condemns ordinary prudence, or absorbable, in that it collapses into a constraint on the shape of the value function that utilitarians may accept without ceasing to be consequentialists.

In making the case, please do the following:

1. State the objector's principle charitably and with precision, as a general schema distinguishing intrapersonal from interpersonal aggregation, and identify what its force is supposed to depend on — the numerical distinctness of subjects, the felt unity of a stream of consciousness, the absence of a single bearer of both cost and benefit, or something else. Distinguish, if you find the distinction useful, a version of the objection that is a claim about the metaphysics of value-bearers from one that is a claim about the structure of justification.
2. Show, for each candidate grounding, exactly where the Merger puts pressure on it, and where — if anywhere — it survives the case intact.
3. Address the two replies an objector will make first. The first is that during the hour there is genuinely one subject, so the aggregation the Merger requires is intrapersonal and the case is no counterexample at all; argue that this reply concedes that the operative criterion is unity of consciousness, and that unity is precisely what admits of degrees. The second is that the sacrifice is uncompensated, since Bo ends the hour with a memory of a pain he did not benefit from; argue about whether the objector can afford a criterion on which the permissibility of a choice now depends on the subsequent branching structure of the subject's continuers, and say what a remembered pain that no longer hurts contributes to the ledger of burdens.
4. Consider a graded variant — a partial merger in which sensation is unified but agency and memory are not, or agency is unified but the phenomenal field is not — and state what the objector's verdict should be there. Explain whether any verdict you can give is consistent with the verdict you gave in the full Merger, and what the pattern of verdicts across variants implies about the objection's form.
5. Conclude by stating what is left of the objection on your account: specify what distribution-sensitivity would have to consist in for a consequentialist to accommodate it, and say whether a consequentialist who accommodates it in that way has made a substantive concession or merely a notational one.

Two constraints. Argue from analysis and from the stipulated facts of the case; do not cite, attribute, or reconstruct the views of particular authors beyond the brief orientation above, and do not supply references or a reading list, since the case is doing the work and invented or half-remembered attributions would only weaken the argument. Finally, identify honestly the single point at which your argument is most vulnerable, and say what an objector should press there.
