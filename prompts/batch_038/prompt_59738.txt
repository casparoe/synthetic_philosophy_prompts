ok, context first, sorry for the ramble. I'm an RN, eleven years in utilization review, and about six weeks ago they moved me onto our plan's medical-policy committee — the people who actually write down what "medically necessary" means in the policy manual. I thought the hard part would be the cost modeling. It isn't. The hard part is that nobody on the committee can agree on what a disease is, and I went down the philosophy rabbit hole trying to fix that and it made the whole job feel unstable, because it's definately the definition that decides the coverage.

So: Boorse's biostatistical account — health as statistically typical functioning relative to a reference class, where the goals of the functional design are survival and reproduction, and the whole point is that it's supposed to be value-free and empirical rather than a dressed-up judgment about what kind of life is worth living. Then the normativists, who say you can't get to "disease" without an ideal of wellbeing. Then harmful dysfunction as the hybrid middle route.

Here are the four cases we're deadlocked on, and I want you to work with these specifically because they pull in opposite directions:

1. Presbyopia at 47. If the reference class is her own age band it's statistically universal, so a Boorsean should call it *not* a disease — and yet we cover the glasses.
2. An asymptomatic 29-year-old BRCA1 carrier. No present functional impairment at all, so the account seems to say "not a disease, just a risk" — and yet our oncologists treat her as a patient, with surveillance and sometimes prophylactic surgery.
3. Infertility at 34, everything else fine. Reproduction is supposedly one of the two goals of the design, so this should be a paradigm case — and our draft policy excludes it as a "life circumstance."
4. Male-pattern baldness at 25. Subnormal follicle function relative to the reference class of 25-year-old men, harmless to survival or reproduction. The account seems to say disease; nobody on earth thinks it is; we deny.

Plus the thing that makes me most uneasy: the reference classes are age × sex × race, and if you can just *choose* the reference class you can choose the health verdict — build a class out of heavy drinkers and their liver function becomes normal by construction, which looks like the health/disease distinction has to be smuggled in to pick the classes. And the race-indexed classes are the ones our compliance people get nervous about, for reasons you can imagine.

Your role: act as a philosopher of medicine who has also had to sit through an actual coverage-policy meeting — you know this literature cold, but you're not emotionally invested in Boorse winning, and you'd rather tell me a number is low than pad it.

Here's the proposition I want a credence on, stated so it has a truth value. Call it N:

There exists a naturalistic (non-normativist) account of disease — Boorse's or a repaired descendant — such that (a) it can be applied without any evaluative premise about how a life should go, (b) it can pick out its reference classes without already presupposing which states are diseases, and (c) if you put its verdicts on 40 stock cases in front of a panel of 100 randomly sampled working clinicians (GPs, hospitalists, nurses, plus one each from psych, ob-gyn, endocrine, onc), at least 70 of them would say it gets the right answer on at least 30 of the 40. The 40 cases include the four above plus anorexia nervosa, gender dysphoria, ME/CFS, obesity, idiopathic short stature, menopause, asymptomatic hypertension, sickle-cell trait, and an epigenetic-clock "biological age" two standard deviations above chronological age.
