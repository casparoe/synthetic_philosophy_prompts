"The most influential version of the higher-order approach is Rosenthal's. The starting point is a distinction he insists on: it is one thing for a creature to be conscious, quite another for one of its states to be conscious, and only the second is the interesting target. His answer is that a state is conscious when, and only when, the subject has a thought to the effect that they are in it — what gives a state its feel is not what it is made of but its being represented by something else in the mind. Rosenthal spells that representation out on the model of Locke's 'reflection': the mind contains a scanning mechanism aimed at its own contents, and when the scanner lights up a perceptual state, that state becomes something it is like to have. Because the scanner is just attention turned inwards, whatever you are not attending to is strictly speaking not conscious at all, a consequence Rosenthal is perfectly happy to accept. The package is attractive — it is cheap, it posits no mysterious stuff, and it leaves plenty of room for unconscious mentality, since your visual system can register the change in the room while the scanner is pointed elsewhere. But it faces two difficulties he never escapes. The first is that the scanner can misfire: you can get a higher-order thought that you are seeing red when no first-order state answers to it, and on the theory that is a full-blooded conscious experience of red out of nowhere. Rosenthal accepts the consequence, and critics — Block most prominently — treat it as a reductio. The second is a regress: if the higher-order thought is what confers consciousness, it needs a thought one level up to confer consciousness on it, and so on without end. Rosenthal's repair is to weaken the requirement, so that the higher-order thought need only be disposed to occur rather than actually occurring; but a merely available thought looks incapable of doing any explanatory work, and the dispositional move is now widely regarded as a retreat. What emerges is that higher-order theory is introspectionism in a fresh vocabulary: consciousness is self-scanning, and the only real question is whether we have an inner eye."
