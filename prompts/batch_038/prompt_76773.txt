I run a monthly get-together for a bunch of semi-serious poker and sports-betting people — twelve of them, sharp about odds, and the only philosophy they've had is whatever they picked up from Twitter threads. I've talked them into letting me do 45 minutes on Lewis's Principal Principle, roughly the idea that if you know the objective chance of something your credence in it should match. Don't teach me the material, I've got that. Plan the session for me.

What I need: the order the pieces have to go in and what each one depends on. I keep running into the problem that if I open with the definition nobody sees why anyone would ever posit a worldly chance in the first place, and if I open with the motivation I lose them on what's supposed to be objective about it. Then which single running case should carry the whole thing. I'd been assuming a coin but I suspect that's wrong, because coins make everybody think long runs and frequencies instead of a one-shot chance, so tell me what the case has to have and what it has to lack — and whether a casino-deck setup would land better for this crowd than anything physics-flavoured.

Then the misconceptions. I'm certain at least half of them will say chance is just what a well-informed person's credence is. I'm nearly as sure someone will insist the principle is a tautology, basically the law of total probability in a hat. And there'll be a guy who concludes that once you know the chance is 1/2 nothing he later learns about the actual toss can matter. For each one: where in the sequnce do I head it off, and what do I actually say, because I don't want to reach the end with them holding something technically correct but useless at a table. Relatedly, the point where the chance number and their own evidence genuinely conflict — glimpsing the bottom card, say — how do I stage that so it illuminates rather than collapsing into "so it was all subjective anyway"?

Give me one question I can drop in the last five minutes that would show whether they got it or just learned the words. And tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level — self-undermining chances, whether chance has to reduce to the Humean mosaic, that sort of thing — and how to say so without sounding like I'm hiding the good parts.
