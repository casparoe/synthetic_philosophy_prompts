I am a liaison psychiatrist, and a growing share of my capacity assessments involve older adults whose everyday thinking is carried partly by things outside their heads: a notebook maintained for thirty years, a phone that prompts every medication and appointment, a spouse who supplies the missing words. The statutory test I work to has four limbs — the person must understand, retain, weigh, and communicate information relevant to the decision — but my service assesses those limbs as though they measured internal machinery alone: we take aids out of the room, and we score a relative's prompting as contamination rather than as part of the cognitive system being tested.

Please convert the extended mind thesis, and the parity principle at its core, into a procedure my team can run inside a forty-five-minute assessment by clinicians with no philosophy training. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which external supports are to be treated as constituents of the person's cognition for assessment purposes and which are merely aids they could do without, and how to treat supports that are unreliable, absent overnight, or controlled by someone with an interest in the outcome. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and say plainly where the thesis runs out — in particular, whether any criterion can be applied to a human partner without the boundary of the mind dissolving altogether.

Then run it on two cases: a retired engineer who cannot reproduce a single date, name, or commitment without his notebook, and who uses it competently and unthinkingly; and a woman with early-stage dementia whose husband answers most questions before she can, and whose own answers improve markedly when he leaves the room. Give me a procedure I could defend to a tribunal, and tell me where it will misfire.
