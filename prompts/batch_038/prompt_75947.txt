For four years I have sat as one of the two non-scientist trustees of the Calderwood Trust, a private foundation that funds long-horizon research in the behavioural sciences. I am a novelist by trade; the Trust's founder, a chemist, wrote into the by-laws that two of the nine trustees must be working writers or artists, on the theory that a body deciding how to spend other people's money in perpetuity needs at least two members with no stake in any discipline's fortunes. I have found the role harder and more interesting than I expected. In November the board votes on whether to renew the Trust's largest single commitment: a five-year, $8.4 million core grant to the Halden Centre for the Study of Cooperation, which since its founding has pursued what its director insists is one research programme rather than a loose collection of projects. Its hard core, unchanged since 2011, has three parts: all human cooperation is produced by a single domain-general mechanism, reputation-mediated partner choice; there is no dedicated norm psychology, meaning no internalised rules that move us independently of expected reputational payoff; and every apparent counterexample is a failure of one of the auxiliary assumptions about cue availability, payoff salience, or how completely subjects model their partners. The director is explicit that the core is not revisable on the strength of any single experiment, that anomalies are to be located in the protective belt, and that the belt is where all of the Centre's formal modelling work actually happens.

Here is the record the board has been given, stripped of advocacy on both sides. In year two the Centre derived, in advance and in print, a quantitative prediction that cooperation in one-shot anonymous pairings would rise by a specified amount when subjects were exposed to a stylised eye-like glyph; the effect landed inside the predicted interval. A second and less publicised prediction from the same model — that cooperation would decay across a session as subjects learned the anonymity was real, which the rival internalised-norms account had predicted in the opposite direction — was also confirmed, and the measurement instrument built for that work is now in use at three unaffiliated laboratories. In years three and four, four unaffiliated replications of the glyph effect failed; the Centre responded with a new auxiliary, that the glyph must be culturally legible as a watching agent, which was not independently testable when introduced and which rendered all four failures consistent with the core after the fact. In year five, cooperation was documented well above baseline in a setting the Centre's own models classified as fully anonymous and reputation-free; the Centre introduced a further auxiliary, that subjects implicitly model the laboratory itself as an audience, and then — to its credit, the director argues — constructed an independent measure of perceived observation and reported that it mediated the effect precisely as the auxiliary required. Two unaffiliated labs have since adopted that measure, one replicating the mediation and one flatly contradicting it. The board is split. One trustee, a physicist, holds that both auxiliaries are textbook rescues of an unfalsifiable core and that the Centre has migrated from predicting novel facts to absorbing anomalies. Another, a historian of science, offers the standard rejoinder: that this methodology yields its verdicts only retrospectively, that the programme its author most liked to cite was degenerating by his own criteria for decades before it turned around, and that a trust with a thirty-year horizon has no standing to act as instantaneous judge of a young programme. The director counters that the year-five mediation test is exactly the kind of independently corroborated auxiliary that separates a progressive problemshift from an ad hoc one. And the staff note separately that the Centre has trained most of the field's strongest methodologists, a benefit that would survive the core thesis's being false.

Please write the opinion I will circulate to the other eight trustees before the November meeting: a recommendation with terms — renew, renew conditionally, or do not renew — that the board could actually vote on, and reasons the director and the physicist could each answer in the room. About 1,200 words. I want a Lakatosian appraisal of this programme rather than a précis of Lakatos; assume every trustee has read the two-page summary in the board pack, so hard core, protective belt, negative and positive heuristic, progressive and degenerating problemshifts, and the distinction between a shift that adds content and one that merely accommodates can all be used without definition. What I need is the hard part. First: does the framework deliver a determinate verdict here, or does the verdict turn on how one individuates the hard core and on which auxiliary one is permitted to count as independently testable — and if it turns on that, state which individuation you adopt and why, rather than observing that the question is delicate. Second: a shift is appraised by whether it carries excess content at the moment it is made, but corroboration may arrive later; tell me which of that pair the board should treat as decisive, then apply your own rule consistently to the glyph auxiliary and to the perceived-observation auxiliary, even if that means grading one of them against the Centre. Third: meet the historian's long-leash objection head on, since it is the one I find most persuasive and I would like either to be argued out of it or confirmed in it. Fourth: decide whether the methodological-training benefit may enter the appraisal at all, or whether admitting it collapses the distinction between a programme's heuristic power and its mere institutional usefulness.

One personal caveat, since you are writing for me rather than for the board. I make my living constructing narratives that feel inevitable in hindsight; I am professionally susceptible to a satisfying arc, and the director is the finest raconteur I have met in fifteen years of trustee dinners. Where the story he tells is carrying weight the evidence cannot bear, I want that named specifically. Nor do I want a survey. Take a position on the renewal, put it in the first paragraph, and give your reasons; if you judge that the framework genuinely underdetermines the decision, say so plainly and then tell me which way you would have me cast my vote, and what would have to become true over the next three years to change your mind. No bibliography, and please do not soften the recommendation into a menu of options for me to pick from — nine of us have to raise our hands, and one of those hands is mine.
