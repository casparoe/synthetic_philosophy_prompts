So I'm helping with a small reading group for a bunch of undergrads who are into environmental philosophy and effective altruism at the same time, and every single session somebody drags the conversation back to whether habitat destruction might actually be good on net for the animals living in the habitat. It's genuinely useful stuff but the two camps just repeat the same slogans at each other — one side says "you're romanticizing nature, go read about what happens to nestlings," the other says "you're doing aesthetic valium masquerading as ethics." I want to hand the group a dialogue they can actually pick apart instead of two press releases, so I'd like you to write one for me.

Set it at a specific place: the two characters are walking the edge of a lowland fen that was drained a few years ago and is now being slowly colonised by scrub, plus there's an active excavation where the bunds are being put back in to re-flood part of it. Both characters are competent and both accept that the welfare of individual animals counts morally — I don't want one of them to be a mystic who thinks suffering is ennobling, and I don't want the other to be a naive bean-counter who thinks you can just tally up pain. One is a welfare-first person who has actually worked on fertility control and thinks the default presumption in favour of "leave nature alone" is a dodge; the other is a process-oriented ecologist who thinks the welfare leddger is being computed on a moving target and that the whole framing smuggles in a bunch of assumptions about baselines and counterfactuals that nobody ever states out loud. They should disagree about two related but distinguishable things: whether degrading or destroying a habitat could ever improve the world's welfare balance, and whether "welfare balance" is even the right unit of moral bookkeeping here.

Please have them hit the actual hard bits rather than circling: the fact that the animals who would have lived badly never exist if the habitat goes, so it's unclear who is being helped or harmed by their absence; whether short brutal death by predation is worse or better than a longer life that ends in starvation, disease or parasitism, and what evidence would even settle that; why the pre-human ecosystem is treated as the reference case when almost every ecosystem on earth is already managed; how you'd weight invertebrates and sheer numbers against a few mammals; and the asymmetry between an irreversible intervention and the option value of waiting. I'd like real back and forth where each of them lands at least one blow that makes the other revise or retreat slightly, and I'd like it to end without a winner but with a sharply stated crux — something like "here is the one empirical or normative fact that would flip me."

A few practical constraints: keep it to roughly 900–1300 words, use invented names for both characters, and let the physical setting do some work (they keep getting interrupted by the site itself, which is useful for pacing). Don't name-drop any real philosophers, books or papers and don't put anything in quotation marks as if quoting someone — everything should be in the characters' own words. A little dry humour is fine, snark isn't.
