I am seventeen and in my final year of high school, and for my school's independent research seminar I have been working on the money pump argument for transitive preferences. I follow the standard version: if my preferences run in a circle, a trader can walk me around the circle one small paid swap at a time, and I finish holding exactly what I started with and less money. What I cannot sort out is a harder question, which is how to tell the cases where a sure loss genuinely demonstrates irrationality from the cases where it does not. So I built a series of cases, recorded my honest verdicts on each, and would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

For all of the cases, assume the fees are small, the goods are things I keep in a closet rather than use, and the preferences stated are complete and stable unless I say otherwise.

1. The bare cycle. I own a red jacket. I prefer a blue one to the red, a green one to the blue, and — this is the circle — the red to the green. A consignment shop swaps jackets for a $1 handling fee. On Monday I trade red for blue, on Wednesday blue for green, on Friday green for red. I have the jacket I started with and $3 less, and I had no idea the later offers were coming. Verdict: irrational, and I want to say the defect lies in my standing preferences, not only in the episode.

2. The intense cycle. Exactly the same as case 1, except each preference is strong: I would pay up to $40 for each swap, and the fees are still $1. Verdict: worse than case 1, more irrational, even though the loss is identical. I am uneasy about this verdict, since a larger margin of preference seems to make each individual swap more defensible.

3. The enjoyable pump. Structurally identical to case 1, same $3, same final bundle, except that I happen to love haggling and swapping for its own sake, and the week was worth far more than $3 to me. Verdict: not irrational at all; arguably good sense. Yet the money moves exactly as it did in the case I condemned.

4. Drifting tastes. Over a month I trade a bicycle for a skateboard, the skateboard for a scooter, and the scooter back for the same bicycle, paying a $5 transfer fee each time: $15 gone, original bundle restored. At no single moment was there a circle in my preferences; each swap was precisely what I then wanted most, and my taste simply moved on afterward. I could not have seen the pattern from the start, and I never rode any of them. Verdict: wasteful and a little humiliating, but nothing irrational occurred, and I cannot identify a mistake anybody made.

5. The foreknowing walker. Same as case 1, except I see the whole sequence of offers at the outset and take all three anyway, reasoning each time that this swap alone is worth a dollar. Verdict: clearly worse than case 1.

6. The refuser. Same information as case 5, except I decline the first swap because I see where it leads. I keep the red jacket and my dollar, though I prefer blue to red and would have accepted the swap in isolation. Verdict: rational, even admirable. It bothers me that the wisdom of refusing seems to depend on offers I will never accept.

7. The unfinished circle. I make the first two swaps of case 1, then the shop closes and the third offer never arrives; meanwhile my taste settles permanently, green becomes my favorite and red drops to the bottom. I end with $2 less and the jacket I now like best. Verdict: nothing irrational happened. But those two swaps are the same two swaps I condemned in case 1, and the only difference is a fact about my later preferences that I did not control.

8. Somebody else pays. A friend covers the handling fees as a birthday present, so I go all the way around the circle and lose nothing whatsoever. Verdict: still irrational, because my preferences are still a circle. This is the case that makes me doubt that any principle stated in terms of losses can be correct.

9. Indifference. I am offered the identical swap many times over a year, I am exactly indifferent each time, and each acceptance costs a penny. I accept every time and finish with $3.65 less and precisely what I began with. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. Each acceptance seems harmless, since I never wanted the item less, but the pattern looks plainly foolish. I lean toward calling it irrational without knowing how to avoid implying that I was obliged to decline swaps that cost me nothing.

10. The subscription. Every few months I pay $5 to rejoin a service I canceled, because the present version of me wants in and next month's version wants out. I have now done this four times and know my own pattern perfectly well. Verdict: clearly irrational — and case 10 resembles case 4 except that here the drift oscillates instead of running in one direction, and here I could see it coming.

My first attempt at a principle is this: a course of choices is irrational if and only if, at the moment the course begins, the agent would have preferred some other whole course that was actually open to them, had they known exactly how everything would turn out. It handles cases 3, 5, 6, 7, and 10 in the way I want. But it condemns case 4, which I refuse to condemn, and the obvious repair — replacing perfect foresight with whatever evidence the agent actually had — condemns case 1 only if the walker could have known what was coming, which by stipulation they could not. So my principle seems to buy case 4 at the price of case 1, and I cannot see the third option.

What I would like: an assessment of where that principle breaks, on my own cases; one or at most two repaired candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply them mechanically to a case I have not thought of, with the particular case that forced each clause identified; what each candidate says about case 9 and about whether the strength of a preference, as in case 2, can ever matter; two further cases of your own that would separate the candidates, with each candidate's verdict on them; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, together with the single verdict you think I should give up. If the resolution is that irrationality sometimes sits in the preference structure rather than in the sequence of choices, please show me how to write that into a principle without collapsing it into a bare restatement of transitivity. I have come across the labels myopic, sophisticated, and resolute choice, and it would help to know which of these, if any, the winning principle amounts to.

I am not asking for citations, references, or a reading list; I would rather have the reasoning worked through than be pointed somewhere. Please write the response in American English, in American spelling and idiom, since that is the convention my seminar requires.
