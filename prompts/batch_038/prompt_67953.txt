The international system declares famines technically. A standardized classification framework sets numerical thresholds for a given area and period — roughly a fifth of households facing extreme food deprivation, extreme levels of acute child malnutrition, and a crude death rate above two per ten thousand people per day — and a review committee of independent experts, whose mandate is to assess strictly on technical and scientific criteria, certifies whether those thresholds have been crossed. The verdict is a statement about the condition of a population: nothing in it asks who produced that condition, deliberately or through neglect, and the usual sequel to a declaration is a humanitarian funding appeal rather than any accountability process. Yet the same legal order treats the intentional starvation of civilians as a method of warfare as a war crime, and the agencies that operate the classification routinely invoke Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis, on which famines are collapses in people's command over food rather than shortfalls in its supply — a diagnosis that points at institutions, and therefore at agents.

What conception of famine does the declaration practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the practice require that a famine be a threshold state of affairs in the world, analogous to a magnitude reading, or does it presuppose only that such states are measurable where agency is not? Separate what it strictly commits us to from what it merely sits well with — evidential caution about attributing intent, donor politics, the operational need for a bright-line trigger. Then say whether technical neutrality can be squared with the war-crime and entitlement framings endorsed by the same institutions, and what would have to change in the criteria, in who certifies them, and in what certification licenses, if famine were classified as a crime rather than a catastrophe.
