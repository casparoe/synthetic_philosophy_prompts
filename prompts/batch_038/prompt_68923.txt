My roommate's intro psycholinguistics reader has a sidebar on the poverty of the stimulus and I want to know how much of it survives contact with someone who actually works on this. It reads:

> Chomsky's argument starts from the character of the child's data. The speech children hear is degenerate -- full of false starts, fragments and slips -- and, more importantly, nobody ever tells them what is ungrammatical. Parents correct for truthfulness, not for structure, so the input contains no negative evidence at all. That means the child cannot rule out a large class of wrong hypotheses about its own language. Consider how a yes/no question gets formed. A child who took the rule to be "move the first auxiliary you come to" would get the right answer most of the time, but for "the dog that was barking is in the yard" it would produce something like "Was the dog that barking is in the yard?" Crucially, children essentially never hear questions built on a subject that itself contains an auxiliary, and are never corrected if they produce one, so nothing in their experience could tell them the linear rule is wrong. Gold's theorem puts this on a rigorous footing: it is mathematically impossible to learn a language of this kind from positive examples alone. So the structure-dependent rule has to be part of the innate endowment, and that is why the empiricist picture of the mind as a blank slate is false.
