So here's the thing that's been eating at me. My brother-in-law is one of those guys who watches a lot of history stuff on YouTube, and at Thanksgiving he went in hard on how the whole Nuremberg business was pure victors justice — the charges were invented after the fact, the judges and prosecutors all came from the winning side, and the guys in the dock weren't allowed to bring up anything the Allies had done, so the entire thing was theater and every international court since then inherits the contamination. My girlfriend said basically the opposite: these people ran extermination camps, who cares about a technicality, and the moment you start pointing at what the winners did you're doing apologia. I felt like both of them were being lazy, but in different directions, and I couldnt articulate how. Rather than keep arguing I want the specification first — what would a process for charging somebody with an international crime after a war actually have to deliver to be legitimate, especially in the hardest case where the conduct wasn't clearly a crime in any statute book beforehand?

So: give me the requirements list. For each one, the consideration or the concrete case behind it — why it's a requirement and not just a vibe. Then tell me which are non-negotiable and which are really just the taste of one camp, like the people who insist on strict no-crime-without-prior-law versus the people who say the wrongness was obvious to any moral agent and demanding a published statute for genocide is a category error. And the part I want most: which subsets of these requirements can't all be satisfied at once. My suspicion is there's a triangle that closes on itself — the law has to predate the conduct, the court has to reach the worst offenders rather than just whoever happens to be in your custody, and the rules have to bind the winners too — and that after a total war where only one side has surrendered you structurally can't have all three. If that's right, say it plainly and say which one has to give. If it's not right, tell me where my triangle is badly drawn.

Some of the stuff I ran into that I'd want the list to actually handle, since I think it complicates the easy version of both sides' stories: the Allies' own 1943 declaration on atrocities mostly contemplated shipping offenders back to the country where the deed happened to be judged there under local law, with the top-tier offenders whose crimes had no particular geographic location to be dealt with by joint decision of the allied governments — note that it says punished by joint decision, not tried by a court. The realistic alternative on the table in 1944 wasn't a fair trial, it was compiling a list and shooting the people on it, which the British and at least one of Roosevelt's cabinet guys favored before the Americans talked everyone into a courtroom. The charter told the tribunal to take judicial notice of official government reports without proof, which is the mechanism the Soviets leaned on when they tried to pin the Katyn massacre on the Germans, and the charge just evaporated from the final judgment with nobody on the prosecution side willing to say out loud why. The submarine commander got a written statement from the American Pacific fleet commander confirming the US had waged unrestricted submarine warfare from day one of the Pacific war, the court admitted it, and it seems to have softened his sentence — meanwhile the general plea of "you did it too" was rejected as a defense. An American lawyer's memo got whole organizations declared criminal so that membership alone could then be used to process thousands of people later, which is an efficiency move dressed as a legal one. The defense's sharpest point wasn't about the camps at all, it was that the anti-war pact of the 1920s banned aggressive war but attached no penalty to anybody, so the aggression count had to be manufactured — and at Tokyo one of the judges dissented across the board on exactly that ground. I want a standard that can be pointed at the present-day international criminal court too, including the complaint that for its first decade it indicted basically only Africans while the big powers stayed out of it, so the list has to be about international criminal justice as a practice, not special pleading for 1945. End with two or three sentences on which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and whether the standard package of defenses of Nuremberg survives your list — but keep that tail short, the list is the point. No sources or reading recommendations, I'm not writing a paper.
