I'm in the second term of a fourth-year seminar on decision and game theory, and in our third week we spent a long session on cooperation in one-shot games — dominance reasoning, the folk-theorem contrast with repeated interaction, and, at one student's request, the idea of superrationality, on which two agents who reason correctly and know that the other is reasoning correctly about an identically structured problem are somehow licensed to expect one another's choice rather than merely to hope for it. Our instructor presented it sympathetically but flagged it as contentious, and the room split. What follows is an exchange I overheard afterward in the corridor between two of the people who had been arguing in the seminar — Nadia, a philosophy student writing her thesis on practical rationality, and Owen, a fourth-year taking the course jointly from computer science and economics. I had been listening, could not decide between them, and left before either had budged, so I would like a careful adjudication rather than a general essay on the topic.

The case they were discussing was the one the instructor had put on the board: two people are separately offered a choice between cooperating and defecting in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Neither can communicate with the other, and neither can bind the other; there is no repetition and no enforcement. The payoffs have the usual structure, so that each prefers the other's cooperation and each does better defecting whatever the other does. Nadia and Owen disagreed about what a rational agent facing that matrix should do and about whether the disagreement has any content at all.

Nadia's opening move was this. (1) She said the standard dominance argument secretly restricts attention to two of the four cells of the matrix — the two off-diagonal ones — and that this restriction is unwarranted here. If both agents reason correctly about a problem they know to be structurally identical, then, she said, they cannot arrive at different conclusions; reasoning is not a lottery, and two people working through the same well-posed problem in the same competent way land in the same place. So the only outcomes genuinely available are mutual cooperation and mutual defection, and of those two, cooperation is better for both. On her account, defecting is not merely risky; it is incoherent, because it is choosing on the basis of a state of the world that the chooser's own reasoning has already ruled out.

Owen's reply. (2) He said this is magical thinking dressed in formal notation. What one agent does in a sealed room cannot affect what the other does; if the payoffs really are those of a prisoner's dilemma, then whatever the partner chooses, defecting pays more, and no story about how the two of them are reasoning can change that fact. He added that this is exactly why causal decision theory is the default in the field, and that Nadia's argument only looks persuasive because she is smuggling in the intuition that her choice is somehow reaching across to her partner's, which it plainly is not.

Nadia's second move. (3) She said he had misdescribed her position: she was making no claim about influence, and would happily grant that there is no causal connection between the two rooms. The correlation she was appealing to is not causal but logical, she said, of the same kind that holds between two people who independently work through the same well-posed puzzle and get the same answer. It can be entirely rational, she added, to be uncertain about what your partner will do even while being confident that whatever you conclude, they will conclude too, because your uncertainty is uncertainty about what your own reasoning will yield; and once you allow that, evidential bearing is enough to make the diagonal outcomes the relevant ones.

Owen's counter. (4) He said that concession is what sinks her, not what saves her. If you are permitted to act on the evidential bearing of your own choice, then you are committed to a general policy of acting on evidence about your own actions, and he produced the familiar case of the smoker whose urge to smoke and whose lesion are both produced by a common cause she cannot influence: on Nadia's principle she should take the evidence and light up, which is absurd. He also said Nadia's talk of logical correlation is unstable in a second way, because the partner's reasoning is fixed once the partner's reasoning is fixed; if she claims to be uncertain about what a correct reasoner will conclude, she is either confessing that she is not one, or she is claiming uncertainty about something that is not a matter of chance at all. He concluded that the doctrine is either incoherent or, at best, a piece of wishful thinking with a nice formal wrapper.

Nadia's third move. (5) She said he was attacking a much stronger thesis than the one she held. On her account, superrationality was never a claim about every opponent one might face; it is indexed to the agent's own assessment of who else in the situation is reasoning as she is. Against someone she has good reason to think is reasoning the same way, cooperation is rational; against someone she has no such reason to think that of, defection is rational and no paradox attaches. So the view does not predict universal cooperation and is not refuted by any case in which a partner defects, because a defecting partner was simply not in the relevant class. The empirical record, she said, gives Owen nothing to bite on.

Owen's closing move. (6) He said that retreat empties the view of content. The probability Nadia now builds the whole doctrine on — that the partner reasons as she does — cannot be assessed independently of the decision it is supposed to inform, because in a one-shot game with a publicly known matrix, the only evidence anyone has about the partner's reasoning is the reasoning one is oneself doing. If you already assign high probability to your partner's cooperating, you do not need superrationality to tell you that cooperation is attractive; if you do not, it tells you to defect. Either it never delivers a verdict that differs from ordinary prudence, he said, or it delivers one only by assuming the very thing in dispute. In both cases, he concluded, there is no position left to defend.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say who is right at each step, rather than only declaring an overall winner. For each of the six moves, assign a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the rating in two or four sentences that engage with the specific argument made rather than with the label attached to it. Then answer three further questions. First, is Owen's charge in move (4) that acting on the evidential bearing of one's own choice commits Nadia to endorsing the smoker's reasoning actually available to him, given that in the case Nadia has in mind the correlation between the two decisions is not produced by any common cause in the agents' histories — or does that difference fail to matter for the reason he would give? Second, does move (6) succeed in showing that the view is empty, or does it show only that the view is difficult to state without circularity, and if the latter, what exactly would a non-circular statement have to look like? Third, and most importantly, tell me what both of them missed. My own suspicion, which I could not defend in the corridor and which you should discard if it is wrong, is that they were treating a decision-theoretic disagreement as though it were a question about the metaphysics of influence, and that at least one of them was describing the view under attack rather than the view actually held. Please say explicitly whether that suspicion is right, and identify at least one live question about this case — for instance, whether the agents should be reasoning about single acts or about the rules they would choose to follow — that neither of them so much as raised. I would rather have a clear verdict with its costs stated than a diplomatic summary of both positions.
