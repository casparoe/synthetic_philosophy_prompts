I am a novelist in the second year of a book about a consortium of eight firms that jointly draw down a shared resource, and I have come to you for a classificatory instrument rather than for prose. I should describe my setup at some length, because its structure is not quite any of the ones I have read about, and the point of the exercise is to sort positions on *this* structure rather than on the textbook version of it.

The situation in the novel has four features that matter.

First, all eight firms individually prefer a world in which every firm restrains its draw to a world in which none does, and restraint, once general, is not a burden anyone secretly wishes to shed: no firm profits by quietly resuming extraction after the others have stopped. Second, arriving there requires one firm to go first — to retire capacity publicly, at a cost that would ruin it if the others did not follow within a season. That first mover's loss is visible, irreversible, and cannot be undone by a change of heart. Third, and this is the wrinkle I care about most, the firms do not agree that they are in this situation at all. Two believe the resource regenerates faster than the consortium's own models suggest, and so see no trap, only a market. One believes it is not genuinely a member — it holds its licence from a different authority and considers itself exempt. A fourth knows the resource is failing but believes the others do not know that it knows, and reasons about their reasoning accordingly. The question "are we trapped?" is itself contested inside the game. Fourth, exit is permitted but oddly costly: a firm may withdraw and continue to draw, yet on withdrawing it forfeits its right to inspect the others' operations, and so forfeits any ability to verify what they subsequently claim.

I have been told that situations of this general shape are called multipolar traps or races to the bottom, and that mine resembles a stag hunt more than a prisoner's dilemma. I am not confident the resemblance survives my additions. Before the main deliverable, then, I want a short plain answer to a prior question: which named game or dilemma does my structure most closely resemble, and at which of my four features does that resemblance break down?

The main deliverable is a decision tree that sorts a person — one of my characters, or a reader who has been handed the setup cold — onto a named position about whether such a trap can be escaped, and if so by what means. Please observe the following constraints.

Build it from the logical space, not from a list of schools. I want six to nine branch nodes, each a single question answerable from the setup above by someone who has no idea where the answer leads. No node may ask what a person is called or which tradition they sympathise with; nodes should turn on structural features — whether an outcome that is stable once reached still needs policing to be reached; whether repetition changes anything given that a defector can conceal itself by exiting; whether disagreement about who is in the game can be settled by argument or only by demonstration; whether the first mover's ruin can be made survivable by side payments from the others; whether the number of players or their beliefs is doing the real work; whether what must change is the players' options or their desires.

The leaves must include, at minimum: the view that nothing escapes without a sovereign power able to punish; the view that self-governing communities solve such problems without one, by means of monitoring and sanctions that escalate; the view that repeated interaction and reciprocity suffice on their own; the view that the trap simply dissolves when technique makes restraint cheap or a substitute abundant; the view that a small subgroup or club can act where the whole cannot; the view that a single dominant actor may bear the cost for reasons of its own; the view that what must be altered is not the game but what the players want; and the deflationary view that there is no trap at all, only a sequence of decisions each of which someone makes for reasons. Add any further leaves the space of my setup calls for — I suspect at least one must concern the epistemic layer specifically, the view that the trap is constituted by the disagreement about whether there is a trap, and that resolving the disagreement is therefore not a preliminary to escape but the escape itself. Each leaf should carry its name, or the word "unnamed" if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment given my four features.

Then walk the space the tree defines. Which combinations of answers does it permit that no leaf occupies? For each empty cell, tell me whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been tried. I am hunting for a character, and an unexplored cell is worth considerably more to me than an unattractive one. Also flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate, rather than papering over them; I particularly want to know whether the substitution-or-abundance leaf and the change-their-desires leaf come apart on any of my branch questions, or whether my instrument collapses them into a single undifferentiated optimism.

Because I intend to write these people, close each leaf with one behavioural tell: something such a person does in a meeting — how they use the agenda, what document they ask to see, whom they decline to argue with, when they leave the room — that reveals the position without anyone naming it.

Two notes on form. My research assistant has no training in economics or game theory, so define any term of art on first use. And give me no bibliography, no reading list, no page numbers and no publication years; where you attach a thinker's name to a leaf, do so only if you are confident the label is genuinely theirs, and otherwise coin a descriptive name of your own. Something I can hold in one sitting — call it 1,500 to 2,200 words.

Finally, an epistemic request, which I would ask you not to omit. State how confident you are in the tree as a whole: which branch question you are least sure actually separates one leaf from another, which attribution of a leaf to a named thinker you would most expect a specialist to correct, and whether any of my four features is one the literature would regard as a restatement of something familiar rather than a genuine complication. If my wrinkle is not a wrinkle, I would much rather be told so now than discover it in a draft.
