I teach a graduate seminar in social epistemology, and this term we have worked through the epistemic defence of majority rule in some detail. For our final meeting I would rather read something aloud than circulate a summary, and I have settled on the Condorcet jury theorem as its subject. I should like you to write the poem, and I should like it as a pantoum of six quatrains, observing the traditional closure in which the final stanza recovers the opening stanza's third line as its own second line and the opening line as its own last line, so that the poem ends on the words it began with.

I have chosen this form deliberately, because its machinery happens to match the proof we have been reading. Each quatrain of a pantoum contributes two new lines while inheriting two from its predecessor, and the standard argument for the theorem likewise admits voters two at a time, keeping the assembly odd so that no tie-breaking rule is needed, then asks what the newcomers can accomplish: they overturn a wrong majority only if both of them are right, and they overturn a right majority only if both of them are wrong. Please exploit that correspondence knowingly rather than incidentally. Let the inherited lines carry the verdict accumulated so far and the two fresh lines be the pair just let into the room, so that a reader feels the assembly growing quatrain by quatrain, and let the closure stand for the limit: the many converging, at last, upon what the one began by saying.

The content must be exact enough that my students recognise the theorem rather than a vague crowd-is-wise sentiment. At minimum the poem should register that there is a fact of the matter about the question put to the jury; that each member is likelier than not to find it, but only barely, so that what is being amplified is a slender edge rather than expertise; that the members are supposed to arrive at their verdicts separately, out of earshot of one another; and the two conclusions, that the larger assembly is likelier to be right than the smaller, and that the majority approaches certainty without limit as the body grows. It should also carry the darker mirror that students reliably forget: strip away the edge and the same arithmetic runs backwards, so that the best jury available is a jury of one. I would prefer no numerals, no formulae, and no naming of Condorcet unless you can place his name once without turning the stanza into a footnote. The register should be plain and slightly austere; I do not want a poem that winks at its reader.

Finally, and this is the part I care about most, the pantoum should understand that it is itself a defective jury. Its lines are not independent: every quatrain recycles what has already been said, so the body swells without acquiring information, and the form's own ideal — that a repeated line should shift its meaning while keeping its words — is precisely what correlated voters fail to do. Let the closing couplet be readable both ways at once, as convergence on the truth and as convergence on the first speaker's error, with nothing in the poem to settle which has happened. My own seminar supplied the model. In November we voted on whether to move our last three meetings online, and it emerged afterwards that every one of us had read the same departmental memorandum, in the same order, that morning. Twenty-two ballots and one opinion. I should like the poem to know what that feels like from the inside.
