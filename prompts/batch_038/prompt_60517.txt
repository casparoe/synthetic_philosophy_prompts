I am writing a novel whose central set piece is a public panel at which a SETI programme is being dismantled, and I have been attending real lectures so that the arguments in it will sound like arguments rather than gestures. At the last one, a sceptic in the audience put the case that if technological civilisations were at all common and at all expansionist we would by now have found artefacts, waste heat, or radio leakage, and that we have found none of these; the astronomer on the panel replied, with more heat than precision, that absence of evidence is evidence of absence, and the room took it for a rebuttal. What troubles me is that I have heard the inverted form of the same sentence repeated just as confidently as a folk warning, which makes me suspect the line is being asked to do work nobody has shown it can do.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence might be making — including the weakest one, which may be nothing more than a remark about likelihoods, any stronger one that would license ruling a possibility out altogether, ones that turn on how much of the relevant parameter space has actually been searched and on what would be detectable even in principle, and ones that quietly swap the hypothesis tested for the hypothesis under dispute — and, for each, say whether it is true, whether it is interesting, and whether it is strong enough to have answered that sceptic? Please commit rather than balance: I want a definite judgement on which readings survive and on what the astronomer is actually entitled to claim after six decades of silence, not a neutral menu I have to adjudicate myself.

Answer in these sections, in this order: "The claim in its weakest honest form" (about 150 words); "Readings" (about 700 words, numbered, each ending in a one-line verdict — platitudinous / true but inert / true and load-bearing / false); "What the exchange actually needed" (about 200 words); "My verdict" (about 120 words).
