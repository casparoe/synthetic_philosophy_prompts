Reading The Remains of the Day with a book group and I can't stop thinking about Adam Smith's impartial spectator — the idea that we judge our own conduct by imagining how a well-informed, uninvolved bystander would see it. Stevens is the most bystander-obsessed narrator I know, except his bystander is Lord Darlington. So is he a case of Smith's spectator working, or of it breaking? I want the reading to take seriously that Smith thinks self-command and dignity can be genuinely admirable and not just repression, and that he also has an account of how we fool ourselves with the help of general rules — Stevens seems to use "dignity" exactly that way. Point me at two or three scenes that are load-bearing here: the night his father dies, the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids, whatever else you think decides it. And end by saying whether the novel shows Smith something his theory cant handle — like whether a spectator can be too impartial, so detached from the life its judging that there's nobody left inside to do the judging.
