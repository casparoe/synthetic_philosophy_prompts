I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I've been sitting on a piece about the DNA-as-code metaphor for a couple of months now. The hook I was handed was synthetic biology — labs rebuilding bacteria with reassigned codons, so a triplet that used to mean "stop" now picks up some amino acid nature never bothered with — and the more I reported it, the more it felt like everyone I talked to was using "code" and "information" for three or four completely different things without noticing. What I want from you isn't a survey of that debate and definitely not a reading list; this is a magazine piece, I'm not citing anything, so give me reasoning rather than references. What I want is a case built to order that pries the senses apart.

So: build me two organisms. Both should be ordinary, plausible-ish biology — no magic, no designers, no aliens — and in both some polymer plays exactly the developmental role DNA plays in us: it's copied, it's read off, its products build the organism, and copying errors are heritable. Variant A: the mapping from sequence to building blocks is systematically non-arbitary. Which residue shows up is fixed by chemical affinity between the triplet and the molecule, roughly the way people speculate the real code partly got started; there's no contingency in the assignment, and the mapping is universal, so any chemist anywhere would predict the same table. Variant B: flip it. The mapping is thoroughly arbitrary — no physicochemical reason any given triplet has to deliver any given residue — but it's private. No two lineages share it, it drifts fast enough that even close relatives disagree, and there's no way in principle for an outsider to learn the key, only to reverse-engineer one organism at a time, laboriously. Keep each variant to a couple of paragraphs, and don't announce the verdicts in the setup; the point is that they should fall out of the theories rather than get stipulated.

Then do the work on them. For each variant, run the four positions I keep hearing in this debate and say what each concludes about whether the organism contains a code, carries information, or means anything: (1) the hardline literal-code view, on which a code needs an arbitrary mapping plus a shared convention, so it's basically a cipher and DNA only counts by courtesy; (2) the natural-code view, which drops the convention requirement and says what matters is that the mapping could have been otherwise and is maintained by an evolved decoding apparatus; (3) the selection-based semantic view, on which a sequence has content because there's machinery whose job, fixed by natural selection, is to interpret it; and (4) the deflationary view, on which all this "information" is just ordinary causation dressed up, and the metaphor does real damage by smuggling in a sender, a message, and a meaning that were never there. For each of the four, give me the verdict in both variants — and, this is the part I actually need — name which single feature of the case is doing the work for that verdict. Is it arbitrariness? Commonality? The presence of readers? Something about error and heritability? Then tell me what happens to each verdict if I surgically remove that one feature. Finish with the strongest objection a defender of (4) would make to my whole exercise — I'm expecting something along the lines of "you built the decoding machinery in by hand and then acted surprised when it looked semantic" — and say whether that objection lands, or whether the A/B contrast is doing something it can't absorb. And if you think the two senses of "code" I'm trying to separate don't genuinely come apart in these cases and I've designed a dud, say so plainly and tell me what tweak would fix it.
