In a number of jurisdictions, mental health law recognises a device usually called a psychiatric advance directive or, when it does something stronger, a self-binding or "Ulysses" directive. A patient with a recurrent condition — bipolar disorder, say, or a psychosis that returns in recognisable episodes — signs a document while well. In it she consents in advance to specific measures she expects to refuse later: compulsory admission, depot medication, a particular ward. When the episode comes and she refuses, struggling and insisting that she is perfectly fine and wants to go home, the clinicians may treat her over that refusal, invoking her own earlier signature as the authority for doing so. Some versions of the scheme add two further features. The directive must be re-signed at fixed intervals while the patient is well, and lapses automatically if she does not re-sign it. And it may nominate a proxy — a family member, a named clinician — empowered to decide, without further appeal, when the patient has become the person the directive was written against.

Meanwhile, in the same body of law, a competent adult's contemporaneous refusal of treatment is treated as conclusive: the settled doctrine is that you may not treat a patient who refuses while she has capacity, however badly she will fare as a result. Advance refusals of treatment are generally binding on clinicians, whereas advance requests for treatment generally are not — a patient cannot, by writing it down in advance, compel a doctor to provide a treatment the doctor thinks inappropriate.

I would like an analysis of what this practice philosophically presupposes.

- Reconstruct the implicit theory of authority. When the directive overrides the episode-self, what exactly is doing the normative work: the fact that a past act of will occurred, or the fact that it occurred under conditions the practice counts as good ones — calm, informed, undistorted, unafraid? Does the practice presuppose that deliberation under favourable conditions has a special standing that deliberation under unfavourable ones lacks, and if so, is that standing a matter of knowledge, of rationality, of authenticity, or of something else?
- Does the practice require an idealised account of a person's good — that her interests are fixed by what she would want under full information and clear reasoning — or would a less demanding account license it? Does it require any particular view of personal identity, such as the sufficiency of psychological continuity, or the denial that the episode-self is in any sense a separate person? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly cannot do without from what it merely sits comfortably alongside.
- Who idealises the idealiser? The directive is usually drafted in conversation with a clinician who shapes its vocabulary, its triggers, and its scope. Does the practice presuppose that this shaping is benign, and what does that assumption imply about whose deliberation is really being trusted? Relatedly, does it presuppose that the delegated procedure reliably converges on the answer the well-self would have given had she been present — and what follows for the practice if the two come apart systematically?
- The proxy who decides, unreviewably, that the patient has become the person the directive was written against: what does installing such a judge presuppose about the relationship between a person's values and her behaviour?
- What does the periodic re-signing requirement presuppose — that the authority of a deliberation decays with time, or that what matters is a continuing disposition rather than a completed act? Would the practice still hang together if directives never lapsed but the patient's values had demonstrably changed?
- Is the practice consistent with the same system's insistence that a competent contemporaneous refusal is absolute, and with the asymmetry between binding refusals and non-binding requests? If there is a coherent picture that reconciles all three, state it; if not, say which piece has to give.
- Where is the practice underdetermined? Set out at least three distinct theories — for instance one built on precedent autonomy, one on idealised desire, one on the narrative unity of a life, one on which the directive is not an order at all but a mandate to an agent — each of which would license it, and say what observable differences in the design of the scheme would distinguish them.
- Finally: suppose we replaced the idealised-deliberation picture with a current-welfare view on which the only subject of interests is the person present in the episode. What would have to change in the practice, and is there any recognisable version of the directive that survives the substitution?
