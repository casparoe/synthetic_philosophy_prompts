I'm three years into a political philosophy PhD and this summer I picked up some paid RA work at a public-policy school — mostly literature wrangling, but last week they dragged me onto a regulatory impact assessment and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. The unit's whole job is putting dollar figures on things that have no price: they monetize a small reduction in traffic fatality risk at something in the neighborhood of ten million dollars per statistical life, they attach a per-tonne number to carbon, they value an hour of a commuter's time as a fraction of the local wage, they put a number on the option value of a wetland nobody visits, and then they rank projects by net present value. When there's no market, they go hunting for the shadow of one — the wage premium workers accept for riskier jobs, the gradient in house prices near a polluting facility, what people pay for an extra bedroom inside a school catchment. I asked my supervisor what makes those outputs prices at all, and he said, roughly, that a price just is an opportunity cost and opportunity cost is what they're estimating. Fine, except the single number that moves the ranking more than any other — the discount rate — isn't read off any market whatsoever, it's a normative pick, and it still ends up in the report with the same straight-faced authority as the fatality figure.

The awkward part is that I've spent the last year reading the calculation debate. On one side: Mises and Hayek insisting that prices aren't the sort of thing anyone can compute, that they're the residue of a discovery process involving real ownership and actual rivalry, so a planner sitting on every available statistic still doesn't know what anything is worth — socialism as impossible, not merely inefficient. On the other: Lange and Lerner saying sure, run it as a trial-and-error pricing board, adjust for excess demand and excess supply, and you've simulated the whole epistemic function. What I want help with is figuring out which side shadow-price cost-benefit analysis is quietly on. Is it a Lange-style position in bureaucratic drag, committing the agency to the view that the relevant price information can be assembled from data plus expert judgment rather than generated by people risking their own money? Or could a Hayekian sign off on it as a second-best heuristic, on the grounds that the agency isn't pricing an economy, only its own decisions, and that it's parasitically borrowing whatever information real markets do produce? And there's the same trick in tax law, where authorities police intra-firm transfer prices by asking what two unrelated strangers would have agreed to for a transaction that by construction could never have happened on a market — is that a further confirmation of the underlying view or a separate problem?

Here's what I actually want out of you. Reconstruct the theory of prices, and of economic knowledge, that the practice presupposes — spelled out, not in the vocabulary the practitioners use. Then separate what it strictly can't do without from what it merely sits comfortably alongside: does it really need cardinal interpersonal comparability, or a fact of the matter about what a life is worth independent of any transaction, or just a defensible-enough number for a spreadsheet? Tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several rival theories would license exactly the same numbers. Then take the internal tensions I listed — the non-market discount rate carrying market-grade authority, guidance that admits the estimates swing by a factor of several across studies while the figures get defended in court as though they were findings of fact, monetizing a life and an hour of time but declining to monetize certain distributional harms and relegating them to a qualitative annex — and say which of those are genuine incoherences on any reconstruction and which are just untidy but livable. Finally, run the counterfactual: if an agency actually internalized the Austrian picture instead of the neoclassical one, what would its work product look like — ranges and no point estimates, deliberation instead of aggregation, market analogues required before any number is used, something else — and would that count as an improvement or as giving up on the thing that made the numbers useful in the first place?
