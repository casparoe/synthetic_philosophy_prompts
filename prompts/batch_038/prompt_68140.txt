I'm co-teaching a cross-listed session on the mid-century English exchange over legal moralism — Lord Devlin's case for enforcing a society's shared morality, written in reaction to the 1957 Wolfenden committee's recommendation that homosexual conduct between consenting adults in private be decriminalized, and Hart's reply — and the room is split between law students and people training to work in trust-and-safety policy. To get the second group to engage, I want the argument transposed into their own institutional genre before class, so that they meet it as something that could plausibly land in their inbox next quarter rather than as a museum piece. Could you write a mock internal policy document, 900–1,100 words, for a fictional mid-sized community platform (call it Hearth, roughly forty million monthly users), issued by its Office of Community Policy to its Trust & Safety Review Board, under a title like "Community Cohesion Standard: A Framework for Enforcement"? Give it the usual apparatus — version number, owner, review date — and organize it into numbered sections: Purpose; Definitions; the Cohesion Thesis; Detecting the Standard; Scope and the Question of Privacy; Proportionality; a Worked Example; Effective Date.

The reasoning in those sections should track Devlin's argument step by step, translated rather than summarized: Hearth is not an aggregate of accounts but a structure of shared standards; those standards are the least visible part of the platform and are noticed chiefly in their breach; a platform that loses them does not merely change but ceases to be the platform its users joined; therefore enforcement is not one product function among others but a condition of there being a platform at all; and conduct that breaches the shared standard should be handled the way Hearth handles attempts to subvert the system from within, which is to say without requiring an identified victim. Have the "Detecting the Standard" section operationalize this quantitatively — the reactions Devlin treated as evidence of a moral consensus rendered as report volume per impression, negative-sentiment ratio, and distress-weighted reach — and define the reasonable user in a way that turns out, on the document's own arithmetic, to be whichever accounts file the most reports. Build that tension in silently; the memo must never notice it. Let the Proportionality section be genuinely moderate — enforcement should lag change, spare any whole way of life, leave a margin of tolerance — so the document is better than a strawman. For the Worked Example, adjudicate an account posting a weekly photo series of a lawful, adult, consensual body-modification practice, with no minors, no commerce, no solicitation, and no user who can name an injury to themselves: show the framework removing it by running its own numbers, not by any independent judgment about the practice.

Then append, clearly labeled, a dissent of 400–500 words from one member of the Review Board — a former philosophy graduate student, now a policy lead — who is permitted exactly one oblique reference to the mid-century English exchange and must otherwise argue only from Hearth's own figures and prior policy history. The dissent should make Hart's moves without announcing them as such: that a measurement of what users currently feel is not a justification for anything, and that the document never distinguishes the standards people happen to hold from the ones that survive reflection; that the cohesion-and-collapse claim is an empirical prediction which the platform has effectively tested several times (past removal waves, a large migration cohort, a retired policy) without the predicted disintegration, and which the memo supports with no data; that the analogy to subversion fails because subversion aims at the platform's existence while a photo series does not; that the reasonable user has quietly been replaced by the aggrieved user, since the older standard was meant as a normative construction — a juror's yardstick — rather than an aggregate of the loudest reporters; that the assertion that everything on the platform is inside the platform is not an argument, and that the difference between conduct exposed to strangers and conduct that is not continues to do work even within a private venue; and finally that a community is not identical to the set of opinions its members currently hold, so that wholesale change in its standards need not be its death.

Throughout, keep the register deadpan and the satire entirely in the fit: no winking asides, no authorial nudges, no joke that depends on the reader knowing which philosopher is being transposed. Do not put verbatim quotations from any real text in either document, and do not cite books, cases, statutes, or thinkers in the memo at all — it should reference only internal precedent, user research, and prior review-board findings. No footnotes, no bibliography, no reading suggestions at the end.
