I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on whether knowledge is closed under known entailment, and the example I built the chapter around provoked an argument between two members of my writing group that I could not referee. The chapter goes to my committee in ten days, so I need to know whether the example survives.

The example: Nils, watching a feeder through binoculars in good light, believes the bird there is a golden-crowned kinglet. On Nozick's tracking account, that belief is sensitive if he wouldn't believe it in the nearest situations in which the bird isn't a kinglet; his belief in a related proposition — that the bird isn't a realistic robotic decoy planted by a rival birder — looks clearly insensitive, since a good decoy would present itself to him in just the same way. Being a kinglet entails not being a decoy, so we get knowledge of the first without knowledge of the second: closure fails, yet Nils keeps his ordinary knowledge. I intended this to show how a tracking theorist can block the skeptic without giving up the everyday.

The argument:

(1) Owen said the example backfires, because the conjunction it licenses is one no one can hold: Nils knows it's a kinglet and knows that being a kinglet rules out being a decoy, yet on my account he doesn't know it isn't a decoy. A theory that requires that hasn't answered skepticism, it has merely restated it.

(2) Marta said it hasn't restated anything, it has blocked it: the skeptic needs closure to travel from "you don't know it isn't a decoy" to "you don't know it's a kinglet," and denying closure removes exactly that step.

(3) Owen said the step is only removed because I've quietly fixed a method. Leave the method coarse, and the nearest situations in which the bird isn't a kinglet include decoy situations, so the kinglet belief isn't sensitive either and the theory delivers skepticism after all. Fix the method finely — binoculars, good light, that feeder — and I've smuggled my conclusion in by stipulation.

(4) Marta said that isn't stipulation but how comparative similarity works: a situation where a ruby-crowned kinglet visits the same feeder instead requires a small change, whereas a situation where someone has manufactured and hidden a robot bird requires a far larger one, and nearness tracks that rather than what a skeptic finds vivid.

(5) Owen said that makes knowledge hostage to imagination. Describe a season in which a well-funded rival has been planting decoys at feeders across the whole county, and now decoy situations are close, Nils's belief is insensitive, and he knows nothing. My account, he said, cannot show the skeptic wrong about our world; it can only show her wrong about worlds she hasn't bothered to describe.

(6) Marta said that is a feature: whether one knows depends on which error possibilities are actually live nearby, and admitting this is the only honest answer to what knowledge is, not a surrender.

Could you go through the exchange move by move rather than simply naming a winner — say where each of them is right and where they overreach, and in particular whether (3) really poses a dilemma, whether (4) is a principled criterion of nearness or a stipulation in disguise, and whether (6) is a concession Marta can afford to make? Close with your overall verdict on the example, and with whatever both of them are missing about it.
