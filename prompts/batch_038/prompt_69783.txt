I would like a case analysis of the 1980 Simon–Ehrlich wager, treated not as an episode in the history of environmental debate but as a worked problem in the epistemology of long-horizon prediction. Here are the facts I take to be settled, so that the analysis need not depend on recalling them: Ehrlich's 1968 book *The Population Bomb* opened by declaring that the effort to feed humanity had already been lost and that hundreds of millions would starve to death during the 1970s whatever crash programs were undertaken, and Ehrlich had publicly said he would take even money that England would not exist in the year 2000. Simon, skeptical of such forecasts, challenged him in the pages of a social science journal to stake money on the claim; Ehrlich and his colleagues selected five metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin and tungsten — bought a notional $200 of each at the prices of 29 September 1980, and set 29 September 1990 as the settlement date, with the inflation-adjusted value of the basket deciding the winner. All five fell in real terms. Over the same decade world population grew by more than 800 million, the largest ten-year increase on record. Ehrlich paid Simon $576.07. Afterwards he maintained that metal prices were the wrong instrument, that renewable resources were the indicators that mattered, that those same five metals had risen between 1950 and 1975, and that the window had been unlucky; Simon offered to raise the stake to $20,000 on any resource at any date, and Ehrlich with a climatologist colleague counter-proposed a bundle of fifteen environmental trends, but no second wager was settled before Simon died. Later recomputations across rolling windows found that Simon's side would have won well under half of the possible twentieth-century ten-year periods, that 1980–90 was among the worst of them for him, and that over 1980–2011 four of the five metals moved against him.

With those facts on the table, please do the following. First, state precisely what was tested. Separate the Malthusian claim itself from the auxiliaries required to turn it into a wager — that a decade is an adequate window, that inflation-adjusted prices of five non-renewable metals measure scarcity, that no substitution or technological offset will intervene — and say, on Duhem–Quine lines, which conjunct the 1990 result actually impugns, and whether the failure could have been located in the theory rather than in the measuring instrument. Second, judge Ehrlich's response: is "wrong indicator, wrong dates" an immunizing stratagem that drains the theory of risk, or an independently motivated refinement? Apply the Popperian and the Lakatosian tests explicitly, and say what would have to be true for it to count as a progressive rather than a degenerating move. Third, and most importantly, evaluate the verdict that Malthus and his successors were right but early. Is that a coherent verdict at all, or does the possibility of deferring the settlement date indefinitely make the claim unfalsifiable in principle? Specify what evidence would entitle a theorist to postpone rather than abandon, and whether the later recomputations across rolling windows supply it. Fourth, confront the deeper difficulty: Malthus's principle fitted every known agrarian society and then stopped applying after roughly 1800, while Malthus himself revised the Essay through six editions to 1826, absorbing criticism and new material as he went. Is a theory refuted when its domain ends, or vindicated up to a boundary, and how should falsificationism handle a claim whose scope has an endpoint in time?

Finally, tell me where these frameworks fall silent — on the choice of test window, on the use of a public wager as an operationalization of credence, on whether a bet is an experiment at all — rather than pressing them for answers they do not contain. I want the mapping laid out explicitly (players, prediction, auxiliaries, outcome, verdict) and a clear statement of what the wager did not test; I am not looking for a reading list, citations, or a summary of the controversy.
