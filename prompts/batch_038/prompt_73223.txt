I'm in my final year and taking a seminar on the philosophy of history; our current unit covers testimony, documentary silence, and the boundary between legend and history, and the term paper is due in three weeks. What follows is a condensed draft — about 850 words standing in for a planned 4,000 — and I'd like comments of a fairly specific kind, which I list after it. Please don't worry about references or further reading; I'm asking only for your judgment about the argument and about whether I have described the evidence correctly.

**Condensed draft**

The stock question — whether the Trojan War happened — is malformed, since it treats the name of a whole tradition as though it picked out a single event that either occurred or did not. A better question is what kind of warrant licenses us in treating a narrative as history at all. My proposal is that the warrant comes from a narrative's survival of tests that a fabricated or heavily transformed story would be unlikely to pass. I use three.

First, the hostile-witness test: does any part of the tradition come from, or get corroborated by, a source with no interest in its being believed and something to lose from it? Second, the bureaucratic-residue test: does the narrative leave traces in the routine, non-narrative records of administrations that had no reason to mention it — treaty texts, annals, tax and corvée documents? Such records are produced for purposes unrelated to the story, so their agreement with it is not manufactured by the story's own transmitters. Third, the gratuitous-specificity test: does the narrative carry concrete detail that does no work in the tale as told — itineraries, quantities, minor names, awkward geography — detail a poet inventing for effect would not produce and a redactor would not preserve at a cost?

The load-bearing move in the paper is that none of these tests can be applied to the moment of origin. They can only be applied to the moment of transmission, because selection operates on the transmitted state, not on the event. This bites hardest on the first test, which I have borrowed, more or less openly, from the criterion of embarrassment in New Testament scholarship: material damaging to the later community is unlikely to have been invented. That formulation is wrong as it stands. A detail that was a liability when it arose but an asset by the time it was written down carries no evidential weight, since the filter that mattered was operating at the later date. The obvious instance is Israel's ancestors as slaves in Egypt. Humiliation there is converted into the warrant for election, so by the time the narrative is fixed in writing the shameful origin is the point of the story, and the test cannot be run on it. I call the correction the liability-at-transmission constraint.

The constraint threatens to be self-sealing: for any apparently embarrassing detail I can always posit a later revaluation that turned it into an asset, which would mean the test can never be passed and therefore can never be failed. I want to block that by requiring that the revaluation be independently documented — visible in the text's own redactional layers or in surrounding literature — rather than merely available as a hypothesis.

The second move is that the tests must be weighted by expected record density. A null result on the residue test means very different things in different settings. Late Bronze Age western Anatolia is a setting where a literate imperial neighbour wrote diplomacy about precisely that region: Wilusa appears as a Hittite vassal, a treaty survives with a ruler named Alaksandu, dated around 1280 BCE, and the correspondence also names a western power called Ahhiyawa. Silence there is informative. Egyptian state documentation, by contrast, is not the kind of record that would log the departure of a servile population, so silence there is weak evidence.

Applied to the three cases: Troy passes the residue test partially — the Hittite documents, plus the destruction horizon at Hisarlik in Troy VIIa, usually placed around 1180 BCE — while failing the hostile-witness test and largely failing gratuitous specificity for the Iliad itself, composed roughly four centuries later. Verdict: a real geopolitical setting and probably real conflict, no warrant for the plot. The Exodus fails the residue test, but only weakly given record density; the Merneptah stele of 1208 BCE treats Israel as a people already in Canaan, which constrains any dating, the narrative's own store-city names point to the Ramesside period, and the wilderness tradition leaves no trace. Verdict: no warrant for the narrative as told, but the constraint structure is not empty. Arthur fails all three. Gildas in the sixth century records the siege of Badon and Ambrosius Aurelianus but never names Arthur; the first substantial Arthur is the ninth-century Historia Brittonum's dux bellorum with twelve battles and no dates; the Annales Cambriae, in a text belonging to the mid-tenth century, places Badon in 516 and Camlann in 537 and adds the detail that Arthur carried the cross on his shoulders; then Geoffrey of Monmouth around 1136 supplies the whole royal apparatus. Each stratum adds specificity without adding independent witnesses, so the third test is not merely failed but inverted: specificity rises as distance from the events rises, which is what fabrication looks like. Hence my ranking, Troy above Exodus above Arthur — with the provocative claim that Arthur is weaker than the Exodus despite the Exodus's total failure of the residue test.

I acknowledge the obvious objection: the ranking looks arbitrary because the three tests are not commensurable. My response is that they are not incommensurable but differently sensitive, and that the ranking is driven by the record-density discount. But if the discount for Egypt is made large enough, Exodus and Arthur converge, and I have not found a non-question-begging way to fix its size.
