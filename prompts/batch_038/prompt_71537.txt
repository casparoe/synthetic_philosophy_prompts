Right — thirty-odd years doing condensed matter physics at a national lab, mostly defects in crystals, which turns out to be rather relevent to all this, and since I retired I've fallen down a hole in the philosophy of science and then, to my surprise, into the social sciences, which I'd always assumed were soft. I've been asked to run a session for our local U3A branch in about six weeks: forty-five minutes, a flip chart, a dodgy projector that only manages static slides, and twenty-five people who are mostly retired engineers, plus a couple of GPs and a former headteacher. Nobody with any economics whatsoever. The topic I've picked is path dependence — whether history genuinely locks outcomes in, or whether you'd have arrived at the same place anyway if you'd rewound the tape. I came at it from the biology end, via Gould's business about replaying life's tape from the Burgess Shale, and I now half-suspect that biologists and economists mean quite different things by the word, which is part of why I want to teach it rather than just read about it.

What I need from you isn't an explanation of path dependence, it's a plan for how to explain it. So: the order the pieces have to go in and why — I keep finding that lock-in means nothing until positive feedback is on the table, but I may have the dependency the wrong way round; which single example should carry the whole session and what that example has to contain and must not contain; and how to meet the objections in the order they'll actually arrive rather than the order I'd like. One of them I can't answer at all. Half that room spent their careers on control systems and magnetics and I'm near-certain someone will say this is just hysteresis and we've had a word for it since the nineteenth century, thank you. Honestly I think they're onto something — the state does depend on the history of the applied field — but I also think the analogy hides the interesting part, whatever that part is. Tell me what I'd say back, and where in the forty-five minutes I should plant it rather than let it ambush me at minute thirty-eight. The others I'm expecting: 'isn't everything path dependent, history always matters?', which I want the session to dissolve rather than answer with a definition I announce from the front; 'so lock-in means the thing that won was worse?', since I've read enough of the counter-literature that took the keyboard story apart to no longer know whether the concept survives without inefficiency baked into it; and 'if it's locked in, how come formats keep changing?'. I also have a vague memory of a paper from the mid-nineties that sorted path dependence into three grades, only the strongest of which is genuinely regrettable, and I wonder whether that's the spine of the talk or a distinction that would eat the whole session.

On examples — not the keyboard. It's the obvious one, the underlying story is contested, and I don't want to spend forty minutes defending a case study I couldn't defend under questioning. Not the video format war either, because there's a retired TV engineer in the group who will hold forth about recording length and licensing and we'll lose the afternoon. What I'm tempted by is the railway gauge, and specifically the daft version of it: standard gauge traced back through the coal tramways to Roman chariot ruts to the width of two horses' back ends, and then onwards to the width of the shuttle's rocket boosters because they had to be railed through the mountains. I'd like to use that as the thing path dependence is *not* — a just-so lineage with no feedback mechanism anywhere in it, and possibly not even true as history, though I haven't checked. Is that a good decoy, or will it just muddy what counts as the real thing? Then tell me what to cut and say out loud that I'm cutting it: I assume the reinforcement urn models, the ergodicity-breaking language I'd instinctively reach for, and the whole political-science row about critical junctures all have to go, but I'd like to know what I lose by dropping them and whether any one of them is worth smuggling in for the two people who'll want more. And I want a single question to put to the room at the end that would show me whether they've grasped the difference between 'the past mattered' and 'the past foreclosed the alternatives' — plus what a bad answer would sound like, so I can tell a muddle I can fix on the spot from one that means I've failed. No reading list, I'm not handing anything out. And if you think forty-five minutes is hopeless for this, say so and tell me what to shrink it to instead of flattering me.
