Consider a case I have been arguing about with two colleagues. A reporter at a regional paper publishes a story alleging that the city water authority falsified its lead-testing results. Within a week the story is taken up by a local television station, a widely read blog, two podcasters, and a national news aggregator; each presents it as its own reporting, and none of them cites the others. Six apparent sources, six apparently separate confirmations. It later emerges that all six drew on the same leaked internal memo and the same single anonymous insider, and that none of them checked the memo's contents against any independent record. I have been calling this a corroboration mirage: the hearer's evidence has the surface structure of many independent reports, but the information channel is one.

Now the argument. Colleague A says a hearer who read all six accounts is in exactly the epistemic position she would have occupied had she read only the first: whatever the appearance of agreement seems to add, it adds nothing, because testimonial justification tracks the actual structure of the channels, not the structure as it appears to the hearer. Colleague B says the hearer is genuinely better off: at the time she had undefeated positive reasons for supposing the reports were independent, and her justification cannot be retroactively lowered by a fact she was in no position to discover. My own credence in A's claim, read strictly, is roughly 0.45.

What I want from you is your own number and its derivation. First, restate the target proposition so that it actually has a truth value: flag anything in my phrasing that smuggles in vagueness (I suspect "exactly" is doing work that "not materially greater" would not, and that doxastic and propositional justification may come apart here), and tell me which sharpening you are scoring. Then decompose your credence into sub-questions, each with a rough probability and a line or two of justification — among them: whether testimonial justification is fixed by the hearer's total evidence or by the real reliability of the channel; whether reductionist accounts, on which a hearer needs positive non-testimonial reasons to trust a speaker, and anti-reductionist accounts, on which testimony carries a defeasible default entitlement, yield different verdicts in this case or converge on the same verdict for incompatible reasons; whether independence between corroborating sources is a causal matter about the channels or an evidential matter about what the hearer is warranted in believing; how much of each colleague's intuition is really a verdict about this case rather than about a nearby one; and any factor I have left out that belongs in the decomposition. Show me how the factors combine into the single number rather than leaving me with a list.

Then answer three further questions with equal directness: which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, such that settling it would move the number furthest; what argument or empirical finding would in fact move you most on that sub-question; and whether A and B are genuinely disagreeing about the corroboration mirage or unknowingly disagreeing about an upstream question that the case merely exposes.

I am asking for a committed answer throughout. Give your number, own your sharpening of the proposition, and argue for it; do not hand me a survey of what a reductionist would say and what an anti-reductionist would say with the choice left to me, and do not dissolve the verdict on A and B into a both-are-partly-right compromise unless you are prepared to state precisely what each of them gets right and why the remainder is wrong.
