I teach a second-year seminar of twelve students, roughly half philosophy majors and half mathematics majors, in a hundred-minute weekly slot. Two weeks ago we were working through unions of finite sets, and one of the mathematicians remarked without looking up that of course you can pick an element out of each of them, that is simply what it means for a set to be nonempty; one of the philosophers then asked what the difference was between that and having some way of doing the picking, and I found that I could not answer her. I have spent the fortnight since reading enough to know that her question was the right one, but I have no appetite for standing at the board and reconstructing the answer for them, partly because they will nod along and learn nothing, and partly because I suspect that the version I would give is still too close to the one I got wrong in the room.

Next week's session is on the axiom of choice. None of them has done set theory; the mathematicians have used Zorn's lemma as a black box in algebra and one of them has heard of the Banach–Tarski decomposition, which he regards as a joke. I have index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard, no budget, and the hundred minutes. What I want is an activity they run rather than a story they hear, in which the wall is hit physically: a first phase in which selection is effortless and nobody thinks there is a problem, then a phase in which the structure that made it effortless quietly disappears — I expect some physical descendant of the familiar contrast between infinitely many pairs of boots, where one can always take the left one, and infinitely many pairs of identical socks, but I want them to arrive at that contrast with cards in their hands rather than have it narrated to them — and then a phase in which they are asked to commit, on paper, to a single procedure that a rival team's mechanical chooser will execute literally and stupidly, and discover that for some of the families in front of them they cannot write one down at all. The distinction I most want them to feel is the one between having, for each set separately, an element, and having something that delivers an element for all of them at once; and, harder, the distinction between being unable to describe a selection and there being no selection to describe, which I expect at least three of them will collapse into each other. I would also like a moment where they are offered the services of an oracle that hands back selections with no procedure attached, and have to decide as a group whether to accept it in view of what it buys them and what it costs them.

So: give me the design, not the lecture. Rules, roles, and a scoring scheme that works with paper tokens rather than money; the sequence of families of sets to hand out and in what order, with a reason for each position; a minute-by-minute timeline including where the break goes; and the point in the run at which nobody has yet said the name of the axiom. Then the debrief, mapping each moment of play onto the piece of the idea it was carrying, and telling me how to handle the interventions I can already foresee — the team that proposes to choose at random, the team that says the whole business is only ever a problem for uncountable collections, the philosopher who wants to conclude from the paradoxical decomposition that the axiom is simply false. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what I should do about it, and be blunt about which features of the axiom the game will misrepresent or cannot reproduce at all — I am thinking in particular of the fact that the obstacle my students feel in the room will be an inability to produce a rule, whereas the interesting question is not about rules, and of the fact that finite selection and countable selection are not on the same footing — together with exactly what I will have to supply in my own voice afterwards to repair each gap. Please do not include a reading list or references of any kind; I want a session plan I could hand to a colleague who has never seen the material.
