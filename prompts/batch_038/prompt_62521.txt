So, kind of an odd one. I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever — I do infra for ML systems. A while back I picked up von Neumann's *The Computer and the Brain*, the unfinished thing that came out of the Yale lectures he never got to give, mostly because people keep name-dropping it in arguments about brains versus accelerators. What stuck with me wasn't the brain-is-a-computer bit, it's how heavily qualified the whole thing is. My paraphrase, from memory, so treat it as approximate: the nervous system looks digital because nerve pulses are all-or-none, but there are analog ingredients too (chemical concentrations, fatigue, thresholds); the components are something like three orders of magnitude slower than the electronics of his day and much less precise, so the brain cannot possibly be achieving what it achieves by having accurate parts — it has to buy reliability some other way, with massive parallelism, redundancy, error-correcting and statistical arrangements, and very long chains of steps, depth instead of breadth; its memory doesn't look like addressed storage, more like recall by association; and then the last part, which he openly flags as a guess, is that the underlying idiom the brain computes in might not be arithmetical at all — maybe some logical or statistical formalism we don't have yet, in which case translating brain processes into our familiar math could be systematically misleading rather than merely hard.

Then on a whim I re-read *Flowers for Algernon*, which I loved as a kid, and it struck me as the exact opposite picture, and I can't let it go. Keyes's story runs entirely on the assumption that intelligence is one number you can turn up. It goes from 68 to 185 and gets treated like a volume knob on a single underlying quantity. The effect they name after Algernon and Charlie is literally a scalar law — artificially increased intelligence deteriorates at a rate proportional to the size of the increase. And the mouse is supposed to license the inference to the man.

I want an actual reading of the novella against von Neumann's account of the brain, not a summary of either. Some things I want you to dig into:

- Is intelligence-as-a-dial even coherent on von Neumann's picture? If what a system can do comes out of the interaction of precision, parallelism, depth, redundancy, error correction, and the idiom it computes in, then what is post-op Charlie — the same machine clocked up, or a different architecture? Point at specific moments that cut one way or the other (the arithmetic and reading speed shooting up while the emotional and social stuff stays stuck; the smart Charlie being unable to remember or empathize with the dumb Charlie).
- Does the Algernon/Charlie decay law survive that? If intelligence isn't a quantity, what exactly is deteriorating, and is the story's clean curve doing philosophical work it can't cash out? What would the decline have to look like if von Neumann were roughly right about how the thing is built?
- The mouse. What is being assumed when Algernon's fate predicts Charlie's — a shared mechanism, or a shared equation? Is there a proper term for the family of views I'm groping toward with something like hardware independence? And does the warning about the brain's idiom give you grounds to be skeptical of mouse-to-man extrapolation here?
- Where the two actually agree: both treat the person as a system whose performance is a property of its construction rather than its substance. But then the doctors talk in ways that imply pre-op Charlie wasn't really a person. Does an engineering frame like von Neumann's support that or blow it up? Is there a reading where the whole point is that he's the same person on worse hardware the entire time?
- The form: the progress reports are Charlie measuring himself with the instrument that is being measured, and the misspelling is the data. Does that self-instrumentation angle buy me anything, or am I reaching?

Also be straight with me about where this lens distorts the book. The novella is about love, being laughed at, dignity, and whether intelligence makes you worth caring about, and none of that is anywhere in von Neumann's frame — so tell me where a brain-architecture reading has to stop, and what the story contributes to the question of whether the brain is a machine of our sort that the treatise structurally can't reach, especially the inside view of a capacity changing.

End with a verdict rather than a shrug: does the novella dramatize something genuinely awkward for the von Neumann picture, or is it a scalar-intelligence fable whose plot his analysis would have predicted goes wrong? Practical stuff — don't quote Keyes, I don't have the book in front of me, so just describe what happens in a scene; and no bibliography or further reading, I'm not looking for homework.
