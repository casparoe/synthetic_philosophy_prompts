In 2010 Nature published a paper by Martin Nowak, Corina Tarnita, and E. O. Wilson arguing that inclusive fitness theory — the framework that had organized the study of social evolution for nearly half a century — was mathematically restricted to special conditions and empirically unsupported, and that eusociality is better explained by ordinary natural selection operating on structured populations, with helping behavior arising from ecological preadaptations such as defensible nests and from competition among colonies rather than from relatedness coefficients. The response was extraordinary: a letter published in Nature the following year and signed by dozens of working evolutionary biologists, maintaining that the paper got the mathematics wrong, got the empirical record wrong, and that its advertised alternative was inclusive fitness theory with the labels changed; the three authors published a reply, and the field largely closed ranks against them.

I want an analysis of this episode through the units-of-selection framework, running in both directions. I know the entomology; what I don't have is a disciplined way of sorting the conceptual claims.

First, the mapping. State precisely what each side took the unit or units to be, and use Okasha's MLS1/MLS2 distinction together with Dawkins's replicator/vehicle distinction to sort out which parts of the dispute were about units of selection at all, and which were about something else — for example whether inclusive fitness is a legitimate general modeling framework, which is a claim about representation rather than about a level at which selection acts.

Second, apply a distinction someone in my reading group proposed and that I have not seen made in the literature: separate the engine question, which asks at what level fitness differences causally arise, from the ledger question, which asks at what level a model must be parameterized in order to account for all the variance in trait frequency. Say which of these the 2010 paper was actually attacking, which the signatories were defending, and whether the two sides ever engaged the same question — or whether the vehemence of the response is partly explained by their not doing so.

Third, tell me where the units-of-selection framework goes silent: which features of the episode it cannot classify. I have in mind especially the Price equation's recurring invocation as a neutral arbiter that both sides claimed, and the fact that a paper whose stated thesis was about mathematical generality was received as a claim about the ontological reality of groups.

Finally, give me your verdict on what the episode shows about the theory itself — a confirmation, an anomaly, or evidence that the units-of-selection debate was never the dispute this paper was prosecuting.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, stating what you think the episode shows, and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of the question back to me.
