Been fighting with this idea for about a year and I finally have to write it down as a two-page proposal for our department's internal fellowship (committee is two historians and one philosopher of science, so it has to wear both hats). The idea came out of an argument I keep losing at the pub. Everyone in it treats "history is written by the victors" as a single claim, and I'm now pretty convinced it's three claims that get traded on each other's credit. Call them the causal claim (which documents were produced, survived, and got catalogued was largely fixed by who held power), the defeater claim (that fact gives us a standing reason to discount what the record says), and the constitutive claim (the concepts available for describing the past are themselves the victors', so no description could be neutral). The relativist-flavored arguments I know tend to establish something in the neighborhood of the first and get received as if they'd established the third, and the anti-relativist replies mostly attack the second, which is honestly the most fixable of the three and the least interesting to defend. If that's right, some large part of the impasse is a scope confusion rather than a deep disagreement about truth, and I want the proposal to say so without turning into a survey.

But the funded project I actually want is narrower and weirder, and its about silence rather than distortion. Given a record produced by a selection process that systematically left some group out, the interesting epistemic question isn't whether the surviving testimony is slanted — everyone grants that — it's what you can infer from an absence. Human-rights statisticians already estimate unrecorded deaths from several overlapping partial lists, and that machinery seems to need something like independence: each list has its own chance of picking up a given event. A victor-shaped archive looks like exactly the case where that fails, because if a group is invisible to every list for the same structural reason, the estimate doesn't just get noisier, it gets confidently wrong in a direction you can't see from inside the data. So my question is roughly: under what conditions does archival silence license a positive inference at all, as opposed to merely licensing wider error bars — and can you tell from inside an archive whether you're in the structural-dependence case, or does that always require a source you don't have? The classic illustration I keep reaching for is plantation record-keeping, where enslaved people show up mostly as entries in ledgers kept by owners, but I want the case treated as a testbed, not as the subject.

So: write me the proposal. I want the question stated so it has a determinate answer rather than a mood, then why the two standing positions both leave it open — the people who say power shapes the archive have an explanation for bias and no account of absence, and the people who say historians' methods can correct for it usually assume the correction is available when you can name the excluded population, which is the thing you often can't do. Then the method, month by month across twelve months, and a defense of why a small toy model of a selection process plus one worked archival case is the right move rather than the two obvious alternatives — a straight case study of a single archive, which would be history and not philosophy, or a purely conceptual essay on standpoint and objectivity, which would just restate the impasse at a higher altitude. Say what a result would look like in both registers: something a philosopher would call an answer, and something a working archivist could actually use to decide whether a gap in the record is evidence. I want an explicit failure condition, including the possibility that the honest answer is that silence licenses nothing without an outside prior, which would be a negative result but still a result and I'd rather say so up front than discover it in month eleven. And be realistic about scope — name what gets cut to fit the year. No bibliography, I'll fill in the citations myself once the framing is right.
