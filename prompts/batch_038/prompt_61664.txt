I head factual commissioning for the history strand of a public-service broadcaster. We receive roughly thirty-five pitches a month across archaeology, ancient and medieval history, and historical science. Each one gets a first pass from an assistant producer who has no humanities training, a decision within ten working days, and — where we decline on the ground that the central claim is pseudohistorical rather than merely heterodox — a written record naming the criterion invoked. Those records are audited annually, and the last two audits have suggested that we decline non-academic film-makers, and claims about regions with thin institutional scholarship, at rates we cannot easily justify. The procedure therefore has to discriminate without being unfair, and it has to be auditable by people who are not specialists.

Please write me a decision procedure my assistant producer can actually run on a pitch document in under thirty minutes. Specifically:

1. The questions in order, each with a stated pass, fail, or insufficient-answer condition. Be precise about what counts as a sufficient answer: a pitch that asserts a conclusion is not the same as a pitch that supplies the thing being asked for. Mark where the procedure terminates in an outright decline; where it terminates in "decline as pitched, commissionable if reformulated," with the required reformulation stated; and where it stops and refers the matter to me because what remains is a question of remit and taste rather than of evidence.

2. Which of the familiar demarcation ideas survive this translation, and in what form. Ground the tests in whatever the philosophy of science offers that is usable at commissioning level — whether a claim could be given up on some specified finding, whether a research line engages its anomalies or explains them away, whether it depends on the methods of specialists whose conclusions it rejects — but give me the tests, not the bibliography, and say explicitly which distinctions you have had to blunt, drop, or rewrite to make them answerable from a pitch rather than from a monograph.

3. The failure modes. Where will the procedure over-block, declining a genuine minority position held by credentialed specialists against a settled consensus? Where will it under-block, passing a claim because it is dressed in the vocabulary of method? And how would I tell, in a given decline, whether I am tracking evidential standards or institutional credentials — which is what the audit is really after.

Then run the procedure on four pitches:

(a) A single film on Plato's Atlantis treated as the history of an idea: its reception from antiquity through the nineteenth-century occult revival to contemporary treasure-hunters, with the claim nowhere endorsed.

(b) A four-part series presenting Fomenko's New Chronology as established fact, with two mainstream medievalists interviewed in the final episode.

(c) A feature on the proposal that a cosmic impact near the end of the last glacial period contributed to a cluster of extinctions and cultural changes — a hypothesis advanced by an interdisciplinary team, published in peer-reviewed venues, and regarded by most specialists in the affected fields as unsupported.

(d) A series arguing that a well-known pre-Columbian monumental site was built with the assistance of a transoceanic civilisation for which no artefactual evidence has been found. A deal memo is already signed, the presenter is our highest-rated talent, and the production company has offered a closing card noting that the thesis is contested.

For (d) I expect an answer I will not like. Give it regardless, and then tell me what the procedure does license: whether the same material could legitimately appear in the strand in some other configuration, and what I would have to change about the framing rather than merely append to it.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot settle at all, and what the annual audit should measure if it wants to detect the procedure's biases rather than merely its output volume.
