I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years as a cell physiologist, and I have taken up philosophy the way other men take up woodworking: imperfectly, but with enthusiasm. For two months I have been working through Dennett's *Brainstorms*, and the opening piece, "Where Am I?", has lodged in me. You will know the apparatus. The narrator's brain is removed and kept alive in a vat, from which it drives his body at a distance by radio link; the brain is nicknamed Hubert, the body Yorick; and a duplicate, B. Hubert, has been running alongside it in a second vat, fed the same inputs, so that when the narrator at last flips the master switch nothing happens — no gap, no blackout, and no way of telling from the inside which of the two brains is now steering.

In the laboratory, when an experiment returned a result one could not accept, the discipline was to write the inference out link by link and find the one that would not bear weight. I have tried that here and failed. What follows is my reconstruction of what I take the switch episode to prove. Each step strikes me as true or at least defensible, and the conclusion at (10) is something I am certain is false. I cannot locate the faulty link.

(1) What a person is, at any moment, is a functional organization: a particular pattern of memories, dispositions, and ongoing perceptual and motor coupling with the world. The persistence of a person consists in the uninterrupted maintenance of that pattern, and in nothing further.

(2) When the narrator flips the switch, the pattern is maintained without interruption. Because the duplicate has been receiving the same inputs and shadowing the original throughout, there is no gap in memory, no discontinuity in experience, and no change in behaviour.

(3) Therefore the narrator survives the switch as the numerically same person — and he has not moved. A substitution that leaves the pattern and its continuity wholly intact cannot have relocated its subject.

(4) Generalize: if a substitution is such that no experience, memory, or test available to the subject could reveal it, then it is not a substitution of subjects. Indistinguishability from the inside is identity of the person.

(5) Before the switch the narrator's pattern is realized in Hubert; after it, the pattern is realized in B. Hubert with unbroken continuity of memory and experience. By (1) and (4), he survives in B. Hubert.

(6) But B. Hubert was receiving the same inputs and shadowing Hubert all along, so it already possessed, before the switch, the narrator's memories, the narrator's dispositions, and an unbroken stream of experience of its own. By (1) and (4), B. Hubert was therefore already the narrator before the switch. The switch did not transfer him from one brain to the other; it only changed which of the two was driving Yorick.

(7) Hubert has exactly the same claim. So before the switch there were two things each of which is the narrator, and by the transitivity of identity they are each other — which they are not, being two distinct brains in two distinct vats. Rather than abandon (1), I conclude that a person is not the sort of thing that occupies exactly one region of space: the narrator is one person, wholly present in both vats, and either realization could be destroyed without his ceasing to be.

(8) Carry this back to the surgery. Suppose the pattern had been realized from the outset by the vat-and-radio arrangement, with nothing at all inside the skull. Nothing the narrator could have observed would distinguish that history from his actual one, and by (4) there is no further fact to distinguish. So the narrator is indifferent to whether his skull contains a brain, and indifferent to which matter realizes him.

(9) Apply (4) once more, to me. Nothing available to me from the inside settles whether the pattern that is me is realized by this grey matter or by something physically indistinguishable from it, nor whether it is realized here rather than there. So by the same reasoning, "Where am I?" has no determinate answer for me either: any location I assign myself is a guess about a bookkeeping convention, a convention about which realization my name happens to be attached to.

(10) That is false. I am in the chair by the west window of my study, and I know it with a confidence I would ordinarily reserve for the reading of a calibrated instrument.

(11) Hence (1) is false: continuity of functional organization does not by itself fix who a person is. Something further must do that work — the particular matter, its position, or its biological career.

A note on what I am asking for. I do not want exegesis of Dennett's own view, a survey of the controversy, or a reading list; treat this argument as mine and judge it on its own terms. I want to know which single step, removed, stops the chain dead; why that step looked so safe to me; and what each rival candidate costs to surrender. If forced, I would rather give up (1) than (10) — but I suspect that preference is a prejudice inherited from forty years of trusting whatever I could put on a bench, and I would prefer to have it argued with than indulged.

Please answer under these headings, in this order:

- **The load-bearing step** (about 250 words). Give its number and state precisely what inference it makes that it is not entitled to make. If you hold that two steps are jointly at fault, say which is primary and why the other cannot carry the blame alone.
- **Why it looked sound** (about 200 words). Identify the ambiguity, the smuggled principle, or the respectable intuition that let the step pass inspection. I want to know what I thought I was seeing when I thought I saw a truth.
- **The alternatives, priced** (about 450 words). Take (1), (4), (6), and (10) in turn. For each, say what a coherent position that rejects it looks like, what it thereby becomes committed to elsewhere, and what it must pay. Be concrete about the payment: name the phenomenon, the intuition, or the practice that gets sacrificed, not merely the label of the theory that survives.
- **Recommendation** (about 150 words). Which rejection you would advise, and — if you think (10) is the step that must go — what I should put in its place, given that I plainly do know where my body is.

One last request. I am accustomed to being told when a measurement is out of calibration, and I would rather hear it plainly than diplomatically. If some step of mine is not merely false but confused — if it does not assert anything capable of being true or false — say so, and show me why.
