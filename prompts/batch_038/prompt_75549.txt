Twice a month I run an unpaid reading group for adults in my town, most of whom left school early; I have no degree myself and have taught myself probability and epistemology over roughly eight years from library books and recorded lectures. As a discipline for all of us, I set a short written exercise each session and one member submits an answer, which I mark and return with comments. The marks go nowhere. I would like you to act as the grader this time, because I want the answer judged to a standard I may not be able to hold it to myself.

The exercise I set was as follows.

"Twenty points. Alice and Bob each assign a probability to the claim that a certain company will be taken over within the year: Alice says 0.7, Bob says 0.4. They have read different trade reports and spoken to different people, and neither knows in detail what the other has seen. But suppose they are idealized Bayesians: they hold degrees of belief that behave like probabilities, they revise strictly by conditionalization on what they learn, and they are logically omniscient. Suppose also that they began from the same prior probability distribution over states of the world, and that it is common knowledge between them that Alice's probability for the claim is 0.7 and Bob's is 0.4.

(a) State what Aumann's agreement theorem says about a situation of this kind, and identify the assumptions it needs in order to say it. [8 points]

(b) A commentator writes: 'Aumann proved that rational people who are honest about their beliefs cannot agree to disagree. So whenever two intelligent, sincere people persist in disagreeing, at least one of them is biased, dishonest, or confused.' Is this a fair reading of the theorem? Explain. [8 points]

(c) Say something about whether the theorem does or does not bear on ordinary real-world disagreements, for instance political ones. [4 points]"

The answer I received was this.

"(a) The theorem, as I understand it, says: take two people who are perfectly rational in the Bayesian sense. Suppose they started out with the same prior probability distribution, meaning they came into the situation with the same evidence base and the same initial opinions. Suppose each forms a posterior probability for some particular claim, and that these posteriors are common knowledge between them: Alice knows Bob's number, Bob knows Alice's, each knows that the other knows, and so on up through every level. Then the two numbers have to be equal. Aumann's result is that there cannot be common knowledge of a difference of opinion. So the case described is impossible: if Alice's 0.7 and Bob's 0.4 genuinely were common knowledge between them, and they genuinely are ideal Bayesians with a common prior, then something in the description has to give. Two assumptions do all the work, the common prior and the common knowledge, and common knowledge is a much stronger thing than either of them merely being told the other's figure once, which on its own would prove nothing.

(b) Mostly fair, though the commentator has squeezed two results into one sentence. Aumann also showed that if two such people actually report their probabilities to each other over and over, revising each round in light of what the other says, the numbers move toward one another and end up equal. That is the constructive half of the picture, and it is why people say that honest exchange of opinion leads to agreement. Taking both halves together, the inference looks reasonable: ideal Bayesians with a common prior cannot knowingly sit at different numbers, so a persistent disagreement between two able and sincere people indicates a failure somewhere. Either one of them is not really conditionalizing on the evidence they have, or is not reporting the belief they actually hold, or the two of them did not really begin from the same evidence base. I would add two qualifications. The theorem does not say which of the two is at fault, and it does not say the fault is a moral one; plain confusion is enough, so 'biased or dishonest' overstates it slightly.

(c) Real disagreements, political ones included, mostly fall outside the theorem, and I think it is a mistake to wheel the theorem out in political argument. Nobody has common knowledge of anyone else's exact credence. When two people at a dinner party say they put something at seventy percent and forty percent, those are rough gestures, not precise posteriors known to infinite depth, and the theorem gets no grip on them. It is a curiosity about idealized agents and endless hierarchies of knowledge. Its practical lesson is at most a caution: if you and someone you respect as rational go on disagreeing, one of you is missing something, so go and look for it."

Here is what I want from you.

Itemized comments I could write beside each part of the answer: what is correct, what is incorrect, and why it is incorrect, stated specifically enough that the writer could repair it without my having to lecture them.
