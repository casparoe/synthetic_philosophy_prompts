I am drafting my third novel, and I have reached the point where the argument beneath the plot has to be right, or at least honestly stated, because my narrator spends four hundred pages deciding something she cannot afterwards undo.

The situation is this. A woman of fifty-eight, recently diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's, writes a directive while she is still unmistakably herself. It refuses feeding tubes and refuses antibiotics for aspiration once she reaches a threshold she specifies: no longer recognizing her daughter, no longer able to follow a conversation, no longer distressed by her own losses. Eleven years later she meets the threshold and is plainly happy. She sings; she is fond of the aides; she has no idea who she was. She also says, several times a week, that she does not want to die — and on other days says nothing of the kind. Anticipating precisely this, the directive contains a clause she wrote herself, which my narrator calls the noise clause: any future statement of hers expressing a wish to go on living, made after the threshold is reached, is to be counted as noise emitted by the disease rather than as speech by her, and is to carry no weight in any decision. She also recorded a video to the same effect, in which she addresses her future self directly and, with some tenderness, tells that self not to believe her. The daughter must decide.

My adviser has given me the positions the book could be built upon, and I have added two of my own.

A. The whole-life view: a life is a narrative authored by the competent self, and her settled reflective values concern what the shape of that life amounts to, whereas the demented woman's contentment, however real, is shallow — a good hour cannot repair a ruined ending.

B. The present-experience view: once the capacity to take any interest in one's own biography is gone, only what is felt now can be good or bad for her; the directive defends someone who is no longer there, and enforcing it kills a contented woman to keep a promise to a ghost.

C. The capacity-to-value view: what confers full standing is not the ability to endorse one's life as a whole but the ability to value at all — to love, prefer, resist, cling — and moderate dementia leaves that intact; the later self is therefore a valuer, not a mere field of experience, though her valuing is less articulate than the earlier scheme it displaces.

D. The connectedness view, in the reductionist spirit my adviser keeps invoking from Parfit: authority tracks psychological connectedness, which comes in degrees, so the directive binds firmly while connectedness is substantial and weakens as it erodes — the answer is a function of stage, not a verdict.

E. The characterization view: a directive does not command, it describes; its function is to tell a surrogate what she cared about so that the surrogate can judge, and the noise clause is exactly the one thing such an instrument cannot do.

F. The promissory view: she made a promise, and to her daughter rather than to herself; promises are designed to bind across changes of heart, and the obligation runs to the promisee, so the daughter is not deciding for her mother but keeping faith with her.

G. The conventional view: the directive has force because an institutional order confers force, and its reach is set by standards of substituted judgment, present best interests, and clinical discretion rather than by any metaphysics of the self; the noise clause would be read down or struck.

H. The residue view: no position wins, the considerations are incommensurable, and the only accurate description of the daughter's act is that whichever thing she does, something real is lost and remains lost.

I do not want a survey. I want these eight ranked by how well each handles the noise clause: whether it can explain both why the later woman's protests do or do not count, and whether a competent person may legitimately pre-commit against her own future speech — without either assuming the thing in dispute or granting the earlier self a frankly tyrannical authority over someone who cannot answer back.

Please give me:

1. A tier list, A through D, of the eight positions on that criterion, with ties permitted but explained.

2. For each position, a number from 0.0 to 1.0 for how much authority over the later self it can coherently leave in the hands of the earlier one — where 1.0 means her directive settles the matter outright and 0.0 means it deserves no more weight than a stranger's opinion about how this woman should live.

3. One or two sentences per position justifying the placement, and an explicit flag wherever its treatment of the clause is question-begging, that is, wherever it can only honor the clause by already having decided who counts.

4. The two positions which, set against each other, would sustain an honest conflict across the length of a novel — meaning neither side possesses a move that ends the argument — together with what each side's strongest single move is and why it fails to be conclusive.

5. The one position most likely to be confidently misread by an intelligent reader, and the content of that misreading, since I would rather build the misreading into a character than be accused of it by a reviewer.

6. For your top three, the consideration or evidence that would demote each by a tier.

If you believe one of the eight cannot be scored because it refuses the criterion — I suspect you will say this of H — say so plainly, and tell me what it would take to make it scorable rather than simply excluding it.

One practical note: please give me no bibliography and no reading list. My adviser handles the literature, and I have no intention of reading around a position I have not yet chosen to dramatize. Where a view has a famous champion you may name them, but argue from the content of the view, not from its provenance; what I need is your judgment, and the reasoning that would let me disagree with it.
