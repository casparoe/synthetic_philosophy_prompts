For an essay I am drafting on the epistemic justification of democracy, I have written down the package I intend to defend, and I should like it audited before I commit to it in print. My commitments are these.

(1) What confers authority on a democratic decision is its reliability rather than the consent it embodies: on a binary question admitting an answer that is right independently of the procedure, if voters are each better than even and vote independently of one another, the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty as the electorate grows. That result, on my view, is what does the justificatory work.

(2) I am a modest epistemic proceduralist: there are procedure-independent standards of correctness, but no one is entitled to claim a privileged grip on them in advance of the procedure, which is why rule by the allegedly wise is objectionable even in the cases where it would in fact prove more reliable.

(3) I am a fallibilist about competence. I do not think we can ever verify, of an actual electorate, that its members are better than even; the premise is a regulative presumption we are entitled to work with, not a fact we are entitled to assert.

(4) Deliberation is constitutive of legitimate procedure, and I do not think it costs me the independence condition, which I read as demanding only independence conditional on the evidence the voters actually share. What imperils aggregation is deference and herding, not the exchange of reasons.

(5) The epistemic case for democracy runs through enlargement rather than qualification. Competence is stubborn and expensive to raise, numbers are cheap, and at a competence above chance, adding voters only helps. So the theorem seems to me to foreclose the epistemic argument for a restricted franchise: the epistocrat owes a demonstration that the wise few are more reliable than a sufficiently large body of ordinary citizens, and I cannot see how that demonstration would go.

(6) I further hold that an electorate may turn the theorem on itself. Whether we are competent and whether we are independent are themselves questions we may deliberate about and vote upon, and that meta-vote enjoys the same standing as any other.

Please tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do. Several pairs will be flagged by readers who have not thought the matter through; I want the flags that survive scrutiny separated from those that dissolve, and the real tensions ranked by severity rather than listed flatly. For each, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs, then identify the single revision that purchases the most consistency for the least damage. Where a disagreement turns on magnitudes rather than on principle, do not assert the result — work it out.

I have no antecedent attachment to any of the six, so please do not assume I am looking for permission to keep a favorite; assume I will follow the argument wherever it leads. Finally, tell me where your own audit is most likely to have gone wrong: which of the tensions you would withdraw if I pressed you, and what consideration would move you to a different verdict about the package as a whole.
