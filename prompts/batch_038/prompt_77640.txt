My brother-in-law has one move in every argument and it's "cui bono?" He says it's a Roman courtroom maxim that Cicero picked up from a judge named Cassius, which is fair enough, but he uses it way past anything a courtroom would allow. Last month a government abruptly dropped a procurement rule, the stock of one contractor jumped like 20% that afternoon, and he wrote a long post concluding the reversal had been arranged, on the sole ground that the contractor was the obvious beneficiary and had donated to the minister's campaign. When I asked what actual evidence there was — emails, a whistleblower, a pattern of the same contractor getting the things it paid for — he said I was the one with the explaining to do, because a random accident wouldn't benefit anybody in particular and I owed him an account of why the rule died on that specific Tuesday. He reaches for the same line about retracted studies, sudden resignations, a bridge inspection that failed right before an election.

So before our reading group meets on conspiracy theories next week I want to get ahead of this — he's def coming and he'll use the same move. Can you lay out the different precise claims "cui bono?" could be standing for, as a menu: starting with the ones that are just platitudes about motives being worth knowing, through the substantive ones that might actually be true, up to whatever strong version my brother-in-law thinks he's deploying. For each reading, say what it commits you to and whether it survives scrutiny, and mark which of them would actually license the step from "the contractor benefited" to "the contractor arranged it," plus which one is doing the work in his burden-shifting reply — because I suspect thats a separate claim entirely and he's sliding between the two.

Two things I half-suspect and want you to either sharpen or kill: (a) that beneficiaries can only be picked out after the event, so almost anything turns out to benefit somebody and the test can never fail; and (b) that even when a group clearly gains, the group isn't an agent that can plan anything — the people who cashed in here are pension fund shareholders who've never met. Then tell me what would have to hold for the heuristic to be genuinely probative instead of decorative — base rates, how concentrated the gain is, whether the beneficiary had the means and the access, whether you could name them before you looked — because I'd rather walk in with a checklist than with a complaint. A numbered list is perfect, no need to tell me about him specifically beyond what the checklist implies.
