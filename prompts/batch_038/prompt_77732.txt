I'm building out the ecology for a tabletop game, and last night a very long argument with a friend — a population geneticist by training — ground to a halt over whether one of my invented organisms counts as a biological individual at all. The creature is a modular colonial animal that encrusts rock in the intertidal zone. It grows by budding: a free-swimming larva settles, and from that single founder a stolon network spreads outward, generating several hundred zooids that stay physically joined by shared tissue through which nutrients and gamete precursors circulate. The zooids differentiate into three castes — feeders, which are sterile and never bud; defenders, which secrete a metabolically expensive toxin and are also sterile; and dispersers, which are the only zooids that produce the larvae that go on to settle elsewhere and found new colonies, each new colony again beginning from a single cell. There is, however, a second route of propagation. In winter storms the stolon mat is regularly torn into fragments, and each fragment regenerates into a complete colony with all three castes, without ever passing through a larval or single-cell stage. I also put a heritable variant into the game's mutation deck: a zooid lineage that stops paying for the defensive toxin and redirects the freed resources into budding. Within any one colony that variant spreads and eventually crowds out the defenders; across the population, the toxin-poor colonies are precisely the ones that get destroyed by predators.

My friend's position is that the colony is straightforwardly an individual — it has a division of labour, it has a sterile soma-like majority, and colonies visibly compete with one another for space and survive or die as wholes. My objection was that the fragmentation route destroys lineage continuity, so that the honest answer is that some colonies are individuals and others merely happen to be spatially contiguous, depending entirely on their ancestry, which struck me as an uncomfortable result for a category that is supposed to be doing serious explanatory work. He then said that the toxin-cheater dynamic is textbook selection at the level of the colony and settles the question in his favor; I replied that all it may show is that colonies differ in their probability of surviving, which is not the same as colonies reproducing. Since that exchange I have been reading an account in the philosophy of biology that treats individuality as graded rather than binary, and that makes it turn on roughly three features: whether the collective reproduces through a bottleneck that re-establishes each lineage from a single founder, whether there is a division of labour among its parts amounting to a separation of reproductive from somatic functions, and whether the collective persists and reproduces reliably enough to have heritable properties of its own.

Please set out the most important implications of applying that account to this organism, organized around the three claims we were actually fighting about. First, does the colony qualify, and what follows from the fact that the two propagation routes score differently on the bottleneck condition — in particular, does the account force me to say that a single population contains both genuine individuals and non-individuals at once, and if so, what does that do to the idea that "individual" picks out a natural kind rather than a convenience? Second, is the cheater dynamic genuine selection at the colony level, or is it within-colony selection whose only group-level consequence is a correlation between colony composition and colony survival; and what specific change to the biology — to the fragmentation route, to the dispersers, or to the timing of storms — would tip it into the first category? Third, the three criteria appear to come apart in my organism rather than co-varying, so what does that case imply about whether they function as a checklist whose conditions are jointly necessary, or as mutually reinforcing indicators of one underlying property? Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, stating plainly which side of the argument the account favors and why, and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating the question back to me.
