I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training, so I'll try to describe this precisely without jargon and hope the description carries the weight.

For the last few months I've been building a checker for a distributed system's coordinator process. The property I care about is whether the coordinator may safely act, given what it has established about the state of the replicas. To reason about that, I represent what the coordinator has established as a set of formulas, and I grow the set by applying a fixed, deliberately short list of inference rules.

The pattern I keep hitting is this: two ways of writing down the *same* situation give the coordinator different amounts of knowledge, even though nothing about the coordinator or its evidence changes.

Case one. The situation is that at least one replica is stale. If I record it as a three-way disjunction over named replicas, the disjunction sits in the store and everything I can derive from it is available. If I record the same situation as the negation of a three-way conjunction — not all replicas are fresh — which my rules cannot convert into the first form, because I never included that rewrite, then the coordinator does not count as knowing it. No message arrived, no clock advanced, no rule was altered. I rewrote a formula.

Case two. The situation is the state of 200 nodes. Written as a conjunction of 200 named literals, the store holds each literal and my rules let the coordinator pull out any single one. Written as one quantified statement over a table, the store holds that statement alone, and the coordinator can no longer be credited with the individual cases — identical world, different bookkeeping.

At first I assumed I had just rediscovered the familiar complaint that logic-based models of knowledge make an agent know every consequence of what it knows, down to all the theorems of arithmetic. That complaint is nearby, but it isn't what I'm describing, because mine survives the standard fix. I already cap derivation length and restrict the rules; the coordinator is intentionally weak. A weak coordinator still gains or loses knowledge purely on the basis of which of two equivalent spellings I happened to pick. If anything, weakening it made the spelling matter more, since a stronger agent could rewrite one form into the other itself.

So: is there an established concept for this — knowledge attributions inside a model that vary with the encoding of a fact rather than with the agent or the world? I'd like the nearest concepts, and for each, exactly how the thing I'm describing differs from it. I'd also like to know whether this is an artifact of my representation choices or something any formal account of knowledge has to absorb, and whether being able to swap in an equivalent formulation is treated as a requirement on such an account or as a demand that has to be given up.

I'm after the edges, not just a label. Would renaming a bound variable count as a change of spelling? Would moving from a first-order encoding to a propositional one? Are two spellings equivalent only via a long proof a different case from two equivalent via a single rewrite step? If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it, and give me the boundary cases that separate it from the neighbouring failures.

Please end with your single best label on its own final line, in the form \answer{...} — a short phrase, being the established term if one genuinely fits and your coined term if not.
