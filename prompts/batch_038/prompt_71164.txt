I teach a polisci seminar and one week is on the anthropology of gift exchange — the idea that a gift isn't a payment but the opening of an open-ended obligation between two people, plus the further claim that what we call corruption (bribery, patronage, clientelism) only looks like corruption once you've already assumed an impersonal state whose offices are held in trust for strangers. My students keep coming out of that week saying "so the mafia is just a private welfare state" and I want to complicate it with the first two Godfather films, which they've all seen and none of them have thought about this way. Give me a reading of both films through that lens.

Things I'd want it to do:

- Sort the exchanges. Bonasera at the wedding with his daughter's attackers; the Don sending Tom to Woltz over Johnny Fontane, and what Woltz wakes up to; Sollozzo's narcotics proposal; Captain McCluskey; Senator Geary naming his price for the casino license and then Hagen arranging the dead prostitute business for him; the young Vito who kills Fanucci and afterwards finds neighbors bringing him their small grievances. Which of these are gifts in the anthropological sense, which are bounded market deals, which are plain bribery of a public official, and which are none of those — tribute, protection, sacrament? I suspect the interesting material is where the films blur the categories on purpose, so point at those specifically.

- There's a puzzle I keep circling and can't resolve. The Don turns down Sollozzo, and his reason isnt that drugs are wicked, its that his judges and politicians would never cover a drug traffic — he's protecting his access to the very state he presents himself as the alternative to. Doesn't that gut the parallel-order reading and make the gift economy parasitic on the legal one? Or is there a coherent reading where the Don is consistent, say that he's husbanding his political contacts as an asset of a gift network rather than buying them the way you buy a bribe? Give me the strongest version of each, and say which one you think the films actually support.

- Michael. He starts as the family's universalist, the war hero who tells Kay the business isn't his, and ends up more particularist and colder than Sonny or his father ever were, with the baptism sequence working as a kind of sacramental contract binding godparenthood to five murders at once. Then Part II is the gift order visibly rotting into instrumentality: Roth, Havana and Batista, the Senate hearings, Fredo. Do the films argue that gift orders are unstable and decay into business, or that they were business all along wearing a sentimental coat? The Don's generation and Michael's both talk about friendship and family and mean opposite things by it — where exactly do the films mark that difference?

- Where the reading breaks. I don't want a paper that turns Coppola into an illustration of Mauss. These are nostalgia artifacts that romanticize the violence the whole gift order rests on, and the horse's head is not a gift on any definition I've seen. Tell me which scenes actively resist the gift-economy reading, and whether that resistance is a problem for the reading or the point of it — e.g. is Kay's abortion best read as a refusal of the gift lineage, or does that over-read her?

- Finish with what the films give the debate that argument alone doesn't: something a theorist who never saw them would miss about how particularist obligation feels from the inside. My own candidate is that Bonasera's humiliation isn't that he has to pay, it's that he has to call the Don his friend and mean it — but tell me if that's right or if there's a better one.

No bibliography please, and don't recite lines of dialogue back at me, I'm not testing your recall of the script. I want scenes reconstructed and an argument about them.
