I write literary fiction, mostly about ordinary people being decent or failing to be, and I've come to suspect that the view of human nature I actually hold is an incoherent patchwork. I keep getting told it's basically Mencius versus Xunzi — sprouts that only need cultivating versus crooked timber that only a frame and a lot of grinding can straighten — and that I have to pick. I don't want to pick on the basis of an argument, I want to pick on the basis of what I actually believe when I'm building a character, and then find out whether that adds up to anything. So here are my verdicts, from books I've finished and characters I've had to scrap.

One. When my character sees a toddler wobble toward an open stairwell and snatches him, I write it with no thought in her head whatsoever — no calculation, no self-image to protect, not even surprise afterward. I believe that scene completely. The pull seems to be there ahead of any training.

Two. That same woman being unfailingly decent to her coworkers for twenty years? I don't believe a word of it unless I've put the work on the page: the grandmother's rules, the humiliations, the people she studied and copied, the years of catching herself mid-sentence. Alone in a room with no witnesses, my instinct is that she'd be petty and a little cruel. So one of her decencies is native and the other is entirely manufactured, and I don't feel any strain holding both — that's the pair I most want examined, because it looks to me like it should be a contradiction and doesn't feel like one.

Three. I cannot write a villain who was rotten as a child. Every time I've tried, the flashback reads as a lie, and I always end up rewriting the childhood as a hillside where something was chopped down and and grazed over for years, so that people looking at the bald slope assume nothing ever grew there. I don't buy born-bad, and I notice I don't write it either.

Four. But a character who is good purely because a school or a discipline or a set of forms made him that way, with nothing innate for them to work on — I think he's really good. Not second-rate, not performing. If he's done the right thing for forty years out of habit and duty, I dont get to say he's a fraud, and I get annoyed at readers who want to.

Five. And yet the moment I write someone who does exactly the right things and feels no pull toward them and no distress at the thought of stopping, the character goes hollow on the page. Readers say so, I agree with them, and I can't argue my way out of it. Four and five are the ones I circle for years.

Six. Smaller, but stubborn: when I move a character into another century or another country, every bit of content changes — what shocks her, what she defers to, what she's ashamed of — but that she is shocked by somethings and defers to something and is ashamed of something never changes. Form universal, content local. Which feels like neither camp, or maybe like water that has no preference for east or west but does have a preference for down.

So: is there a principle about human nature that delivers all six, or five of the six, or a revised version of the ones I'm fudging? I'd like it stated precisely enough to work as a rule I could actually build characters with — which clause is doing the work for which verdict, whether it needs a distinction I haven't thought of (I suspect something like the difference between having a sprout and the sprout ever showing itself), and what it says about cases I haven't listed. Two in particular: the man raised carefully and never once tested, who falls apart the first time he's genuinely in danger — was he hollow the whole time, or just never given a frame? And the character whose spontaneous, untrained impulses are reliably kind but whose trained behavior is reliably cruel. Also tell me plainly which of my six it has to break. My guess is the fourth, and I'd like to know whether that guess is right or just the one I'd least mind losing.
