I've been a working novelist for about twenty years and I teach a craft class, so I'm fluent in the standard vocabulary: want vs. need, scene objective, beats, the wound, telling detail, flat vs. round, "does the plot happen to them or do they make it happen," free indirect style, Chekhov's gun, the whole kit. What I want from you is a translation exercise, and I want it done honestly — please don't smuggle craft assumptions in and then hand them back to me labelled as philosophy.

Here's the material. I've been reading Dennett on the frame problem in AI (the essay with "cognitive wheels" in the title, I think) and his use of the digger wasp. Sphex paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow, goes inside to inspect, and if you move the prey a few inches while she's in there she comes out, finds it, hauls it back to the threshold, and starts the inspection routine over — and you can keep doing this and she never breaks the loop. He uses that to make a claim about flexibility and free will: the wasp isn't free because her behavior is triggered rather than responsive to reasons. Then there's the run of robots in his thought experiment — one goes in for a spare battery and drags the wagon out with the bomb still riding on it, the next is built to think through the implications of every action and stands there deducing until the thing goes off, and the third is built to ignore irrelevances and spends all its time outside sorting relevant from irrelevant. And there's an ethologist, Keijzer I believe, who says the wasp story is overblown: the wasps do eventually adapt after enough repetitions, and re-inspecting a disturbed nest is probably just good sense anyway.

So: translate that whole apparatus — the sphexish/flexible distinction, the frame problem as he poses it, and the free will claim riding on top of it — into craft vocabulary, term by term. Where does "sphexish" land in my language? Is it consistency? Is it a character with no interiority? Is it a want that never updates? Don't just pick the flattering mapping.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want:
- which claims map cleanly onto things I already say to students;
- which only fit if you bend them, and exactly how you bent them;
- which have no counterpart in craft talk at all.

And answer these directly. Craft's cardinal virtue is a character acting consistently with an established motive, but the defect Dennett is naming is consistency itself — so does my vocabulary have any way of seperating a character who does the same thing twice because their reasons persist from one who does it twice because a trigger fired? Or is that distinction simply invisible from where I sit? Also: I can imagine a writer saying the residue shows craft is missing something real, and a philosopher saying it shows the sphex/flexibility line is rhetorical rather than factual, which is where Keijzer's objection bites. Tell me which of those you think is right and why. Finally — can a character be deliberately, tragically sphexish and still read as a person rather than a device?

That last question isn't academic, because my editor says the woman in my current draft is a machine. The scene: Miriam's husband walked out in chapter three. She doesn't look up. She sets the second fork anyway and tells him dinner's at six. He sits. I wanted the reader to feel something give way; my editor says she's a broken record and I need to break the loop on the page. Run your translation on that scene and tell me which of us is right, or whether the question is one my framework can't even state.
