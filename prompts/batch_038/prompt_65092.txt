Next month I'm presenting at a workshop on intertemporal choice — the room is mostly behavioral economists who work on hyperbolic discounting and naive vs. sophisticated agents, plus two formal epistemologists, and basically nobody who works on money pumps directly. My thesis is a bit weird so I want to walk in ready.

Sketch of it: the classic money pump against intransitive preferences (A > B > C > A, with a small fee tacked on at the first swap) only goes through if you treat each node of the sequence as a fresh decision problem with the current status quo as the reference point. I want to argue you should count the whole sequence as one decision problem iff the agent has no reason to expect her future self to depart from her present plan. If she does expect that — a recognized self-control problem, or expected news that will change her ranking — then the nodes really are separate problems, and at each separate problem she's choosing correctly by her own lights, so the pump stops being a reductio. So diachronic rationality gets indexed to self-trust. I want to defend this as a substantive criterion, not just a relabeling of the naive/sophisticated distinction and not resolute choice with extra steps.

Give me the prep sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually press, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased in their idiom (they'll talk commitment devices, present bias, dynamic consistency, time-consistency of plans — not sure-loss and Dutch books), with a two-minute reply I can actually say out loud and a flag on where that reply is soft. Also tell me which one or two I should concede early to buy credibility, and the question im praying nobody asks. No need for references, I know the literature — I need the dialectic.
