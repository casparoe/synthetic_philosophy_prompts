so I've been maintaining this spreadsheet for like two years now, basically a miracle-calculator. you put in a prior for the event (I use 1 in a million for "a law of nature got violated on this one particular occasion"), a per-witness reliability number (99% right / 1% wrong, meaning they'd report it happened when it didn't about 1 time in 100), and a witness count, and it multiplies out the likeyhood ratios and hands me a posterior. I sanity-checked it on ordinary stuff — my cousin's friend who really did win the lottery, the guy who got struck by lightning twice, a coworker who swears she saw a deer on a downtown street at noon — and the outputs track my gut well enough that I trust the sheet.

then I ran the religious cases through it and got something I didn't expect. at a 1-in-a-million prior with four witnesses at 99%, the sheet says I should be ~99% confident the miracle happened. twelve witnesses and it's numerically indistinguishable from 1. I poked at it for bugs but it's just that 99^4 is around 10^8, which steamrolls 10^-6. there's no way around it, the multiplication wins.

my conclusion is that Hume has to be assuming a prior of exactly zero, because if the prior is any nonzero number the arithmetic obviously goes the other way — enough witnesses always out-multiply it. and if the prior is literally zero then the whole thing is question-begging, he's smuggling in the conclusion as a premise, and I should give it zero weight next time a theist friend brings up the resurrection.

what's tripping me up is that the passage I half-remember from the Of Miracles section doesn't sound like a zero-prior claim. he writes: "When anyone tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened." and then the bit about how only if the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the event can the witness "pretend to command my belief or opinion." that reads to me like a comparison that he just declares the winner of in advance, which is functionally a zero prior wearing a trenchcoat.

so — is that the right reading? is the argument literally a zero prior dressed up as a weighing of probabilities, in which case it's dead and I can stop steelmanning it? or is there something about how I set the sheet up, or about what he's actually claiming, that means "four independent witnesses at 99%" isn't a legal input at all? I'd like to know which knob is wrong before I bring this to the meetup, because right now I'm planning to present the spreadsheet as a refutation and I have a nagging feeling I'm the one who's confused.
