I run a monthly reading group that's mostly rationalist-adjacent people — half of them have thoroughly internalized welfare-based metrics (QALYs, the whole effective-altruism happiness arithmetic) and the other half are market types who think any non-welfare egalitarian metric is just vibes. Nobody has ever really taken Dworkin or the capabilities crowd seriously, and I want to shock them out of that with a designed case rather than a lecture. So: build me a distributional scenario that produces a clean three-way split between the metrics in the "equality of what?" debate. The three readings I want are (1) resourcism in Dworkin's sense, where equality is tested by the envy test over bundles, (2) welfarism, where what matters is how well off people are in terms of satisfaction or happiness, and (3) the capability approach, where what matters is the real set of functionings each person can actually get to. The verdicts I need: the resourcist says the two people are equal, the welfarist says they're equal too, and the capability theorist says there's a glaring inequality that neither of the other two can even see. Two of three agreeing is the whole point — it lets me press on why the capability theorist thinks the other two are looking at the wrong thing for two seperate reasons rather than one.

Constraints, because otherwise this collapses into the stock cases everyone's already seen. Realistic contemporary setting, no sci-fi, scenario itself under about 250 words. No impairment, illness, or disability of the disadvantaged person herself — I don't want this to be the usual wheelchair-ramp case wearing a hat. No expensive tastes either (no opera lover, no someone who just finds her bundle less fun). No price differences or geographic cost-of-living gaps that would sneak in an actual resource inequality: the bundles have to be genuinely the same, not the same in sticker price. The inequality has to come out of conversion factors plus how the person's own preferences got formed. For seeding, here are some mechanisms you could use, pick whichever generates the split most cleanly rather than the most picturesque: a municipal guaranteed-income pilot that hands every participant an identical cash-plus-in-kind bundle, where one recipient is a freelance coder with no dependents and the other has a care obligation for an adult relative; or a workplace that gives every employee the identical flexibility stipend, transit pass, and laptop, where one employee can convert that into evening study time and the other can't because of a household schedule constraint; or something involving a norm about who can be where after dark. One of the blocked functionings I'd like in there is the one behind Sen's favorite Adam Smith example, the linen shirt — being able to show up in public without shame — because it's concrete in a way my group will feel.

Then do the checking work, and this is the part I care most about: after the scenario, list the two bundles explicitly and run the envy test person by person, saying why neither prefers the other's; state each person's capability set and name which functionings are blocked and by what; state the welfare levels and give me the actual mechanism that makes them equal rather than just asserting that she's content with her lot. Say which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you pull it out — does the envy test start failing so Dworkin can see the inequality after all, or does the case degrade into the disability case I banned? Then give me the attacks: how a Dworkinian resists (I expect the hypothetical insurance market to get an airing — does it already cover this, so that the resources were never really equal to begin with?), and how a welfarist resists. And please tell me straight up if my spec is jointly unsatisfiable or if the only way to hit all three verdicts is to stipulate them instead of generating them from the theories — a case that cheats is worse than no case, since half my audience is actively looking for a reason to dismiss the whole debate.
