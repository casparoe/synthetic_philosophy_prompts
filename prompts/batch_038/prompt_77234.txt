so our reading group on fluid concepts is doing a session on Copynet-style analogy making and I'm building a tiny clone as a demo. my microdomain is strings of runs of two letters: seeds like `p pp ppp → p pp pppp` (grow the rightmost run) and `q qq → q qqq`, and then a target `p q p`. half the group says `p q pp`, half says `p q q`, and one person insists you need a double slip and that the answer isn't even well-defined. what makes the case nasty is that p is already at both ends, so "the rightmost letter" isn't singling out anything distinctive.

here's what's bugging me though. in the original architecture the slipnet's link strengths — how far "rightmost" sits from "leftmost", how tightly a letter's identity is bound to its run length — are all set by hand, and the codelets and the temprature parameter just do a stochastic search over that fixed structure. but then isn't every analogy the thing can ever solve already written in? whoever decided that group-length is one hop from letter-identity has effectively pre-committed the answer to `p q p`, and the workspace is just an elaborate retrieval mechanism. same worry for "conceptual depth" — feels like a redescription of the designer's hunches rather than a property of the concepts.

so: what's the cleanest way to measure how much of this kind of model's performance is genuine slippage versus baked-in lookup? my plan is to freeze the slipnet, perturb the strings, and look at accuracy falloff as a proxy for how much was really stored. is that the right decomposition, or is there a better one?
