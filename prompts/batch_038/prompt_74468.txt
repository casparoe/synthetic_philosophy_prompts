I'm an engineer on a small team that does reliability monitoring for a fleet of maybe two thousand industrial pumps — the job is predicting which ones are going to need intervention in the next quarter. Most failure modes have tons of data, but there's one (a seal degradation thing that has only occured three times in eight years, and half the fleet runs firmware that never even reported the signal we'd use) where any probability we publish is basically pulled out of the air. Right now the dashboard shows a single number with a squiggly band nobody trusts, and we've split into two camps. Half of us want to start publishing an interval as a first-class object — "failure probability for this mode is somewhere between 0.2% and 4%" — maintained as a whole set of priors that all get updated when new data comes in, on the grounds that one number claims a precision we don't have and then quietly drives our maintenance thresholds. The other half says that's cowardice dressed up as rigor: eventually you have to pick an action, an interval doesn't let you rank two pumps against each other, and (they claim) it also breaks learning, because when the data is thin the interval never narrows or even gets wider, so we'd be stuck forever.

Then I actually ran the toy version and the annoying thing is they might have a point about the wider part. I set up two failure modes on the same unit, seal and bearing, kept it deliberately small so I could check by hand: across every prior in my set I pinned the marginal bearing rate to exactly 0.3 and let the correlation between the two modes float over anything consistent with that. So before any evidence, my interval for "bearing is degraded" was the degenerate point [0.3, 0.3]. Then I conditioned on "seal is degraded" on one unit, and the interval blew up to roughly [0, 0.6]. I checked the code three times and the arithmetic checks out too, so I don't think it's a bug — but I have no idea wether my set of priors was just pathologically constructed or whether this always happens, and it left me genuinely unsure which camp I'm in. Our director wants one number for the risk register by Friday regardless.

So here's what I want: a dialogue between the two camps, set in our Monday reliability meeting with the toy example written on the whiteboard. Call them Point and Range, or whatever names feel natural. Both should be sharp and neither should be a strawman — I want the Range side to actually explain what a set of probabilities is capturing that the single number is hiding (including what my 0.3-in-eight-years situation looks like from each side), and the Point side to press the two hard objections: that what I found looks like learning in reverse, and that any agent holding an interval has to sneak a number back in the moment it chooses an action, so the honesty is only cosmetic. They should argue over my specific setup — is it a real feature of the math or a contrived artifact, and what would change if I'd let the marginal itself be vague instead of pinning it — and each side should land at least one hit the other can't fully answer by the end. Format: exactly five exchanges, numbered 1 to 5, where one exchange is one turn by each of the two characters, with the speaker labeled. No intro, no conclusion, no wrap-up paragraph telling me who won.
