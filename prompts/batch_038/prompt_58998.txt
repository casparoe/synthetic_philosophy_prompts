So I'm TA-ing a first-year seminar on science and the novel, and I've talked the professor into letting me run one session on Slaughterhouse-Five paired with the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for four-dimensionalism. The thing is, most of what I can find on the book treats Tralfamadorian fatalism as a vague "time is all an illusion" sci-fi conceit, and my students have mostly encountered the pop version of the argument through Penrose's Andromeda example — two people passing on the street with the alien fleet already launched or not yet decided. I want a reading that takes the actual argument seriously rather than the vibe of it: relativity of simultaneity, the frame-independence of what's real, and whatever that gets you. Can you do that reading for me, and be honest about how much of it survives?

Here are the knots I keep circling. First, I'm pretty sure Rietdijk's original paper pitched the argument as a rigorous proof of determinism from special relativity, not merely as a proof that past and future are equally real — so the conflation is baked in at the source, and Vonnegut's Tralfamadoreans seem to inherit it, announcing four-dimensional sight and fatalism in the same breath. I can't even remember whether relativity gets named in the book; I think they just say they can see in four dimensions. Does the novel actually need the simultaneity argument, or would plain predestination do the job? Second, Billy's whole condition of being "unstuck in time" looks like a moving spotlight scanning a block, which is not what the Rietdijk–Putnam argument delivers at all — it delivers a block with no vantage hopping around it. And the standard objection I've seen (Stein, Savitt) is that the present is a local notion that can't be stretched into global hyperplanes, which is exactly what Billy's hops presuppose. Is there any coherent reading where both things are true, or does the novel want an inconsistent package? Third, the consolation: when the Tralfamadorians see a corpse they say the person is only dead in that particular moment and very much alive in a lot of others. That sounds like temporal-parts talk — does the consolation work only on a perdurance reading, and does it collapse if you read it as a spotlight instead? And how does the "So it goes" refrain sit against chapter 1, where the narrator says something to the effect that there's nothing intelligent to say about a massacre and that everything should go quiet afterwards except for the birds? One of those two refuses to make death mean less; the other is a machine for making it mean less. I'd like to know which one the book is on the side of.

What I want back is criticism, not a plot summary or a lecture on the philosophy of time: where the pairing illuminates a scene or a narrative choice, where the novel actively resists the reading — including whether my colleague is right that the Tralfamadorians are just Billy's dissociation and the metaphysics lens flattens that — and finally what the book adds to the debate that arguments alone don't. My students are lit majors with no physics past high school, so tell me where I'd be forced to fudge or drop things to teach this honestly. Also please flag clearly which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested — I'd hate to walk in there presenting some disputed position (about whether the argument works at all, or about whether eternalism has any fatalist consequences) as though it were the consensus view. No references or suggested readings needed, I just want the reading itself.
