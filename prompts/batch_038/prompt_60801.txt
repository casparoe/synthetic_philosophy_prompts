Retired wildlife biologist here, thirty years banding gulls and piping plovers on the Atlantic coast, and only in the last couple of years actually reading philosophy and economics instead of just grumbling about it. Last month our county held a hearing on whether to spend real money now on a marsh-berm project where most of the flood protection lands somewhere around 2070. A consultant showed slides where the benefits fifty years out had been shrunk down to almost nothing, and when a woman from the audience asked why a flooded house in 2075 counts so much less than a flooded house in 2030, he said, well, a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. The room nodded, the chairman moved on, and that was that. Nobody in there had read anything about discounting, me included, but I walked out feeling like a switch had been pulled and I couldn't see which one. So: what are the seperate precise claims that proverb could be making, from the ones that are just true and boring to the ones that are controversial or flat-out wrong? As best I can tell it might mean any of -- that a good now is worth more than the identical good later even with no risk at all, pure impatience; that the two in the bush might simply not show up, so it's really a statement about probability; that the bird in your hand can be put to work in the meantime, so it's about returns on capital; that you might be dead before the bush birds come due; that whoever is around in 2075 will be richer than us so a dollar does them less good; or that this is just a fact about how human brains are wired, with no moral weight whatsoever. Some of those sound like they're about my own psychology and some sound like they're about the world, and I suspect the trick at that hearing was sliding between the two without anyone noticing.

For each reading I'd like you to say it precisely, then say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and -- this is the part I really want -- whether it survives the trip from one person deciding about next Tuesday to a whole county deciding about people who aren't born yet. Because when I banded birds I never had a hand-bird that was worth two anywhere else, and looking it up the proverb goes back to medieval times in one form or another, so it was written for a person with a short horizon, a mortal body, and a bush he could walk over and check. None of those apply to a public agency. I also read a bit afterwards that a mathematician-economist in the 1920s, Ramsey I think, considered discounting the future for no reason other than it being later ethically indefensible, just a failure of imagination -- is the proverb that, or is there a respectable version hiding in it? And there's an arithmetic question I can't get past: one now against two later doesn't imply any rate until you say how long the wait is. What rate does the proverb actually commit you to if the delay is an afternoon, a year, sixty years? The consultant was using something like 7 percent; does any reading of the sentence get him there?

Finally, tell me which reading you think people who repeat this line actually have in mind most of the time, and which one that hearing needed in order to work. I'd like it laid out as a menu, in plain language, that I can bring to the philosophy group I sit in on at the library -- they're a mix of a retired accountant, two teachers, and me, and we get through maybe one page a week. No reading list, please, I've got a bad knee and no access to a university library, I just want the reasoning.
