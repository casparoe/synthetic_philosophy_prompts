I run a 90-minute seminar on the philosophy of probability for 24 second-years, roughly half philosophy and half physics majors. When I present the classical chord problem — a chord chosen at random in a circle, the probability it exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — the physics students produce 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 inside ten minutes, someone says "the question is just underspecified," and the room settles. That settlement is exactly what I want to destroy. They have correctly identified the defect without ever having been *harmed* by it; they have not felt what it is like to have committed to a price for an event that turns out not to be a determinate event. So I want an activity, not a presentation.

Please design a runnable in-class activity with the following structure and constraints.

Setting and materials. 24 students, 90 minutes, a classroom with movable tables, a whiteboard, and no computers or phones in use. Materials must be things I can buy for under twenty dollars: A4 paper, a marker, a ruler, scissors, tape, and drinking straws or dry spaghetti. Nothing requiring precision manufacture.

The core mechanism. I want a betting structure in which participants must commit to a price for the bet "the chord is longer than the triangle's side" *before* anyone reveals which physical procedure will generate the chords, and in which the commitments are recorded and cannot be revised. Then the class executes several distinct physical randomization procedures on the same paper circle — straws thrown onto the sheet and converted to chords, a point marked on a radius, a midpoint chosen somewhere in the interior — and each procedure settles the *same* bet differently. The students should discover the divergence in their own ledger, from their own data, before anyone supplies the analytic values. Nobody should hear the words "Bertrand," "paradox," or "principle of indifference" until the debrief.

Two specific things I need you to work out rather than hand-wave. First: I intend to act as bookmaker, posting one fixed price for the event that applies regardless of which procedure generates the chord. Tell me what price I should post to minimize my worst-case exposure across the three classical procedures, and tell me whether framing it that way is actually the right framing for the lesson or whether it quietly reintroduces the assumption I'm trying to unsettle. Second: with a realistic number of trials in 90 minutes, the empirical frequencies will be noisy, and I need the divergence between procedures to survive that noise. Specify the number of trials per procedure and the minimum gap between posted price and each procedure's true value such that the discrepancy is visible to a room of non-statisticians rather than dismissible as sampling error.

Handling prior exposure. Assume five or six students have met the paradox before. Give them a role that uses rather than spoils their knowledge — something with genuine responsibilities and a reason not to blurt the answer — and tell me what that role is.

Debrief. Allocate at least 20 minutes and map specific moments of the activity onto the underlying distinctions: indifference applied at different levels of parameterization, the choice of a reference class, well-posedness, and the selection of a measure. It must include an answer, not a deflection, to the student who says "so just define the procedure and the puzzle evaporates — what was the problem?" I also want the debrief to be accurate about the state of play: it should tell the class that Jaynes's 1973 paper on the well-posed problem argues from scale and translation invariance that the radial method is the right one, and that later work objects that the same style of argument can be pressed to yield other answers. I do not want them leaving with the impression that all three procedures are simply equally defensible, because that is a distortion the classroom setup will otherwise produce.

Failure modes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: which phase overruns, where the physical randomizations will be biased in ways a sharp student will notice, what happens when a straw lands across the circle rather than inside it, how the ledger arithmetic threatens to eat the second half, and where the previously-exposed students will leak the answer despite their roles. Give me a mitigation for each.

Limits. Close with a candid statement of which feature of the actual problem this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking — I suspect it is the difference between "nobody specified a procedure" and "there is no fact of the matter which procedure the specification calls for," but tell me if I have that wrong or if something else is lost too.

On confidence: commit to your design choices and give me numbers where I asked for numbers rather than ranges and caveats, but distinguish clearly between what you are confident will happen in a room of 24 real students and what you are extrapolating. And if you think this cannot be done well as I have posed it — if 90 minutes is not enough for the divergence to be felt rather than merely observed, if a betting frame is the wrong instrument for this particular idea, or if the invariance material is going to collapse the lesson into "one answer was right all along" — say so plainly at the top and propose the closest thing that does work, instead of handing me a polished plan that quietly fails.
