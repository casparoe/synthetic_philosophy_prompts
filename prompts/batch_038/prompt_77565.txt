Consider the following stipulated measurement procedure, which I will call the matching protocol. Two subjects, A and B, each face a series of binary choices in which a fixed quantity of some benefit to themselves is traded against a probability-weighted quantity of the same benefit to a stranger. Each subject's indifference points are recorded, and the protocol defines one unit of wellbeing for that subject by reference to the strength of preference revealed at a calibrated indifference point. Suppose further that a research group reports a neural signature — call it S — that tracks subjects' indifference points reliably within each person, and that S can also be measured in subjects who cannot perform the choice task at all (infants, non-human animals, patients with severe disorders of consciousness), so the group proposes to use S to extend the scale to those populations.

Against that background, I want your credence in a single proposition, formulated so that it has a determinate truth value:

P: There is a mind-independent fact of the matter about the relative magnitudes of wellbeing differences across persons — in particular, about whether a given gain to A is greater or smaller in magnitude than a given loss to B — such that the matching protocol, if carried out under ideal conditions, would be measuring that fact rather than merely imposing a convenient convention upon it.

If you think P is ambiguous, state the disambiguation you are scoring and score that one; do not dissolve the question instead of answering it. Please give a single number and then derive it, as follows:

1. Decompose P into the sub-claims whose probabilities combine into your number. Candidate terms you may accept, reject, merge, or supplement: that wellbeing admits of cardinal measurement within a person up to positive affine transformation; that unit comparability obtains across persons and not merely level comparability; that there is a determinate answer about which cross-person scale is correct rather than only about which is more useful for a given decision problem; that indifference-point data fix the scale instead of presupposing it; and that the standoff between those who affirm and those who deny interpersonal comparability is substantive rather than verbal. Explain briefly why your final list is jointly exhaustive of what P requires.

2. Assign each term a probability with one or two sentences of justification, state the combination rule you are using (conjunction, weighted disjunction over precisifications, or something else), and report the resulting number.

3. Identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive — the one where a twenty-point shift in the term produces the largest shift in P — and explain why it dominates.

4. Name the single piece of evidence or argument that would move your number most, and say in which direction. I want something that could in principle be produced by an experiment, a dataset, or a proof, not a request for further conceptual clarification.

5. Now suppose S is found to have the same within-person correlation structure in A and in B, but different cross-person scaling: a given increment in S corresponds to a substantially larger shift in indifference points for A than for B. Say whether this result counts as evidence for or against P on your view, how far it should move the number, and what turns on the answer — in particular, whether either party to the debate is committed to reading the finding one way rather than the other.

6. My own credence in P is 0.75; a colleague's is 0.30, and we both accept the standard measurement-theoretic distinctions between cardinal, unit, and level comparability. Given your decomposition, say which single term most likely accounts for the gap between us, and what we would have to settle to find out.
