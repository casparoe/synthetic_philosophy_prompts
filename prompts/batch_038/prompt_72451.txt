I am a novelist, presently in the second draft of a book whose middle section unfolds inside a persistent online world of the text-only kind: no graphics, no rendered rooms, only lines of description the server returns in answer to what players type. Two of my characters marry there. A third has his character deleted, and his widow grieves in a manner I want readers to find wholly appropriate rather than faintly ridiculous. I have come to suspect that the metaphysics beneath these choices is far less settled than my prose assumes, and I would rather test it under pressure than read about it, because I want to hear the strongest form of a view I find attractive and cannot yet defend.

So: play a committed realist about virtual worlds — someone who holds that the chair in the game is a genuine object rather than a picture or a description of one, that the marriage contracted there is a real marriage, that being defrauded of an item there is a real loss, and that what happens in such a world is capable of harming the people who inhabit it. Play them sharply and generously. They know the objections and have resources against them; they will concede a point freely when it costs nothing and will never concede the main thesis. Do not name real philosophers, and do not cite books, papers, or articles — I want the argument, not the apparatus — and if you find yourself needing a fact about my game, ask for it plainly rather than inventing one. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that this reads as a conversation at a table and not a lecture, and stay in character until I say stop.

After every in-character reply, add a single bracketed paragraph out of character telling me which of the moves you have just made amount to common ground among people who work on these questions, which are genuinely disputed among them, and which you have extrapolated or invented for the sake of the exchange. Where the field is split, show me the split rather than the winning side; do not hand me a contested position dressed as consensus, and if the realism you are defending is itself a minority stance, say so there, so that I am not surprised later.

My opening move. You must distinguish the chair in my game from the chair in the novel I am writing, and I want to know what does the work. Both, so far as I can see, are patterns of information realized in physical stuff — yours in a server's memory and a client's display, mine in ink and in the heads of readers. Both are objects about which one can simply be wrong: a reader who takes my chair to have four legs when the text gives it three is mistaken about my chair, just as a player who takes yours to be sittable when it is not is mistaken about yours. Yet you call one of them real and the other a fiction. Give me the criterion. Is it interactivity? Then a branching hypertext, or an improvised character in a chatbot, slides across your line without my permission. Is it that your world is shared and runs on whether or not anyone is present? So, in the only sense that matters to me, is my novel's world: the text does not change when readers close the book, and two readers who disagree about it are disagreeing about the same thing. And what of the chair in a room no player has entered in a week — the data is there, but nothing has described it to anyone? Does it exist while unrendered, and if it does, in what does that existence consist beyond the description?

Then tell me what your criterion makes of the case I actually care about. My widow's grief is real in the ordinary, untechnical sense; the thing she grieves is, on your account, real in some further sense. I need to know whether those two senses can touch — whether her grief can be about her husband at all, rather than about a representation of him that she mistakes for the thing itself — because if they cannot touch, I have written a novel about a woman who is grieving a very elaborate nothing, and I would prefer to know that now.
