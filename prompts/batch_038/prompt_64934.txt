I spent thirty-five years in solid-state physics and have come to philosophy only in retirement; my reading group has asked me to set out where I have landed on Sartre so that the others may attack it, and I should prefer a consistency check beforehand. My commitments are these.

(1) Consciousness is not a thing with a settled nature, so no motive, no character trait and no prior state can determine an act: at every moment the agent could have chosen otherwise, and reasons underdetermine the choice entirely.

(2) Freedom is nonetheless always freedom within a situation: my body, my past and my social position are simply given and cannot be wished away, and it is only against that kind of resistance that any project gets a purchase.

(3) Because I am the one who confers meaning on what befalls me, I am answerable for my whole situation, including wars and injustices I did nothing to bring about.

(4) The one genuine fault is hiding this freedom from oneself — as when a man plays his role so completely that he presents himself as a waiter by nature rather than by choice — and yet I also accept that no project can be criticised from outside, since all of them are equally groundless.

(5) I find Sartre's objection to psychoanalysis decisive: a mechanism that hides something from its own owner must already know what it is hiding.

(6) As a scientist I continue to think that stable dispositions are what make other people's behaviour predictable at all, and that I have some knowledge of my own.

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on Sartre. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which merely look contradictory and dissolve once the terms are separated, and how severe is each genuine tension? Rank them, say for each what would have to be surrendered or weakened, and identify the single revision that would repair the most at the least cost. I should be grateful if the reply were written in British English.
