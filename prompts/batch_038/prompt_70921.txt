so — im six months into a novel that has a hospital ethics committee in it, and the whole middle section turns on one decision: there's a single course of some ruinously expensive treatment left in the region's budget, and it's either one patient who needs all of it or six who could each get by with a sixth of it. I want the scene to feel like real moral thinking rather than people shouting intuitions at each other, so I've been reading around, and a philosopher friend of mine explained Scanlon's contractualism to me as "a series of duels." Her version: you never add complaints together, you never put them on a scale — you march them out one at a time, two at a time, and ask whether the principle you're defending could be reasonably rejected by this person matched against that one. If the six win, it isn't because six beats one; it's because each of the six has a complaint just as strong as the one's, and no single one of them can be told to lose. That's what makes it sound like the numbers sneak in the back door while everyone's busy denying they came in the front.

I've started writing the committee's reasoning in duel terms and I want the analogy audited before I trust it. Can you lay out the correspondence for me — what's the referee, who's allowed to be a second, what counts as refusing to fight, what's a foul, does the crowd get a say — and then the places it breaks? Not the cosmetic ones. The ones that would make my characters reason badly if they took the duel picture seriously. My instinct is that duels assume two people with matched weapons and a neutral judge, whereas my committee has six people who never actually fight each other, a patient who isn't born yet, and a drug rep in the corner. And separately: does the pairwise thing actually deliver "numbers count" the way my friend says it does, or is the analogy doing the heavy lifting there? Walk me through what happens when the one's harm is much worse than each of the six's, and when the six's harms are trivial — does the duel survive those cases, or does it need a whole different picture?

Finally, tell me what analogy I should be writing with instead, if there is one. courtroom? queue? something weirder? I'll take anything that gives my chairwoman a line of dialogue that isn't a slogan.
