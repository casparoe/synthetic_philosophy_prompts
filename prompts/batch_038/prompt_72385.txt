My brother died in a car wreck almost two years ago and my mom has latched onto the multiverse thing hard. Every time his name comes up she says some version of "there's a universe where he took the train that day and he's still alive in it," and you can watch her shoulders drop, it genuinely helps her. My cousin took a modal logic course in grad school and will not let it go: apparently nothing can exist in more than one world, so at absolute best there's a "counterpart" of my brother in the train universe, and a counterpart isn't him, so what she's saying is either false or incoherent. She asked me to settle it. I obviously can't.

I don't want a lecture on modal realism and I don't want to be told everyone grieves differently. I want to understand the sentence itself. What are the different precise claims that "there's a universe where a version of him is still alive" could actually be making? Give me the whole menu, from the ones that are basically platitudes to the genuinely radical ones, and for each: what it commits you to (concrete other universes? just ways things might have been? identity that spans worlds, or similarity doing all the work?), whether it's defensible, and whether it's the kind of thing that could make my mom's sentence true.

Specific things I want pinned down, because this is where the argument actually lives:

- Which readings is my cousin even objecting to, and does "it's only a counterpart so it's not him" follow from counterpart theory, or is that a separate claim about what makes a person the same person that he's smuggling in? I suspect he's sliding between those.
- Does it matter that she means one specific nearby world — the one where he caught the 7:14 instead of driving — rather than just some world where someone like him is breathing? Like, does the smallness of the difference change who counts as him?
- The version she actually wants isn't "alive somewhere." It's alive, still married to Sarah, still my niece's dad, still the guy who'd call me about the game. Which sounds like it requires way more downstream stuff to line up than taking a different train. Is there a reading that delivers that whole package, or does every reading that delivers the package also make the sentence false?
- My cousin keeps bringing up many-worlds quantum mechanics like its a trump card that settles the metaphysics. Is Everettian branching even the same question as the one they're fighting about? Does it help her, hurt her, or is he just changing the subject?

Then rank the readings so I can see which ones everyone in that kitchen would agree to without knowing it, which ones are real philosophical commitments with a price tag, and which ones are flat-out false. And close with the honest bottom line: what, if anything, could make her sentence true, and whether that truth would do any comforting work or turn out to be cold comfort — because if the answer is "technically true but not about your brother," I'd rather know that's the shape of it before I say anything.

List format please, something I could actually read out loud at Thanksgiving without sounding like I'm defending a dissertation.
