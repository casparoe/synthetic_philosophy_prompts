I write a long-form science and ideas column for a British broadsheet, and I have been commissioned to produce a 5,000-word feature on the definition of life — the kind of piece that will be read by working astrobiologists as well as by the general reader, so I want my own house in order before I begin interviewing anyone. Over the years I have accumulated a working position, set out below. I should like a candid audit of it: which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which merely look as though they do, and what the cheapest repair is in each case together with what that repair costs me elsewhere.

Here is where I have landed.

1. I am deflationary about the project of defining life. I do not think the term picks out a natural kind, and I am sceptical that a list of necessary and sufficient conditions is there to be found; I would rather write about mechanisms than referee a word.
2. All the same, when I must decide whether a claim merits the word life in a headline, I use the operational gloss that has been standard in exobiology for decades — life as a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.
3. I take individual organisms to be the paradigm cases. This dog, that oak, the reader: whatever a criterion says, an individual organism is alive, and I regard any account forced to deny this of some particular individual as having gone wrong somewhere.
4. I think life admits of degrees rather than being all-or-nothing, and that this is precisely what makes the marginal entities interesting rather than merely excluded.
5. Dormancy does not remove something from the category. A bacterial spore, a seed in a vault, a tardigrade dried into its tun state in a museum drawer and revived a decade later: alive throughout.
6. Viruses are not alive, and I say so in print without hedging.
7. A discovery claim should be reported as a binary. Either a team has found life, or evidence of it, or it has not; formulations of the possibly-lifelike or life-ish variety are in my view a cop-out that costs readers' trust in the science desk.
8. On detection, I favour tests that are agnostic about the underlying chemistry — atmospheric disequilibrium, molecular complexity or assembly-style scoring, isotopic fractionation — over anything tied to cells, to DNA, or to terrestrial metabolism.

Three things I am currently covering make me uneasy about the package.

First: a group has published on an engineered bacterium maintained in a chemostat for several thousand generations. The strain has had the gene for a subunit of its own replication machinery deleted and persists only because the medium is continuously supplemented with the missing component; within the flask it has evolved resistance to a virus introduced as a control. My readers will want to know whether that flask is alive, and I cannot see how commitment 2 yields an answer that commitment 3 will accept — not least because the same clause looks unmeetable by an oak in a garden, which is also being fed by somebody.

Second: an obligate endosymbiont living inside the specialised cells of an insect host, its genome pared back to a few hundred genes, impossible to culture outside that host, yet reproducing, mutating and evidently under selection. In the same piece I want to discuss a sterile mule and a worker ant.

Third, and the one with a deadline: an untargeted mass-spectrometry survey of a deep subsurface brine has flagged a family of molecules whose complexity scores sit far above anything the abiotic chemistry in the same sample produces, and which shows a strong bias towards one handedness. Nobody has cultured anything; nobody can point to a metabolism; a rival group maintains the pattern is an artefact of ionisation bias in the instrument. My commissioning editor wants a verdict rather than a range, and I have six weeks.

What I want from you is not a survey of the literature and not a better definition; unless a new definition happens to be the cheapest repair available, please leave it out. Give me, in roughly 1,200 words: (a) the genuine tensions, ranked by severity rather than presented as equally bad, each with the specific reason the two commitments cannot both stand and the minimal revision that restores coherence, together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list; (b) the pairs that only appear to conflict, with a sentence saying why they dissolve on inspection; (c) the three cases above run through the package exactly as it stands — what verdict it delivers for each, and where it delivers none, which commitment fails to fire; and (d) your judgement on which single revision would resolve the largest number of problems at once. I have no attachment to any item on the list and no settled view about which should go, so please do not write as though I were defending one of them.
